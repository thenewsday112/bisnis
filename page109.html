#3478. The Essential Skill You Start Losing in Your 20s—and Runners Often Neglect to Train

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Explosive power is key to speed and running economy, but it also helps absorb impact and reduce injury risk. Here’s how to get it back—and keep it as you age.

Ann Chisholm, owner and coach at Tropos Fitness Club in Victoria, British Columbia, stands before a crowd of about 20 women huddling in a circle under an outdoor tent, eager to learn about strength training despite the rainy weather.

“What are three common weaknesses in endurance athletes I see in the gym?” she asks them.

The group shouts out guesses. The first two are easy. Glutes! Adductors!—Chisholm agrees.

What’s the third one? Hip flexors? They’re up there, but there is something more important, Chisholm says.

This is the last session on the last day of FeistyFest, a three-day movement and educational festival for women held in September in Breinigsville, Pennsylvania, and none of the previous groups guessed the third one correctly, Chisholm says, before revealing the answer: “Power explosiveness!”

You can think of your explosive power as those first couple of seconds of your movement, the initial burst, Chisholm says. You’re recruiting fast-twitch muscle fibers, which quickly contract and generate high force, and activating your nervous system, which fires signals to your muscles at a high speed to help you coordinate complex movements.

“Injuries don’t happen when you’re just simply running forward often,” Chisholm says. When she sees them happen most? While moving laterally—“when you have to move quickly or in a different direction.” And that’s where explosive power comes in to help.

When you run, your body absorbs two to three times your body weight, which puts a lot of pressure on your joints and ligaments. Explosive power helps you control those forces safely and efficiently, while also improving speed, stride efficiency, and joint resilience.

Let’s start with the bad news: a 47-year Swedish study from 2025 published in the Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle concluded that explosive power peaks in women at 19 and in men at 27. That’s when your body starts losing its first line of defense against sudden physical stressors, and you become more injury-prone.

The good news? “We can train that, so your body is prepared to absorb the impact,” Chisholm says.

Explosive power complements strength training, building on it. While strength training is all about the maximum amount of force a muscle can generate, explosive power helps you apply that power as quickly as possible.

Especially if you’re new to training your explosive power, adding one or two movements to your strength training session to start and slowly building up from there is the way to go, Chisholm says. “It could lead to injury if you do too much too fast, versus the actual goal that we want from it: to help with injury prevention,” she explains. “It’s really demanding, it’s hard on our body, and so we want to train it in lower reps.”

Besides how much, when you train explosive power also matters—you want to prime your body with explosive work before you get into strength. “When we want to train and get better at the explosive and power work, we need that fresh nervous system,” Chisholm says.

Chisholm is an ultrarunner, but she also used to play basketball, where agility and explosive power are essential and require training. Blending these two experiences, as a trainer, she programs athletic-style workouts that always include explosive power-focused moves in strength sessions. She starts with three Rs.

During all workouts, you want to train lateral movement—whether that is in the power and explosive section (for example, a lateral bound) or in the resistance work (for example, Copenhagen plank or lateral lunges).

#4007. Two Personality Traits Linked To A Sharper Mind

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

How you respond to beauty may have more in common with your thinking skills than you realise.

How you respond to beauty may have more in common with your thinking skills than you realise.

Intellectual curiosity and an appreciation of beauty are both linked to stronger thinking skills.

People with these qualities tend to perform better on tests of literacy, numeracy and problem-solving.

The findings suggest that a person’s interest in ideas and aesthetic experiences may offer clues to their cognitive abilities.

Curiosity could encourage people to seek out opportunities to learn, while stronger thinking skills could make exploring complex ideas more rewarding, with both processes reinforcing one another.

Intellectual curiosity involves enjoying ideas, wanting to understand things and being drawn to questions that require thought.

Aesthetic sensitivity describes an appreciation of art and beauty.

Both qualities belong to the broader personality trait of openness to experience, which captures how receptive people are to unfamiliar ideas and experiences.

However, openness also includes creative imagination, and this aspect showed no significant independent association with cognitive performance.

Imagination, curiosity and an appreciation of beauty may overlap in everyday life, but their relationships with thinking skills differ.

The conclusions come from an analysis of almost 68,000 people across 12 countries, including Canada, Germany, Italy and New Zealand.

They completed personality questionnaires alongside assessments of literacy, numeracy and the ability to solve problems as circumstances change.

Intellectual curiosity and aesthetic sensitivity were linked to better performance across all three assessments, even after accounting for other personality traits.

The connections between personality and cognitive skills also varied between countries.

Educational systems and cultural practices could help explain such differences, although the reasons remain uncertain.

Overall, the findings link both a desire to understand the world and an appreciation of its beauty with stronger thinking skills.

The study was published in the journal Large-scale Assessments in Education (Rammstedt et al., 2026).

#3486. Are You Restricting Your Breathing? 5 Ways to Learn Better Breath Awareness.

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

In Yoga Journal’s Archives series, we share a curated collection of articles originally published in past issues beginning in 1975. This article about restricting your breath first appeared in the November-December 1992 issue of Yoga Journal. You can find more of our Archives here.

Our breath is constantly rising and falling, ebbing and flowing, entering and leaving our bodies. Full body breathing is an extraordinary symphony of both powerful and subtle movements that massage internal organs, oscillate joints, and alternately tone and release muscle tissue.

The waves of our breath also carry emotional content, sexual stirrings, and messages from the psyche, and they open us to our own creative potential. All human movement is informed by this internal oceanic tide. Learning to move with our breath, rather than against it or without its support, can transform our yoga practice and our life. When we move in harmony with our breath, our outer actions will be in accord with our deep inner experience.

Because respiration is primarily regulated by involuntary controls through the central nervous system, we might wonder why anyone should bother to “work” with their breath. After all, our bodies are breathing automatically day and night. But is calm, rhythmic breathing the usual state of your body? My observations as a yoga teacher have led me to conclude that shallow breathing and chronic breath holding are so prevalent in our culture that a person who can breathe freely is rare. This is a frightening state of affairs, considering the impact poor breathing has upon our health, well-being, and quality of life.

What does it mean to breathe in a healthy, functional way? This question is more difficult to answer than one might think. As pioneering breath-worker Ilse Middendorf points out, at one end of the spectrum is the unconscious, involuntary breath; at the other end is breathing that is controlled and regulated by the will, such as the classic pranayama exercises. In between these two extremes lies the “essential” breath, a conscious flow that arises out of the depth of our being and dissolves effortlessly back into our core. To access this essential breath, we must first be able to focus on and perceive our own breathing process; that is, we must make the unconscious conscious.

Some schools of yoga have incorporated breath and movement by designating specific patterns of regulation—for example, inhaling for 10 counts as you lift your arms. While this approach may give the practitioner a certain confidence by providing a structure to rely on, it does not offer access to the essential breath.

Other schools treat the breath as something that is added (if at all) only after the asanas are perfected. This latter approach is analogous to trying to learn to sail without knowing what wind is. If we ignore the breath, we will rely on willpower to create and sustain our poses.

When our will is driven from an external reference point—a mental concept of how the poses should look or how our breath should move—we will practice to become who we think we should be, rather than discovering, through the self-acceptance that is implicit in free breathing, who we actually are.

Movement arises from breathing, and breathing is stimulated and shaped by movement. Each informs the other. Therefore, to learn basic movement we have to learn to perceive the fundamental flow of the breath—not in an artificial, controlled form, but in its purest state.

Even the most advanced yoga practice is of little practical use if we can’t extend our awareness into our everyday lives. After you’ve practiced observing your breath in controlled situations, pick one of the following exercises and work with it for at least a week before you continue to the next one.

Record the activities and situations in which you notice yourself breathing shallowly or holding your breath. Especially note your interactions with people with whom you are uncomfortable or anxious. Observe whether the other person breathes shallowly or quickly or holds her breath. Are you matching her breath pattern? Do you hold your breath during discussions that involve conflict or stressful topics, or when you find it difficult to tell someone the truth?

Choose one simple activity in which you have regularly noticed yourself holding your breath. It should be an activity where there are no time constraints or pressures, such as making your bed. Practice allowing your breath to move freely as an integral part of the activity.

Set aside one meal a day in which you do not feel any time constraints. Let yourself breathe slowly as you eat. Notice how it feels to allow your belly to release as you chew and swallow your food. Observe whether your meal was more enjoyable. If you tend to overeat or have digestive problems, did monitoring your breathing help you stop eating when you felt your stomach becoming full? How did you feel after your meal—energized, fatigued, satisfied?

Practice reading a short paragraph aloud while allowing the breath to support your words. Read at a speed that is congruent with your breath capacity, letting yourself pause when necessary. Then proceed to monitoring your breath during telephone conversations. When you feel ready, watch yourself as you talk with a casual acquaintance. Your graduation exam for this exercise is to practice breathing during an argument. How does this practice change the way you interact with others and the outcome of your interactions?

Punctuate your day with moments where you check your breathing. You might use a cue, like observing your breath every time you hear a phone ring or see your favorite color. Try not to chastise yourself if you find yourself holding your breath. Just note the situation you are in, notice how you are feeling, and gently return to a more relaxed breath pattern.

#1301. This Woman Lifted Weights 5 Times a Week for 4 Weeks—Here’s How Her Body Changed

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Makari Espe swapped two 20-min weights sessions a week for five 30-min sessions—and documented the results.

If ever you’ve wondered exactly what upping the amount of weight training you do could do for your body, let Canadian YouTuber Makari Espe’s story give you an idea. Already accustomed to 20-minute strength sessions twice a week, she committed to five 30-minute workouts every week for four weeks. The aim? To become “harder, better, faster, and stronger.”

To put her results to the test, she recorded her strength before and after the challenge, from how long she could hold a plank to how many chin-ups and push-ups she could do. Along the way, she also documented the reality of training five days a week on her YouTube channel. By the end of those 28 days, she wasn’t just feeling stronger—she could perform more reps, hold exercises for longer, and lift heavier weights. Here’s what happened.

“My current weightlifting routine is 20 minutes a day, two times per week, but I want to be harder, better, faster, and stronger. So, for the next month, I'm going to lift weights five times a week for 30 minutes.”

“Basically, the idea is to do two big muscle groups per workout so that each muscle group is rested by the time you work it out again and we're not all out here hating our lives. For example, it’s 7:00 in the morning and today we're going to be doing back and biceps. Tomorrow I'll be doing legs and shoulders, and then day three is a rest day.”

“I didn't come up with this routine by myself, by the way. I'm not a personal trainer and I'm very lazy when it comes to planning my own workouts. I wish this was a sponsor, but it's not. I'm just here to tell you that I'm getting all of my workouts from the Peloton app. Woo! This week I'll be doing the beginner 5-day program, and for the next 3 weeks I'll be doing these intermediate programs.”

Espe performed a baseline strength test at the start of her 28-day challenge and the same test at the end of the 28 days. She recorded the following results:

Before:1:30 plank / 1-minute wall sit / 2 chin-ups / 10 push-ups / 25 × 10-lb shoulder presses.

After four weeks:2-minute plank / 2-minute wall sit / 4 chin-ups / 15 push-ups / 25 × 15-lb shoulder presses.

As Espe’s body got used to the change in training frequency, she noticed her soreness increased. That post-workout pain has a name: delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS), the stiffness and tenderness that can kick in a day or two after a challenging or unfamiliar session as your muscles repair and adapt.

By week two, she said, “My triceps are killing me more so than any other muscle group this whole challenge so far, my body is sore in many places and I'm very tired.” She added that her hamstrings were “so sore” and her “legs hurt so bad.” By week three, she said, “The place where it hurts right now is my shoulder blades; it feels like somebody hit me with a hammer.”

The DOMS might have intensified, but she did work out an effective salve: active recovery. For Espe, that meant gentle cycling, swimming, and stretching, which helped increase blood flow to the sore areas and to ease the stiffness without adding to her training load.

She said: “It's 7:30 a.m. and I just went for a bike ride and now I'm going to go for a swim. I've been wanting to do that every day this week and I finally did it, and it was glorious. Best morning ever. And now I'm going to go stretch. The swim was amazing for many reasons, but the main one was it was so nice on my sore muscles.”

She didn’t set out with aesthetic goals, and it was her performance that really improved. On week three, she said: “I've crossed the threshold and I’ve found the joy in weightlifting again. I'm doing it because I know I can and I'm super strong. I just feel really strong and that's awesome.” A week later, by week four, she added, “I actually feel even stronger than I did as a teenager.”

As her fatigue increased in those initial weeks, by week three, she decided to take stock of what she was putting into her body—and her energy soared: “I'm putting so much more good fuel into my body which is giving me more energy to perform in the gym,” she said.

As the challenge came to an end, she noticed that her mindset had improved as well as her physical strength. “This is going to sound really cheesy, but it's because not only do I feel physically stronger, but I feel stronger in my brain. Like, I feel like I'm more resilient for having been able to push through all of the self-doubt,” she said.

“I will say that roughly 3 and 1/2 hours every week is a lot of free time to be able to put towards working out, but ultimately it makes me feel so much better outside of the gym, and that's kind of why I do all this.”

“So if you decide to do a challenge like this, absolutely take pride in your physical accomplishments, but please be sure to notice the benefits outside of the gym too.”

#890. A <i><em>Great British Bake Off</em></i> Finalist Is Finally Answering Our Biggest Behind-The-Scenes Questions

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

There are a lot of things The Great British Bake Off manages to cram into an hour of television: three bakes, judging, Paul Hollywood handshakes, and at least one person staring desperately through an oven door. What we don't see is everything happening around it.

Luckily, Christiaan de Vries has been filling in some of those blanks. The 2024 GBBO finalist, who made it all the way to the Series 15 finale alongside Dylan Bachelet and eventual winner Georgie Grasso, has been answering behind-the-scenes questions on Instagram about what life in the tent is actually like.

1. The cast really does stay close after the showApparently the bonding happens fast when everyone is thrown into the same high-pressure baking bubble, and those friendships don’t just disappear once filming ends.

2. A chat with the judges can cost you precious minutesThose workbench conversations happen on the bakers’ actual clock, so stopping to talk with Paul, Prue, Noel, or Alison can be one more thing to juggle when you’re already racing a timer.

3. Even going to the bathroom requires strategyThere’s no convenient restroom tucked behind the tent, which means bakers have to leave the set entirely. At one point, De Vries was so desperate he had to recruit someone from production to babysit his caramel.

4. The bakers basically live together while they’re filmingThey stay nearby and spend plenty of time together outside the tent, including post-filming dinners. Depending on the schedule, they may also get stretches at home between episodes to practice.

5. Contestants aren’t personally footing every grocery billThe show helps cover ingredients for practice and supplies what’s needed during filming. Production can get pretty specific, too: De Vries says they even sourced a flour he wanted from the Netherlands.

6. There’s no sneaky Googling when things go wrongPhones are taken away while the cameras are rolling, so once a bake starts going sideways, contestants are on their own. It also explains why so much of the cast’s behind-the-scenes imagery comes from production rather than their own phones.

7. What we hear during the countdown isn’t always captured exactly as we see itThe bakers are genuinely working against the clock, but some of the time-check moments used in the episode may be filmed separately. Crucially, that doesn’t mean contestants get bonus minutes.

8. Some challenges come with homeworkContestants have time to prepare and practice for the Signature and Showstopper, which is why those elaborate ideas don’t materialize out of nowhere. The Technical is the challenge where that safety net disappears.

9. The finished bakes don’t get chucked in the trash after judgingOnce filming is done, plenty of people around, including the cast and crew, help make a dent in everything the contestants produced.

10. Continuity requires a pretty unglamorous wardrobe sacrificeThose matching outfits across an episode are intentional, even though filming stretches over multiple days. Meaning, yes, the bakers may be returning to clothes that have already survived a full day of tent-induced sweating.

Obviously, an episode of Bake Off has to turn hours of baking into something we can watch in one sitting, so plenty will always end up outside the edit. But none of these details really puncture the show's magic; they only make what they pull off in that tent even more impressive.

Two large carriers from the world’s largest air market, the United States, bring capital, supplier leverage and connecting traffic, and Azul needed all three coming out of bankruptcy.

Abra, which controls Gol and Avianca, challenged the approval as any reasonable business would. CADE overruled the challenge and conditioned approval on a Merger Control Agreement that makes earlier private safeguards directly enforceable.

Abra is a competitor, so its motive is obvious, and a minority stake of about 8% is far from control. The concentration question remains reasonable not only for the Brazilian and broader South American markets, but also for the US. While collusion between these two are unlikely, sharing a common goal and insider knowledge as it pertains to Azul and Brazil could muddy the water.

It’s also important to note that American Airlines already partners with Abra in Gol and United partners with Abra as Avianca is a member of the Star Alliance.

Commercial agreements between American and Azul that have not been implemented, and Azul expects them only after antitrust immunity is approved (assuming that it is.) If it stalls, American will have paid $100MM for a minority position that looks similar to United’s. Just this week, American purchased two revenue generating assets (Boeing 777s) that almost certainly falls below that amount. #4270. Agentic AI in Production: Why Your Agent Keeps Answering After Its Tools Fail

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Publish AI, ML & data-science insights to a global community of data professionals.

When a tool breaks, an agent can keep going and write a confident answer from nothing while the dashboard stays green.

Picture an agent that answers questions about customer invoices. It has one tool, a lookup against an internal API, and a task that needs four lookups. The first two go through fine. Then the credential behind that API expires, and every call from the third one onward comes back as a 401.

What can happen next is the part that bothers me. The agent calls the tool again, and again, each time tweaking its arguments a little, as if the API had a problem with its phrasing. After a while, it gives up on the tool and writes a tidy, confident answer that covers all four customers. Nothing in that answer admits that the tool stopped working two calls in. The run just ends with a success status, and the dashboard stays green.

This article is about that quiet ending. It looks nothing like the failures we usually plan for in agentic AI systems, and I think that is exactly why it survives so long in production.

Most of the talk about agents in production is about the loud failures. Gartner expects more than 40 percent of agentic AI projects to be canceled by the end of 2027, mostly because of cost, unclear business value, and weak risk controls. That is fair enough, but those are the failures that somebody eventually notices, because a huge bill or a missing business case shows up in a meeting.

An expired credential is the opposite kind of problem. Normal software has a settled answer for it, which is to throw an error, page someone, and stop. An agent has no such instinct. To a language model, the error message is just more text in its context, and if the instructions say to answer the user's question, it will find some way to do that, with data or without it.

This is why I am not convinced that a smarter model is the fix. A smarter model is simply better at writing a convincing answer, and that makes the one built on nothing even harder to spot. What I would rather ask is what the system around the model does when a tool dies, and whether anything forces the agent to stop answering at that point.

It is tempting to treat every tool error the same way, and that is where things start to go wrong. A timeout, a dropped connection, a rate limit, or a 503 will probably work if you wait a moment and try again. A 401 or a 403 or a 404 will fail the same way a hundred times in a row, and every retry just burns tokens while the agent looks busy.

There is also a third group that I will not pretend to solve in code: the call that half worked, or the action nobody wants repeated, like a payment that may or may not have gone through. A wrapper cannot judge that. The best it can do is stop and hand the problem to a person.

The model cannot tell these groups apart, because to it they are all just error strings, so I take the decision away from it. The sorting happens in plain code before the model sees anything. Here is the function I would start from.

A few things in there are worth a closer look. The retry count has a hard ceiling, because a flaky tool should not be able to turn into a runaway loop. Timeouts and dropped connections get handled too, since they never produce a status code and are easy to forget. The sleep has a bit of jitter, so a fleet of agents hitting the same broken service does not all retry at the same instant. And a 401 gets raised on the first attempt, because waiting around will not renew a credential. One gap I left on purpose: a 200 response with a body that is not valid JSON would raise from resp.json() and slip past the wrapper, so catch ValueError there if your tools can do that.

One more detail: some servers send a Retry-After header with a 429, and honoring it beats a fixed backoff. It can be a date as well as several seconds though, so parse it with some care.

Capping retries stops the storm, yet the agent can still lose the tool and write a confident reply anyway. So the fix has two parts, and the first is what the model gets to see when a call fails. The wrapper below sits between the agent loop and the tools.

Each run starts with run = {"succeeded": 0, "failed": []}. Every failure gets recorded with the tool name and status, and the model receives a short instruction instead of a raw error body. Hand the model a bare 401, and it will improvise, so I give it a short sentence telling it not to guess.

The second part is a check at the very end of the run, and it asks a blunt question: did any tool actually come back with an answer?

The comment in the middle is there because of a mistake that is easy to make. My first version of this guard asked whether the result was truthy, so a tool that correctly answered "zero matching invoices" with an empty list looked like a failure and triggered a refusal. The guard now counts every call that returned, even when the result was empty.

Fair warning: this guard is crude on purpose, and it has a real hole. One success out of five calls is enough to pass it, even if the four failures were the ones the answer depended on. The stricter version states which tool each task needs, for example, that this question requires get_invoices to succeed, and refuses when that specific call failed. I would ship the crude one first and tighten it once you know which tools matter most.

The refusal message deserves a little care too. A reply saying the agent could not finish the job is far more useful to a user than a smooth paragraph that happens to be wrong. It is also useful to you, because it turns a silent failure into something you can count.

This is where observability earns its place, although uptime will not help much here. An agent can sit behind an API with perfect availability and still keep answering after its tools have failed on every single run, so the numbers worth watching are about what the agent actually did on each run.

The run record from earlier already holds most of what you need: how many calls succeeded and which tools failed with which status. The two log lines cover the rest, a warning for each failed call and an error whenever the guard refuses a run.

From those you can get three views that tell most of the story: the share of runs that ended without a single successful tool call, which tool and status fail most often (a sudden cluster of 401s usually means one credential expired), and how many times the guard had to refuse a run.

That last number is the one I would put on a dashboard first. If it stays at zero for months, either your tools are remarkably reliable or the guard is not wired to anything, and I would check the second possibility before celebrating. If it suddenly climbs, you just learned about an expired credential from your own logs instead of from a customer. If you also want retries per run, add one log line inside the retry loop of call_api, since a rising retry count is often the first hint that a dependency is quietly going bad.

All of this fits in a small record, two log lines and a decision about what counts as an incident, which is cheap next to finding out weeks late that your agent has been answering questions out of thin air.

If you run an agent in production, I would start with the least glamorous job and sort your tool errors into the ones worth retrying and the ones that are not. Go through the statuses your tools can return, decide which group each one belongs to, and put that decision in code, outside the prompt, where the model cannot argue with it. Anything that needs a human, like a payment that may have gone through, should stop the run instead of being retried.

After that, add the check at the end of the run, even in its crudest form, and give the agent an acceptable way to say it could not do the job. Then log failures with the tool name and status so that the refusals land somewhere a person will look.

The model is exactly as smart as before, and I think that is fine. An agent that keeps answering after its tools fail usually can spare, but nobody built the part of the system that notices a tool went quiet and says so out loud. Build that part first, and the smarter model will have something solid to stand on.
