#518. Two new siphonophores, one fiery red, emerged from 20 years of robot dives, and a biologist named the other after his mother

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Two new siphonophores, Bargmannia marlena and B. pyrosa, were named from MBARI robot dives, bringing the genus to eight species.

A dive by the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute (MBARI) off central California, researchers found two new species of siphonophores, which are deep-sea jellyfish relatives.

The first came from a depth of 6,440 feet (1,963 meters). It had long, bright orange feeding zooids – the units that make up a colony – and 11 swimming bells, and it now sits at the Yale Peabody Museum as YPM IZ 112077.

Maciej K. Mańko, of the University of Gdańsk in Poland, named it Bargmannia marlena after his mother, Marlena.

Mańko and colleagues from MBARI, New York University and Yale University described it this month, with a second new siphonophore.

The team based each species on two preserved colonies, a holotype and a paratype, a second reference specimen. All four came from waters off California.

Related to jellies and corals, siphonophores are gelatinous animals that MBARI counts among the main predators of the midwater, the open water between the surface and the seafloor.

Cat Munro studied their evolution as a graduate student at Brown University and wrote about them in a 2015 MBARI expedition log.

“Siphonophores operate as an integrated unit, and are the most complex of all colonial animals,” she wrote. In her description, each zooid works like an organ that swims, eats or breeds for the colony.

The Portuguese man o’ war, split into four species in 2025, has the same kind of colony but floats at the surface. Bargmannia lives deep, and it swims by firing dozens of bells at once.

In 1954 British zoologist A. K. Totton described the genus and its first species, B. elongata, from swimming bells caught in nets. Gillian Mapstone of London’s Natural History Museum added B. lata in 1998. A year later Philip Pugh added B. amoena and B. gigas.

In a 2019 Zootaxa paper, Pugh named B. profunda and B. stenotes, each from one incomplete specimen. That made six species. The new paper went online on October 16 and makes it eight.

MBARI’s October 22 announcement singled out the orange stomachs of B. marlena, which can stretch. On the holotype these gastrozooids reach about 1.5 inches (3.8 centimeters), and the whole colony measured 4.4 inches (11.2 centimeters).

The paratype came up 18 years earlier, on October 9, 2004, from 4,045 feet (1,233 meters) off California. Yale keeps it too.

Of the known species, B. lata looks most like it, but its bells and bracts (shield-like plates along the stem) differ in shape. A B. marlena colony also carries male and female parts on one stem.

The name Bargmannia pyrosa joins the Greek pyr, fire, with the Latin suffix -osa, full of, so it means fire-colored. MBARI says it has “a fiery red body.”

Most of its zooids are red in life and turn pale orange in preservative. Each colony is either male or female.

Its holotype came from 3,268 feet (996 meters) off California on March 23, 2002. It had 14 swimming bells, and the biggest stood 24.2 millimeters (just under an inch) tall. The paratype came up on May 18, 2014, from 7,451 feet (2,271 meters).

Mańko’s team then reread Pugh’s 1999 account of B. amoena and found it “was a compound one, combining B. amoena and B. pyrosa.”

The bells of related species grow along paths that partly converge with those of B. pyrosa. The authors think that helped the red species slip past earlier workers.

MBARI photographed a live B. pyrosa in 2015 in the Gulf of California at 5,564 feet (1,696 meters). The same year its cameras caught B. marlena in the gulf, at 3,652 feet (1,113 meters).

The team went through 155 specimens kept in formalin, most of them at the Yale Peabody Museum. The Smithsonian loaned its B. profunda and B. stenotes type specimens. For the B. amoena types, the team went to the Natural History Museum in London.

They measured the bells and the stinging cells, and the Yale Center for Genome Analysis sequenced the DNA. From the reads, the team put together mitochondrial genomes.

One genome came from a single bell of a B. elongata colony from 2004 that had sat in formalin since. The authors got the whole mitochondrial genome out of it with a method they describe as new.

Their tree puts B. lata and B. marlena on one branch. B. stenotes, B. profunda, B. pyrosa, B. amoena and B. elongata share the other.

On the lata and marlena side the mitochondrial genes changed order. The authors suggest that those colonies also went from separate sexes to both sexes. Their data cannot establish which change came first, they wrote.

The paper thanks the pilots of Ventana, Tiburon and Doc Ricketts, the MBARI robots that filmed the live colonies, and the crews of the R/V Rachel Carson and the R/V Western Flyer.

MBARI found seven Bargmannia species in its video archive, and the eighth only in museum jars. In its photos, the shallowest is B. amoena at 1,329 feet (405 meters) off central California, and the deepest is B. profunda at 10,712 feet (3,265 meters).

Seven of the eight species turned up off California, where the authors found their ranges structured along depth.

MBARI footage of deep-sea Bargmannia siphonophores, including the two newly named species B. marlena and B. pyrosa. Video: MBARI (Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute)

Steven Haddock, a senior scientist at MBARI, runs its Biodiversity and Biooptics Team. In March 2015 he led a Gulf of California leg on the Western Flyer. The ship left on Saturday, March 7, for dives with the ROV Doc Ricketts.

Munro, then in her second year in Casey Dunn’s lab, was on her third cruise aboard the ship. “Many siphonophore species are fragile and fall apart easily when they are collected by trawl nets. The best way to sample siphonophores is by blue-water diving or with an ROV,” she wrote.

A caption in the second expedition log says Munro “very carefully removes a delicate orange physonect siphonophore,” a B. amoena, from a detritus sampler.

Doc Ricketts also spotted the bumpy snailfish, one of three new snailfish species from the deep Pacific. MBARI says its research and technology have helped find more than 300 new species.

Public database records give some of these species very broad ranges. Specimens assigned to B. elongata appear there from near the surface down to 18,766 feet (5,720 meters). They also span tropical to polar seas.

The authors think the broad ranges of B. elongata and B. lata probably hide lineages nobody has told apart yet. They had few data for B. stenotes. The larger stinging cells of B. lata and B. marlena they read only as a possible shift toward bigger prey.

Siphonophores show up as well in deep-sea DNA surveys in the Indian Ocean. The David and Lucile Packard Foundation and the US National Science Foundation paid for the work. Poland’s National Agency for Academic Exchange and the University of Gdańsk also contributed.

At Yale, the colony cataloged as YPM IZ 112077 now carries the name of Mańko’s mother.

The full study was published in the journal Zoological Journal of the Linnean Society.

#2848. Octa Fleece vs Polartec Alpha Direct FAQ

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Octa Fleece and Polartec Alpha Direct are both ultralight, highly breathable synthetic insulations, but they achieve this through different fabric constructions. Octa generally provides a smoother, more abrasion-resistant outer face, while Alpha Direct maximizes airflow, low weight, compressibility, and drying speed at the expense of durability.

Octa is made from a specialized hollow polyester fiber with eight radial projections, giving the yarn a gear- or octopus-like cross-section. The hollow center and projecting “fins” create insulating air spaces while using less polymer than a conventional solid yarn.

In finished fabric, Octa is commonly made as a two-sided construction:

Some Octa fabrics use a grid or diamond-like knit, while others resemble a conventional fleece with a flat outer surface and fuzzy interior.

The main design advantage is that the insulating pile is protected on the inside, while the outer face resists snagging, abrasion, and wind. This makes Octa more practical as a standalone midlayer than very open fleece fabrics, although it is still not windproof and generally benefits from a wind shell in exposed conditions.

Alpha Direct is a low-density, open fleece structure designed to function without a separate backing or lining fabric. It consists of loosely arranged polyester fibers and fleece tufts held in a light lattice-like structure; in lighter versions, the fabric may be partly transparent.

The absence of a face fabric is central to Alpha Direct’s performance. It removes weight and lets moisture vapor and body heat escape easily, but it also exposes the fuzzy structure to snagging, abrasion, pilling, and tearing.

Octa feels more like a conventional technical fleece. Its inner pile provides warmth and softness, while the smoother exterior slides more easily under a shell and is less likely to catch on vegetation. The denser outer structure also slightly reduces convective heat loss, so it can feel warmer in cool, calm conditions without an extra wind layer.

Alpha Direct feels more like wearing a breathable insulation web. It is particularly effective during stop-and-go activity because it provides insulation without trapping as much excess heat as traditional fleece. During sustained exertion, its open structure lets sweat vapor and heat escape quickly.

The difference becomes most noticeable in wind. Octa’s face fabric offers some protection, while Alpha Direct lets wind pass through readily. A lightweight windshirt can turn Alpha Direct into a highly efficient system: the shell blocks convective heat loss while keeping the fleece light and breathable. Without that shell, Alpha Direct works best in sheltered conditions or during high-output activity.

In short, Octa is a more protected, versatile fleece construction, while Alpha Direct is a more open, specialized insulation construction. Octa sacrifices a small amount of breathability for durability and wind resistance; Alpha Direct sacrifices protection for exceptional airflow, low weight, and fast drying.

#526. Crows make decisions twice as fast as humans and scientists say it is not just an automatic reflex

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Carrion crows can make deliberate visual decisions twice as fast as humans or monkeys. This incredible speed is tracked in their brains and likely evolved to help them fly safely.

Calling someone a bird brain is meant to be an insult, but is it really? A carrion crow can tell which way a cloud of dots is moving in about a quarter of a second, roughly half the time a monkey or a human needs.

And it has none of the layered cortex mammals use for that same job, which suggests there’s more than one way to build a brain that makes quick and correct decisions.

Philipp Schmidbauer and Andreas Nieder of the University of Tübingen trained two carrion crows on that test and recorded from the birds’ brains during it.

The crows lost nothing in accuracy for their speed, and the neurons they recorded escalated towards making a choice the way primate neurons do, only faster.

In an interview with Earth.com, Nieder said the result “tells us that bird brains – crow brains, at least – are built for life in the fast lane.” A mammal’s cortex, he added, isn’t required for fast, accurate decisions.

The crows watched a patch of moving dots on a screen. Most of the dots jumped around at random, but some of them moved together: left or right, or up or down.

On different trials, 3%, 9%, 27%, or 81% of the dots moved together. On some trials, none moved, so the crow had to guess.

A trial started when a crow held its head inside an infrared beam in front of the screen, and two targets showed up on either side.

When the dots came on in the middle, the crow gave the researchers an answer by pulling its head out of the beam and pecking the matching target. Schmidbauer and Nieder timed each decision from the moment the dots appeared to the moment the head began to move.

Monkeys and people have done the same task for more than 20 years. In those experiments, neurons in part of the primate cortex add up evidence over a few hundred milliseconds.

The animal commits to a choice once that activity reaches a threshold. The open question was whether a brain with no such cortex does anything similar.

One crow did 55 sessions and the other 102, close to 280 trials in each session. Both responded in about 235 milliseconds on average.

The crows barely slowed down when fewer dots moved together. With none of the dots moving together, the first crow took 254 milliseconds, and with 81% moving together it took 220.

They were right more often when more dots moved together: more than 99% of the time at 81%, and about three quarters of the time at 9%.

The crows also took longer on the trials they got wrong, which is what a deliberate decision looks like.

Monkeys given the same self-paced test took roughly 410 to 600 milliseconds across three earlier studies, and humans took roughly 450 to 820 milliseconds.

Those primates answered by moving their eyes. The crows answered by moving their whole heads, which take more effort to get going, and still finished in about half the time.

The speed, Nieder told Earth.com, was “much faster than we expected.”

The crows stayed just as fast when the researchers changed the number of dots and the way the motion was generated. So the speed factor comes from somewhere in the birds’ brains, the researchers wrote, not in the test.

While the crows worked, the researchers recorded from 248 neurons in a high-level region of the crow’s forebrain.

Birds use that region for flexible decisions, much as primates use the prefrontal cortex. Fifty-eight of those neurons, about a quarter, fired harder for one direction of motion than for its opposite.

Nieder said the speed was only part of the surprise. “But what fascinated me most was the neural activity,” he said.

The direction-sensitive neurons started firing faster for the direction the crow was about to choose, about 67 milliseconds after the dots appeared.

That was roughly 170 milliseconds before the crow moved its head. From there the firing climbed steadily until the head moved, and the clearer the motion, the steeper the climb.

When a crow was slower to answer, the climb was slower too. In the last 50 milliseconds before any head movement, though, the firing reached about the same level on fast trials and slow ones.

That climb to a shared level is the pattern primate researchers have described for more than 20 years: evidence builds until it reaches a threshold, and then the animal acts.

Nieder said that despite “the very different organization of the crow brain, this pattern looked remarkably similar to what has been observed in primates.”

On the trials where no dots moved together, the crows had nothing to judge and could only guess. Even then, the same neurons began firing harder for whichever target the crow was about to peck, about 96 milliseconds after the dots appeared.

The same thing happened when a crow got a trial wrong: the neurons followed the choice, not the dots.

The researchers then trained a computer program on the firing patterns from correct trials. From the firing alone, it picked the target a crow was about to choose 87% of the time on correct trials, 73% on the guesses, and 68% on the mistakes.

Runs on shuffled data, used as a control, scored above 65% about once in every 20 runs.

Asked by Earth.com for a finding worth highlighting, Nieder picked those guessing trials. The result, he said, “suggests that decisions are influenced not only by sensory evidence but potentially also by internal biases and fluctuations in the decision process.”

Birds and mammals last shared an ancestor more than 360 million years ago. That’s long before the dinosaurs died out, Nieder said.

Since then the two groups have evolved different brains: mammals grew a cerebral cortex in layers, the neocortex, and birds grew clusters of neurons instead.

Yet the corvid family, crows and their relatives, has repeatedly shown sophisticated thinking: ravens can plan ahead, and crows can count their own calls.

Nieder said the speed may come from efficient connections between neurons and fast visual processing. Brain areas may also communicate quickly, he said, and a crow’s brain is compact, so signals have less distance to travel.

Bird eyes may also help explain the speed: some songbirds can see a light flickering 145 times a second rather than seeing a steady glow.

A key visual center in a bird’s brain responds within about 5 milliseconds of something appearing in view. The pressure to be that quick, Schmidbauer and Nieder wrote, may come from flight, from spotting predators, and from fast beak movements.

“Studying crows alongside primates allows us to identify principles of decision-making that may be shared across animals, rather than assuming that intelligent behavior depends on having a mammalian neocortex,” Nieder said.

Two crows took the test, and the recordings came from one brain region. The firing on fast and slow trials came close to one level just before the head moved but didn’t match exactly. So something besides the evidence may also be affecting the firing.

Nieder listed three things to test next. He wants to give crows four choices instead of left and right and see whether the same mechanism holds.

He also wants to know whether crows have something like confidence: can a crow tell a strong-evidence decision from a weak one, and how does its brain represent the difference?

The biggest question, he said, is where the speed comes from. The team has recorded from one high-level region, and what the rest of the brain does in that quarter of a second hasn’t been measured.

Nieder wants to follow the information through the whole brain, from the eye to the head movement that gives the answer.

He also wants to learn whether the speed holds for decisions that aren’t about visual motion. Until then, one region of the crow’s brain has been timed, and the rest has not.

The full study was published in the journal Science.

You don't have to put up with political calls any longer.

It’s election season, and that means political campaigns are trying to contact folks about their candidate or political issue. While they’re well-meaning, these calls can be annoying if you’ve already voted by mail or have made up your mind about who to vote for. Luckily, if you keep your  iPhone updated to the most recent iOS version, you can enable call screening on your device.

Apple introduced call screening in iOS 26. It isn’t an Apple Intelligence feature, so any iOS 27-compatible iPhone, such as the iPhone 14 Pro, can use it. I enabled the feature over a year ago and my iPhone has diligently held back spam calls, like the Hoover Dam, ever since. But you have to enable it first.

Here’s how to enable call screening in a few easy steps and what to know about the new feature.

1. Tap Settings.2. Tap Apps near the bottom of the menu.3. Tap Phone.

Under the section Screen Unknown Callers, you’ll see three options: Never, Ask Reason for Calling and Silence. Here’s what you should know about each of these options and which one is right for most people.

Choosing Never is the default option and lets calls from unsaved numbers come through and ring on your phone. It also documents missed calls in your Recents list in your Phone app, just like before call screening was available.

If you tap Ask Reason for Calling, your iPhone will ask anyone who calls you from an unsaved number why they are calling you without ringing your iPhone. After the caller answers a few questions, your iPhone will notify you with a transcript of the caller’s answers. Then you can decide whether you want to answer the call.

You can also pick Silence, which is the nuclear option for phone calls. Any unsaved numbers that call you will be silenced and sent to voicemail, no questions asked.

Ask Reason for Calling is the best option for most people who want to screen calls. I chose it, and while it notified me when scam callers answered some questions, which they rarely did, it also notified me when my doctor’s office called to go over some test results. If I had chosen Silence, I would have missed my doctor’s office (and choosing Never would mean checking every call if I was expecting something important).

If you choose Ask Reason for Calling and dislike it, you can always follow the steps above again and choose one of the other options. And remember, you can always silence your iPhone and not be bothered by any phone calls at all. My wife has not taken her phone off silent in months, and she remains blissfully unbothered by spam calls.

#4428. Should AI Decide Your Court Case? This Judge Says Yes

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In a few days, judge Geoffrey Vos will retire as Master of the Rolls, the most senior position in England’s civil justice system. As one of his final acts, he’s trying to stoke a debate about whether—and how—judges should be using artificial intelligence.

Under the latest guidance, judges in England are advised to confine their use of AI to administrative tasks, and Vos tells WIRED he uses Microsoft Copilot all the time to locate far-flung documents and summarize large bodies of text. But recently, he visited a court in Shenzhen, China, where civil disputes are already being decided by judges with heavy assistance from AI. Impressed by what he saw, Vos believes that courts across the globe should consider following.

That road is littered with pitfalls. Multiple lawyers have been caught citing case law hallucinated by AI, and in a first for the UK, somebody recently lost their license to practice in part because they misused the technology. Meanwhile, with chatbots as their legal counsel, litigants are flooding the courts with an unprecedented number of claims, some of them bogus.

Yet Vos remains convinced that AI will someday help resolve civil disputes more rapidly and in ways that cost far less. While complicated commercial disputes and sensitive family cases might require a human judge, he believes that personal injury claims, low-value professional negligence cases, and the like could easily be adjudicated by AI.

“What most people desperately want in civil disputes is resolution,” Vos tells WIRED. “The question is, where is the line to be drawn?”

Given his impending retirement, the debate for Vos is largely a philosophical one. But for everyone else in the profession—and for coders, cybersecurity experts, math professors, and a whole host of others—the rules are changing fast.

In October, WIRED met with Vos at his office at the Royal Courts of Justice, London. We discussed the ways that AI is spilling into the courts, the practical and ethical issues it raises, and how the justice system might adapt to serve a new generation raised alongside the technology.

This conversation has been edited for length and clarity.

WIRED: You’re just a few days away from retirement. How do you feel about leaving the post at this particular juncture, as AI begins to reshape how law is practiced and justice administered?

GEOFFREY VOS: When I started as a lawyer, we had foolscap paper, typists, and Tipp-Ex. We moved rapidly into word processors, faxes, and eventually computers.

Lawyers are conservative with a lowercase c. But that doesn’t mean that they should be backward-looking or retrogressive. It means they should be adopting change cautiously, with their eyes open.

The legal system is for the people of this country. You can’t be effective in providing access to justice if you’re not on the same technological wavelength. But it’s time to leave it to a new generation of leadership judges.

How have you built AI into your practice to date?

I don’t use it for writing judgments, but I use it for finding things out, navigating large quantities of material, and checking things. It saves me time. I have 11 meetings a day. I know there’s a document created years ago that’s relevant to my meeting—and Copilot finds it in seconds. I do that every day.

In law, isn’t the devil always in the details? Doesn’t confidence in the legal system depend on the understanding that a judge will review and consider every inch of every court submission?

I’m a little more cautious about using it to read papers for my cases, but that’s because I do the most important cases in the system.

People have confidence in our justice system because they believe that our judges have absolute integrity. In order not to lose that integrity, you have to explain how you’re adopting technology and achieve a broad consensus that it is appropriate.

How is AI spilling into the courts right now? Is it changing how litigants and legal professionals behave? What issues is it throwing up?

It’s changing everything remarkably quickly. We see a 10 percent rise in the number of county court cases started every year—and it looks as if this may increase. That’s because everybody who has a legal problem now puts it into Gemini, ChatGPT, or Claude. Ask it to create a particulars of claim, a case, a claim form—it can.

What proportion of these claims are based on sound legal principles, though? How often are people led by a chatbot to mistakenly believe they have a claim?

We don’t have the data. But I think it’s a mistake to assume that just because a claim is created by AI, it’s totally frivolous.

In a way it’s a good problem, because it shows there’s greater access to justice because of technology. One of the problems with justice in the past is that it’s only been available to rich people.

If people have a problem sufficient to bother ChatGPT, they probably genuinely think they have a claim. When you explore their problem in detail, you might find it is actually a bit different, but it may still be a legal problem and may still be reasonable for them to have wanted to bring it to attention.

People are not generally time wasters. They’re not generally trying to make trouble.

What about the opportunity for AI to fabricate evidence on which judges have previously been able to rely—images, video, voice recordings? Where does that leave the courts?

Often, judges have to decide whether a person is telling the truth. That is the most difficult thing to do, because humans are not bad at lying. But judges make these decisions every day. There’s absolutely no difference—in scale or qualitatively—in somebody producing a false image created by AI.

The only reason we’re all getting so excited about deepfakes is because they are so new. But actually, that is the stuff of judging.

But we’re talking about a whole category of evidence that’s now somewhat compromised. Doesn’t that affect a judge’s ability to decide whether somebody is lying?

It was always thus. We always had to decide whether wills were forged, because people used to do that. It’s exactly the same with voice recordings, and video. It’s just an extension of the type of false information that is produced to the court.

People will find out when they produce fake images to the court that they are committing perjury and they will go to prison, just as if they’d forged a will.

I think we need to be cautious about predicting disaster. I’m very optimistic.

You’ve previously described an “inevitable” future where simpler civil cases are adjudicated entirely by AI. Why are you confident that’s where we’re headed?

You need to find a way to deliver justice that is within the cultural grain. That doesn’t mean you just conduct all your cases on TikTok. But if you don’t understand that people are asking LLMs for the answer to almost every question under the sun, including legal questions, you’re likely to get it wrong when you try to devise a process fit for the 21st century.

It’s all about consensus. It will only be possible to ask people to accept algorithmic decision-making if they consent to the process in a particular case, or if society decides through legislation.

When people go to court and get an adverse outcome, even if they regard the outcome as idiosyncratic or even wrong, when it’s delivered in a human way, it can be accepted over time. With AI, we have to be careful; it may be less easy for people to accept an adverse machine-driven decision.

I very much want to see a very open, international discussion about how we should resolve disputes.

I suspect part of the reason people object to trial by AI is because we can’t know precisely how a model has arrived at a decision. What’s to be done about this problem of explainability?

I don’t think it’s about reasoning; it’s about outcomes. The AI can produce very plausible reasoning. You can’t see how it produced that reasoning, but you can see the reasoning it produced, and you can judge the plausibility.

I don’t think you’ll ever do away with human judges. There’s something almost biblical about the human judge; it’s in the human psyche to want to be judged by their fellow man. But that does not mean that in some situations people wouldn’t rather have a quick outcome—even if it’s not a human outcome.

I don’t think judges are yet in the position to say. It’s a question for society, politicians, and legislation to resolve. That debate is really important.

I think that custody and family cases are one of the areas where essentially human judgment is expected, even by the younger generation. In complex commercial cases, I think that’s also the case. But I think we will whittle away at how commercial cases are decided by using AI to collate information to make the decision-making process better and easier.

We will allow more people access to justice more cheaply and more quickly using technology. That will be an overall good for the economy and for the people we serve.
