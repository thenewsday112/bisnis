#863. Vietnamese Coffee Martini

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

You get dessert and a cocktail all in one with this Vietnamese coffee martini, which adds sweetened condensed milk to the classic espresso martini for a rich, frothy sip.

Whipping up a Vietnamese coffee martini after dinner means two things: You don’t have to make dessert, and you aren’t going to sleep anytime soon. This cocktail is next-level sweet and caffeinated, and it makes your regular espresso martini look like child’s play. Sure, this cocktail shares some of the same elements (vodka, coffee liqueur and espresso), but it includes sweetened condensed milk for a rich, luxurious texture, signature caramel sweetness and super-frothy finish.

Vietnamese coffee is typically made with dark-roasted robusta beans, which taste bolder and more chocolatey than the arabica beans in most coffee blends. In traditional preparations, the grounds drip slowly through a small metal filter called a phin, straight into a glass with a layer of sweetened condensed milk waiting at the bottom. Fresh milk was hard to come by and didn’t keep well in Vietnam’s heat, so canned milk became the go-to. It’s almost as thick as syrup and tastes a little like melted caramel. Shaken into a cocktail, it lands somewhere closer to a creamy Baileys espresso martini than a classic one, only richer.

Fill a cocktail shaker three-fourths full with ice. Add the vodka, espresso, sweetened condensed milk and Kahlua. Cover the shaker and shake hard until condensation forms on the outside, 10 to 15 seconds.

Strain the cocktail into a glass and top with the coffee beans. Serve immediately, while the foam is at its fluffiest.

Editor’s Tip: Chill your martini glass or coupe for 10 to 15 minutes before you start mixing. A frosty glass keeps the cocktail colder longer and helps the foam hold its shape.

Enjoy a Vietnamese coffee espresso martini right after you shake it, since the foam starts to deflate as it sits. If you’ve mixed up a little extra, store the unshaken mixture of vodka, coffee, condensed milk and Kahlua in a sealed jar in the refrigerator for up to two days. Give it a good stir, then shake it over fresh ice right before serving to revive the froth.

Making Vietnamese coffee martinis ahead of time is a smart move for hosting. Brew and chill the espresso or cold brew up to a day ahead, then mix the cocktail without ice. Refrigerate the batched cocktail until guests arrive. When it’s go time, shake each drink to order with fresh ice so every glass gets that signature foamy top.

For a dairy-free Vietnamese coffee martini, swap the regular sweetened condensed milk for sweetened condensed coconut milk. You should be able to find it in most grocery stores near the baking aisle. It shakes up just as creamy and sweet, with a subtle toasted coconut flavor that plays beautifully with the coffee. Kahlua is dairy-free, so the rest of the recipe stays the same.

Vietnamese coffee is usually made with robusta beans, which have nearly twice the caffeine of the arabica beans used in most espresso blends. Ounce for ounce, phin-brewed Vietnamese coffee can rival a shot of espresso, which is why it stands up so well to sweet condensed milk.

The foam comes from the oils in freshly brewed coffee, so espresso gives you the most dramatic froth, while cold brew and instant coffee create a slightly thinner layer. The condensed milk helps, too, adding body that traps air bubbles as you shake. Use plenty of ice, shake like you mean it and strain right away into a chilled glass so the foam settles on top in a smooth, creamy cap.

A former assistant women’s volleyball coach for San José State University on Oct. 13 filed a renewed lawsuit alleging that her dismissal for raising concerns about a transgender player constituted sex discrimination and a violation of the First and 14th Amendments.

The plaintiff in Batie-Smoose v. The Board of Trustees of the California State University, a Christian who “holds a sincere religious belief that God created human beings male and female,” opposed the inclusion of the player on the team’s roster. In 2024, she filed a complaint and request for investigation with SJSU’s Title IX office and other officials, after which she was suspended. SJSU declined to renew her position in 2025.

Initially, the plaintiff filed her lawsuit against SJSU and other defendants in the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California in September 2025. But months later, a federal judge granted the defendants’ motion to transfer the case’s venue to the court’s Northern District.

The plaintiff said she learned of the player only a short time after she began her employment at SJSU in 2023. The team’s head coach allegedly confirmed the player was transgender to the plaintiff, warned her not to disclose this to players, recruits, families or staff and said she would be terminated if she did so.

The plaintiff then began to raise concerns, telling the head coach that she “could not in good conscience treat a male athlete as female.” She also became concerned that SJSU’s treatment of the player, compared to other female athletes, may have constituted sex discrimination under Title IX.

After a series of April 2024 news articles publicized that the player was transgender, the plaintiff alleged that SJSU assigned its assistant athletic director of diversity, equity and inclusion to “monitor” her and ensure that she did not discuss the issue. She further alleged that similar monitoring was not imposed on other coaches.

In September of that year, a co-captain of the team joined a lawsuit against the team’s conference alleging violations of Title IX. The plaintiff said she again spoke up to the head coach about her concerns, only to be told that raising the issue further could result in her termination. She submitted her Title IX complaint shortly thereafter and allegedly disclosed the complaint to the media.

SJSU acknowledged receipt of the complaint and requested a meeting with the plaintiff. However, the plaintiff alleged that this meeting did not occur until after she had been removed from the volleyball program and placed on administrative leave. SJSU sent the plaintiff a notice that her position would not be renewed while the Title IX investigation was pending.

SJSU referred HR Dive to the California State University system for comment. Cal State declined to comment.

In a memorandum submitted in 2025 as part of the plaintiff’s original lawsuit, SJSU argued that the plaintiff’s claims failed as a matter of law because the allegations revealed that the university had a non-discriminatory and non-retaliatory motive in suspending her.

Among other bases, SJSU said it had done so because the plaintiff disclosed personally identifiable information about the player, an act it claimed violated the Family Education Rights and Privacy Act of 1974. The plaintiff disputed this finding in her complaint, claiming that the information she disclosed “was from public or personal knowledge” rather than FERPA-protected.

In January 2026, the U.S. Department of Education’s Office for Civil Rights published its finding that SJSU violated Title IX in part by subjecting the plaintiff to disparate treatment in employment on the basis of sex. It noted that SJSU suspended the plaintiff for her alleged FERPA violations but did not similarly suspend the head coach, who discussed the matter with the media.

The legal saga has led to a variety of related litigation, including an ongoing lawsuit filed against the Trump administration by Cal State challenging the former’s threat to withhold federal funding over the latter’s inclusion of transgender athletes.

It is also part of a broader shift at the federal level with respect to the occasional clash between employees with sincerely held religious beliefs and the concept of gender identity. The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission has been at the center of that shift. Earlier this month, EEOC described as accommodations conduct that its Biden-era counterpart once classified as discriminatory towards transgender people.

#1649. Teachers spend about 8 hours per week on student well-being

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Nearly all surveyed teachers said they provide student mental health supports, but half say this task has recently become harder, a Rand Corp. analysis says.

The most common student well-being supports teachers said they provided were checking in on students, helping foster empathy or peer relationships, and collaborating with a school counselor or social worker. Teachers reported referring students or families to social services as the least frequent method of support, although nearly 75% of teachers still did so at least once during the 2025-26 school year.

Higher percentages of high school and middle school teachers — compared to elementary educators — reported more moderate or serious challenges in most areas impacting student well-being. For example, 77% of high school teachers cited student apathy as a challenge compared to 57% of elementary teachers.

However, high school and middle school teachers were less likely to frequently provide mental health supports compared to elementary educators. About 29% of high school teachers, for instance, said they frequently helped students navigate home or personal issues compared to 48% of elementary teachers.

About 2 of the 8 hours per week that teachers spent addressing student well-being took place outside of teachers' regular contract working hours, Rand Corp. found. Teachers who reported that they provided more student well-being supports were more likely to say that teaching was emotionally draining.

Teachers who said it had become harder to support student well-being since fall 2024 were more likely to say they were experiencing burnout, frequent job-related stress, difficulty coping with that stress, and symptoms of depression.

The analysis, which was sponsored by the National Education Association and the American Federation of Teachers, was drawn from the 2026 State of the American Teacher survey, a nationally representative poll of 829 K-12 public school teachers.

To help teachers support student well-being, the Rand Corp. analysis recommends districts implement wraparound services for students and families such as mental health care, food assistance and housing support. Schools could also partner with community organizations to help address these needs, the analysis said.

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I can smell the embers of a Nordic trend in the making from miles away. After all, even before the Swedish ethos of lagom (optimal balance) captured the world a few years ago, I’d been unpacking this Nordic mindset around wellness and lifestyle in my storytelling for years. I would go on to write an international bestseller, Lagom, with over 15 foreign editions.

From Swedish massages to Finnish saunas, the region is synonymous with wellbeing. While Denmark gave the world hygge (intentional presence), Norway friluftsliv (open-air living), Finland sisu (grit), and Iceland birthed “Viking wellness” with hot baths and cold dips, the reality is that all Nordic cultures share these values under different names. What makes lifestyle trends from the region particularly appealing globally is the foundations on which they’re built.

From sustainability-focused mindsets and a return to simplicity to curb undue stress, to government-sanctioned free access to nature (think Allemansrätten in Sweden), which fosters symbiotic relationships with the outdoors, and overall accessibility for everyone because they are egalitarian countries. The Nordic communal goal is a focus on holistic well-being that can be sustained over the long term by every individual, from professional athletes in their prime to elders.

The latest Nordic wellness trend, however, has rather practical origins. Ever wonder how cross-country skiers train when there’s no snow? Introducing Nordic walking; not to be confused with hiking with trekking poles.

According to its governing body, the International Nordic Walking Federation (INWA), which was founded in 2000, Nordic walking is now practised in over 40 countries for health, fitness, recreation, social connection, and competitive sport. As early as the 1930s, competitive cross-country skiers used poles with running shoes for summer training. But it was physical education (PE) teacher Leena Jääskeläinen who brought the concept to the general public in 1966, making it the first documented use of ski poles outside competitive training.

“I started Nordic walking in 1998 because it is a fun, social and efficient form of physical activity,” shares Aki Karihtala, president of INWA. “It takes people back to nature.” At the time, he headed the Sports division at Finnish Sporting goods company Exel, the world's largest cross-country ski pole manufacturer.

It was Karihtala himself, alongside his team at Exel, who coined the term Nordic walking, which has been widely adopted “regardless of age, religion and culture, because there are no boundaries,” notes Karihtala, highlighting its accessibility. In 1997, Exel designed the first Nordic Walker® poles specifically for the activity. A year later, over 500,000 Finns, in a population of roughly five million at the time, had tried Nordic walking. It's said to be especially effective because it engages the whole body while reducing pressure on your knees with ergonomically designed poles. The federation developed its progressive INWA 10 Step Method™ to teach people the simple technique safely and effectively.

Finnish trend and branding expert Katja Presnal is a newer convert to Nordic walking. “I’ve studied trends for over 20 years,” shares Presnal. Her personal lifestyle digital platform was read by over 100,000 monthly readers and millions annually. The irony of Nordic wellness being the hottest lifestyle trend at the moment isn’t lost on Presnal, who grew up in the Turku archipelago surrounded by bays and lush forests. According to Presnal, after two decades of travelling, she stopped “preaching trends” and started “walking the walk” by purchasing a 100-year-old former dairy tucked away in the Finnish countryside for a slower, more intentional way of life surrounded by nature, lakes, and walking trails.

“It was about making everyday life a wellness regimen and creating my own 365-day retreat,” adds Presnal, noting that these were the type of slow living wellness experiences her family would seek in places like Tuscany while travelling. “So I have, in essence, returned to my Nordic wellness roots, which in hindsight was the richness of my childhood.”

The low-threshold accessibility, from a physical and financial standpoint, is what makes Nordic Walking perfect right now for newbies like Presnal. Another benefit Presnal notes is connectivity with people.

“People are now discovering Nordic lifestyles,” explains Presnal. “You don't recognise your Nordic way of living as something special that could become a global trend or could be taught and shared with somebody because it's just a part of who you are.”

Actor Christina Applegate was diagnosed with multiple sclerosis (MS) over five years ago — but this week, she announced that a recent reevaluation found that she doesn't have the condition after all. In the wake of that revelation, she's been left without a diagnosis, and she said on a podcast that she felt she was "back at square one, where I don't know what's going on with me."

Applegate's case is not totally unique, although MS diagnosis has improved over the years. Studies offer different estimates of how frequently MS is misdiagnosed, with rates ranging from 5% to over 40%, depending on the patient population and clinical setting. There is also some degree of underdiagnosis — cases in which people with MS aren't flagged as having the disease.

So, how difficult is MS to identify, and what does that mean for patients? Live Science spoke with experts about the current state of MS diagnostics and the improvements that may be coming in the future.

"It's also really important that doctors and patients be aware that it's not inappropriate to revisit a diagnosis — to make sure it's right," Dr. Ari Green, chief of the Division of Neuroimmunology and Glial Biology at the University of California, San Francisco, told Live Science.

MS is an autoimmune disorder in which the immune system attacks the central nervous system, meaning the brain and spinal cord. These attacks harm neurons directly and also take aim at myelin — a fatty, insulating substance that coats neurons' outgoing wires and helps them send signals efficiently.

This damage leads to distinctive scarring in nerve tissue and causes a range of symptoms, including vision problems, muscle weakness, tingling sensations, dizziness and bladder control issues. Patients with untreated and advanced MS can lose the ability to walk and stand or, in some cases, develop partial or complete paralysis.

You're trying to be as proactive as possible with this.

These severe outcomes are becoming rarer as treatments have improved significantly, Green said. Current treatments keep immune attacks at bay and thus prevent further nerve damage.

"It's moved from people ending up with severe, profound disability at 10 or 20 years of disease, and now it's at like 30 or 40 years of disease, they have modest disability," Green told Live Science. The disease most often emerges between age 20 and 40, although it can occur in any age group. Importantly, Green said, "the earlier people initiate treatment who have MS, the better they do over the long term."

For that reason, it's important to reach an MS diagnosis efficiently, so that patients can start that critical treatment, said Dr. Yohance Allette, a neuroimmunologist and researcher at Penn State Health. "You're trying to be as proactive as possible with this."

However, there is no single, surefire test that can reliably show that a person has MS. "With MS, it comes down to the keyword 'criteria," Allette said.

MS experts have compiled a list of criteria, which are updated periodically, that reflect how the features of MS show up in lab tests; brain, optic nerve and spinal cord scans; and patients' symptoms. (Green was among the dozens of experts involved in the latest update, which was conducted in 2024 and published in 2025.)

So, what does diagnosis look like for a patient today? The first step is to recognize symptoms that might point to MS.

Those early symptoms — such as issues with balance, vision or bowel control — vary significantly from person to person and may not immediately raise red flags that the patient has a neurological problem, Green said. The way those symptoms emerge can be a clue, though; typically, MS symptoms worsen over the course of a day or a few days and then dissipate, he noted.

Once concerning symptoms are flagged, MRIs of the brain and spinal cord are taken. The scans may reveal evidence of myelin damage, and neurologists note the shape, size and location of those lesions. Damage often appears near blood vessels, since that's where immune cells access the brain, Green noted.

Other conditions — like migraine, uncontrolled diabetes or vascular disease — can also come with some myelin damage, so it's key to look for specific patterns related to MS. Neurologists who specialize in MS are practiced at this task, while doctors in other neurological specialties may not know how to distinguish MS-related myelin damage from that caused by other conditions, Allette and Green both noted.

Myelin (yellow) helps neurons send messages efficiently. In MS, the immune system attacks myelin, causing it to break down.

MRIs of the optic nerves can also show MS-related damage, and these scans are emphasized in the latest diagnostic criteria, Allette noted. Electrophysiologic tests, which assess the function of nerves, are also "super important because they tell you something totally different than just seeing a lesion on MRI," Green said. Further signs of MS can be found via spinal taps, which sample the fluid around the brain and spinal cord that may carry proteins and inflammatory cells related to MS.

In addition to collecting these clues, doctors must rule out diseases that look similar to MS. These include neuromyelitis optica and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) — two other autoimmune conditions that affect the central nervous system. Both these diseases are associated with distinctive, rogue immune proteins, called autoantibodies, that latch onto various parts of the nervous system; those specific antibodies are not seen in MS.

Other conditions — such as the inflammatory disease lupus and neuromuscular disease myasthenia gravis — can sometimes look like MS at first glance, as well. Some of these "MS mimics" can be ruled out with data from spinal taps or antibody tests, while others require a more laborious battery of assessments.

"Ultimately, it's a diagnosis of exclusion," Allette said. "There are things that you can rule out quickly," while others take time to investigate thoroughly.

Conditions like MOGAD and neurosarcoidosis, an inflammatory condition sometimes mistaken for MS, can actually be worsened by MS medications, Green noted. So if a patient's MS flare-ups worsen after they start a treatment, that could hint that something else is the culprit, he said. But because there may be long gaps between flare-ups, doctors may need to track a patient for some time before their worsening becomes apparent.

The updated diagnostic criteria for MS include new guidelines for how to examine brain MRIs. These criteria could likely be improved even more in future, Green noted.

Given all the tools available, a patient can be reasonably confident in their MS diagnosis, Green said.

"In general, there's a fair amount of diagnostic confidence that comes with MS diagnosis today," he said. However, that confidence is highest when the tools are in the hands of a specialist who is following the most up-to-date criteria, he noted. The test results require interpretation, and that's one place where uncertainty can creep in.

Regarding Applegate's circumstances, "to speak bluntly, I think the case came as a bit of a shock because we always expect that part of society to have quick access [to high-quality medical care]," Allette said. For some patients, the road to accessing specialty MS care can be long, which can result in missteps, and even misdiagnoses, along the way. Applegate went five years with an incorrect diagnosis, but "would that have been seven or eight years for somebody who doesn't have that access?" Allette speculated.

Without knowing the details of Applegate's case, Allette and Green said it would be difficult to speculate on what resulted in her misdiagnosis. (Applegate mentioned that her brain scans had been reassessed with a "new technology," but didn't go into specifics.)

Looking toward the future of MS diagnostics, Green and Allette think emerging proteomic tests hold a lot of promise. Such tests generate a profile of the proteins and smaller fragments, called peptides, in a patient's blood or cerebrospinal fluid. They can be designed to look for proteins involved in driving the underlying immune attacks, as well as signs of nerve damage that MS wrecks, they explained.

These in-depth, protein analyses could help paint a more comprehensive picture of MS, which could ultimately help scientists "look at that whole system in concert" to reach a strong diagnosis, Allette said.

No proteomics-based diagnostic tests for MS have been approved yet, Green clarified. And even with those upcoming advances, Green doubts there will ever be a simple, one-time test that can determine a patient has MS. "I think that's probably unlikely, that it would ever be that straightforward," he said.

He does think that, in time, scientists could pare down the number of tests required to reach a diagnosis, but he argued that the clinical evaluation conducted by a neurologist will likely remain crucial. "I'm doubtful that there's going to be a test that will fully replace that," he said, "at least in the foreseeable future."

#793. The Largest Canyon in the Solar System is Still Tectonically Active

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

For many decades, scientists assumed that Mars was geologically inactive. While its surface is covered in features that attest to its volcanic past, all evidence seemed to indicate that Mars was geologically dead. This is consistent with the fact that Mars' crust has no tectonic plates and instead consists of a single plate, making it a "Stagnant Lid" planet. However, with the arrival of NASA's InSight (Interior Exploration using Seismic Investigations, Geodesy and Heat Transport) lander, indications that the planet was still "alive" began to accumulate.

Beyond "marsquakes" caused by impactors, InSight detected evidence of tectonic activity from within. In a recent study, scientists at Imperial College London and Columbia University presented seismic evidence that a marsquake originated beneath Valles Marineris. According to InSight's data, the event (S0976a) was caused by rocks slipping along fault lines deep within the crust. These findings suggest that faults formed billions of years ago, when Mars was much more active, are unstable enough to rupture under stress.

The research was led by Jung-Hun Song, an Assistant Professor at Imperial College London's Department of Earth Science and Engineering. He was joined by researchers from Columbia's Department of Earth and Environmental Sciences and the Department of Earth Science and Environmental Change at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. The study describing their findings was published in Nature Communications.

One of two Mars InSight's 7-foot (2.2 meters) wide solar panels was imaged by the lander's Instrument Deployment Camera. Credits: NASA/JPL-Caltech

Like Olympus Mons and the Tharsis region, Valles Marineris is the Solar System's largest geological feature of its kind, dwarfing similar features on Earth. The vast canyon system spans 3,900 km (2,423 mi) long, up to 600 km (373 mi) wide, and more than 9 km (5.5 mi) deep. Meanwhile, the largest Canyon system on Earth, the Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon, stretches roughly 505 km (313.5 mi) long, up to 113 m (371 ft) wide, and reaches a maximum depth of 6 km (3.7 mi). As Song explained:

Valles Marineris is one of the most spectacular geological structures anywhere in the Solar System, but until now we didn't know for certain that its faults are still active. Our study opens up exciting possibilities for discovering ongoing seismic activity on other planetary bodies that we might otherwise consider geologically inactive.

The most widely accepted theory is that the valley was formed by rift faults created by tensional tectonic forces that pulled the crust apart. This process is believed to be related to the intense volcanic activity that formed the Tharsis Bulge during the Noachian to Late Hesperian periods (4.1 to 3.0 billion years ago). It is also theorized that the canyon was enlarged by subsequent erosion and the collapse of the rift walls, similar to the East African Rift.

For their study, the team analyzed the S0976a seismic event that was recorded by InSight on August 25th, 2021. This was the only marsquake detected in Mars's western hemisphere, and it originated on the opposite side of the planet. Previous studies placed the marsquake near the western part of Valles Marineris. However, its cause was unclear, with possibilities ranging from meteoroid impacts to landslides and other forms of mass movement common to Mars.

Tharsis Montes, with Olympus Mons (upper left) and Valles Marineris (right). Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech

The team built models to test these explanations, but they couldn't reproduce the observed seismic waves. The researchers also consulted satellite images of the region taken by NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO) after the event, but found no evidence of an impact crater or landslides. The best fit was a strike-slip marsquake, in which blocks of crust move horizontally past each other along a steep fault about 20 to 40 km (12 to 24 mi) beneath the valley. Said Song:

What makes the result compelling is that several completely different types of evidence point in the same direction. The seismic waves tell us that there was a sideways sliding motion on a fault well below the surface, while the geology seen from orbit shows a region crossed by long-lived fault systems.

The results also match what scientists would expect if pre-existing fault structures slipped due to long-term cooling, volcanic stresses, and local effects associated with the canyon itself. They also add to the list of regions where InSight detected evidence of active faulting on modern-day Mars. This includes the Cerberus Fossae fissures and the southern part of Elysium Planitia, which suggest that stresses in the Martian crust do not follow a single planet-wide pattern.

Lastly, the results show that four years after the mission ended because of dust buildup on its solar panels, InSight continues to provide valuable insight into the Red Planet. Said Dr. Doyeon Kim, an Assistant Professor in Earth and Planetary Science at Imperial and a senior author on the study:

S0976a has turned out to be an extraordinary event, playing an important role in our understanding of the Red Planet. Its waves traveled through the core and helped us investigate the deepest part of Mars, and now they are telling us what happened at the source itself. From a single marsquake, we can learn both about the center of the planet and about an active fault beneath one of its most remarkable landscapes.

First introduced by Italian glassmaker Carlo Nason in 1969 as the LT305, the light remained in production for only a few years. Nevertheless, it went on to develop a cult following among design collectors, who nicknamed it the “Lotus lamp,” a moniker that Gubi embraced for the revival.

The design’s enduring appeal is a testament to just how ambitious it was when it debuted. Growing up in a family of Murano glassmakers, Nason acquired an expert command of the Italian island’s glassblowing traditions. But while most of his ancestors and peers applied their skill to highly decorative forms, Nason was eager to move in more experimental, contemporary directions.

Influenced by the rise of industrial product design—particularly the Space Age style popular in the 1960s—Nason developed the LT305 lamp with a bulbous, streamlined silhouette. But behind this pared-back profile is some fairly complex glasswork. The light is composed of four distinct mouth-blown shades—each one nested within the next to create an arrangement that calls to mind a lotus flower in bloom. “The four layers of mouth-blown glass give it an almost architectural presence,” Schmidt says.

This construction method also contributes to the lamp’s carefully calibrated glow, which is free from both glare and harsh shadows. While the design’s opaque central portion, where its four shades overlap, completely obscures the bulb inside, the fixture’s outer curves—expressed with just a single layer of glass—exhibit delicate transparency.

Adding to the design’s dynamic sense of depth, each mouth-blown shade is produced using a Murano technique known as “pulegoso,” in which small air bubbles are blown into the glass to give it a textured, semi-opaque appearance. Originally developed in the 1920s, the method further highlights the human skill behind each element.

“Each of the four shades is mouth-blown, and the natural variations in the glass and the distribution of the bubbles mean that no two lamps are exactly alike,” Schmidt explains. “I think that is an important part of the design’s appeal. The craftsmanship is visible, and it gives each piece its own character.”

While staying faithful to Nason’s original specifications, Gubi did make one contemporary refinement: The lamp’s steel base, which houses the bulb socket and secures the four glass shades, has been re-engineered for greater stability. The updated construction also makes it easier to disassemble the lamp for cleaning and bulb replacement.

“These are discreet improvements that do not change the expression of the design but ensure that it works beautifully in everyday use,” Schmidt notes. “Whenever we bring a design back into production, our starting point is to understand the original intention and preserve what makes it special. We worked closely with Nason’s son, Alberto, throughout the development process.”

The result is a reissue that preserves the sculptural character that has made Lotus a favorite among design collectors for decades.

Nason’s death earlier this year gives the revival added significance as a continuation of his legacy. Alberto is especially grateful that Gubi’s reissue will allow a much larger audience to experience his father’s design firsthand.

“The more you observe it, the more it unfolds, layer by layer, allowing you to discover its beauty over time,” Alberto says. “There is a quiet magic to it: a softness, a depth, a tactile presence that makes the light feel almost like a material in itself.”

Like the flower it’s named for, Lotus is back in bloom.

It sounds a lot like downsizing, but the two aren’t quite the same. Downsizing usually means moving to a smaller house or getting rid of belongings. Downshifting doesn’t necessarily involve moving at all.

#1356. <b><strong>7 Myths You’ve Heard About Creatine That Aren’t Actually True, According to Experts</strong></b>

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If there’s one supplement on everyone’s lips, it’s creatine. But, with all the talk about this well-studied supp that was once for gym bros and is now beloved by, well, everyone, it can be hard to separate fact from fiction.

Creatine is a compound your body makes naturally from three amino acids called arginine, glycine, and methionine, but you can also get small amounts from meat and fish, says Kristin Kirkpatrick, RDN, a dietitian at the Cleveland Clinic Department of Wellness and Preventive Medicine. “I like to describe it as the power plant for your cells,” she says. “It helps regenerate adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the energy molecule your muscles and brain rely on for quick, high-intensity work.” Without it, your movements won’t be as explosive in the gym, and you may take longer to recover, as Women’s Health reported previously.

The body produces about half of the creatine it needs, and the rest has to come from diet—or supplements. But as creatine’s popularity has exploded, so has the misinformation surrounding it. We asked experts to debunk some of the most persistent myths about the supplement. Here’s what they said.

Meet the experts: Jay Luthar, MD, is an internal, integrative, and lifestyle medicine physician and a professor at Harvard Medical School. Kristin Kirkpatrick, RDN, is a dietitian at Cleveland Clinic Department of Wellness and Preventive Medicine.

You may have heard that when you first start taking creatine, you should do what’s called a loading phase—which is taking about four times the regular dose for up to a week—to saturate your muscle stores with the supp.

A standard dose of creatine is 3 to 5 grams per day; a loading phase calls for about 20 grams per day, split into four doses, Kirkpatrick explains. Why four doses? “Essentially, there’s a ceiling on how much creatine the gut can absorb and deliver to muscle at any one time,” says Jay Luthar, MD, an internal, integrative, and lifestyle medicine physician and professor at Harvard Medical School. “Taking 20 grams at once doesn’t mean 20 grams reaches your muscles; it means a portion gets excreted, and you likely experience GI discomfort in the process.”

The idea is that a loading phase fills your muscles' creatine stores faster—in about a week—but it’s totally unnecessary for most people. An athlete who wants results before an upcoming competition may benefit, for example, but if you don’t have anything timely on the calendar, you can skip the load completely, Kirkpatrick says. Doing so might also save you from bloating and stomach upset and other side effects that most often cause people to stop taking the supp. “A steady daily dose is simpler, easier on your body, and just as effective over time,” Kirkpatrick says.

This one is partly true—but stay with us. Creatine works by drawing more water into your muscles, which means that it’s possible you may experience some bloat at first. Key words: At first. Research in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition from 2021 found that creatine does not cause long-term water retention.

“In the initial few weeks, creatine draws water into muscle cells,” Dr. Luthar says. “That means the bump on the scale is evident, but it’s water weight—not fat. Once muscle saturation is reached and you transition to a maintenance dose, this stabilizes.” As for how long that takes? About two to four weeks, he says.

Also keep in mind that your dosage and creatine type matter. “The bloating and stomach discomfort people complain about are usually tied to large doses of creatine monohydrate, especially during a loading phase,” says Kirkpatrick. “Creatine monohydrate isn't very soluble, so large amounts can sit in the gut and cause GI distress.” If this is a concern, Kirkpatrick recommends creatine HCl, which is much more soluble and better absorbed, so it works at a smaller dose. That means less bloating, less stomach upset, and less water retention, with the same benefits creatine is known for.

For years, creatine was relegated to lifters, but now there is research showing benefits far beyond the gym. “We now have strong evidence that creatine supports healthy aging and muscle preservation during weight loss,” says Kirkpatrick.

For example, a new study in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition found that adults ages 45 to 65 gained lean tissue and strength on creatine without a structured exercise program. “That's why I see interest [in creatine] from people who have never lifted a weight: midlife women, older adults, people on GLP-1 medications, and anyone thinking about longevity,” says Kirkpatrick.

Plus, endurance athletes can see some perks from the supplement too. Emphasis on some. Although creatine doesn't appear to improve aerobic capacity, meaning it won't make you a faster marathoner on its own, endurance athletes can still see benefits when their sport includes explosive movements like sprints, surges, or hill climbs, as well as in the strength training they do to support injury prevention, per a 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in Sports Medicine.

“Creatine’s primary mechanism is supporting short-burst, high-intensity energy production, which isn’t the primary energy system in steady-state endurance events,” Dr. Luthar says. “That can make it useful if a marathon runner, for example, is doing weekly sprint workouts to improve speed. It might have less of an impact on a weekly long run or three-hour cycle ride.” There’s also interesting emerging evidence that creatine helps reduce recovery time between workouts, shares Dr. Luthar. For endurance athletes and lifters alike, this could be another potential benefit.

Some of the most exciting creatine research is happening outside the weight room, especially for women. First, a few facts to keep in mind, per Kirkpatrick: Women naturally store less creatine than men, and beginning around age 30, we lose 3 to 8 percent of our muscle mass every decade, with the loss accelerating after 50. The good news is that creatine supplementation has been shown to support strength, muscle, and recovery in women at every life stage, particularly when paired with resistance training, according to a 2021 study published in Nutrients.

Perimenopausal women have measurably lower brain creatine levels, and those deficits are linked to difficulty concentrating, according to recent research in Nutrients. Consistency matters far more than the timing of your creatine intake, says Kirkpatrick. “Creatine works by gradually filling and maintaining your muscles' stores, so what counts is taking it every day, including rest days.”

You don’t need to be super exact on when you take it every day, either. Creatine works through muscle saturation maintained over time, so if you miss your usual window by a few hours, there won’t be a huge effect on stores, says Dr. Luthar.

Kirkpatrick’s advice is to stack creatine onto something you already do every day—just maybe not your morning coffee. When mixed with hotter liquids, creatine powder may become ineffective, so it’s best to stick to room temperature or cool liquids.

This myth comes from a bit of a misunderstanding. Creatine gets broken down into creatinine, which is often measured in routine bloodwork as an estimate of kidney function. Elevated levels of creatinine can indicate an issue with kidney function. But, if you’re taking a creatine supplement, you may have elevated levels of creatinine simply because you’re taking in extra amounts—not because your kidneys aren’t functioning well. For that reason, Kirkpatrick says to tell your doctor you take creatine before bloodwork, so a slightly higher creatinine number is interpreted correctly.

When researchers looked into the relationship between creatine and kidney health, they found that creatine did not harm kidney function, per a 2026 meta-analysis of 26 randomized controlled trials published in International Urology and Nephrology.

Overall—for a supplement—creatine is pretty low stakes. “It's one of the most researched supplements available, with a strong safety record,” Kirkpatrick says. A 2025 analysis published in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition evaluated 685 human clinical trials and found no significant difference in reported side effects between creatine and placebo. That said, you should talk to a doctor before taking any new supplement, especially if you have kidney or liver disease, diabetes, or bipolar disorder, or are pregnant or breastfeeding. (For individuals diagnosed with bipolar disorder, Kirkpatrick says there is some preliminary research suggesting that creatine supplementation may increase the risk of manic or hypomanic episodes.)

You’ll also want to choose a brand that is third-party tested, since supplements aren’t regulated like medications. (You can find our recs here.)

No supplement is a must—and that includes creatine. Kirkpatrick says you can absolutely build strength and muscle without it. “Consistent resistance training, enough protein, adequate sleep, and a well-balanced diet will always do the heavy lifting,” says Kirkpatrick. “Creatine is just the add-on, not the foundation.”

That said, Kirkpatrick thinks it deserves the love it gets, particularly when we look at the growing body of research surrounding its benefits. “What makes creatine particularly exciting is the strong evidence supporting its role in muscle strength, performance, and recovery, along with emerging research exploring potential benefits for brain health and cognitive function,” says Kirkpatrick. “For most healthy adults, creatine is considered safe when taken at recommended doses. While the research is especially compelling for muscle health, I think the potential applications for healthy aging make this an exciting area of nutritional science.”

Dr. Luthar agrees and says that creatine is backed by decades of sports nutrition research, including a fair number of studies with human participants. Still, the supplement has taken social media by storm and the hype is taking on a life of its own. “Some of the benefits posted online are exaggerated or incorrect, which is why it’s best to discuss creatine or any supplement with a health care provider before taking it,” he says.
