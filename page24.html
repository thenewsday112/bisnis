Elke Sommer, the striking blonde bombshell of German descent who was a mainstay of 1960s Hollywood comedies and espionage movies including “The Prize” and “A Shot in the Dark,” has died. She was 85.

Sommer died at home in Bel Air, in Los Angeles, on Wednesday, her stepson Sebastian Walther told the German outlet Bild. The Los Angeles Police Department told The Associated Press that it responded to the death of an 85-year-old woman on Sommer’s block Wednesday night.

With big almond eyes and golden locks, often styled high in a beehive, Sommer worked with some of the biggest stars of the time, including Paul Newman in “The Prize,” Peter Sellers in the Pink Panther film “A Shot in the Dark,” James Garner and Dick Van Dyke in “The Art of Love,” Bob Hope in “Boy, Did I Get a Wrong Number!” and Dean Martin and Sharon Tate in “The Wrecking Crew.”

Sommer was born Elke Schletz in Berlin in the midst of the World War II, on Nov. 5, 1940, though the family had to evacuate to the south in 1942. Her father, who died when she was 14, was a Protestant pastor.

She’d intended to study languages, but during a trip to Italy with her mother, Renata, in 1958, she caught the eye of someone in the Italian film industry who invited her to appear in her first film: “The Friend of the Jaguar.” Actor and filmmaker Vittorio De Sica noticed her too, and soon she was working nonstop throughout Europe, in Italy, Spain and England, with the newly adopted surname Sommer.

Her big Hollywood break came from producer Pandro S. Berman, who had seen her in a German drama that screened for the Golden Globes, and offered her a role in “The Prize” where she played chaperone and love interest to Newman’s Nobel Prize-winning character. In the trade publication Variety’s review, they wrote that Sommer, “hasn’t a very scintillating role, but has the looks to make that a secondary issue.” And it would win her the Golden Globe for most promising newcomer.

In Blake Edwards’ Pink Panther sequel “A Shot in the Dark,” she played Maria Gambrelli, a maid framed for murder, who also sparks the romantic interest of Sellers’ Inspector Clouseau. She also posed for Playboy Magazine around that time, in photographs shot by her first husband Joe Hyams. They married in November 1964 and divorced in 1981.

In 1993 she married German hotelier Wolf Walther, who died in July at age 77.

Sommer didn’t take the sex symbol designation too seriously, saying that it was essentially meaningless. She told Film Talk in a 2014 interview that some of her favorite actors, like Rita Hayworth and Ava Gardner, were also considered sex symbols. Besides, she said, she was always just acting the part.

“My whole life, I have been directed, I have been produced, I spoke the lines written for the character,” she said in the interview. “I was always playing, and the only thing I could bring was my energy. So what is it, this sex appeal? Why was I voted in this list of most sexy women? It’s not because of my looks or anything; it’s because of the energy.”

The influential Institute for Fiscal Studies warns that ‘no amount of temporary cost of living packages’ will fix the lack of household income growth, in a damning indictment of Andy Burnham’s approach to government

John Healey’s “risky” Budget has exposed Britain to further economic shocks and failed to tackle the “worst decade for growth” for household incomes on record, an influential think tank has warned.

The chancellor was told that the reduced financial headroom he has left himself with as a result of his first Budget could leave the country “more exposed to future bad news”, as government departments also face real-terms value cuts to their spending.

In a damning assessment of the government’s spending plans, the Institute for Fiscal Studies (IFS) hit out at the “temporary cost of living measures” unveiled, which it said were no substitute for “a strong economy”, appearing to take aim at Andy Burnham’s approach since he became prime minister of introducing only minor reductions to energy bills and capping the price of bus tickets at £2.

It comes as another leading thinktank, the Centre for Social Justice (CSJ), found that a “£33bn stealth tax on workers” which it claims has been “wiped out” with increased payments on benefits.

The CSJ has looked at the impact of continuing to freeze income tax thresholds which drag more people into paying the basic rate of 20p, higher rate of 40p and top rate of 45p - equating to £33.1bn in extra revenue. But it noted that benefits are set to rise by £3.4bn.

Joe Shalam, policy director at CSJ, said: “Sickness and disability benefit spending is now forecast to exceed £100bn by the end of the decade and 6.7 million people are on out-of-work benefits, while the rest of the country is dragged into higher tax to pay for inaction. Public support for the system there to protect the most disadvantaged is being pushed to breaking point.”

In its analysis of Mr Healey’s Budget, the IFS said there were “risks” in his approach, which come amid a period of dismal growth for household incomes.

IFS director Helen Miller said while it was “reasonable” to allow headroom to fall to absorb higher borrowing costs, she cautioned: “Higher borrowing will need to be paid for. A lower buffer leaves the Chancellor more exposed to future bad news. There are risks to this approach.”

The government’s fiscal headroom has been slashed by over £8bn to £15bn ​in the 2029/30 fiscal year, down from the budge watchdog, the Office of Budget Responsibility’s (OBR), March estimate of £24 bn under Rachel Reeves.

Ms Miller said the chancellor must do more to tackle slow economic growth, added: “It’s depressing. No amount of temporary cost of living packages will fix that. We need a stronger economy.”

The headline cost of living measure unveiled by the chancellor on Wednesday was the £100 payment for around 20 million homes to help with the cost of energy bills.

While it will likely be welcomed by some of the poorest household, many have said it does not go far enough.

The Resolution Foundation, set up to help people on lower and middle incomes, said the Budget would do little to address “the sharp challenges facing Britain”.

The rebuff for Mr Healey's package of measures is particularly embarrassing because until two years ago the think tank was headed by pensions minister Torsten Bell.

The Confederation of British Industry (CBI) also said the chancellor had delivered a “nothing” Budget which did not “meet the scale of that ambition” set out by Mr Burnham.

Meanwhile, the OBR said the UK’s “economic and fiscal position remains very challenging” after almost two decades of shocks and stagnation, pushing up the national debt.

Professor David Miles of its Budget Responsibility Committee (BRC) warned it marked a “pretty grim picture”.

“If you go back to just before Covid and take that as your base, where we are right now which is what some seven years later, is virtually no growth in per person real household disposable income,” he said.

“That’s pretty unusual in the history of the UK.” He added: “It’s been a dismal period in British economic history.”

Ms Miller also criticised Mr Healey’s “small, unhelpful fiddling with the tax system”, leaving it in a “mess” and calling his new Founders’ Relief, a new capital gains tax relief for founders and early investors of high-growth UK companies, “bonkers”.

“It is far more complex than it should be,” she said.

“And that is because, Budget after Budget, chancellors make seemingly small tweaks that lack principle and don’t systematically move us to a better place. This approach gives us more complexity and more instability. Both have real economic costs.”

The IFS also warned Mr Healey’s decision to leave government departmental statement largely unchanged means “means that the real-terms value of budgets has been cut” in the face of higher inflation.

In Wednesday’s speech in the Commons, Mr Healey committed to spending 3 per cent of economic output on defence by 2030, and to “fully funding” the £4.7bn black hole in the defence investment plan (Dip) left by Sir Keir Starmer.

Departments across government will be asked to reduce their capital budgets by 1 per cent each between 2027-28 and 2029-30 to raise some £3bn. The remaining £1.7bn shortfall will be made up from an “unallocated contingency in the Treasury’s capital reserve”, the Budget said.

“It effectively implies that some public services will see real-terms cuts in next year’s Spending Review,” Ms Miller said.

“Avoiding the cuts implied by both higher inflation and tight envelopes could plausibly require a top-up of £17 billion in 2030-21. That’s true even before thinking about the £11 billion needed to fund defence plans.”

Bee Boileau, a research economist at the IFS, said the plans imply “really very tough times ahead for public services” as a result of inflation, slow spending growth and commitments to defence spending.

“The government could deliver these, but these will imply setting out lots of real terms cuts to departments over the coming years, and there’ll be huge pressure to put in some top-ups,” she said.

“Next year’s spending review will be the real crunch point for this government.”

Ms Miller also said Mr Healey’s decision to fund a £4.7bn black hole in defence spending in part by drawing down on reserves weakens the government’s ability to weather future global shocks.

In a scathing rebuke of the lack of clarity on defence spending in the Budget, she said she was still “no wiser” as to how Mr Healey planned to hit his target of spending 3 per cent of GDP on defence by 2030.

“I am grumpy about how the chancellor decided to fund the relatively small hole in the defence investment plan – that’s the plan that gets defence spending to 2.7 per cent of GDP next year and keeps it there until 2029-30,” she said.

“Much of it came from further cuts to the capital budgets of other government departments. Fine. Priorities change but the rest came from raiding reserves in future years.

“This is worth drawing out: John Healey has now spent 25 per cent of the capital reserve set aside for the next three years. He’s also spent 40 per cent of the day-to-day reserve.

“This is categorically not what the reserves are for. Drawing down reserves is another way the government has reduced its buffer against future shocks.”

#596. 'Dixie' map that divided the US into North and South fetches nearly $1 million at auction

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The hand-drawn map showing the Mason-Dixon Line that eventually divided free from slave states in the U.S. sold Thursday for $950,000.

Forty-five bidders battled just under three minutes for ownership of what is perhaps the most consequential map in American history.

Bidding started at $400,000 for the survey of states divided by the Mason-Dixon Line, finished in 1768 by Charles Mason and Jeremiah Dixon. The sale was held online and in person at Brunk Auctions in Asheville, North Carolina.

While it didn’t fetch the hoped-for $1.2 million high estimate, auction house Vice President Lauren Brunk said she was pleased.

“It’s a very high price for a map," she said.

Brunk said the buyer asked to remain anonymous, at least for the time being.

The survey was originally commissioned to settle a border dispute between what were then the colonies of Maryland and Pennsylvania. It was used in the 1820 Missouri Compromise to establish the limits of human bondage in the still adolescent United States and became synonymous with the fundamental divisions that exploded into the Civil War four decades later.

The late publisher Malcolm Forbes bought the map in 1982 for $360,000, beating out the Library of Congress. The document was resold in 2002 for $556,000.

Brunk said the Library of Congress was not among Thursday’s bidders.

“I would like to say I’m thrilled that this is going to a good home and we are so happy to have had it in our presence for these last months,” Brunk said. “It also feels like a privilege to have been entrusted to steward this one owner to the next.”

A vertical section of the map showing the Delaware-Maryland border is housed at Princeton University.

A former Adidas boss who bought a £1.3m dream home in the country only to be ordered to partly demolish it because it is two metres "too big" is suing the developer who sold him it for £900,000.

Former managing director of Adidas in Korea, Edward Nixon and wife Sally paid a total of £1.3 million for former farm outbuilding West Barn, in Holdenby Road, East Haddon, Northamptonshire, commissioning developer, Barry Howard, and his company to convert it into a sprawling rural home fit for the 21st century.

Mr Howard - head of Northampton-based house building and development company, Barry Howard Homes - sold the couple the £600,000 parcel of land where West Barn stands and had already built a “shell of a house” on the site.

He and the Nixons then struck a £700,000 deal for the developer to “finalise the construction of the house in accordance with drawings provided to the Nixons”.

The couple say they were told by Mr Howard during a video call in July 2020 that “the house is built and the planners are happy with it”.

But they were horrified when in June 2021 the local council imposed an enforcement notice, demanding that the Nixons either demolish their new home or carry out comprehensive alterations, telling them the 36m former barn was bigger than the approved plans and two metres too long.

The case has now spilled over into court, with the Nixons suing Mr Howard for £900,000 for "fraudulent misrepresentation" on the basis he must have known the house was bigger than it should have been when he filled in an enquiry form.

But the housebuilder is disputing the couple's claim, insisting he had no idea that the renovated barn’s dimensions had gone over the limit when he sold it.

Mr Nixon, 56, is an ex MD of Adidas and founder of Brand First Clearance Solutions, a company specialising in advising on clearing excess stock.

Barry Howard, 57, has enjoyed a long career as a successful developer and housebuilder in the Northampton area.

Daria Gleyze, the couple’s barrister, claimed at Central London County Court that Mr Howard made a series of misleading statements which induced his clients to go ahead with buying into the barn conversion project, including a video call in July 2020 in which he declared: “the house is built and the planners are happy with it”.

And in September 2020, when filling out the standard enquiries form questionnaire accompanying the sale contract, Mr Howard answered “no” to the question, “are you aware of any breach of planning law in relation to the construction, use or occupation of the property," the barrister said.

But in truth the finished house project exceeded its permitted length by two metres, she told Judge Nigel Gerald, with West Northamptonshire Council demanding drastic alterations due to “its sheer bulk and mass deviating from the character and form of the original approved building.”

The court heard that the house ultimately produced by Mr Howard’s company, Barry Howard Homes, exceeded its permitted length by around two metres and its “ridge height” by just under one metre.

The local authority decided that the changes had “detracted from the character and appearance” of the original barn, and in December 2020 the Nixons discovered the planning rules had been broken - three months after completing the barn’s purchase.

In June 2021, the local council imposed an enforcement notice demanding that the Nixons either demolish their new home or carry out comprehensive alterations.

The following year council officers finally approved “remedial works” which will result in a “significant decrease in size and a different layout of the house”, explained Ms Gleyze.

“Their new house will have to be much smaller,” she told Judge Gerald.

The Nixons’ legal team say the couple ended up buying the project on the strength of the “false representation” made in the enquiries form, and are now claiming £900,000 compensation from Mr Howard - partly to cover the costs of “making their house smaller”.

They are also suing for “fraudulent misrepresentation” on grounds that Mr Howard must have known the outsize building had breached planning rules when he filled in the enquiry form.

But developer Mr Howard is fighting the Nixons’ claim, insisting he had no idea that the renovated barn’s dimensions had gone over the limit when he signed it away to the couple.

Mr Howard, representing himself in court, insisted he “had no knowledge that this building was being built in breach”, also claiming that Mr Nixon had been fully aware of all the planning developments in relation to the barn, being “involved in every detail of the building’s design”.

But the couple’s barrister branded his defence “demonstrably untrue”, arguing: “It is inconceivable, and wholly fanciful to assert, that Mr Howard, who prides himself on being a professional builder with ample experience of building houses, would not have realised that he had deviated from the plans approved when he constructed the house, especially given the very significant discrepancies between the house as-built and as approved.

“It is clear that the claimants suffered loss as a result of these misrepresentations.

“They were forced to incur significant costs in attempting to remedy the breach of planning and they will end up with a property significantly smaller and less valuable than what they had purchased.

“A valuation dated 13 October 2025 estimated a difference of £350,000 between the value of the house as built and as it would be after remedial planning measures are implemented,“ added Ms Gleyze.

During a court hearing last week, the Nixons’ lawyers asked Judge Gerald to order “summary judgment” against Mr Howard on grounds that he has “no realistic prospect” of defeating the claim against him.

But after a brief hearing, the judge agreed to Mr Howard’s request to have the case adjourned in order for him to gather more evidence and instruct solicitors.

“It seems fair to give Mr Howard a relatively brief opportunity to find new solicitors and put in a witness statement,” he decided.

“The essence of his witness statement would be to explain why he didn’t know at the time that the representation which was made was wrong,” the judge added, although also noting evidence produced by the Nixons from the project architect “on one reading to the effect that prior to September 22 Mr Howard had been told that the planning regulations had been breached”.

The judge adjourned the case until late November to permit Mr Howard time to prepare his case.

#483. Somali piracy surges as Iran war diverts US naval attention - CNN

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Piracy off Somalia has reached its highest level since 2013, CNN reported Thursday, as maritime security experts warned that the Iran war and Houthi attacks on shipping were creating new opportunities for pirates.

At one point this summer, six vessels were simultaneously held by pirates, with 90 crew members taken hostage, according to the European Union's Operation Atalanta naval mission.

The resurgence has led to several major hijackings, including the MT Eureka oil tanker and the Honour 25 diesel tanker, whose crew remained captive for more than five months.

Maritime security experts cited by CNN linked the increase to the diversion of naval resources toward the Persian Gulf and Bab al-Mandeb Strait, as well as rising fuel prices and economic hardship in Somalia.

Houthi attacks have also pushed some commercial vessels closer to Somalia's coast, exposing them to greater risks.

"As long as Iran and the Houthis continue to threaten maritime commerce, America and its regional partners will remain primarily focused on those threats, allowing piracy to flourish," Matthew Reisener, a senior national security adviser at the Center for Maritime Strategy, told CNN.

The Honour 25, carrying 2,700 tons of diesel from Oman to Somalia, was hijacked in April, with its 17 crew members held captive for more than five months. Pirates initially demanded $7 million in ransom, later reducing the amount to $1 million, according to survivors interviewed by CNN.

Five crew members were killed in late September during an operation by Puntland maritime police to free the vessel.

Tim Walker, maritime project leader at the Institute for Security Studies Africa, said ships without armed guards were particularly vulnerable, while rising ransom demands had made piracy more profitable.

"It's more lucrative to attack now," Walker said. "For one activity, pirates can get a very large payout – even a couple million dollars."

Experts also pointed to worsening poverty, drought and rising food and fuel costs in Somalia as factors encouraging piracy.

Iranian officials have warned that any further attack would trigger a stronger response.

The diplomatic meetings come as Saudi Arabia seeks to prevent the conflict from spreading to its critical infrastructure, putting the Mecca defense pact and regional mediation efforts under growing pressure.

Iran still has the knowhow, middlemen and shadow-fleet infrastructure it spent years developing to sell sanctioned oil to China. But it has now run out of oil outside Iran to put through that system.

The US naval blockade has exposed a limitation in Tehran’s sanctions-evasion machinery: ship-to-ship transfers, false paperwork and steep discounts can disguise and sell crude once it is at sea, but they cannot get fresh cargoes out of Iranian ports.

For months, Iran was able to keep selling oil to China because millions of barrels had already left the country on tankers and were sitting in Asian waters. That reserve is now effectively exhausted.

Kpler analysts cited by The Wall Street Journal on Wednesday estimated Iran had about 135 million barrels in overseas storage when the war began in February. By October 5, that had fallen below one million barrels.

Vortexa gave a different figure, estimating Iranian stocks off Singapore at roughly six million barrels on October 9. The difference reflects varying tracking methods and locations, but both show that the offshore stocks that sustained Iranian exports have been largely depleted.

Malaysia sits at the heart of how Iranian oil has reached China, serving mainly as a transfer point rather than the true source of much of the crude.

The Wall Street Journal reported in May on ship-to-ship transfers of Iranian crude off the Malaysian coast.

A sharp drop in China’s purchases recorded as Malaysian crude could therefore provide another indication that the flow of Iranian oil through the system has dried up.

But the figures are not a one-for-one measure of Iranian exports. Chinese customs data record the declared origin of cargoes, while ship tracking can miss transfers or vessels operating without transponders.

The timing nevertheless coincides with the depletion of the offshore stocks that had fed the trade. If no new Iranian crude is available for transfer near Malaysia, fewer cargoes would be expected to appear in Chinese data under a Malaysian origin.

China remains Iran’s most important potential buyer, but its independent refineries are already lining up alternatives.

Reuters reported on October 6 that Chinese “teapot” refineries had purchased at least 12 million barrels of Iraqi and Qatari crude for delivery in October and November. Traders suggested the total could reach 15 million to 20 million barrels.

Iranian oil had been attractive largely because of its steep discount. Iraqi and Qatari cargoes may cost more, but they arrive through normal channels and carry less risk of sanctions trouble or sudden interruptions.

Iran could offer deeper discounts or rely more heavily on intermediaries to retain Chinese customers. Neither solves the central obstacle: the oil still has to be loaded and shipped.

Reuters reported in August that offers of Iranian oil to Chinese buyers had already fallen as stored cargoes were sold.

Jask, on the Gulf of Oman, is often cited as Iran’s alternative export outlet. The Goreh–Jask pipeline and terminal were built so oil could reach the sea without passing through the Strait of Hormuz.

So far, however, the facility has not shown it can replace Iran’s main export system.

The International Energy Agency’s February 2026 factsheet described Jask as effectively out of regular service. S&P Global later reported tanker activity there between March and July, indicating occasional use.

That suggests the route can handle some cargoes, not that it can support exports on the scale Iran needs. A port outside Hormuz is also not necessarily beyond the reach of a blockade aimed at Iranian ports.

Rail and trucking through neighboring countries offer still less capacity. They face high costs, limited infrastructure, border procedures and sanctions risks.

If crude cannot be exported, Iran’s problem moves from the sea back to its oil fields.

Tehran can send more crude to domestic refineries, use available storage or cut production. Kpler estimated earlier in the blockade that Iran had already begun reducing output as inventories rose and usable storage space tightened.

Usable storage is not the same as total tank capacity: some tanks hold condensate, and not all can simply be switched to crude.

Further production cuts would mean less revenue and could make restarting output more difficult. But continuing to pump oil with nowhere to send it risks filling remaining storage and eventually forcing deeper cuts.

The financial impact will also emerge gradually because payments for cargoes already delivered can arrive weeks later.

The United States says the blockade has denied Iran at least $25 billion in oil revenue, a CENTCOM estimate cited by the Journal rather than an independently verified measure of Tehran’s net losses.

Iran has not forgotten how to evade sanctions or lost the network it built to sell oil despite them. But before Tehran can disguise a cargo, transfer it between ships or sell it at a discount, it first has to get the oil out of Iran.

The industry previously made changes to safety equipment to compensate for the growing waistlines of offshore workers

Offshore workers have been turning to weight loss jabs in order to keep their jobs ahead of new weight restrictions coming into force next week.

From November, updated guidelines published by trade body Offshore Energies UK (OEUK) will require everyone working offshore to weigh less than 124kg (19.5st).

OEUK stated that the rule is required strictly on safety grounds, as it represents the maximum allowable weight for a person being winched into a helicopter during an emergency platform evacuation.

Since announcing the policy in 2025, the trade body said it has supported operators to identify and implement measures aimed at slimming down their offshore workforces.

OEUK health and safety leader Graham Skinner noted that this included a "wide uptake" of GLP-1 weight-loss jabs, with some employers installing additional refrigerators to store the medication.

However, he explained that weight-loss jabs are merely one of the measures workers are using to drop weight, rather than being treated as a "blanket solution" to the problem.

The number of workers over the 124kg limit has fallen from about 2,200 in early 2025 to 840 earlier this year, with the OEUK saying it is “delighted” so many have achieved it.

However an estimated 241-300 workers are projected to still be above the weight limit when the policy comes into effect, meaning they could face losing their jobs.

Mr Skinner said it is possible some of these people no longer work offshore in the UK, or that they will be moved to onshore roles by their employers.

“We are hoping that the vast majority of workers will have found a way through this,” he said on Thursday.

“But the reality is also that if somebody has chosen not to lose weight (or) doesn’t particularly want to comply with the policy for any reason, then there will be a route out of employment as well.

“Ultimately, it’s a safety policy, and it is related to capability.

“If somebody cannot mobilise for their work because they’re over the weight limit, then they will not be able to work.

“In the worst cases, that would mean somebody will lose their job.”

In the past, the industry made changes to safety equipment to compensate for the growing waistlines of offshore workers.

This included making lifeboats larger and more robust, and ensuring larger workers were seated next to the largest windows when travelling on helicopters.

However Mr Skinner said this was a case of only “addressing certain symptoms” of the problem, and the industry had needed to find a solution.

He explained that while the 124kg limit is based on helicopter winches, capping the weight of workers will benefit “all aspects” of life-saving equipment on offshore platforms.

He also said it could reduce the risk of medical evacuations for health-related reasons – including things like heart attacks and strokes – which in 2025 accounted for 78% of all medivacs.

He said those with the most weight to lose have had the “greatest concerns” about the policy since its announcement last year.

“It’s fair to say that everyone’s gone through that psychological process, that change curve of feeling that initial disbelief, anger, and all those emotions that go on when you’re faced with change,” he said.

“That really landed with those that are greatest affected. Those that have had to face a challenge of losing a significant amount of weight have obviously had the greatest concerns.

“However, the vast majority of workers who are already under 124kg and really unaffected by this are very pleased with the introduction of the weight limit.”

The policy has met with some pushback, with the RMT union saying it is concerned it could provide employers with a “convenient mechanism” to sack workers without proper redundancy procedures or financial compensation.

RMT general secretary Eddie Dempsey said: “Nobody disputes that safety must be the overriding priority when working offshore.

“However, safety policies must be proportionate, evidence-based and developed through meaningful consultation with the workers they affect.

“RMT is calling on OEUK to withdraw the policy and engage in meaningful consultation with trade unions to establish a fair, proportionate and evidence-based approach to offshore safety.”
