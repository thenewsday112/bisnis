This further narrows Mr Healey’s options to keep his spending plans on course in the event of another global economic shock, the think tank said, while suggesting the Chancellor could face rampant speculation about tax rises ahead of the next budget.

The reserve may also be needed to top up departmental budgets facing inflationary pressures, according to the IFS.

The Conservatives have promised to cut the amount of interest paid on some student loans amid widespread concerns over costs.

#557. US files lawsuit claiming Google wouldn’t give employee new job following military leave

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Sign up now: Get ST's newsletters delivered to your inbox

Google is accused of failing to give an employee his old job back or put him in a similar job following one year of military leave as a US Army reservist.

NEW YORK – The US filed a lawsuit accusing Google of failing to give an employee his old job back or find him a similar job after he took one year of military leave as a US Army reservist.

The Oct 29 case in Manhattan federal court is a rare instance of the US government suing a private employer for violating the Uniformed Services Employment and Reemployment Rights Act of 1994 (USERRA), which protects the civilian employment of military personnel called to active duty.

According to a complaint filed in Manhattan federal court, Mark Davisson had spent 2½ years as a Google key account executive, handling cloud services accounts for asset manager BlackRock and hedge fund Bridgewater Associates, and managing the US business of the former Credit Suisse.

Upon Davisson’s return from leave in May 2023, Google allegedly refused to let Davisson handle any customer accounts, depriving him of career advancement though he kept his pay and title.

The complaint said Google assigned Davisson in January 2024 to a cloud solutions position that also involved no management of customer accounts, and fired him seven months later following another military leave and as part of a broader workforce reduction.

Davisson, a Bronze Star and Meritorious Service Medal recipient, has a home in Charlotte, North Carolina, but lived and worked in Manhattan while Google employed him, the complaint said. Google’s parent Alphabet is based in Mountain View, California.

“We disagree with the claims in this complaint,” Google spokesman Atle Erlingsson said in an e-mail.

“Google takes compliance with employment laws, including USERRA, seriously and investigates allegations of workplace wrongdoing.”

The lawsuit was brought by the civil rights unit of the US Attorney’s office in Manhattan, with backing from Assistant Attorney-General Harmeet Dhillon, the Trump administration’s top civil rights enforcer.

It seeks to recoup lost wages and benefits as well as additional damages for Davisson, and requires Google to ensure a non-discriminatory workplace including by improving training regarding USERRA.

“Federal law ensures that our brave soldiers, who sacrifice so much to keep us safe, are not punished by employers when they return to the civilian workforce after completing their military service,” US Attorney Jamie McDonald in Manhattan said in a statement. REUTERS

#337. Driverless big rigs have already hit US roads but three challenges are slowing expansion

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

Given those challenges, companies are testing automated trucks on roads that lack complications

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Driverless trucks with artificial intelligence on board could dramatically change the landscape of freight shipping. A few companies have begun testing early models on some limited routes in Texas and California. Simulations of automated fleets show the potential benefits of the technology: The vehicles could improve safety, enhance fuel economy and lessen transit times.

Truck makers and operators still have to solve some major issues, however, including how automated trucks will operate in variable weather and road conditions, and whether drivers should still be inside big rigs.

I am an engineer who studies human factors and transportation safety, and in my view, it is likely that automated trucks and human drivers will be working together for quite some time.

Fully automated trucks, which can drive on an interstate highway in good weather without human intervention, use a combination of AI, machine learning, computer vision, pattern recognition and adaptive decision-making. Torc, one company developing these vehicles, uses AI to combine data from multiple sensors and generate models that predict truck performance.

Data taken from modern big rigs that are equipped with certain technology such as automatic emergency braking and lane-keeping assistance systems indicate that fully automated, driverless trucks could reduce the number and severity of crashes. They could also save fuel and therefore cost by optimizing acceleration and braking, and by running multiple vehicles in a close-knit convoy or platoon. Driverless trucks could reduce parking needs as well as emissions from idling because they could run almost 24 hours a day and would not need to idle when they do park, which is common for regular rigs at truck stops.

Finally, automated trucks could help ease the ongoing driver shortage. Ironically, automation could make driving less stressful and less physically demanding, improving job satisfaction among drivers.

The main reason automated trucks are not common today is that they have difficulty operating in variable weather, under changing roadway conditions and alongside unpredictable drivers.

Automated trucks require sensors, radars and cameras that gather information about the environment around them to make decisions. However, snow and rain remain a problem for all automated vehicles, including cars, because they prevent the technology from “seeing” the roadway. Driverless taxi company Waymo, for example, only operates driverless cars in areas that get no snow.

Furthermore, any stretch of road where there may not be a predictable, clear surface and prevalent lines is difficult to handle. Imagine a construction zone with orange cones, narrow lanes and missing lane lines. Driverless car companies still try to avoid these areas. And, oddly, some research shows that automobile drivers tend to crash into driverless cars at a higher rate than they do cars piloted by people.

Shannon Roberts is an Associate Professor of Industrial Engineering at UMass Amherst. This article is republished from The Conversation under a Creative Commons license. Read the original article .

Given those challenges, companies are testing automated trucks on roads that lack complications.

For example, autonomous truck maker Aurora is operating large freight trucks on I-45, a relatively flat and straight highway between Dallas and Houston. Rival Kodiak’s robotrucks are delivering sand to industrial sites in Texas over roads few people use. Few if any automated trucks are being tested in chaotic urban environments, given the pedestrians, bicyclists, traffic lights and elements of city traffic.

Some companies that were developing automated trucks have scaled back ambitions, changed direction or exited the market.

In 2023, Embark, TuSimple and Locomotion closed down. Waymo began to focus solely on cars. Other companies narrowed their focus to trucks that would operate only along a few dedicated routes between two large cities or manufacturing sites.

In my view, for years to come, automated trucks with AI-based controls will still require a driver.

The best technology will support a symbiosis between human and machine, where the person and the truck each do what they do best. A human would likely drive the truck across the “first mile” – the uncertain and crowded warehouse grounds and local streets to a highway. The driver would then stop using the steering wheel, gas pedal and brake and let automation take over. The human would take back control during the “last mile” to the truck’s final destination.

Research my colleagues and I conducted shows that human-machine interfaces that communicate between drivers and autonomous vehicles will be key. For example, a screen or heads-up display inside the cab would tell the driver how the technology is doing. Verbal messages or a vibrating seat could signal that the automation cannot handle a developing situation, and the driver would take over.

Developers will have to be aware of possible biases. So-called automation bias could lead to a driver who trusts and relies on the truck’s decision-making so much that they defer to it. Algorithmic bias could cause truck sensors to recognize certain groups of people on the road more than others, such as only detecting pedestrians of a certain size or shape.

The long-term vision is to have no driver, in part to lower costs. But driverless trucks do not seem to me to be practical on a large scale in the near future. It is more realistic to envision the driver and the automated truck as a team. Vehicle manufacturers, technology developers, regulators and drivers will need to work together to realize the benefits.

Top officials from Donald Trump’s administration had previously denied knowing details about the president’s plans to turn a 250-foot “triumphal” arch in the heart of Washington, D.C. into a “top-grade military complex.”

But top Cabinet secretaries and Department of Justice officials are now arguing that the arch must be built to fend off drones and other “national security” threats to the nation’s capital.

In Wednesday court filings, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin outlined plans for a militarized monument that can deploy snipers and counter drones and target other “continuously evolving” threats.

“The Arch is a generational national defense opportunity to mitigate these evolving threats,” Hegseth wrote to the court. “It will be utilized as a dual-purpose structure: a major national monument that also incorporates military utility to support Homeland defense.”

The monument will also house “counter sniper personnel,” he said. Mullin argued that the administration needs a “centralized and elevated infrastructure node at a key geographic location” for drone “detection” as well as “disruption and mitigation.”

According to the administration’s latest filings, construction could begin as soon as November 5, with above-ground construction set to begin in May 2027. A finished arch is not expected to be completed until 2030, according to the Justice Department.

Administration officials are trying to throw out a lawsuit from veterans who have argued that the massive arch at Memorial Circle near the entrance to Arlington Memorial Cemetery, where thousands of veterans are buried, would “dishonor” their service and legacy.

Vietnam War veterans Jon Gundersen, Michael Lemmon and Shaun Byrnes, who are suing to stop the project, “all swore an oath to defend the U.S. Constitution,” Gunderson said in a statement.

“And unlike Trump, we have upheld that oath,” he said. “His illegal Arch serves his ego, not the American people. An arch wedged between the line-of-sight of the sacred ground of Arlington National Cemetery, the Lincoln Memorial and Washington Monument dishonors our veterans and our nation. It is the wrong monument, built in the wrong place without Congress’ say.”

The lawsuit also accuses the administration of illegally subverting congressional authority, and advocates have argued that the structure is subject to the Height of Buildings Act, which limits buildings to 130 feet.

In August, the National Park Service acknowledged in a lengthy report that the arch will unavoidably create “adverse effects” and harm historic sites, but the administration plans to go through with the plans anyway.

Last month, after terminating a federally mandated public review process, the Trump administration-led Advisory Council on Historic Preservation told advocacy groups who have raised alarms about the project that the government is moving forward with construction.

But the council said the administration has not submitted any plans that line up with the president’s vision for the project.

Newly released site maps and visual renderings from the Department of Interior also don’t include any information about Trump’s proposed “military” features.

The Trump administration has repeatedly invoked “national security” arguments to push plans for his pet projects, from constructing a massive ballroom and blocking reporters from the White House to banning gender-affirming healthcare for transgender inmates.

In September, the president unveiled plans to turn the arch into a military installation on his Truth Social account, claiming that the request came from the U.S. military for “national security purposes.”

“At the strong request of the United States Military, and for National Security purposes, I have agreed to convert the magnificent Triumphal Arch, planned since the Civil War Era many years ago, at the Receptive Circle adjoining the Arlington Memorial Bridge, into a top grade Military Complex/Triumphal Arch, to house, store, and have the rapid ability to use large numbers of drones, plus Snipers, on both the roof and plaza areas, and additionally have and hold large quantities of sniper ammunition in storage,” Trump wrote.

“There will be no facility like this anywhere in the World,” Trump added, before adding: “Out of the 59 top cities and Capitals, Washington, D.C., IS THE ONLY ONE IN THE WORLD THAT DOES NOT HAVE A TRIUMPHAL ARCH.”

U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan is set to hear arguments Friday about whether to stall construction.

“An injunction would forestall the opportunity for these significant national security benefits and prohibit the government from taking important steps to mitigate an ever-growing threat to the Nation’s capital,” government lawyers wrote Wednesday.

“These significant national defense concerns ‘plainly outweigh’ Plaintiffs’ asserted aesthetic interests,” they added.

Veterans groups and Democrats have been organizing against the project, including staging protests at the site and launching messaging campaigns designed to mobilize other veterans and their families.

“We don’t need tourists overlooking funerals. I’m just appalled,” Karen Meredith, whose son is buried at Arlington, said in a recent video from political advocacy groups VoteVets and Home of the Brave. “If Trump thinks we’ll be quiet about this proposed arch, he’s wrong.”

A reply is imminent, but Iran is reluctant to give upfront and irreversible commitments on its nuclear programme, such as exporting its stockpile of highly enriched uranium to a third country, if the compensatory US steps, such as lifting sanctions on Iranian oil exports, are reversible.

Some form of third-party guarantee that both sides will adhere to their commitments has been discussed, with China often mentioned as the only country of sufficient status to underwrite it.

The return of Vance, a sceptic about the war, to the negotiations is significant and a sign the US has not abandoned diplomacy.

One Democratic senator briefed on the targets the US military had identified in Iran said the plan for a renewed attack was “insane” and would lead to “the likely destruction of our commitment to the rule of law”.

In a sign of Iran’s reluctance to meet US demands about sequencing, its chief negotiator, Mohammad Ghalibaf, wrote on X: “Iran’s conditions have been repeatedly and clearly stated to the mediators. The carrot-and-stick approach has no effect on Iran. Progress can only be achieved once Iran’s seven conditions have been agreed upon.”

Those conditions include lifting sanctions, ending asset freezes and ending the war on all fronts, a phrase that requires an Israeli ceasefire in Lebanon.

The US wants front-loaded, specific commitments about the removal of Iran’s stockpile of highly enriched uranium. Tehran is reluctant to give up its bargaining leverage, and is also resistant to the return of UN nuclear inspectors for fear they might provide the US with intelligence on the whereabouts and state of the stockpile.

It has proposed blending the stockpile down so it is no longer usable to make a nuclear weapon.

The Vance letter was sent to Rezaei rather than Ghalibaf or the foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, because Washington judged that decision-making power rests with those closest to the Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps. In practice, Iran tries to reach decisions through consensus at the highest level of government in a six strong committee.

Ghalibaf and Rezaei met the supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, last week to discuss the risk of a new war. Khamenei has said he would like an end to the current state of neither war nor peace. Iran has well-laid plans ready to strike back against US interests in the region, by hitting desalination plants, internet cables and electricity grids across the Gulf.

For a third US attack in two years to have any rationale, or prospect of inducing Iran’s capitulation, the plan would require the US, probably in conjunction with a caretaker government in Israel, to mount attacks on previously off-limits targets. Europe would not join the attacks, but the views of the Gulf states, the chief targets of Iran reprisals, would be key.

The Connecticut senator Chris Murphy told a briefing at the Middle East Institute: “I did get a fairly detailed conversation about what the war plan would be … I will just say this their plan is insane. It will lead to devastating escalation and the likely destruction of our commitment to the rule of law.

“There has been open reporting as to the civilian infrastructure we may be targeting … I think the plan they have developed will lead, in my judgment, to a prolongment [sic] and escalation of this war that will be devastating to our security interest.”

Although Tehran trusts Vance marginally more than most White House politicians and envoys, the Iranian high command is still suspicious about his status after he negotiated a 14-point memorandum of understanding which then fell apart when other members of the US administration took charge of its implementation.

That led to unilateral US steps to reopen the strait of Hormuz that Iran said contravened the terms of the agreement. Trump also came to detest it because it delayed the start of nuclear talks for as long as 60 days.

In an offer made by Tehran on the margins of the UN general assembly recently, it proposed foreshortening the timeframe before nuclear talks started, saying they could start withinsix or seven days of the US restoring the sanctions waiver on its oil exports and the reopening of the strait of Hormuz.

A judge on Thursday appeared sceptical of one of the challenges, which James’ office has said are “without merit”.
