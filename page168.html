Archaeologists in Russia unearthed a flat wooden "pizza paddle" that had a board game carved into it.

Archaeologists have unearthed an 11th-century wooden kitchen gadget with an ancient board game carved into the back. Medieval bakers may have used the wooden paddle not just to transfer bread into an oven but also to pass the time while they waited for the food to cook, the researchers said.

"We discovered a fragment of a fairly large shovel," Sergey Toropov, director of Novgorod State University's Center for Archaeological Research, said in a translated statement. "Most likely, it was a bread shovel, which was used to bake bread in an oven. On the reverse side, however, three squares have been carved, one inside the other."

The "bread shovel" — also known to contemporary home bakers as a wooden bread peel, a pizza paddle or a pizza peel — was found at the archaeological site of Pyatnitsky in the center of Staraya Russa, a medieval town between Moscow and St. Petersburg where last month, archaeologists reported their discovery of a "snake amulet."

Archaeologists discovered a wooden baking implement — likely a pizza-paddle-like tool — dating back to medieval times.

The preserved portion of the bread peel measures about 12 by 16 inches (30 by 40 centimeters), and it was likely crafted from oak. While one side is flat, likely for baking tasty treats, the other side had been carved with a board for the ancient game known as merels, merrelles, mills, nine-men's morris or cowboy checkers.

The origin of the game is unknown, but historical records and archaeological artifacts suggest a similar two-person logic game was played by the ancient Romans two millennia ago — and the game may be even older than that. In the Middle Ages, merels became particularly popular across Europe and Asia, possibly because it requires little equipment and can be set up quickly.

A typical merels (also known as nine-men's morris, mills or merrelles) board and pieces.

A typical merels board has three nested squares connected by horizontal and vertical lines. Each player has nine pieces of the same color. The goal is to arrange the pieces into columns of three along the lines. When a player forms a line, they can remove any one of their opponent's pieces. In this way, merels is a mixture of tic-tac-toe and checkers. A player wins when their opponent has only two pieces left or is completely blocked. (You can play an online version here.)

"Perhaps special boards for playing merels existed in antiquity," Toropov said, "but more often, one finds playing boards scratched or carved into the flat surfaces of household objects."

The archaeologists are not sure whether the wooden bread peel served two functions or whether it was repurposed as a game board after it broke and was no longer useful.

"At any moment, one could mark out a board on a flat surface, find suitable pieces — for example, small stones lying underfoot, and while away some time," Toropov said.

Merel boards and playing pieces have been recovered from ninth-century archaeological sites in the kingdom of the Rus', whose ancestors were Vikings who arrived in what is now Russia in the eighth century, according to the statement, but "a find as well-preserved as the one from Staraya Russa is practically unique."

Kristina Killgrove is a staff writer at Live Science with a focus on archaeology and paleoanthropology news. Her articles have also appeared in venues such as Forbes, Smithsonian, and Mental Floss. Kristina holds a Ph.D. in biological anthropology and an M.A. in classical archaeology from the University of North Carolina, as well as a B.A. in Latin from the University of Virginia, and she was formerly a university professor and researcher. She has received awards from the Society for American Archaeology and the American Anthropological Association for her science writing.

#1589. 6 Colors You May Regret Painting Your Kitchen, According to Designers

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

The design and functionality of your kitchen are integral to the overall language of your home. It’s possibly the most important room—it’s where the meals are prepped and guests gather, which means it’s somewhere you should feel comfortable and content. Something that can greatly affect the happiness you feel in a space? Paint color.

Interior designers have a lot to say about which paint colors look best or most expensive in a kitchen, meaning they can also weigh in on what colors do not look good in this space. Below, find the six colors that these experts think you may regret painting your kitchen, from canary yellow to stark white.

For Shannon Kadwell, the kitchen and bath director at Anthony Wilder Design Build, cool gray is a shade she avoids in the kitchen. “A kitchen is meant to be the warm, welcoming heart of the home where people actually want to gather—not a sterile commercial office or a hospital corridor,” she says. It can be hard to make gray feel anything but cold and uninspiring, and it completely drains the energy right out of a room, Kadwell adds.

While Sharon L. Sherman of Thyme & Place Design loves black, she recognizes that it can be a difficult shade to use in the kitchen. “I know there is an idea that black hides a multitude of sins, but in reality it shows everything,” she says. “Dust can cling to oils that settle on the cabinets from cooking, and it’s easy to ding cabinet drawers when removing heavy pots and pans.” Plus, it’s also hard to touch up. She would prefer a softly distressed finish for cabinetry if her clients want to incorporate the dark shade.

“School bus yellow is often seen as a risky option,” says Darla Bankston May, principal designer of Bankston May Associates. “In small amounts, this bold, saturated shade can make a lovely accent. But when overused, the effect changes. A kitchen painted in bright yellow quickly becomes loud, visually overwhelming, and even stressful to be in.”

“I would not use a stark white,” cautions Kelly Neely, founder of Kelly Neely Interiors. “White kitchens are classic, but going too white can feel cold in a space that is typically the hub of the home.”

“In kitchens, avoid super saturated yellows or reds,” says Marissa Stokes, founder of Marissa Stokes Interior Design. “They can feel overwhelming in a room meant to be welcoming. Kitchens really shine with softer neutrals or muted tones that let the food and life in the space take center stage.”

“I would not choose a color that I need to vacillate on," says Libby Baker, founder of Baker Design Co. “For example, I would never paint a kitchen lime green as it would be too overwhelming and jarring during mealtimes. Don't get me wrong. I love bold colors, and I love to use them unexpectedly, but painting a kitchen lime green would create an overstimulating experience and feel more like commercial design than home.”

#4218. Typography: Server Mono by Blaze Type for INTDEV

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Blaze Type and INTDEV team up on Server Mono, an open-source monospaced font built for terminal legibility and crisp web typography.

Developers stare at terminal windows all day. If a zero looks too much like an uppercase O, you're going to waste twenty minutes hunting down a broken variable. When Jimmy Lee at Seattle's Internet Development Studio Company (INTDEV) reached out to French foundry Blaze Type, the goal was straightforward: build the foundry's first commissioned open-source font under the SIL license, free for anyone to download, modify, or ship in production code. Type designers Matthieu Salvaggio and Tim Vanhille looked back at Apple's San Francisco Mono and classic ASCII art, drawing a two-style family in Regular and Slanted that holds its edge across low-res command lines and wide web layouts.

Every glyph sits inside the exact same horizontal box. That fixed width is what keeps code indentation honest. Look closely at the vermilion-orange character sheet and you'll spot how Salvaggio and Vanhille solved the classic disambiguation traps in monospaced typography: the numeral 0 carries a crisp diagonal slash, the figure 1 plants a flat base serif against a hooked top flag, and the character set packs both double-story and single-story lowercase a alternates alongside tall curly braces and brackets. Nothing in the spacing feels cramped. Even in the three-column Python specimen running generate_random_string and recursive factorial functions across vermilion, slate-grey, and off-white cards, the vertical rhythm stays locked to the pixel.

The specimen art direction refuses to play it safe. #1597. Don’t Throw Out Your Raked Leaves—Here Are 7 Smart Things to Do With Them Instead

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Doing any of these will help your spring garden look so lush.

Golden yellow, vivid red, and bright orange fall foliage is one of the best parts of the autumn season. When the leaves fall off the trees and into your yard, though, they don’t look as charming—especially when it’s time to rake them into piles. Instead of putting fallen leaves into trash bags and taking them to the landfill, use them to your advantage and set your yard up to be beautiful and lush in the spring.

Keep reading to find seven things you can do with raked leaves besides throwing them away.

If your leaf layer is less than one inch thick, you can mow over leaves, breaking them up to add nutrients back to your lawn. Let leaves dry as much as possible, then go over them with a mower. A mulching mower, which keeps grass particles suspended to be recut into tiny pieces, is best. For a non-mulching mower, you can do a second pass at a right angle to chew the leaves up into small particles.

If you aren’t sure what kind of mower you have, search online for your model number. You can also change the regular blades to mulching blades on some mower models.

According to the National Wildlife Federation, you can rake leaves into garden beds to provide winter cover for beneficial insects, spiders, turtles, toads, and small mammals that spend the cold months under leaf litter. Moths and butterflies may also remain in their chrysalis stage in underbrush until they emerge next year. Keep these leaves whole for better coverage.

Use your mower’s bag attachment, or rake the leaves into a pile, then run them over with the mower a few times, as described above. You can then use the shredded leaves as mulch around shrubs and perennials, according to the University of Delaware. A two- to three-inch layer is sufficient to keep down weeds and reduce soil erosion. This mulch can also insulate roots and prevent plants, especially newly-planted shrubs and perennials, from heaving out of the ground with the freeze-thaw cycles that can occur during the winter.

Shredded leaves work best when tilled into garden beds, but you can use small amounts of whole leaves, if necessary. Till into beds in the fall, or spread over surfaces and work into the soil in early spring. You don’t need a tiller; an old-fashioned garden fork or garden spade works fine to turn over the soil a few times and bury the leaves.

You can invest in a composter or start a simple pile in a corner or behind a shed. You can even make an inexpensive composter by using chicken wire or hardware cloth. Then save a bag of leaves, called “browns,” to add to the composter throughout the next growing season to ensure the proper ratio of green and brown materials for efficient decomposition. If you don't have space in your own yard or garden, check with your local municipality; many towns and cities offer free leaf and yard debris pickup, which they turn into compost that's later offered free to residents.

If there’s an area in your garden that’s always muddy, or you want to convert a path from expensive mulch to free leaves, now’s the time to take advantage of the fall bounty. Again, shredded leaves are best. Whole leaves can be slippery, and they tend to mat down too easily.

A thick layer of leaves (either whole or shredded) can extend the growing season of many greens such as Swiss chard and perennial herbs like sage, parsley, and oregano. Simply mound leaves up three to five inches all around and on top of the plants. Keep the leaves in place by making small cages of chicken wire to contain them. You should be able to harvest for much of the winter, even in cold climates.

#2280. Stop the Blame Game: Time To Restore Bipartisanship in Foreign Aid

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Last year saw the lowest number of people dying of AIDS around the world in over 30 years. This should be big news, given the millions the disease has killed and the billions the United States government has spent to combat it. Yet it isn’t. Why?

It’s not just the media’s natural aversion to reporting good news. The real problem is when that good news undercuts the foreign aid industry’s narrative that President Donald Trump’s dismantling of the U.S. foreign aid apparatus has been an unmitigated global catastrophe.

The U.S. Agency for International Development’s demise last year prompted severe criticism. For thousands of people that lost their jobs and careers it is understandable. But one should expect more calibrated responses from prominent foreign aid advocates.

It was the opposite. U2 frontman Bono cited an estimate suggesting that as many as 300,000 people may already have died as a result of the cuts, while acknowledging that the figure was unproven. Bill Gates warned that millions could die if cuts are not reversed. UNAIDS warned that the collapse of U.S.-supported HIV services could lead to around 4 million additional AIDS-related deaths between 2025 and 2029. A Lancet study projected that continued USAID defunding could result in more than 14 million additional deaths from all causes by 2030.

Missed amid the histrionics was that according to UNAIDS, 570,000 people died of AIDS worldwide in 2025, the lowest in years and a 73% decline from a peak of over 2.1 million in 2004. While still too high, the vast improvement is a testament to America’s unique and durable ability to mobilize massive resources—particularly through the U.S. President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief, or PEPFAR, and taxpayer generosity—to respond to any global crisis anywhere. The predicted mass death did not happen last year, nor so far this year, and the industry looks unserious.

>>> Response to Venezuela’s Crisis Proves That America Can Still Help in Substantial Ways—Even Without USAID

It is over 18 months since Trump ordered a review and suspension of U.S. foreign assistance, leading to USAID’s subsequent dismantling, and the reimagining of foreign aid as an unapologetic tool of national security. This was the basis for USAID’s establishment during the Cold War.

We can now better assess the impact. Emerging is a picture not of death and doom but, rather, a growing self-reliance from once aid-dependent countries where social services were regularly outsourced to foreign groups. They are now stepping up.

An accelerated journey to self-reliance

As the Democratic Republic of Congo struggles to contain its 17th Ebola outbreak—amid concerns about reduced U.S. assistance—in a region beset by armed conflict, neighboring Uganda was declared free of the Ebola virus by the World Health Organization in August.

Kenya, meanwhile, has mobilized testing, contact tracing, and surveillance after confirming its first imported Ebola case in October. As of Oct. 11, authorities had reported no additional infections.

Both prove Africa’s growing competence to handle serious epidemics.

Ghana responded to its aid funding gap by removing a cap on the National Health Insurance Fund, freeing up an estimated $300 million, to “take our destinies into our own hands.”

African leaders are mobilizing continentwide to develop new financial models not with donor agencies but with the private sector. They rightly see local manufacturing of medicines as “central to health sovereignty and long-term development.” Africa CDC estimates that the continent imports around 70%-90% of its medicines and medical devices and 99% of its vaccines.

Kenya’s President William Ruto, one of the continent’s vocal advocates of a shift away from dependence on foreign assistance, sums up Africa's reaction to U.S and Europewide foreign aid cuts: “Africa wants investment and equal partnership, not aid.”

Aid cuts are not just a MAGA obsession. European socialist and conservative governments alike are also reducing aid budgets amid fiscal pressures and changing priorities, and questions of aid effectiveness.

The new U.S. bilateral health agreements

In part, the African continent’s accelerated move to self-reliance springs from the U.S. administration’s restructuring of its global health programs. It has struck 36 government-to-government agreements. The administration says that countries will receive predictable multiyear financing and technical support to transition to full financial ownership of these lifesaving programs. I understand this is designed to cut out international NGOs, U.N. agencies, and contractors in favor of direct engagement with the private drug producers, and is this not what was needed?

Corruption and the legacy of foreign aid

For years, open-ended foreign aid subsidized African budgets that, in many cases, fostered a culture of corruption and rewarded bad economic policies. Meanwhile, aid diversion risks undercutting congressional and popular support for foreign aid.

>>> PEPFAR: From AIDS Relief to Leftwing Funding Apparatus

If the new approach creates stronger incentives for accountability and domestic ownership, that would be a welcome change.

Also, fears that placing America’s global disaster response capacity under the State Department proved misplaced, as shown by the U.S. response to Venezuela’s earthquakes.

Nevertheless, the sector’s condescending dismissal of foreign aid critics continues. The administration’s approach to foreign aid is based on working oneself out of a job by transitioning from traditional grants-based aid to what we all know creates wealth: foreign investment and trade.

This was the core of USAID’s Journey to Self-Reliance during the first Trump administration. Throughout the decades, foreign aid achieved much in advancing American interests and doing good, but it went on too long without a credible and sustainable pathway to self-reliance.

Still, much of the media coverage focuses on the disruption caused by the dismantling of USAID, covering views of former aid officials and defunded partners. Without an honest accounting of both the costs of the cuts and the results of the new approach, rebuilding bipartisan confidence in foreign aid will remain difficult.

The Supreme Court has also apparently upheld the death sentence of another man, Yousef al-Manasif, who was convicted of crimes allegedly committed when he was 15 and 16, putting him at risk of imminent execution. At least three other men convicted of alleged crimes committed as children are on death row, despite allegations of torture, forced confessions, and prolonged solitary confinement.

“The execution of Hassan al-Faraj is unconscionable compounded by alleged torture-tainted evidence and a well-documented record of unfair trials,” said Joey Shea, senior Saudi Arabia researcher at Human Rights Watch. “With the Supreme Court’s recent decision, Yousef al-Manasif is also at serious risk of execution.”

The death sentence for al-Manasif was recently upheld by the Supreme Court, the European Saudi Organisation for Human Rights (ESOHR) reported. In October and November 2022, Saudi Arabia’s notorious Specialized Criminal Court handed down death sentences to al-Manasif and al-Faraj, as well as Ali al-Mabyook, Jawad Qureiris, Ali al-Subaiti, and Mahdi al-Mohsen for alleged terrorism crimes they committed as children, according to a report by the ESOHR and court documents. 

Saudi authorities arrested al-Faraj on November 27, 2017, held him in solitary confinement for six months, and charged him with various counterterrorism offenses, including one allegedly committed when he was 14, which relates to meeting and developing friendships with other detained and wanted individuals, based on the charge sheet and court documents. The Specialized Criminal Court sentenced him to death on November 3, 2022, despite allegations that he had been tortured and forced to sign the confession on which the conviction was based, his defense said. 

Court documents reviewed by Human Rights Watch show that authorities arrested al-Manasif on April 6, 2017, and al-Mabyook on January 29, 2018. Al-Manasif was arrested for protesting Saudi authorities’ treatment of its Shia minority and attending funeral processions in 2011 and 2012 when he was 15 and 16, according to the ESOHR and United Nations experts. Al-Mabyook was arrested for attending funeral processions before he turned 18, ESOHR and court documents said.  

The court convicted and sentenced them to death on November 2, 2022, under broad and vague counterterrorism laws based almost exclusively on confessions that their defense alleges were obtained under torture, the documents showed. The authorities did not inform al-Manasif of his charges until months after his arrest or provide him with legal representation during his interrogation and held him in pretrial detention for over two years, including six months in solitary confinement, the documents show. 

Both al-Manasif and al-Mabyook appealed to the Appellate Chamber of the Specialized Criminal Court on March 19, 2025. The appeals were based on them both having been under 18 when the alleged offenses were committed, the court documents said. On August 26, 2025, the appellate court rejected that defense, referring to the dates in their confessions, despite allegations that the confession was obtained under torture.

Saudi authorities arrested Qureiris on December 31, 2020, court documents say. Saudi authorities charged him under the counterterrorism law for alleged offenses at the ages of 15 and 17. The Specialized Criminal Court convicted and sentenced him to death on November 5, 2022, despite allegations of severe beatings during interrogation to make him sign a confession, the documents say. Qureiris was also held in solitary confinement for 270 days to force the confession, according to the documents.

The decision was submitted to the same court for review on November 12, 2024, where Qureiris stated that he did not commit any of the alleged offenses except participating in a protest in 2010 when he was 15, court documents showed. The court approved his death sentence on March 2, 2025. 

The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention concluded in November 2024 that the detentions of al-Manasif, al-Faraj, and Qureiris were arbitrary and based on the men’s exercise of their right to free speech when they participated in protests against Saudi Arabia’s treatment of the country’s Shia minority. 

On April 9, 2026, Saudi authorities executed Ali al-Subaiti for alleged crimes committed when he was 12, according to the Saudi Press Agency. On October 23, 2017, Saudi authorities arrested al-Subaiti, who was then 19, and charged him under counterterrorism provisions including joining and financing a terrorist organization and attempting to undermine the social fabric and national security by inciting protests, according to the charge sheet. The charges included participating in a demonstration when he was 12 and “covering up for terrorists” when he was 17. The court sentenced al-Subaiti to death on October 17, 2022, according to the ESOHR.

Saudi Arabia’s Juvenile Law of 2018 and a 2020 executive order ended the use of the death penalty against individuals who were under 18 when the crime was committed, in cases that do not involve the 2017 counterterrorism law. In non-terrorism cases, the death penalty is replaced with a maximum 10-year prison sentence. Human Rights Watch has documented Saudi Arabia’s long-standing misuse of counterterrorism provisions to silence dissent.

In 2025, Saudi Arabia executed Jalal al-Labbad and Abdullah al-Derazi, both accused of committing crimes as children, as part of the country’s unprecedented execution spree, despite calls from UN experts and rights groups to halt the executions. 

Human Rights Watch has documented long-standing due process violations in Saudi Arabia’s criminal justice system, including restricted or denied access to legal representation and forced confessions, making it highly unlikely that any of the eight men received a fair trial. 

The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, to which Saudi Arabia is a party, strictly prohibits the use of the death penalty for offenses committed by individuals under 18. Article 6(2) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and article 6 of the Arab Charter on Human Rights oblige countries that use capital punishment to use it only for the “most serious crimes.” Human Rights Watch opposes the death penalty in all countries and circumstances due to its inherent cruelty and irreversibility. 

“Saudi Arabia’s execution spree, particularly for offenses by children, shows no signs of slowing down in 2026,” Shea said. “This underscores the urgent need for the international community to press the Saudi government to immediately halt executions and abolish the death penalty once and for all.”

#1607. Every 2027 Color of the Year So Far, From Moody Greens to Warm Earth Tones

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

In 2027, the design industry is going green—literally.

Color of the Year season is in full swing as brands continue to unveil their picks before the end of the year. While the 2026 shades focused on bold, passionate reds and creamy, near-white neutrals, this year’s choices, informed by cultural analysis, trend forecasts, and annual sales, are leading retailers across the industry to reach for shades that channel comfort, warmth, and a sense of ease.

For 2027, Earth is the inspiration. That’s right, the data shows that we, as a collective, are practically aching to touch grass. Lush greens, deep browns, ocean blues, and muted clays define the honorees, which make a stunning, nature-inspired palette.

Some brands are still planning their big reveal, and we’ll be here to report as new shades break, but for now, here are the colors of the year so far.

Valspar Color of the Year: Cottage Door (8004-38E)

Cottage Door (8004-38E), Valspar’s pick for 2027, is a cool, mid-toned blue taking the spotlight as a new era neutral. Meant to channel comfort, security, and respite, the slightly grey shade balances warm and cool undertones for an inviting yet versatile feel.

Dutch Boy Paints Color of the Year: Deep Rooted (311-7DB)

In line with the cultural shift toward joy-filled living, Deep Rooted (311-7DB) combines nature’s warmth with red and brown undertones to reveal a bold, earthy orange that pairs effortlessly with classic neutrals, inky blues, and smoky charcoals.

“Our 2027 Color of the Year feels alive with possibility while being rooted in the familiar warmth of nature. Deep Rooted offers a color that feels rich, grounding, and deeply human,” said Lisbeth Parada, Color Marketing Manager, Dutch Boy Paints, in a press release.

Behr Color of the Year: Grounded (T27-01)

Behr’s Grounded (T27-01) is a moss-inspired homage to the beauty of nature.

“It moves between moss and olive, with subtle brown undertones that give the green warmth and weight. There’s something sheltering about that combination, and the moss notes bring life,” says Kayla Kratz, Head of Color at BEHR.

According to data shared with House Beautiful, about 77 percent of Americans report feeling overwhelmed by the demands of daily life. Nature has become a common solace for many looking for a pause in the chaos. Through Grounded, folks can bring the serenity of the outdoors in.

Glidden Color of the Year: Artifact (PPG10-16)

Glidden’s Color of the Year, Artifact (PPG10-16), is a smoky, sturdy blue that’s meant to reset your home and mindset through grey and turquoise undertones. Paired with creamy neutrals, smoky greys, and pastel greens, the hue channels the serenity of a stormy sky, ocean waves, or a babbling creek.

Sherwin-Williams and HGTV Home Color of the Year: Celery (SW 6421)

According to Sherwin-Williams and HGTV Home, Celery (SW 6421) is the new butter yellow. Inspired by the humble garden patch, this organic, earthy, herby, soft hue embraces the growing shift toward the “lived-in” aesthetic and eco-inspired designs.

“Homeowners and designers are increasingly prioritizing wellness at home, seeking soothing environments that support mental health and overall well‑being. This shift has also fueled interest in eco-inspired design, with a greater emphasis on bringing elements of the natural world indoors through color, while a sustainability mindset is shaping preferences for hues that feel enduring,” said Ashley Banbury, color marketing manager at Sherwin-Williams, in a press release.

Clark+Kensington Color of the Year: Vantage Point (38B-4)

Inspired by the beauty of a clear sky, Clark + Kensington’s 2027’s Color of the Year is Vantage Point (38B-4). The serene, sophisticated, slightly dusty shade is a move toward new neutrals that embrace bold expressions of personality.

Little Greene Color of the Year: Marle (355)

Brown is primed for a comeback, and Little Greene knows it. For their Color of the Year, the eco-conscious, family-run luxury retailer selected Marle (355), a hue from the recently launched Colour Scales collection.

“Often dismissed as being a little dull, it’s wonderful to see browns being embraced and recognised as warm, earthy and indulgent shades that are perfect for delivering restful spaces that radiate balance and tranquillity,” said Little Greene’s Creative Director, Ruth Mottershead, in a press release. The complex shade balances bold yellow ochre, black, and red oxide tones to reveal a refreshing neutral filled with warmth.

Dunn-Edwards Color of the Year: French Press Coffee (DEBN07)

Dunn-Edwards Color of the Year is a full-bodied brown with deep plum undertones. French Press Coffee (DEBN07) channels luxury, comfort, and the coziness of routine.

“French Press Coffee is meant to feel like a slow morning: the ritual of a ceramic mug in your hands, warm light, a moment of stillness before the day starts,” says Lauren Hoferkamp, color marketing manager at Dunn-Edwards Corporation. “It's comforting and familiar, but elevated. Culturally, it reflects a real fatigue with the cool, pared-back neutrals that have dominated interiors.”

Minwax Color of the Year: Pickled Oak (MW260)

Minwax, a company specializing in interior wood stains, crowned Pickled Oak (MW260) as its 2027 Color of the Year. Gentle and warm, the ultra-light shade highlights the beauty of natural grain while pairing effortlessly with jewel tones, pastels, and neutrals alike.

Benjamin Moore Color of the Year: Webster Green (HC-130)

Webster Green (HC-130), Benjamin Moore’s 2027 Color of the Year, is a lush shade that channels historical roots. Rooted in an air of storied sophistication, the hue was originally launched in 1976 and was inspired by the 18th- and 19th-century design trends that came to define great North American architecture.

The green color family’s ability to adapt and transform makes it incredibly timeless,” said Andrea Magno, vice president of brand, color, and integrated marketing at Benjamin Moore, in a press release. “Sitting at the center point is Webster Green HC-130, a mid-tone that serves as a reminder of the past but feels right and relevant today.”
