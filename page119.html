#2404. Trump Sues to Block Alaska Senate Election Results Because of Potential Voter "Confusion"

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

The administration claims voters might be confused between two candidates named "Dan Sullivan."

Today, the Trump Justice Department filed a federal lawsuit seeking to block Alaska Senate election results on the grounds they might be influenced by voter "confusion" between incumbent Republican Senator Dan S. Sullivan and a separate Republican candidate with a very similar name (Dan L. Sullivan). I previously wrote about the issues raised by the two Dan Sullivans here. As I pointed out in the earlier post, there is no legal basis for overturning congressional election results based on voter confusion, and any such action would likely violate the First Amendment. See also this analysis by election law specialist Rick Hasen.

I would add that any such federal action also runs afoul of states' primary authority over election procedures. The Elections Clause of the Constitution gives states primary authority over "The Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives," subject to Congress' power to modify these rules by legislation. Congress has not enacted any statute allowing overturning of election results based on possible "voter confusion."

If "voter confusion" were a legitimate reason to overturn election results, that would imperil a great many election winners, as voter ignorance - and exploitation thereof by candidates - is a widespread and longstanding problem. I covered some of these points in my previous post about the two Dan Sullivans:

Donald Trump is a particularly egregious example, going all the way back to his first campaign in 2016, and more recently with his lies about the 2020 election and other issues. But more conventional politicians also often resort to lies and deception, even if not to the same degree.  President Obama, for example, famously  lied when he claimed that, under the Affordable Care Act, "if you like your health care plan, you can keep it." That deception won the 2013 Politifact Lie of the Year award. But it still helped Obama get reelected in 2012. I am not sure exploiting voter confusion about names is any worse than exploiting their ignorance about policy issues like crime, immigration, and health care….

Ultimately, both exploitation of voter confusion about names and other deceptions used in campaigns seek to exploit widespread voter ignorance. Because of the combination of "rational ignorance" (lack of incentive to seek out political information) and "rational irrationality" (lack of incentive to engage in unbiased evaluation),  most voters know little about government and public policy, and often do a poor job of evaluating the information they do know. This makes them vulnerable to deception and manipulation, and incentivizes politicians to lie and otherwise distort the truth.

Voter confusion about candidates with similar names is just another example of this broader problem….

There is no easy solution to the problem of voter ignorance. But I assess a range of possible options in my 2023 article on "Top-Down and Bottom-Up Solutions to the Problem of Political Ignorance, and in my earlier book Democracy and Political Ignorance. I believe the best approach is to make fewer decisions at the ballot box and more by "voting with your feet," where incentives to seek out information and evaluate it wisely are much better. But I  recognize that any effective approach will take time, and there may be no one fix that is sufficient by itself.  We may need to pursue multiple strategies. In the meantime, voters are likely to continue to be ignorant and confused about a variety of issues, and politicians and interest groups are likely to keep on exploiting that.

Many of these other voter ignorance problems are far worse than any potential confusion between candidates with similar names, a problem that only comes up relatively rarely.

DOJ has no power to decide which candidates can appear on the ballot. The sole basis that DOJ asserted for trying to block the state's certification process is the National Voter Registration Act's preamble that reads that the act is designed to "protect the integrity of the electoral process." That preamble of course gives no power whatsoever to the federal government to do anything. It does not give DOJ the power to determine ballot access or block certification of a federal election. The Alaska Supreme Court decided the state law ballot access question back in June, allowing the second Sullivan to appear on the ballot.

#3318. Replacing Your Bike Chain Is Easier Than You Think—Here’s How to Do It at Home

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A step-by-step home repair guide to measuring, removing, and installing a bike chain.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

Replacing a chain is a great way for someone to tap into their home bike maintenance potential. It’s a pretty straightforward process once you’re used to it, especially if you have the existing chain to measure from. I typically recommend being familiar with a few skills before diving into replacing your chain. The first skill to know is adjusting your front and rear derailleurs. The second is knowing how to use a chain checker. The third is being familiar with a pre bike check. This last one is especially helpful because it will allow you to run through the bike to see if there are any other issues before diving into swapping out your chain. Again, these skills are not required skills to replace a chain, but having some familiarity with the basic mechanics addressed above will only help make this process easier. Here we’ll go through the tools you’ll need and the basic process to replace your chain at home.

How often should you replace your chain? Before answering that question, it helps to understand the process of chain wear. As your chain ages, each link’s internal bushings slowly lengthen. In turn, your now-longer chain puts added pressure on your cassette cogs and chainring teeth, causing them to wear faster. This also hampers shifting quality.

To avoid this accelerated wear of your cassette and chainrings, a general rule of thumb is to replace your bike’s chain every 2,000 miles. Mind you, this is just a starting point. No two chains will wear at exactly the same rate because no two riders treat their chains the same.

These differences in riding style are why it is best to periodically use a chain wear measuring tool to determine the condition of your chain. With the tool in hand, apply pressure to a pedal so the top of the chain is taut, and then place the measuring tool in place on the chain as shown in the video above and read the results. Indicators on the tool will tell you whether it’s time to replace your chain.

Cutting a new bike chain to the correct length for installation and removing a damaged link for an on-trail chain repair are essentially the same job. Make life easier by using a full-size tool at home, and save the compact tool for midride fixes.

To start, you’ll need to pick up the right size chain for your bike. We’re not talking about the length of the chain here (you’ll address that later), but width. This is dictated by the number of cogs on your cassette; a six-speed bike uses the same width chain as an eight-speed, but a nine-speed needs something different, and so on. (Check out Park Tool’s chain compatibility guide for more on this.) The easiest way to ensure you get the right chain is to buy the same kind of chain that came on your bike. If you decide to go with a different chain, it’s best to get one from the same manufacturer.

If your chain has a master link such as a Shimano Quick Link or SRAM PowerLock, use a pair of master link pliers to compress the link and undo it. (If it’s a newer chain, you might be able to do this with your hands.)

If there isn’t a master link, remove the chain using the chain breaker tool. Back out the tool’s pin to make room and fit a chain link into the outer slot, and line up the tool’s pin with a pin on the chain. Tighten the tool until its pin puts light pressure on the chain’s connecting pin. Crank the handle, making sure the tool’s pin stays straight. If it twists, stop and realign everything so as not to bend the tool’s pin. If you bend it, most tools have replaceable pins available; some even come with one inside the handle. Then push the pin far enough through so you can remove the extra links.

Remove the chain from your bike. Then, measure out the length of your new chain to match the length of your old chain, taking into account the master link. Remove any excess links from the new chain using the chain breaker tool, and install the master link. Thread the new chain onto your bike’s cassette and chainring and connect the ends with the master link, making sure the link is secure and locked in place.

A remarkably preserved 150-million-year-old bird fossil discovered in China is challenging long-standing ideas about how birds evolved. Rather than developing increasingly sophisticated flight abilities, some of the earliest birds appear to have taken a very different evolutionary path, including losing the ability to fly altogether.

The newly identified species, Yuezhuornis yangyuanensis, lived during the Late Jurassic period (150-148 million years ago). Described in a study published in Nature, the crow-sized creature provides evidence that early bird evolution was much more complex than scientists once believed. Instead of evolving primarily to become better flyers, ancient birds were adapting to a variety of environments and ways of life, including those that favored living on the ground.

Researchers from the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology (IVPP) of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) and the Fujian Institute of Geological Sciences uncovered the fossil in the recently recognized Zhenghe Fauna of Fujian Province in southeastern China. According to their findings, Yuezhuornis represents the earliest known example of a bird lineage losing its ability to fly.

An Ancient Bird With Dinosaur Teeth and Claws

One of the most remarkable features of Yuezhuornis is its unusual combination of bird and dinosaur characteristics. Although it possessed skeletal features associated with birds, it also retained anatomical traits that would have been familiar among small predatory dinosaurs.

Its jaws contained closely spaced, serrated teeth resembling those of deinonychosaurs, a group of meat-eating dinosaurs that included agile predators related to birds. Such teeth are not normally associated with early birds.

Even more striking was its second toe, which could bend backward unusually far and carried a curved, sickle-shaped claw. This specialized feature is strongly associated with deinonychosaurs, including dromaeosaurids and troodontids, two groups of predatory dinosaurs.

"When we examined Yuezhuornis, we were struck by its extraordinary mix of features," said Dr. Min Wang, corresponding author of the study. "It possesses bird-like pelvic and hindlimb structures yet retains the specialized predatory foot and teeth of a small meat-eating dinosaur. It shows us that early bird evolution was far more complicated than we previously realized."

To understand how Yuezhuornis was related to other prehistoric birds and dinosaurs, scientists carried out detailed analyses of its evolutionary relationships. They used two approaches, maximum parsimony and Bayesian tip-dating, to compare anatomical characteristics and reconstruct the species' position on the evolutionary tree.

Maximum parsimony identifies evolutionary relationships that require the fewest changes in characteristics, while Bayesian tip-dating also incorporates statistical estimates of evolutionary history and information about when fossils lived.

Both methods produced consistent results, placing Yuezhuornis within Avialae, the evolutionary group that includes modern birds and their closest extinct relatives.

The findings indicate that Yuezhuornis belonged to an early branch of bird evolution rather than simply being a small predatory dinosaur with bird-like characteristics.

The researchers also examined how Yuezhuornis might have lived. Using measurements of its skeletal proportions and statistical comparisons with other animals, they investigated whether its anatomy was better suited for flying or moving along the ground.

The results showed that its limb proportions closely resembled those of modern ground-dwelling birds and certain other prehistoric dinosaurs closely related to birds. This similarity suggests that Yuezhuornis spent its life on the ground and was likely unable to fly.

The findings point to an unexpected development in the history of birds. Rather than simply progressing toward stronger wings and more efficient flight, some early bird lineages appear to have moved in the opposite direction, adapting to lifestyles in which flying was no longer necessary.

This challenges the traditional assumption that the earliest stages of bird evolution were driven primarily by the demands of flight. Instead, birds were already exploring different ecological roles and developing a surprisingly wide range of body forms.

A 150-Million-Year-Old Discovery Rewrites Bird Evolution

The discovery of Yuezhuornis suggests that the evolution of birds was never a simple, one-directional process. Even during the Late Jurassic, when the earliest bird lineages were emerging, some species were developing adaptations that favored life on land rather than in the air.

Its unusual combination of bird-like anatomy, dinosaur-style predatory features, and apparent flightlessness provides a revealing glimpse into that evolutionary experimentation. The fossil shows that early birds were more anatomically and ecologically diverse than previously recognized.

It also underscores the scientific importance of the Zhenghe Fauna, a fossil assemblage that is helping researchers investigate a poorly understood period in bird evolution. As additional discoveries emerge from this region of China, scientists may gain an even clearer picture of the surprising evolutionary paths taken by the earliest birds.

#930. Glioblastoma breakthrough reveals how brain cancer cells become more aggressive

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Glioblastoma is among the most lethal forms of brain cancer, and scientists have uncovered a surprising factor that may help its cells become more invasive. The fluid surrounding the outer edges of these tumors is much thicker than the fluid near their centers, forcing cancer cells to encounter increasing resistance as they move into healthy brain tissue. Rather than simply slowing their movement, however, this environment appears to help the cells adapt in ways that make invasion easier.

To investigate this unexpected behavior, researchers created an innovative microfluidic chip that recreates the physical conditions encountered by glioblastoma cells. They found that spending extended periods in thick fluid caused the cells to become smaller, more flexible, and better able to squeeze through narrow openings. The discovery suggests that fluid viscosity does more than obstruct cancer cells. It can actively influence their behavior and potentially make them more aggressive.

How Thick Fluid Influences Brain Cancer Invasion

Cancer research has traditionally concentrated on chemical signals and the stiffness of the tissues surrounding tumors. Far less attention has been given to the viscosity of the fluid through which cancer cells travel.

In glioblastoma, this difference is particularly striking. Fluid at the tumor's advancing edge is approximately eight times more viscous than fluid in its dead tissue at the center. As cancer cells move outward, they must push through an increasingly resistant environment.

Studying this process has been difficult because conventional closed microfluidic devices cannot accurately reproduce these conditions. Their enclosed channels can limit access to oxygen and nutrients, while friction against the walls introduces additional forces that influence cell behavior. These limitations also make it challenging to observe how cancer cells respond to prolonged exposure to viscous fluid.

Researchers therefore needed a way to isolate the effects of fluid viscosity from other mechanical influences and determine whether extended exposure produces lasting changes in glioblastoma cells.

A New Device Reveals How Cancer Cells Adapt

Scientists from Chongqing General Hospital and Chongqing University in China developed a specialized open microfluidic platform to address these challenges. Their findings were published in Microsystems & Nanoengineering.

The device consists of a two-layer open microfluidic membrane equipped with a removable cap and an array of tiny pillars. This arrangement allows researchers to control precisely when cancer cells begin migrating while observing how their nuclei change shape as they move through confined spaces.

Unlike traditional enclosed systems, the platform also supports continuous cell culture for up to one month. This makes it possible to examine how prolonged mechanical stress changes the movement and invasive behavior of glioblastoma cells.

The researchers tested two human glioblastoma cell lines, U-251 and LN-229. Both were grown for a month in a thick fluid designed to reproduce conditions at the tumor's invasive outer edge (7.1 cP).

When the cells were subsequently examined using the chip, those that had adapted to the viscous environment traveled faster and covered greater distances than untreated control cells. This result was especially surprising because thicker fluid would ordinarily be expected to impede movement.

Brain Cancer Cells Become Smaller and More Flexible

Microscopic observations revealed how the adapted cells overcame this resistance. After prolonged exposure to the viscous fluid, they had become smaller and easier to deform, allowing them to maneuver through the narrow gaps between the device's microscopic pillars.

As the cells squeezed through these restricted passages, their nuclei underwent visible deformation. Researchers also observed an accumulation of YAP, a protein involved in sensing mechanical forces, inside the nuclei. This is a recognized sign that cells have activated pathways involved in responding to physical stress.

Although both glioblastoma cell lines developed similar changes in shape and movement, their underlying molecular responses were unexpectedly different.

U-251 cells underwent a form of mesenchymal-like reprogramming, activating genes associated with cancer invasion, including CD44, FN1, and MMP9. LN-229 cells, by contrast, became more flexible and mobile without showing substantial lasting changes in gene expression.

Further analysis using Western blotting showed that protein changes remained detectable even after cells were transferred back into fluid with normal viscosity. This indicated that at least some of the adaptations were persistent rather than simply short-lived responses to their surroundings.

Scientists Discover a Strange Kind of Cellular Memory

One of the most unexpected findings was that exposure to thick fluid appeared to leave a lasting imprint on glioblastoma cells.

The researchers had anticipated that viscosity would primarily function as a physical obstacle. Instead, their experiments showed that prolonged exposure could alter how cells behaved even after the original mechanical conditions were removed.

This phenomenon points to a form of mechanical memory, in which cells retain changes produced by earlier physical stress.

The open design of the new chip was essential for identifying this effect. By separating resistance from the fluid itself from the confinement imposed by channel walls, the researchers could investigate viscosity without the additional mechanical interference that complicates conventional experiments.

Observing changes in nuclear shape and migration patterns after weeks of exposure provided evidence that glioblastoma cells can adapt to their mechanical environment in lasting ways.

The contrasting molecular responses of the two cell lines were particularly revealing. Although both appeared to undergo similar physical transformations, only one showed extensive changes in its gene activity. This suggests that glioblastoma cells may acquire comparable invasive characteristics through different underlying biological processes.

A Potential New Approach to Studying Brain Cancer Treatments

Beyond uncovering an overlooked influence on glioblastoma invasion, the researchers believe their device could provide a valuable tool for investigating potential cancer therapies.

The open microfluidic platform fits into standard multi-well plates, allowing it to be incorporated into existing laboratory procedures for cell culture and imaging. Its accessible design also enables researchers to stain cells directly and observe them over long periods without the clogging problems that can occur in closed systems.

These features could make it useful for testing drugs that interfere with the cellular pathways responsible for detecting and responding to mechanical forces.

For glioblastoma, the results raise the possibility that highly viscous environments may favor cancer cells that are better adapted to invasion. Treatments targeting YAP signaling or the remodeling of the cytoskeleton, the internal framework that helps cells maintain and change their shape, could therefore be investigated under conditions that more closely resemble those inside tumors.

The technology may also prove useful for studying other cancers in which fluid viscosity varies across different regions. By examining how tumor cells respond to these mechanical conditions, researchers could gain a better understanding of why some cancers are particularly invasive and potentially identify patients whose tumors depend on mechanical adaptation to spread.

Although further research is needed to determine how these laboratory findings translate to tumors in patients, the discovery highlights an important and previously underexplored aspect of cancer biology: the physical environment surrounding tumor cells may actively shape their ability to invade healthy tissue.
