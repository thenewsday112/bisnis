#889. I Tried Turning Rice Krispies into Brains, and They’re Almost Too Creepy to Eat

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It's surprisingly easy to make an innocent dessert look this disturbing.

There’s a narrow window each year when making food look actively unappetizing counts as good hosting. In October, you can set a platter of bloody brains on the table and reasonably expect someone to compliment your work. I appreciate a holiday with that much range.

Rice Krispies treats happen to be particularly well-suited to that assignment. They’re wonderfully accommodating when it comes to upgrades and seasonal makeovers—even ones that leave them looking a bit medically concerning. Their bumpy texture does most of the brain decorating for you, leaving you to handle the shape and decide just how much gore is appropriate for dessert.

After spotting them on Instagram, I made a batch myself. My Rice Krispies treats rarely get more elaborate than tidy squares (unless you count making single-servings in the microwave just for me), but this version makes a remarkably convincing little horror show.

Rice Krispies brains are marshmallow cereal treats shaped into small ovals, with an indentation added down the middle to create hemispheres. A pale pink color and a little red decoration finish the effect. There’s no special mold, and you don’t need to pipe an intricate network of squiggles to mimic anything anatomical. The cereal’s uneven surface does most of the work, which is ideal for me since “vaguely disfigured” is actually within the scope of my limited decorating abilities.

Underneath all the Halloween horror, the treats still have the familiar combination of crisp cereal, butter and melted marshmallows, with the same chewy texture and buttery sweetness I want from a regular batch. The only real adjustment was getting comfortable eating something that deliberately resembles an organ.

I started by melting 4 tablespoons of butter and 2 cups of marshmallows together in a large saucepan over low heat, stirring until the mixture was smooth. I added a few drops of red food coloring to give it a pale pink tint, then I stirred in 4 cups of Rice Krispies cereal until they were evenly coated. Pink marshmallows would also work well here, if you can find them at your store.

Once the mixture was cool enough to handle, I lightly oiled my hands and shaped portions into small, slightly flattened ovals. The oil makes the mixture much easier to work with, leaving you to play mad scientist instead of picking sticky cereal off your fingers.

For a Halloween party, these deliver an impressive effect without much decorating skill. And since they’re supposed to look a little misshapen, there’s no reason to make them all look identical. All that’s left is to arrange them on a platter and enjoy being asked who brought the brains.

#3520. One of the World’s Top Marathoners Has Been Provisionally Suspended After an EPO Finding

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Peres Jepchirchir’s out-of-competition sample was collected before her Sydney Marathon victory.

Peres Jepchirchir won the Sydney Marathon on August 30, adding another major victory to an already stacked résumé. On Friday, however, the Athletics Integrity Unit announced a development that could have major consequences for what comes next in her career.

“The AIU has provisionally suspended reigning World and former Olympic champion, Peres Jepchirchir, after an out-of-competition sample, collected in Kenya prior to her recent Sydney Marathon victory, returned an adverse analytical finding for EPO,” the organization said.

At this point, the AIU announcement isn’t a final ruling that Jepchirchir committed an anti-doping violation, and the case still has to work through the formal process.

Jepchirchir has spent years collecting titles across multiple distances. She won Olympic marathon gold at the 2021 Tokyo Games, then returned to Japan four years later to win the world marathon championship. Before either victory, she had already won two world half-marathon titles and set three world records at that distance. Her marathon personal best of 2:14:43 makes her the ninth-fastest woman of all time.

Sydney Marathon organizers responded Friday with a statement of their own, addressing the case and the event’s anti-doping efforts.

“Our commitment to clean sport is unwavering. Every athlete who lines up in Sydney deserves to race on a level playing field, and every participant and spectator deserves to trust the result they see at the finish line,” organizers said. “That conviction is why, alongside our fellow Abbott World Marathon Majors, we have invested in increased out-of-competition testing in partnership with the AIU, and why we will keep standing firmly with the athletes who compete cleanly.”

The World Marathon Majors separately pointed to the potential consequences if Jepchirchir is ultimately found to have committed a doping offense.

“Under our rules, in addition to any punishment imposed by World Athletics, national federations or any national anti-doping agency or government, any runner who has been found by such body to have committed a doping offence (at any competition or out of competition) and has been subject to a ban of three months or more, shall be banned from all WMM events for life unless otherwise agreed by WMM and be ineligible any time to receive any WMM prize money or WMM points,” the organization said.

Jepchirchir doesn’t currently meet the rule’s stated conditions: While she has been provisionally suspended, she hasn’t been found to have committed a doping offense or received a ban.

What comes next will depend on how the anti-doping case plays out. Runner’s World also reached out to Jepchirchir’s athlete representative, Gianni Demadonna, for comment.

“I can only say that I feel terrible about what happened,” Demadonna wrote in an email, “and I think it’s only right that an athlete who uses the performance enhancing drugs should be disqualified.”

#3529. How Many Marathons Is Too Many?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Experts explain how often to race 26.2 without risking burnout, injury, or declines in performance.

It’s easy to catch the marathon bug. Influencers and everyday runners flood social media talking about their training, their accomplishments, and their speedy splits. And when your options range from exciting destination races and multiday challenge events to the coveted World Marathon Majors, it’s easy to get caught up in signing up for 26.2 after 26.2.

What does a race calendar that brings out your best performances—while still supporting your recovery and life outside of running—actually look like?

We talked to an exercise physiologist and a sports psychology researcher to find out the physical and mental effects of marathoning and how to determine the number of races per year that keeps you motivated and feeling accomplished without overwhelm or burnout.

It’s absolutely possible to go overboard on marathoning, Janet Hamilton, CSCS, an exercise physiologist and the founder and head coach of Running Strong, tells Runner’s World. Many factors play into the number of races per year that best serves an individual, she explains.

One of the most important to consider? Your overall goal.

“Do you have some sort of a time goal, like you want to qualify for Boston? Or do you just want to be able to say you’ve completed one every month? Those two goals are very different,” she explains.

Racing a marathon at an all-out effort places much more stress on the body than jogging one at your easy pace. Of course, both efforts still require recovery, adds Hamilton. “But if you have a specific performance goal, it behooves you to train with that purpose in mind,” she says.

This means setting aside a full cycle of training to properly base-build, add in speed, gradually taper, and finally, race. To achieve your best result, you can’t rush any part of this process.

Hamilton’s guidance for most runners who want to race well is to aim for a maximum of two, maybe three, marathons per year. “Can you get away with more than that? Sure. Some people complete streaks of marathoning every weekend,” she says. “But that can be really hard on your body, physiologically speaking.”

Significant physiological changes occur following a long-duration hard-effort exercise bout such as the marathon, explains Hamilton. This can include weakened immune function—part of the reason many runners come down with a postrace cold—elevation of enzymes that indicate cardiac damage, increased levels of the stress hormone cortisol, and a decrease in testosterone levels.

“Generally, your body will come back in balance with a proper recovery cycle,” Hamilton says. But if you never give yourself a break from racing, it becomes much more challenging for you to fully recuperate. “Over time, this takes a toll on your body. And you might not always be able to necessarily feel it.”

Some signs, she explains, would only show up in specific blood tests or diagnostic scans, such as an elevated cardiac calcification score, which can increase the risk for heart problems. “We can certainly do blood tests to look for markers of creatine kinase [an indicator of tissue trauma from microtears in the muscle], but how many of us get blood tested after every marathon?”

Fortunately, you don’t need a lab test to notice every red flag. Some more obvious symptoms that you’re overdoing it include persistent fatigue, getting sick more often, trouble sleeping, low mood, decreased motivation, and nagging aches or pains that don’t resolve with rest. You also might find that your usual pace on easy runs feels noticeably harder to maintain.

More and more exercise does not necessarily mean better and better physical fitness, Hamilton says. “At some point, that correlation between volume and health kind of starts to fall apart,” she says. And that not only goes for physical health but for mental health, as well.

According to a study published in Acta Psychologica, multimarathoners—defined as individuals who had completed at least 100 official marathons—had higher depression and anxiety scores compared with the general population, though still below clinical thresholds. “We found that high levels of physical activity don’t necessarily equate to good mental health,” says the study’s lead author, Leo Lundy, PhD.

Of the 576 runners in the study, about 30 percent had few or no symptoms of anxiety and depression, he explains. However, about 25 percent experienced higher levels than average. “What was particularly worrying was that 8 percent—which is a significant percentage—showed signs of severe depression or high anxiety, way above what you’d expect in the general public,” says Lundy.

This doesn’t mean that multimarathoning causes these symptoms; it does, however, show an association. And to understand the full picture, we need to examine the type of individual who is drawn to multimarathoning in the first place, Lundy explains.

“When you look at the type of person that's attracted to long-distance running, two areas of the personality were significant in the research,” he says. “One was conscientiousness: These individuals love to follow the rules. The other was emotional stability,” which assessed how calm or even-keeled they generally tended to be.

In psychometric tests, marathoners tended to score very high in conscientiousness but lower in emotional stability, which could correlate to higher levels of depression and anxiety, explains Lundy.

Enter: the marathon. This race provides exactly the environment that this personality type needs to thrive. “The marathon is very structured: You follow the rules, you turn up on time, and you know what’s going to happen. It’s all very predictable in that regard,” he says.

Add in the meaningful social connection that comes from gathering with fellow runners and the sense of achievement from completing a full 26.2 miles, and the picture becomes clearer: These benefits all help marathoners manage those lower levels of emotional stability. And by providing a reliable framework for them to succeed and belong, the race itself becomes a valuable coping mechanism for underlying anxiety and depression.

However, racing marathon after marathon can also mean sustained physical fatigue, competition pressure, increased injury risk, and sacrificing other aspects of life. So, Lundy explains, if a runner relies too heavily on marathoning and continues despite mounting physical, emotional, or lifestyle costs, it becomes a source of stress. As a result, it may contribute to the very symptoms a runner was trying to relieve.

Both mentally and physically, the amount of effort you put into 26.2 matters when taking on multiple marathons each year. But for some runners, especially those scoring high in conscientiousness, it can be difficult to toe the line and not give their best effort.

“As a coach, I consider the individual runner’s personality: How well will they be able to adhere to just doing it at a training effort when that gun goes off?” says Hamilton. Many runners will want to push the pace beyond an easy jog.

So if someone really wants to do a series of mostly easy marathons, she still advises the majority of athletes to complete no more than four marathons per year.

Take Lundy himself, for example, who, at age 62, has completed more than four hundred marathons, including seven just this year. Lundy says he still enjoys running marathons regularly, but emphasizes this crucial element: Fast performances are not the goal.

“The most successful multi-marathoners that I’ve come across are people that run at a sub-maximal level. They really are quite slow, quite determined, some running into the seven-hour ranges,” he says. “They’re running for lots of other reasons besides performance.”

Some of those reasons? Just to finish, to enjoy the social environment, to travel, to stay active and healthy. “I’ve run marathons in close to 25 countries; most of my friends are in running communities, so I see them regularly, and achieving new milestones continues to make me happy. That’s really what drives me,” Lundy says.

While the marathon gives you a specific goal, you don’t have to keep chasing finish lines to reap many of the same benefits Lundy mentions. Running in general supports cardiovascular and cognitive health while also providing a sense of fulfillment, connection, and belonging, he says.

So, you might decide to race 26.2 once, many times, or maybe not at all—the right number of marathons for you is the number that keeps you feeling strong, healthy, and happy enough to keep on running.

#1366. London Hotspot Berenjak Just Opened Its Third, And Perhaps Best, U.S. Location

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In Chicago, at its fourth Soho House–based restaurant, the Persian standby plays all the hits.

It only takes a few moments—say between 30 seconds and a minute after passing the host’s stand—inside Berenjak’s new Chicago outpost to realize the space is dialed in to the highest degree. This is Berenjak’s 12th location, its ninth public one (Brooklyn and the Cotswolds are exclusive to Soho House members), its fourth in partnership with Soho House, and the third stateside. It sits just above the house’s ground-floor bar in the city’s hot Fulton Market District. And everything about it makes it feel like the most true-to-the-original edition we’ve gotten this side of the pond.

The location is part of a meteoric rise that started only eight years ago with a cramped (founder Kian Samyani does not mince words about the original location’s footprint) spot in Soho. Berenjak Chicago runs with the efficiency of restaurant number 12, but it has a lot of the heart that the original location has.

It’s not small, around 50 seats at a glance, but it feels intimate. The two-tops are close enough to scout dishes as they come out next to you but not so close that you feel as if you’re sharing a table. There’s a lovely six-top in the middle of the room. And about one third of the seating is at counters, one facing the charcoal grill in the kitchen and the other facing the bar. Three-quarter café curtains on the one wall of windows keep the eyes within the space, and a mostly candlelit dining area makes the room feel smaller (charmingly smaller) than it actually is. There’s a lot going on; this is a serious operation. But it’s laid out in a way that doesn’t feel too different from that kababi in Soho.

The real difficulty when scaling restaurants is the menu. Here, Samyani has the team playing all the hits. The Kabab Koobideh, the restaurant’s cornerstone minced-lamb-and-onion kebab, still holds the top spot, and just below it is the chicken Jujeh Kabab. The Jujeh Kabab Tond—meaning spicy—adds a bit of a kick, more up-front spice than lingering spicy, to the chicken kebab. Though the real stars lie within the bread and mazeh section. The black chickpea hummus is divine, a lot deeper and earthier than your standard chickpea spread. Of the masts—traditional Persian yogurt spreads—the Mast O Khiar is my favorite. With cucumber and mint, it’s a fresh bite that stands out against all the deep flavors of the meat-heavy menu.

One of the most interesting bits about Berenjak Chicago is that it has an expanded wine list, and it brings over a few of its classic cocktails that were previously exclusive to the UK locations. The big callout is the Olive Leaf Martini, which has a bit more of an oily texture, similar to that of a potato-vodka martini, that lets it go down incredibly smooth. The best on the menu, for my money, is the Grown Up Doogh, which adds gin to Iran’s favorite yogurt drink. As with the cucumber spread, it just adds a lovely tart hit that complements all the fatty meats and spices. Then there’s the Phel Phel Margarita, which is a nice homage to Soho House’s spicy margarita. The rich Fig Leaf Old Fashioned is sweet and gets a spot on the dessert menu next to black tea.

Since this location, like the Los Angeles version, is open to the public, it should immediately go onto any and all Windy City food and drink lists.

#2191. Westminster Abbey’s Roman sarcophagus mystery remains have been identified!

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

If you’re like me, you’ve been waiting with bated breath to find out just who is inside the Roman sarcophagus inscribed as carrying Valerius Amandinus that was unearthed at Westminster Abbey in 1869. And folks… we finally have an answer!

This late-Roman/fourth-century limestone sarcophagus was discovered on the grounds of Westminster Abbey, near the nave (the west wing of the Abbey that contains many graves) in 1820 but not dug up for forty-nine more years. (If you’re wondering why anyone was shocked to find a coffin in a churchyard, in the first place, I get that… though bodies buried at Westminster are not buried in the ground; furthermore, the area where the sarcophagus was found had been the yard of a sacristy during the reign of Henry III, who ruled from to 1207 to 1272, and not a traditional burial ground of any kind. So okay, I get why they may have been surprised.)

But it was dug up, and it was discovered to be late-Roman, thanks to the Latin words and the Roman symbols carved on the side. There was just one problem… the body inside it definitely wasn’t the original one that belonged in there. There was nothing Roman about the burial, at all, and no other Roman items were found nearby. (Modern scholars, researching the sarcophagus in 2018, were surprised by the body, as well, considering that the 19th century records said that the body was lost after it was discovered.)

Radiocarbon dating allowed researchers to determine that the corpse was from the late 10th century or early 11th. Scientists further determined that the body was male, and, when he died, stood at 5ft 9in tall and and was between 35 and 49 years old. Prof Warwick Rodwell, an archaeological consultant at Westminster, said there are two candidates for who the body could belong to: an abbot named Ælfwig, who died in 1020, or an abbot named Wulfnoth, who died in 1049. Rodwell told The Guardian that “the re-used sarcophagus was most likely interred in the eastern arm of the abbey church, with the lid as a floor slab, before being dug up and reburied during the construction work of Henry III.”

It’s thrilling to have this mystery come to a close.

From compression boots to pizza, an ultramarathoner breaks down what recovery really looked like after the Leadville 100.

Welcome to Trail Tested, a monthly column from entrepreneur and athlete Jack Espy as he trained for the grueling Leadville 100 ultramarathon. Trail Tested followed Jack along his journey to do his first 100-miler to see what his body is capable of and what his mind can endure. Throughout his training and post-race, he’ll be giving us a rundown of gear he’s been putting to the limit.

CONSIDERING I RAN a 100-mile ultramarathon, my body held up surprisingly well. I didn't get a single blister, which says a lot about the socks and trail shoes I was wearing and about staying on top of foot care throughout the race.

My biggest issue was my right knee. I started experiencing pain around Hope Pass, somewhere around mile 45. Luckily, I had packed a knee sleeve in my drop bag at the next major aid station around mile 50, and getting that on made a huge difference.

Outside of the knee pain, my body stayed tough through all 100 miles. I definitely hurt, but I never reached a point where I felt like I was completely breaking down. I’m gonna give a rundown of how I kept fresh on race day as well as how I’ve been recovering since I crossed that finish line of the Leadville 100 back in August.

I used a handful of different tools at aid stations throughout the race, but the ones I relied on most were a knee sleeve, a Hyperice massage gun, anti-chafe ointment and spray, and Biofreeze.

The leg sleeve became especially important because I had been dealing with some lingering pain in my right knee from a previous 50-miler. I used the Flytex leg compression sleeve. The compression helped the knee feel more secure and manageable as the miles added up.

Anti-chafe ointment was another must. I used Skin Slick and Body Glide. I stayed on top of lubricating my feet and toes throughout the race, and somehow made it through all 100 miles without a single blister—the Hoka Tecton X 4 had a big part to do with that as well.

I also used a Hyperice Hypervolt 2 massage gun at aid stations to loosen up my quads and calves. When you're running for 28-plus hours, anything that can help your legs feel a little fresher is huge.

Finally, Biofreeze became one of my go-to tools once my knee started bothering me around mile 42. I knew that if I wanted to make it another 58 miles, I needed to stay ahead of the discomfort rather than wait for it to become unbearable.

I was pretty banged up the day I finished, but actually not as destroyed as I expected to be. I could still walk, which definitely wasn't the case for everyone crossing the finish line.

My quads, hips, and feet were incredibly tight, but my feet probably hurt the most. Being on them for more than 28 hours takes a serious toll.

My parents and crew were amazing. They got me whatever I needed, kept me hydrated, and basically made sure I didn't have to do anything except recover.

The rest of the day was pretty simple: I stayed in bed with my Hyperice compression boots on, drank multiple Strives to replenish fluids and electrolytes, and eventually crushed an entire large pizza. After consuming gels, waffles, and race “food” for more than a day, getting a big meal with plenty of carbohydrates and calories back into my body felt incredible.

I also took magnesium before bed. Sleep can be surprisingly difficult after an ultra because your body is still so wired from the effort. Even though you're completely exhausted, it can take time for your system to settle back down. I experienced everything from chills to sweating after the race, so that first day was really about letting my body come back to normal.

It took me about four weeks before I truly felt fully recovered from Leadville.

My biggest priorities were sleep, nutrition, and giving my body time to recover. I aimed for at least eight hours of sleep every night and tried to sauna around five days a week because I found the heat relaxing and helpful for how my muscles felt.

I also used my Hyperice compression boots constantly. They became one of my favorite recovery tools throughout the entire training process, and I continued using them after the race to help my quads, hamstrings, and calves feel less stiff.

My feet needed a lot of attention too. I rolled them out every morning and again before bed. Nutrition was another major focus: lots of whole foods, enough calories, and plenty of protein to support recovery.

The biggest lesson was that just because your legs start feeling normal again doesn't necessarily mean you're fully recovered. My muscles bounced back relatively quickly, but my overall energy and sleep took much longer.

My coach told me to take two full weeks off from running. Naturally, I made it about six days.

I ran roughly six miles the Friday after Leadville, and shockingly, my legs felt great. Muscularly, I felt almost back to normal, which was wild considering I had just run 100 miles less than a week earlier.

But that was a little misleading. While my legs felt good, my sleep was still off and my overall energy levels were way down. Those didn't fully return to normal for about four weeks.

Then, three weeks after Leadville, I ran 30 miles around New York City for my 30th birthday. Viruses may be minuscule, but one could have influenced major differences between human and chimpanzee brains.

Millions of years ago, a virus left DNA behind in an African ape’s genome, and the genetic material stuck around in modern day chimps. Those ghosts of infections past appear to influence genes involved in neuron development, which may help explain how human and chimp brains ended up so different, researchers report October 23 in Science Advances.

Compared with the brains of their chimpanzee kin, human brains are three times larger and more cognitively powerful. Yet “most of their protein-making genes are very similar,” says Johan Jakobsson, a neuroscientist at Lund University in Sweden. In the absence of large differences in the genetic code, Jakobsson and his colleagues decided to look at the parts of the human and chimp genome that control when and where genes are turned on or off.

Changes in gene regulation can come from viruses. Some viruses called retroviruses insert their own DNA into their hosts’ genomes to command host cells to build more viruses. But those parasitic bits of DNA can linger long after an infection. If this happens in reproductive cells, the viral DNA gets passed down with the host’s DNA.

These leftover bits, called endogenous retroviruses, no longer code for the production of infectious viruses, but they can have a big impact on the host’s biology — especially if they make copies of themselves across the genome. And they have. Such viral remnants make up at least 8 percent of the genomes of humans and chimps alike. More generally, these DNA leftovers have been linked with some major evolutionary events, such as the evolution of the mammalian placenta.

Jakobsson and his colleagues focused on a particular viral vestige in chimps. The holdover came from an infection in an ancestral African ape somewhere between 5 million and 7 million years ago. In chimpanzees, the viral DNA had stuck itself into the genome at 158 different locations. However, it is curiously absent in humans. It’s still unknown why.

The researchers wanted to know how that exact pathogen footprint might affect chimpanzees’ brains compared with those of humans. Working with chimpanzee stem cells, they removed the viral DNA using CRISPR, a gene editing technology that allows for precise, targeted cuts in DNA. Once the viral DNA was gone, a nearby gene called LINC00662 — normally silenced in chimpanzees — turned on, making RNA the team could detect. This matches the normal condition in human brain cells, in which LINC00662 is active. The team also used CRISPR to shut off LINC00662 in the human cells, matching the silenced condition seen in chimpanzees.

When LINC00662 was active in chimp or human cells, the researchers noticed activity boosts to other genes that may be important to brain development — such as genes involved in the development and maturation of neurons and in making new neural connections. It’s not yet clear exactly how the viral DNA’s shutdown of this brain gene network affected the biology of chimpanzee brains. However, this genetic difference may have contributed to a divergence in how human and chimpanzee brains evolved, Jakobsson says. “Our findings show one way an ancient viral [DNA] insertion could create a lasting difference in how a gene is regulated in humans and chimpanzees,” Jakobsson says. Other genetic and environmental changes could be involved in the puzzle as well, he says.

Researchers have previously looked to single letter swaps in DNA to help explain how humans and chimp brains drifted apart in their evolution. But the new study highlights that creatures can rack up genome changes in other ways, says Giorgia Modenini, an evolutionary biologist at the University of Padua in Italy who was not involved with this research.

The researchers do a convincing job of showing that these particular viral leftovers influence developmental differences between human and chimp neurons, says Ravinder Kanda, an evolutionary geneticist at Oxford Brookes University in Oxford, England. “They’ve proved that absolutely. How far that goes, I don’t think we can quite say yet.”It could be that other controls on genes’ activity are also involved or that this particular ancient lever-puller may represent just a sliver of the viral holdovers that have shaped brain development, Kanda says. “I think there are many, many more retroviruses to be discovered, and at the moment we’ve only touched the tip of the iceberg.”

Busyness and productivity can look identical from the outside, but they’re not the same. You can stay busy all day tackling small, urgent-feeling tasks without ever touching the work that really counts. Being productive means doing the tasks that matter, which requires staying intentional about where your time and energy go.

So what's getting in the way of your productivity, and what can you do about it? Here’s a closer look at the everyday friction that can slow you down, plus a few shifts to help you make the most of each day.

Most productivity drains are minor, routine points of friction that are easy to miss, yet they add up fast. A few usual suspects:

Context switching: Research shows that every jump between tasks costs you the time it takes to mentally reset, even when it feels instant.

Constant interruptions: Responding to notifications and drop-in requests can feel productive, but it turns your attention to other people's priorities.

Unclear priorities: When everything feels urgent, you often default to whatever's quickest instead of what matters most.

Running on empty: Skipping breaks seems like it saves time, but it slows you down later and wears down your focus.

Procrastination: When a task feels overwhelming or unclear, it’s tempting to reach for something more manageable instead of starting the job you need to do.

Good news: you can reduce these points of friction without turning your whole day upside down. The tips below aren’t a set system, just a few ideas you can implement on your own terms. Start with one or two, and notice what feels different.
