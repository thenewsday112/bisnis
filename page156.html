#4074. Graphic Design: SIPF by Foreign Policy Design

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Explore the graphic design for the Singapore International Photography Festival by Foreign Policy Design, built around a shutter-split grid.

Photography doesn't happen in a vacuum. When Foreign Policy Design took on the 7th edition of the Singapore International Photography Festival (SIPF), they noticed a clear disconnect in past editions: the festival mark and the exhibited photographs barely spoke to each other. For the 2020/2021 edition, Festival Director Gwen Lee anchored the programming around the theme Departing & Arriving, reflecting on migration, belonging, and personal journeys across two centuries of Singaporean history. Drawing directly from editorial layouts where captions and images share the same narrative frame, the studio built a flexible system where the logotype physically wraps around every photograph.

The type moves like a camera shutter. Instead of locking the festival name into a static corner badge, Foreign Policy Design sliced the clean grotesque wordmark mid-word in Illustrator and InDesign so syllables step, stagger, and hug the edges of rectangular image blocks. It's a simple mechanical rule that never gets old. On the blush-sand cover of the printed Festival Guide, dark forest-green letters split around Michelle Chan's photograph Koufu, while the unfolded interior reveals crisp isometric floorplan maps of the 37 Emerald Hill venue alongside saffron-yellow and plum-purple print variations.

Color carries real photographic history here. The studio pulled its palette directly from the analogue film work of William Eggleston, Stephen Shore, and Saul Leiter â pairing warm ochre, terracotta, and mustard yellow against deep navy, cyan, and charcoal. You can see how well those tones hold up across the physical merchandise and passes. Rounded-corner access cards for the Discussions and Talks Pass, Emerald Hill Pass, and Ultimate Festival Pass nest color-coded ticket badges right inside the split typography, while lanyard inserts weave in line-drawn heritage rooflines. Even when pandemic delays pushed the schedule from August 2020 into January 2021, the system flexed cleanly from cobalt-printed orange tote bags, cotton t-shirts, and die-cut camera stickers to live-streamed talk graphics.

The chocolate ears are only part of the Halloween appeal.

Earlier this month, Starbucks announced its 2026 holiday menu, which officially launches November 5. But the coffee chain just gave us a very good reason not to rush through spooky season just yet: the Black Cat Frappuccino.

After teasing the drink in September, Starbucks is finally adding the limited-edition Frappuccino to menus just in time for Halloween.

Now, I’d consider myself something of a Starbucks expert—it is part of my job to taste everything on the menu, after all—but I’ve always been somewhat of a Frappuccino skeptic, usually gravitating toward cold brew, espresso drinks, and lately, matcha.

Still, a drink called the Black Cat Frappuccino was obviously going to get my attention. I wanted to know whether it lived up to the Halloween hype or was mostly tricks and no treats.

The Black Cat Frappuccino is a chocolate cake-flavored crème Frappuccino topped with whipped cream and chocolate cat ears. There’s no coffee in the base, so chocolate is very much the main event here. Intriguing, right?

Despite my skepticism, the Black Cat Frappuccino had me under its spell after the first sip. The inky-black color and chocolate ears perched atop a cloud of whipped cream certainly make it look the part of a Halloween drink, but thankfully, there’s more to it than presentation and vibes. It’s the flavor that really takes the cake.

Because it uses a crème base rather than coffee, there’s nothing competing with the chocolate and it’s able to take center stage. It’s rich and decadent without teetering into sickeningly sweet territory expected from a Halloween Frappuccino. The chocolate ears also make a nice little snack on top, à la olives in a dirty martini.

Honestly, I was pleasantly surprised by how much I enjoyed the Black Cat Frappuccino. I went in expecting a sugar rush you get from too much Halloween candy, but this reminded me more of chocolate mousse or flourless cake, just sippable and dressed up for the season.

#582. AI could turn scientific papers into research assistants

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Scientific papers are typically static records of knowledge. Paper2Agent turns them into tool sets for AI agents. People can then use their favorite AI platform to interact with these papers.

This is a human-written story voiced by AI. Got feedback? Take our survey . (See our AI policy here .)

Scientific papers may be getting a 21st century update.

A system called Paper2Agent lets scientists do more than read a new scientific study. When a paper comes with code, methods and data, the system packages these into a tool set that an AI assistant can use to work with new data, researchers report September 16 in Nature.

The system is essentially “a virtual author of the paper,” available around the clock, says James Zou, a computer scientist at Stanford University.

In tests, Zou and colleagues tried to convert 100 computational biology papers into working tool sets and succeeded with 74. The others had obstacles such as missing code or data, or software that wouldn’t run. With the papers that were converted, the Paper2Agent tool set could run analyses of the published work and answer questions about the results. It did so more accurately and also more than twice as fast as the same agent working from the original papers and accompanying code.

Making another scientist’s code usable can take substantial work, says Peter Clark, a senior research director at the Allen Institute for Artificial Intelligence in Seattle who was not involved in developing Paper2Agent. Researchers already share code on platforms such as GitHub. But Clark says he often has to “grub around” for half a day to figure out how to get it running.

A general-purpose AI assistant such as Claude can help, he says, but it typically generates new code on the fly. Paper2Agent instead packages code associated with a paper into reusable tools, tests them against reference outputs and writes step-by-step instructions guiding an AI assistant on which tools to use and in what order. This streamlines and standardizes the process of using AI to interact with scientific research, Clark says. It’s “an exciting step” toward papers that actively help scientists.

To Zou, transforming scientific papers into agents seem like a natural next step for transmitting knowledge. We’ve already moved from stone tablets and papyrus to paper and then to digital formats, he says, and now it’s time to move from passive to active formats for human knowledge. A paper-agent can help one human scientist quickly use another’s code and methods. The tool can also search for paper-agents about similar topics with the aim of alerting their human authors about potential collaborations.

Zou says his team has already created “over 100 different paper-agents” from their own work and that these have already helped him find other scientists to collaborate with.

Clark notes that Paper2Agent specializes in helping scientists reuse methods and code, and not in answering general questions, like a true virtual author should be able to do. Also, he worries about what could happen if millions of low-quality papers get turned into agents capable of pestering scientists. That could make things “worse rather than better,” he says, recalling the days of Clippy, the much-maligned Microsoft Office assistant. But he’d love for a “non-annoying agent” to pop up and tell him about work that he should be paying attention to.

For more open-ended questions about a paper, Clark says he would still want to talk to the people who did the research. “The reason I would write to a corresponding author is for precisely the things they didn’t put in the paper.”

#3925. Art Basel Paris dispatch: what to see beyond the fair this week

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The twelfth edition of Paris Internationale, which champions emerging galleries, opened earlier this week in the Canal Saint-Martin district, a distance from the city centre. The popular fair, held in a different venue annually, is located on Quai de Valmy, hosting 56 galleries and five non-profit spaces until 24 October. The fair, say the organisers, has a “human scale format that encourages direct encounters between artists, galleries and audiences”.

This collegiate ethos is clearly in evidence, says George Vamvakidis of The Breeder in Athens which is selling works by the Greek artist Kostis Velonis and Istanbul-born Eleni Pitari-Pangalou: “It feels manageable cost-wise, there is less pressure and it makes a nice change after Frieze.” At Glaswegian gallery Kendall Koppe's stand, works by the Scottish artist Keith Farquhar depicting a fleece and a humongous nose, are a hit, while Lucy Beech’s video, photography and sculpture works with Champ Lacombe gallery are another highlight (her stirring Storm film documents a five-hour storm stimulation modelled on a worst-case North Sea tempest).

The events programme is varied and intriguing, featuring significant speakers. Try and catch for instance “Building a Code of Conduct for an Art Organisation: Discomfort and Transformation” on 23 October (panellists include the ethics practitioner Piergiorgio Pepe and Grégory Castera, head of commissioning at Kanal-Centre Pompidou in Brussels).

Other satellite fairs include Post-Fair Paris, which makes its debut in the Marais district, bringing together 35 galleries, project spaces and independent exhibitors from 15 countries. Founded in Los Angeles by gallerist Chris Sharp in 2025, Post-Fair was conceived as "a carefully-crafted, boutique, and invitation-only alternative to the conventional art-fair model", according to a statement. Visitors can expect a more intimate and fluid experience, the perfect complement to the booths of Art Basel.

Tracey Emin's bronze sculpture with a view of the Eiffel Tower

Tracey Emin, Parvis des droits de l’homme, Cité de l’architecture et du patrimoine

The Miami collector Jorge Pérez and his wife Darlene were among the throngs of visitors to the Place du Trocadéro on Tuesday evening, many there for the Eiffel Tower’s light show, but a few pausing over three monumental bronzes by Tracey Emin installed in the shadow of the tower. Part of Art Basel Paris’s public programme, I Lay Here for You (2018), I Followed You to the End (2024) and I Will Not Be Alone (2025) show a female figure in a range of poses, moving from withdrawal to submission and, ultimately, to defiance.

The sculptures, presented by White Cube, also bring an intimate reckoning with loss and survival into a very public setting. Writing on Instagram on 19 October, just after the bronzes were installed, Emin notes how she came to Paris straight after her mother died, taking the first train from London to avoid spending the day crying in Spitalfields. Alone in the city, she found comfort in its museums, visiting Rodin’s studio and the Louvre. “Now I’m here in Paris, missing my Mum, missing my brother Alan, thinking about life and death, time and events, what is real and what doesn’t feel real,” Emin writes. “I’m very grateful to Paris. Paris is a city that stops me from crying and makes me know who I am.”

Kuba by Kutluğ Ataman, installed in Paris

Kuba by Kutluğ Ataman, Chapelle des Petits-Augustins

Another talking point public art piece is Kutluğ Ataman’s video installation Kuba (2005) presented in the Chapelle des Petits-Augustins at the Beaux-Arts de Paris as part of Art Basel Paris’s public programme and presented by Niru Ratnam gallery. The work, which shines a light on a tightly bounded settlement on the outskirts of Istanbul,made waves when the Artangel commissioning organisation first premiered it in London in 2005.

Telegraph critic Alastair Sooke wrote at the time: “Forty battered televisions, bristling with analogue knobs, have been placed before 40 shabby armchairs. On each screen, a resident of Küba, one of Istanbul's most downbeat districts, with a heavy Kurdish population talks fluently about everything from scavenging for bread in the garbage to the impossibility of stopping smoking and the terror with which local psychopaths rule the roost.”

The piece certainly feels prescient twenty years later. Questions of migration, urban transformation, displacement and belonging have become ever more pressing, says a gallery statement, while “debates surrounding who has the authority to represent communities remain central to contemporary culture”.

© Musée du Louvre 2026. Photo: Audrey Viger

Les Hôtes du Louvre: Kader Attia and Elizabeth Peyton, Musée du Louvre

The artists Kader Attia and Elizabeth Peyton set up their own studios at the heart of the Musée du Louvre to mark the museum’s 230th anniversary in 2023, working on site from December that year to June 2025. Their residencies are part of a contemporary art initiative at the museum entitled “Les Hôtes du Louvre or "Hosts at the Louvre" (“hôtes” can also mean guests in French).

The works produced at the Louvre are displayed in a joint exhibition in the museum’s Chapel Room (until 14 December). Peyton immersed herself in the Louvre collection, working alongside young artists from Paris’s Ecole des Beaux-Arts, copying and reproducing notable works dotted around the museum. Her 2005 portrait of Napoleon Bonaparte hangs alongside a later depiction of film star Jennifer Lawrence (2025).

Attia meanwhile brings to the fore the crimes of Marshal Bugeaud whose statue stands on the Louvre façade. In a stirring video, he highlights how, in the artist's words, Bugeaud is ”lauded as a hero in France, yet embodies the monstrosity of colonialism in Algeria” (the 19th-century military commander was responsible for the enfumade attacks in Algeria where resistance fighters and civilians alike were asphyxiated, says Attia). Attia also shows a series of “witches’ mirrors” scarred with gashes and pockmarks that disorient the visitor's image and perspective.

View from Ibrahim Mahama's The Harvest season at Fondation Cartier pour l'art contemporain

© Ibrahim Mahama; Photo © Benoît Fougeirol

Ibrahim Mahama, The Harvest Season, Fondation Cartier pour l'art contemporain

The Ghanaian artist Ibrahim Mahama continues his art world ascent after being ranked at the top of ArtReview's “Power 100” last year. The Fondation Cartier, one of France’s most prominent private art foundations, showcases not just Mahama’s recent works and installation but also pieces from nine artists and collectives, all of whom have long-standing ties to him. Highlights include paintings by Gideon Appah such as Cecilia (Triptych) (2020-21) and screenprints by Zohra Opoku.

Visitors entering the gallery first encounter Mahama’s startling, vast parliamentary chamber known as Parliament of Ghosts which acts as a metaphor for failed democracies, linking to the miliary coup in 1966 in Ghana and the dissolution of its government.

Another show-stopping installation is Things Fall Apart (2026). Comprising masses of glass bottles and crates, it fills the exhibition hall; the materials were all extracted by Mahama from an abandoned glass bottle factory located in Aboso, southwestern Ghana, and taken to his Red Clay art complex in Tamale. “By reactivating the factory in the form of an artwork that makes use of the original materials, the artist succeeds in placing Ghana’s industrial heritage within a narrative focused on the future,” reads a wall text.

Private museums take centre stage in Paris with another key establishment joining the pack this week. The French capital's newest contemporary art centre opened last Saturday (17 October) on the Ile Seguin, in Greater Paris's western reaches. Dubbed simply Large (or “le Large”), it is the latest philanthropic venture from the Emerige real estate group and takes centre-stage in the wider Pointe des Arts development.

The inaugural exhibition echoes the island’s automotive history as the site of car-maker Renault's primary manufacturing plant for the better part of the 20th century. Entitled Imaginary Engine and curated by Cecilia Alemani, the show places more than 50 works from Renault's own collection, the Fonds Renault pour l’Art et la Culture, in dialogue with 17 new commissions from contemporary artists.

The Renault production plant became a de facto exhibition space, which displayed works including a chromatic mural by the late Argentine-born artist Julio Le Parc in its café (Peintures en frise, Frieze Paintings, 1971–74). The Op-Art pioneer’s vibrant 45-panel rainbow artwork, made with automotive paint, was physically integrated into the architecture of the basement staff cafeteria and now neatly takes pride of place in the inaugural show.

Other works featured look to the technology and aesthetics of automobile construction in innovative ways. The US artist and choreographer Madeline Hollander tracks the Earth’s rotation through a series of flickering car headlights (Sunrise/Sunset, 2021) arranged in columns, forming a “ballet of light”, says the exhibition catalogue. A series of poignant photographs by Shirin Aliabadi dating from 2005 (Girls in Car) show Iranian women posing in cars, resisting societal restrictions.

The old way of thinking is that an elk hunter should carry the biggest, hardest-hitting rifle he can handle, because even a well-hit bull can travel into tough terrain. #3407. The Swedish Sport of Swimrun Is Spreading Across the U.S. I Tried It and Chaos Ensued.

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Outside features editor Frederick Dreier participated in the October 4 Ötillö Swimrun race at Horsetooth Reservoir, Colorado. Dreier has competed in triathlons, bicycle races, and other endurance sports for 25 years.

A few memories come to mind from my recent Ötillö Swimrun, the endurance race that combines open-water swimming with trail running.

In one, I flounder in the chilly waters of Colorado’s Horsetooth Reservoir near Fort Collins, my arms feeling heavy as concrete as I struggle to swim in a straight line. In another, I sit by the side of a rocky trail plucking cactus spines out of my backside, moments after a clumsy toe-strike sent me tumbling into a clump of prickly pear.

Both of these scenes, however, are superseded by a memory with an altogether different vibe. That, of course, is crossing the finish line and exchanging a flurry of high-fives and hugs with a bunch of random sweaty strangers, all of us clad in wetsuits, goggles, and running shoes.

You see, I had a blast racing my first Ötillö Swimrun race despite the dead-tired arms and cactus butt.

If you have never heard of the swimrun racing format, here’s a quick primer. Picture a triathlon, minus the biking. Now, chop up the swimming and running portions, stitch them together in smaller portions, and spread the whole event out on a point-to-point course. You run, then swim, then run again, then swim, and so on. The event I did was a “sprint” distance event, comprised of five swims and six runs spread out across 12 miles of trails and lake.

It took me nearly three hours to complete, and at no point was I sprinting.

Unlike triathlon, where you abandon your gear after each portion, swimrun requires you to carry all of your stuff from start to finish. Thus, you must swim while wearing your running shoes, run while clad in your wetsuit, and carry your pull buoy, hand paddles — swimming gear that is allowed — for the entire journey. This makes for some hilarious blunders along the way (more on this later).

Within the swimrun world, Ötillö is the proverbial Ironman — the most recognizable brand of events. Outside has a somewhat rich history of chronicling the Swedish company, first in 2013, then in 2014, and also in a 2017 Brad Stulberg column. In 2019, longtime contributor W. Hodding Carter wrote an Outside feature story about his foibles at the Ötillö world series race in Cannes, France, where he and his partner paddled across the green-blue Mediterranean and jogged through the French Riviera.

In all of those stories, Ötillö swimrun races come across as grueling, fun, and distinctly European. The racing format, after all, was developed in Sweden and first held on the chain of islands just east of Stockholm, which is still the site of the annual Ötillö world championships. The name “Ötillö” is a Swedish word that loosely translates to “island to island.”

Good news for Americans: Ötillö is no longer just for the Euros. In 2023, the series expanded to North America after it purchased a budding collection of U.S. swimrun races called the Odyssey series, that were co-founded by a guy named Lars Finanger. Finanger and I were roommates in Boulder 20 years ago, back when we were both dirtbag triathletes chasing a daily endorphin high.

Now, Finanger is trying to spread the Swedish swimrun gospel in the U.S., where jogging trails while wearing a wetsuit is about as popular as pickled herring.

“We started out with a lot of triathletes, Ironman finishers, Eco-Challenge racers — it was a really eclectic group of endurance athletes,” Finanger told me. “Now we have a small but dedicated following of athletes who see themselves as Swimrunners first, even though they might do a triathlon or a bike race, too.”

Finanger first learned about Ötillö from the 2013 Outside story. He was burned out on triathlon and road bike racing and was looking for a new challenge, so he signed up for the world championship event in Stockholm, which is comprised of 38 miles of running and 5.7 miles of swimming, and requires participants to compete in teams of two. It’s a grueling test of endurance and grit that takes many teams upwards of 10 hours to complete.

“I had this big grin on my face throughout the whole thing, even though the water was so frickin’ cold and the trails made you feel like you were running through the hobbit scene from Lord of the Rings,” Finanger told me. “I realized we didn’t have anything like it in the USA.”

Finanger wanted to start his own race but didn’t know how. As luck would have it, he wasn’t the only person intrigued by the format. Jeffrey Cole, a veteran of the endurance sports industry and a longtime triathlon race director, emailed Finanger about the swimrun format. Cole said that Casco Bay, Maine, near his home in Kennebunkport, was a perfect place to recreate the island hopping of Sweden’s Ötillö. In 2016, they started the inaugural Odyssey event along the Maine coast.

“I think about the history of triathlon, where athletes raced in Kona and then went back home to put on their own races where they lived,” Finanger said. “We wanted to produce a swimrun that we’d have wanted to do ourselves.”

Their model was a success. Finanger and Cole started other swimrun races in the U.S.: Orcas Island, Washington; Cedarville, Michigan; Austin, Texas; and even one on California’s Catalina Island. These events are held annually, and the participation numbers now surpass 400 at some races.

Cole died in 2018, and Finanger kept the business going alongside a handful of new partners. In 2023, Ötillö approached them about purchasing the Odyssey series and bringing it under the corporate umbrella. Finanger said it was a no-brainer.

“It meant partnering with the gold standard in our sport,” he said.

These days, the former Odyssey races award points to elite athletes that help them qualify for the world championships race in Stockholm. But they also boast shorter distances, solo categories, and a laid-back vibe. Because the purpose of the events, Finanger told me, is to attract newbies like yours truly to the format.

“We are still this meeting ground for all of these different types of athletes — swimmers, cyclists, triathletes,” he said. “The one they have in common is they are adventure-minded.”

Qualifying for a race in Europe was not on my mind as I prepared for the 2026 Colorado Ötillö. The shorter sprint distance did not offer qualification points toward the Ötillö World Championship; however, the much longer World Series race in Colorado (23 total miles) did.

When I asked Finanger for strategic advice, he just laughed me off. “Just have fun out there, dude,” he said. He reminded me that the whole point was to have a personal adventure while swimming and jogging my way around the lake.

The laid-back atmosphere at the start line reinforced Finanger’s advice. Over the years, I’ve nervously waited for the start of Ironman triathlons, marathons, and almost every type of bike race out there. At Ötillö, the vibe was extremely chill — like what you’d expect at the start of a 5K color run or a boozy bicycle parade and not a three- to five-hour test of stamina.

Still, I had to remind myself to have fun more than a few times during the early moments of the race. Following a fast one-mile run to start the race, we plunged into Horsetooth Reservoir for the first swim.

I’m a former NCAA Division III swimmer and multiple Ironman finisher, and I’m accustomed to swimming in choppy water filled with thrashing arms and legs. But I struggled to find my groove with the swimrun gear, and I had to stop a few times to position my pull buoy between my legs and learn to swim while wearing sneakers. An even bigger challenge hit when I exited the water.

I stepped onto the clay beach and immediately stumbled around like a newborn fawn. I felt like I’d just gulped a tequila shot or three. I lumbered toward the trail and zigzagged my way up a hill like a punch-drunk boxer.

Upon exiting the water, seasoned swimrunners fastened their pull buoys to their waists with a cord, tucking the apparatus to their lumbar sections like a fanny pack. I fumbled to secure mine as I trotted along, the buoy bouncing off of my butt. As I twisted and twirled the buoy, one of my plastic swim paddles slipped from my hand, and I heard it crash into a bush. Should I stop to look or keep going? I wondered. I chose to stop.

The short pause allowed my thumping heart rate to chill. Soon, my brain fog cleared, and I was able to focus. I found the paddle, and as I jogged along the trail, I familiarized myself with the goofy gear. I practiced tucking and untucking my pull buoy into its tether. I threaded the fingers of my right hand through the elastic loops on my hand paddles to keep them secure.

During a triathlon, you’re constantly using products that are over-engineered for the specific modality of the moment: aerodynamic bike stuff, cushioned shoes, high-visibility swim goggles. Part of the fun quirk of a swimrun is learning to manage the wrong gear when it’s not used for the right job.

My confidence increased, and I picked up the pace, jogging past swimrunners and moving to the side as others jogged past me. People seemed stoked to be on the trail. Nobody seemed so caught up in their own struggles as to skip the customary “Lookin’ good!” or “Go get ’em!” during a pass.

Perhaps it was my soaring body temperature inside my wetsuit, or my lingering buzz. But as I closed in on the guy ahead of me, my mind wandered, and I tripped on a rock, completed a somersault, and landed in the cactus. Bewildered, I made a mental note about swimrun: shit can hit the fan if you don’t pay attention.

The fall meant that I had to spend a few minutes plucking spines from my backside once I crossed the line. But the ordeal delivered one of my lasting takeaways from the race.

Perfection does not exist during a swimrun. Even during a shorter distance race, you’re bound to inhale water, drop your gear, battle through exhaustion, or trip and stumble. How you deal with these setbacks defines your experience out there.

I’ve spent the last 25 years trying out different endurance sports. And I’m familiar with the progression that most athletes follow with these sports. You try out a sport and fall in love with the adventure, camaraderie, and community. Then, as the years go by, you focus more on your results, and before you know it, you’re completing a training regimen, following a performance-oriented diet, and dreaming of a top-10 finish.

I will absolutely compete in another Ötillö Swimrun event, but I don’t envision myself ever getting too hardcore about the format. Simply doing a swimrun gave me enough feeling of adventure and carefree silliness, without an ounce of self-seriousness. Sucking at the format provided half of the joy. And I’m more than happy to chase that feeling again.

Diagnosis comes from your history plus a dermatologist’s evaluation of the pattern and distribution of the redness.

You don't need dramatic symptoms to be diagnosed with rosacea either—Dr. Nazarian says many people show up with early forms, like chronic flushing, small acne-like bumps or a dry-eye sensation. She doesn't consider any type of rosacea "mild," because it often progresses if untreated or if the person isn't educated on triggers.

That’s why Dr. Nazarian is bullish on controlling skin inflammation even in the earlier stages to keep skin healthy and prevent progression. “For many women under 40, rosacea may mimic acne and lead to incorrect diagnosis or delay in appropriate treatments," she adds, and on darker skin tones, "melanin can mask the blood vessels changes, appearing more purple or dark brown." The clues that still point to rosacea? "The stinging and burning sensation, and different inflammatory lesions."

IPL is one of the tools dermatologists use for exactly this: redness from vessels that no longer constrict. "IPL is a light treatment that uses a broad-spectrum of light to minimize blood vessels and can improve background redness," Dr. Nazarian says. "It works by emitting a spectrum of light that's absorbed by the blood vessels, causing them to collapse down and disappear." A 2021 review in the journal Dermatology and Therapy explains that the blood in your vessels absorbs the light and converts it to heat, which damages the vessel walls so they close off. The same review found IPL and pulsed dye laser performed comparably.

IPL has also been used for years to treat rosacea. In a 2020 trial in Experimental and Therapeutic Medicine, people with late-stage rosacea and visible vessels first got anti-mite treatment (medication that targets Demodex, tiny mites that naturally live on skin and can trigger rosacea). They were then randomly assigned to three IPL sessions four weeks apart, or no further treatment. The IPL group had a higher total efficacy rate (95.33% vs. 30.83%) and lower recurrence over a two-year follow-up (8.41% vs. 48.33%). And a 2025 study in the American Journal of Translational Research followed 112 people with Fitzpatrick skin types II to IV and redness-dominant rosacea. After three sessions four weeks apart, narrow-band IPL reduced redness more than pulsed dye laser or broad-band IPL. It also caused the fewest pigment changes, about 3%.

One of the best parts of the treatment is that IPL fits real life. It's non-invasive and can improve the appearance of skin in a few weeks, Dr. Nazarian says. And because it requires little downtime, people "can continue to go to work, socialize, and generally continue most daily activities without feeling self-conscious," she says.

I tried it, and the best description I have is that it feels like someone is snapping a rubber band against your skin… over and over and over again. Dr. Nazarian treated my entire face, then went back over my cheeks and T-zone, where my redness is most concentrated. On occasion, she made a second pass. There's no numbing cream and no cooling step. The snap feels the same everywhere, but it's way more intense on my cheeks, especially near my eyes. The whole thing takes about 10 minutes.

The aftermath is far gentler. My skin was red right after, but it settles within an hour or two. Unlike some lasers, IPL didn’t leave me in pain once it's over. Your timeline may differ, though. Treated skin can stay red and sensitive for a few hours, like a sunburn, with some sensitivity lasting a couple of days.

The one rule Dr. Nazarian gave me: stay out of the sun before and after. Providers typically advise no tanning for about four weeks beforehand, and a broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher with a physical blocker like zinc afterward, reapplied every two hours.

Plan on more than a few sessions, and expect subtle progress. "Although several treatments are typically required to appreciate improvement and minimize blood vessels," Dr. Nazarian says, "expectations should be to experience slow and subtle improvement in skin tone with each session, and to use it concurrently with medical therapy." Treatments can continue often on a monthly basis—basically, until you're satisfied.

The National Rosacea Society describes a similar rhythm: typically three or more treatments at four- to six-week intervals. That's roughly my cadence. I've done three sessions, spaced four to six weeks apart, although my most recent one came after a two-month gap.

As for results, they show up a few weeks after a session, not the day of. Three sessions in, my skin looks creamier and more even, and closer to the color of my neck. I sometimes head to work without makeup now. But I wouldn't call my redness solved. It's a process that takes maintenance and more than a few sessions, so be ready to invest.

Dr. Nazarian says that's by design. Results aren't considered permanent "because patients cannot remain perfect in life. We expect them to slowly accumulate more damage and blood vessel inflammation... mostly, again, because they're dealing with a chronic condition." Her fix is to "minimize flares in order to preserve benefit."

There's no finish line. "This is a chronic condition," Dr. Nazarian says. "There is no 'one-and-done' any more than there is for someone with eczema, asthma or any other chronic condition."

Unlike me, she doesn't see that as grim. "With the right education, many patients live happily without having to actively think about their condition," she says. The catch is that it takes consistent care. "Although not all patients progress, from my own professional experience, I can attest that a vast majority of patients will continue to evolve and build continuous damage to skin if not treated," says Dr. Nazarian.

Which brings me to the unglamorous part: the in-between. Between sessions, my job is to keep up with my topical, a clindamycin lotion or. Dr. Nazarian stressed that staying consistent with topicals helps reduce inflammation and the acne that can further damage skin. IPL clears the backlog, and the lotion helps keep new damage from piling up.

Drug overdose deaths unexpectedly took a nosedive in the United States a few years ago. But it doesn’t appear to be due to more people being revived than before.

At the peak in mid-2023, there were around 111,000 drug overdose deaths per year in the United States. By the end of 2025, the number had fallen to under 71,000 per year, and researchers are still piecing together why. One theory: The wider availability of naloxone, a medication that reverses opioid overdoses, made it possible to revive more people than before.

But a study of U.S. emergency medical services agencies found that emergency medical responses for nonfatal overdoses dropped along with deaths, and the use of naloxone also declined. That suggests that fewer overdoses overall occurred, not that naloxone stopped deaths more often, researchers report October 21 in the Journal of the American Medical Association.

“Naloxone still saves lives every day,” says Michael Barnett, a health services researcher at Brown University School of Public Health in Providence, R.I. “But it doesn’t look like the reason for the sudden turn” in overdose deaths.

State policies, community distribution programs and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s approval of the nasal spray version of naloxone as an over-the counter drug have expanded access.

Barnett and colleagues analyzed nearly 186 million emergency medical service activations — an ambulance or other EMS response after a 911 call — nationwide that occurred from May 2020 through December 2025. Of 2.5 million responses for nonfatal drug overdoses, 40.5 percent involved opioids.

From May of 2023 onward, EMS activations for nonfatal opioid overdoses fell on average by about 6,000, or around 33 percent, below what was expected. Total administrations of naloxone dropped as well, by around 4,700 on average, or 21 percent below what was expected. The study suggests that naloxone was not used more often than before by bystanders prior to the EMS response, but did not include data on cases when a bystander provided naloxone and no one called 911.

Additional possibilities to explain the steep drop in overdose deaths — which followed a three-decade increase — include changes to the illicit opioid supply or with the population of people at risk for overdoses. “My own read is that a disruption in the fentanyl supply best explains how sudden and widespread the turn was,” Barnett says. “Underneath it, the population at risk has probably been shrinking for years.”

But “a supply-driven decline can reverse,” Barnett says, noting that the decline in overdose deaths slowed in 2025. Traffickers can adapt “by finding new chemical sources or turning to more potent synthetic opioids.”
