#2799. Whatever You Think of Him, Mark Dowdle Is Built for the Backyard Ultra

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

At lap 87 of the Last Soul Backyard Ultra in Bornheim, Germany, this August, Mark Dowdle transformed (or regressed, he’s not entirely sure) into the football and lacrosse player he was in college.

To be fair, Ayyoub “Ello” El Osrouti pretty much started it. A backyard ultra, as you probably know by now, is a last-person-standing race not much different from “The Long Walk”—the movie about 100 boys who walk until there’s one left—except the runners in backyard races aren’t shot after they drop.

Now at 4 a.m. on August 18—three days and 15 hours into the race—it was just down to El Osrouti and Dowdle, who were engaged in a Hamilton-like duel to determine the winner. Ello busted out of the gate. The German had been trying to run faster laps to break Dowdle, but this was, by comparison, a sprint. Dowdle, deviating from his tactic of running his own race, decided to stay right with him.

Dowdle completed the lap in 38 minutes—his fastest of the race by over 5 minutes. El Osrouti got up and finished four minutes later, also his fastest lap.

But about half way through the lap, El Osrouti stopped and sat down. Dowdle stopped, too, and just stared at him in silence. It was both competitive and a little chilling, like Dowdle thought the Last Soul race was a literal invitation to collect his competitors’ souls along the way.

Dowdle’s behavior left many of his hundreds of thousands of followers on Instagram, Youtube, and TikTok a little stunned. The 29-year-old Minnesotan has become a wildly popular Internet figure through dishing out zen advice and positivity. Rich Roll, the addict turned endurance athlete who serves up mindfulness every week on his podcast, called Dowdle a “changemaker” when he had Dowdle on the show. There was a good reason for it: Basically every time Dowdle took a breath, he said something enlightening or inspiring about hard work, endurance, or self-improvement.

With two backyard ultra wins in 2026 alone, there’s little doubt that Dowdle is one of this year’s ultrarunning breakout stars, partly because of his success in the backyard format and partly because of his social media savvy. But he didn’t get that way by reading scripted platitudes on TikTok; Dowdle isn’t faking that zen.

When I told him about the Taco Bell 50K and how it attracted those who wouldn’t normally even consider running an ultra, he said, “people want to be challenged. Sometimes they just don’t know it yet.” Even when he was a football and lacrosse player at the University of St. Thomas in Saint Paul, Minnesota, drinking way too much and trying to please others at the expense of himself, he would think about the meaning of life and why people do the things they do. He attributes his personal growth to running, but also to journaling.

“These are the kinds of things I think about all the time,” Dowdle said. “I was constantly thinking about this connection between words and action and who I wanted to be.”

Dowdle works as a motivational speaker now. But deep down, he’s got an edge.

It’s that edge that makes him stand out (and ruffle feathers) in a sport known for its kumbaya positivity, especially the camaraderie-focused backyard ultra community. Dowdle likes being a little weird and competitive and even scary: He once made a YouTube video called “Ultrarunning Has A Mark Dowdle Problem.” He compares himself to Dennis Rodman, the wildly divisive and brilliant NBA player.

“I’m sure that some of these other guys who are competitive will want to put that competition right back on me,” Dowdle said. “I welcome that.”

His friend and rival Harvey Lewis seems like a golden retriever to Dowdle’s heeler. Dowdle sort of agrees with that comparison and sort of doesn’t. He points out that he and Lewis aren’t that different. (Lewis agrees with him, for what it’s worth.)

“I think Harvey Lewis is competitive in a way that’s much tricker to identify,” Dowdle said. “But I don’t hide it. There are times I don’t care. I’ll show everything on my sleeve.”

Lewis believes that Dowdle’s zen, more than his competitiveness, is what’s carried him to so much recent success. On October 17, 2026, they came together as teammates: Lewis, 50, and Dowdle lined up as part of a U.S. team of 15 against the world’s best at the 2026 Backyard Ultra World Team Championship. Team USA competed on the Big’s Backyard Ultra course in Beltbuckle, Tennessee, the original backyard ultra devised by Gary “Lazarus Lake” Cantrell. Dowdle went on to run 107 laps, or 446 miles—a personal best—to lead Team USA to victory over Australia. Lewis ran 83 laps.

The Backyard Ultra World Team Championship is held every two years, alternating with the individual world championship. The 2026 race began at 6 a.m. Mountain Time on Saturday, October 17, with 69 national teams competing simultaneously around the world.

The format is simple: Each team of 15 runners runs a 4.167-mile loop every hour, on the hour. The country with cumulatively the most loops completed across all 15 runners wins the team title. Team USA races on the original backyard ultra course in Gary “Lazarus Lake” Cantrell’s backyard in Bell Buckle, Tennessee.

Mark Dowdle led Team USA to the win with a cumulative 1,120 laps (yards) to defeat Australia by 51 yards. He was the last American standing with 107 laps, or 446 miles—a personal best. Belgium’s Ivo Steyaert and Merijn Geerts broke the individual backyard ultra world record by each running 124 yards (512 miles) before deciding to call it quits due to boredom, on-the-ground photographer Jacob Zocherman reports.

Since its inauguration in 2020, Belgium and the U.S. have each won two team titles.

Lewis said Dowdle’s “why” is perhaps the strongest he’s ever seen. It “cements his discipline and his unwavering desire to not give up.”

“Mark has a great energy force,” Lewis said. “He has a level of enthusiasm that’s immeasurable.”

But what, exactly, is an energy force? Is it zen or competitiveness? Dowdle likes to ask it another way: Why can’t it be both?

Take a wild guess who inspired Dowdle as a collegiate football and lacrosse player to turn his life around and start running. It wasn’t Joe Rogan, but if that was your first guess, you’re on the right track.

Feeling sorry for himself on the bus ride home after losing two of three lacrosse games his senior year, he listened to David Goggins on Joe Rogan’s podcast. Goggins talked about how running carved him into the person he was today. The way he bragged about pissing blood, shitting himself, and eating only saltine crackers during his long runs spoke to Dowdle. Dowdle already knew he needed to change.

He went for a run when they got back to campus—even though it was 2 a.m.

If Goggins lit the fire, James Lawrence, the Iron Cowboy, stoked it. Lawrence had completed the 50/50/50 challenge, or 50 Ironman-length triathlons in 50 days across 50 states. The way he leaned on his family during that journey spoke to Dowdle. Dowdle, to this day, gives all the credit to his wife, Lucy, for centering him and scolding him whenever he acts, for lack of a better description, too much like a bro.

Dowdle tried for many years to make it as a running guru—some might say influencer—and ultrarunner. He found some limited success. Runner’s World interviewed him in 2023 for his calendar challenge, when he ran the number of miles corresponding to the day of the month, for a full month. (For example, 26 miles on the 26th.) By the beginning of 2026, he had 40,000 Instagram followers and a modest apparel sponsorship that netted him $200 a month. And then he entered the BPN Go One More backyard ultra this April.

Dowdle had already won the Arrowhead 135-miler in January, finishing in 35:48:00—a four-hour improvement over his 2025 fourth-place finish. But he chalked that up to his ability to withstand the Minnesota winter conditions that dropped to minus 40 degrees Fahrenheit.

The BPN Go One More was another story; he hadn’t even made it to 100 miles in a backyard ultra before. He could sense other peoples’ doubts—they didn’t drive him, but they did irritate him a little. He mumbled at one point that they “had no fucking idea.”

Dowdle ended up running 306 miles in 73 hours of rainy and muddy conditions, and the way he did it captivated people: He looked fresh and happy most of the time, like he was playing tag on the playground. In a few days, his Instagram following jumped to 108,000, and brand deals were knocking at his door. He also began booking keynote speaking events, sometimes for conferences that he helped set up the year before for one of his odd jobs to get by while he pursued running.

He proved that BPN was no fluke two months later, finishing second to Lewis at the Mid-State Mile, a last-person standing event in Franklin, Tennessee.

But Last Soul sent him into a new stratosphere. After their fateful showdown on lap 87, Dowdle and El Osrouti dueled for another six hours. And despite the German banking a cumulative five hours of extra rest over the course of the race, after that 93rd lap he gave out. Dowdle ran the final lap solo to win the race with 94 loops, or 391.7 miles. The race attracted 10 million viewers to the livestream and more than 100,000 at any one time, attention that Lewis called “freakishly different” than any other ultra in history, even Western States or Rachel Entrekin’s historic overall win at the Cocodona 250.

After Last Soul, Dowdle’s Instagram following jumped from 108,000 to over 400,000.

“It’s been everything I’ve been trying to build for several years now,” Dowdle said. “But nothing really has changed. It’s sort of weird because I’ve been saying the same thing now that I have over the past seven years. But people are willing to listen more when I go 94 hours in a backyard.”

Dowdle, a Christian who has described his goal in races to experience suffering in a way that brings him closer to Jesus Christ and to others, says he’s handled his newfound success because of his wife. Everyone, he said, should get a Lucy. On the way home from the BPN race, Lucy saw his phone blowing up and reminded him that many of those people were loving him because of what he just did, but that she and his friends loved him for who he was.

Dowdle’s breakthrough would not be possible without the backyard ultra format, and not just because he’s one of the best in the world at it. The race, as Lewis alluded to, is insanely popular: What started as another one of Laz’s harebrained ideas with the Big’s Backyard Ultra in 2011 has ballooned to over 1,000 independent backyard ultras across the globe.

Lewis was perhaps the event’s first breakout star. He was known, he said, after winning Badwater in 2014, but the attention was far different after he won Big’s Backyard in 2023, his second victory, and set a backyard world record by running more than 450 miles. The format changed the high school teacher’s life.

“I was doing other cool stuff but never got noticed,” Lewis said. “But after the backyard, I was invited to all these running events, at no cost, places I’d never get to go to as a teacher. Australia, Botswana, all over the place. In Botswana, people knew who I was, and I was like, ‘really’? It just created so many more doors opening.”

Backyard ultras favor a unique skillset. Neither Lewis or Dowdle have the legspeed to compete for a top spot at a “fast” ultra like Western States. But, Lewis counters, a top contender at Western States may struggle at a backyard ultra.

“I’ve seen collegiate-type runners, and they get totally destroyed,” Lewis said. “It’s a different type of grit that’s required for running five straight days.”

Lewis and Dowdle possess that special mindset that doesn’t have much to do with a training plan. Lewis is writing a book on mindset, in fact, that will be released next fall. For Dowdle, the ultimate challenge (and intrigue) comes in the unknown.

“You have no opportunity to say ‘in 10 miles this race will be over,’” Dowdle said. “You just have to wrestle with the discomfort.”

Dowdle does that, he said, by using skills he learned from his parents. His relentless, even disturbing, will comes from his mother, and he got an addict’s penchant from his father. His dad, he said, has been sober for nearly 30 years by using his addictive personality for good, investing his energy in Alcoholics Anonymous and helping others overcome their itches.

But Dowdle also excels by finding out who he was as a person: “Running helped me figure out the person I was running away from and also running toward the person I wanted to be.”

There is a dual narrative in his head, one that every ultrarunner facing the highs and lows of a long race knows. The first tells him he’s not worthy. The other feels only elation. Neither, he said, is true.

“I know that the narrative playing in my head at that moment is not the full truth,” Dowdle said. “And the narrative that tells me I’m not good enough. As long as my feet are still moving forward, I can combat that thinking and know that’s not the full truth.”

Proving that imposter syndrome wrong is his part of his “why,” the magic power that he and Lewis seem to possess that helps them run much farther than just about anyone else. But that’s not his only motivation.

Dowdle was, at one point, a football coach. But when he examined his life, he didn’t think football was the right avenue for what he truly wanted to do. He wanted to connect with young men and try to help them crawl out of the same situation he was in years ago, before he began running and journaling. So he traveled across the country and hosted beer miles. He still does this, though he drinks non-alcoholic varieties now, or he simply challenges athletes to beat his beer mile time. (It’s 5:51.57.)

When the race is over, he sits down with athletes and talks with them about their lives. He tells them he was like them once, a little lost and full of doubt and pain, with only alcohol seeming to help. There are other ways, he tells them. Running could be one. A personal journey, like that of the Iron Cowboy, could be another.

“I watched the Iron Cowboy documentary, and it made me realize that I do have control, and that I can do hard things and I’m capable of more,” Dowdle said. “That’s the gift he gave me in that moment. Now I’ve been on a mission to try to give that gift to other people.”

#2792. 9 Things To Do Now So The Holidays Aren’t Such A Cluster, Mentally

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

We don’t just mean “make a list and check it twice.”

Text your girlfriends and ask if you’re exchanging gifts this year, or if you know you’d rather not, say so (money’s tight for everyone right now!). Call up your family and your in-laws and let everyone know where you’ll be spending the holidays, how long you can stay when you visit, and any other plans that may have caused confusion or hurt feelings in the past.

“Let them know all the things that you’re wanting and all the things that you can and cannot do so that there’s less upset later. We don’t want there to be any assumptions that people have or expectations that aren’t met because one of my favorite quotes, and I forget who even said it, is expectations that are not communicated are resentments in the making,” says Morton.

(Editor’s note: This quote, “Unspoken expectations are premeditated resentments,” is from The Truth: An Uncomfortable Book About Relationships by Neil Strauss.)

Once you’ve decided on what you’d like to do for the holidays and how much you’re capable of, make that clear to your relatives.

“‘Just so you know, Mom, we’re only able to stay two nights this year. Was looking at our calendar for Thanksgiving, that’s what we can do,’” Morton says. “Or, ‘I’d love to host, but I’m going to need everyone to bring a dish.’ Or texting all your siblings or friends or whoever: ‘We’re going to keep gifts under $50 this year.’ One text could save you weeks of stress later.”

If you know your uncle always brings up politics at the table and you’re going to need to take a few deep breaths to hold back your fire-breathing rage, don’t let that moment be the first time you practice calming yourself down. Find what actually makes you feel better (Morton says a lot of people genuinely hate breathing exercises) and practice it in advance.

What helps you feel better? Is it deep breathing or is it just doing a little body shake? Is it going for a walk? I personally love qigong; it makes my anxiety just calm down. Try some things out now so that we’re not trying a coping skill for the first time at Thanksgiving dinner or Christmas Eve,” she advises.

Morton recommends sitting down and making a list of all the tasks involved in making the holidays happen. You’re going to Thanksgiving dinner but need to bring a dish. Do you have to be the one who shops for the ingredients, prepares it, and washes up after? When gift shopping, are you the only one in the house who can determine a budget, choose gifts, physically go to the store or sit down to order them, and then wrap them?

“Often women, especially mothers, take on all the tasks when a lot of that can be offloaded,” she says. “You can delegate to your spouse, to your other children, to other people in the family for certain things.”

Or, “Let me check my calendar and get back to you.” We often feel pressured to say yes to things in the moment — party invites, family visits, volunteer tasks — and then we feel overwhelmed by them later. Get in the habit of not saying yes right away, but giving yourself space to get back home and figure out how saying yes will actually fit into your life.

“I don’t think we often consider all of the labor involved in saying ‘yes’ to something. If we’re like, ‘Oh yeah, I’ll go to that potluck party where I not only have to bring a dish, but then I also have to bring a gift that’s $30 or less,’ those things take time,” she explains. “When are you going to go get that gift? When are you going to get the food and make that thing or buy that thing? Having a calendar where you start recognizing travel days or prep days, that really allows you to block off some days for rest so that we don’t overcommit.”

“We schedule parties and all sorts of other things for our kids and our families. What are you going to do for you? Are we making sure we have downtime?” Morton asks.

#3434. The Quintana Roo Service Course Studio NEXT Is a Serious Aero Race Bike

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

QR brings its triathlon roots to road with a configurable aero bike that clears 36mm tires.

Quintana Roo’s Service Course Studio NEXT is a mouthful of a name, but the idea is straightforward: a premium aero road bike from a brand with decades of triathlon experience. Its configurable build lets buyers choose more of the details up front than most race-bike brands allow.

The Service Course is QR’s aero road bike. Studio NEXT is its premium version, not a different frame concept or unique geometry. It uses the same frame shapes and fork as the standard model, with a higher-grade carbon layup that saves about 150 grams.

Brad DeVaney, QR’s lead engineer, told me the Studio NEXT frame uses more 46T carbon and less 30T fiber than the standard version. The internal mold components are similar, with adjustments to meet the weight target.

Like the standard Service Course, the Studio NEXT version is offered through QR’s online line configuration tool, which has multiple drivetrain, wheel, and other customization options. Additionally, QR will work with the customer to dial in gearing, crank length, and cockpit sizing. The Studio NEXT Service Course gets more premium paint options, painted logos instead of decals, and a few other different configuration options.

The Service Course has an extended headtube that comes to a prominent point, wide-set fork legs, dropped stays, and a deep downtube. Above the fork crown, a molded ridge QR calls a chine is designed to redirect air disturbed by the fork legs.

The bike clears tires up to 36mm. That doesn’t make it a gravel bike, but it does leave room to wider tires when conditions merit. The frame also uses a Universal Derailleur Hanger, threaded T47 bottom bracket, and non-proprietary upper headset bearing diameter that is compatible with several internal routing systems.

QR claims the painted size M Studio NEXT frame weighs 924 grams with the seatpost binder installed, but without axles or headset parts. QR also lists claimed weights of 360 grams for the fork and 240 grams for the seatpost with saddle hardware.

At 924 grams, the frame isn’t in the ultralight class. Specialized claims 687 grams for the S-Works Tarmac SL9 frame, and Trek lists 796 grams for a painted Madone SLR Gen 8 frame in size ML without hardware. Among dedicated aero frames, Canyon claims 960 grams for the Aeroad CFR.

These figures are rough context, not a clean ranking: frame size, paint, and included hardware vary between brands. Still, 924 grams is a competitive claim for an aero frame with clearance for 36mm tires.

Quintana Roo has run the Service Course in the wind tunnel, but it has been reticent to share specifics about the results or how the bike compares with its competitors. QR claims the Service Course is at least 3.6 watts faster than its previous SR-series road bike platform.

Not that I’d buy into QR’s data at face value even if it was provided. Data can be massaged and cherry-picked, and results can shift with the margin of error, bike-only versus rider-on-bike testing, bottle setup, wheels, tires, hood position, and more. Modern aero bikes can move up or down in a comparison depending on the protocol.

To QR’s credit, lead engineer Brad DeVaney gave me useful context. The relevant Service Course test used two standard water bottles. They also tested the Service Course with aero bottles, and it was, unsurprisingly, faster than it was with standard bottles, but DeVaney said they chose not to emphasize those results because most riders don’t use them.

DeVaney also said QR controlled handlebar stack and hood placement across comparison bikes, removed saddle position as a variable in non-rider testing, and used the SR as a calibration bike between test sessions.

That’s helpful. It still doesn’t answer the big question: How does the Service Course compare with the fastest aero road bikes from Specialized, Trek, Canyon, Cervélo, Giant, Factor, Pinarello, and the rest? QR says it has competitor data, but didn’t provide numbers I can publish.

So, is it aero? Yes. How aero? Much harder to say.

The frame has the hallmarks of a modern aero bike: a narrow, extended headtube; wide-set fork legs; a deep downtube; dropped stays; an aero seatpost; and aggressive shaping around the fork, headtube, and downtube. QR knows aerodynamics from its long history in triathlon.

And based on my riding, the Service Course feels like it punches through the air better than a non-aero bike. After decades of testing aero and non-aero bikes, that’s something I can usually feel.

I’m confident calling it competitively aero and likely on the faster side of the bell curve. I just can’t tell you where it sits in the current superbike hierarchy.

There’s a new split emerging in high-end race bike geometry.

On one side are bikes like the Factor One, Ridley Noah Fast 3.0, Ridley Falcn RS 2.0, and Eddy Merckx 525R. These aren’t simply aero frames with hidden hoses and deep tubes. They’re built around a forward, hoods-primary riding position: the rider shifts forward relative to the bottom bracket and stretches farther over the hoods. The geometry aims to preserve balance and handling as the rider’s mass shifts forward.

Factor is particularly explicit about this with the One. Ridley and Eddy Merckx describe similar goals for their newer race bikes, including steeper seat angles and longer reaches.

The Service Course is not that kind of bike, at least not on paper.

That’s interesting because Quintana Roo is a triathlon-first company. A forward road-racing position has a lot in common with a triathlon position, and QR has spent decades thinking about aerodynamics, rider position, and how to make the rider faster when the body is the biggest thing in the wind. If any brand had permission to push harder on forward-position road bike geometry, it’s QR.

So I’m surprised the Service Course’s geometry is not more progressive. Its fit is closer to a modern traditional road-race bike like the Trek Madone or Specialized Tarmac than to the Factor, Ridley, and Eddy Merckx bikes above.

That doesn’t mean you can’t set up the QR for a forward position. The 0mm-offset seatpost, saddle-rail adjustment, crank-length choices, and cockpit options give the user room to dial in that kind of position. But being able to set up a bike for a forward position isn’t the same as designing the frame around one.

The newer forward-fit bikes build that position into the chassis: their seat angles, reach, front centers, cockpit layouts, and handling are designed around a rider moving forward, using shorter cranks and narrower bars, and spending more time on the hoods. The goal is to preserve balance and handling as the rider’s mass shifts forward.

That isn’t automatically better. Traditional road geometry is familiar and easier to fit, and it may avoid some handling compromises that can come from moving a rider forward on a chassis and altering the handling geometry around that weight shift.

DeVaney pushed back on my read. He said a more forward position was part of its design foundation. He also pointed to QR’s 0mm- and 20mm-offset seatpost options as evidence that the bike is intended to cover a broad range of race positions.

Still, I can’t help wondering what QR does next. A Service Course V2 that leans harder into the company’s triathlon DNA and the latest forward-fit thinking could get very interesting.

One other geometry detail: the Service Course is offered in only four sizes: extra-small, small, medium, and large. Most high-end frames come in six or more sizes.

Because QR uses a configurator, the Service Course Studio NEXT does not fit neatly into the usual “this model comes with these parts and costs this much” box.

That configurator is one of the more interesting things about buying a QR. Beyond the choices you can click on, you can specify details like gearing and crank length instead of immediately planning your first round of upgrades.

At the time I’m writing this, the Service Course Studio NEXT builds start at $7,800 with Ultregra Di2 with 4iiii power meter, DT Swiss ARC 1600 Dicut wheels, and a Vision Metron 5D ACR EVO integrated cockpit.

Note that the current configurator differs from the one I used when I built my review bike. The biggest difference is that, at that time, SRAM Force AXS was an option, and now it isn't. Based on the assumption that a Force AXS build is slightly more expensive than Ultegra Di2 with power meter build, I’m estimating my review bike’s price is about $8,300

The current QR configurator offers Shimano Ultegra, Shimano Dura-Ace, and SRAM RED builds, with wheel options including Zipp, DT Swiss, ENVE, and HED. Current tire options include Continental GP5000 and Vittoria Corsa Pro and Corsa Pro Control tires.

But as I found out, the configuration tool changes, so available options come and go. But QR encourages customers to reach out with off-menu requests, so you can contact their team for component requests not shown in the configurator.

The Service Course Studio NEXT lands in one of the most competitive parts of the road market: the high-end race bike.

That means it is up against the obvious big-brand machines from Specialized, Trek, Giant, Cannondale, Canyon, Cervélo, Scott, BMC, Factor, Pinarello, and others. Some have more racing visibility. Some have larger dealer networks. Some have more frame sizes. Some are lighter.

The Service Course Studio NEXT gives you a claimed 924g painted aero frame, 36mm tire clearance, UDH, T47A, a claimed service-friendly headset, and a much more customizable buying experience than most major brands offer.

The rider who wants a WorldTour-shaped object with a massive dealer network probably already knows where they are going. The rider who wants a fast aero road bike but also wants to choose bar width, stem length, crank length, saddle, wheels, tires, color, and cockpit before the bike ships should look harder at QR.

QR has credibility—just not the usual road credibility. In triathlon, the brand’s history means something. In road, it still has to prove itself to riders who reflexively reach for the familiar names.

Whether that matters is up to the buyer. It should not matter more than the bike. But bikes are emotional objects, and logo gravity is real.

The Quintana Roo Service Course Studio NEXT, hereafter the SCSN to save my fingers from an overuse injury, met all my expectations for a modern, high-end race bike.

It feels quick, moves efficiently, has light and precise handling, and offers a reasonably compliant ride while transmitting appropriate feedback to the rider.

I did find it a little loud. Road noise, shifting, and braking are amplified by the frame, which isn’t surprising for a stiff frame with big tubes.

Some of the race bikes I’ve been riding concurrently, like the Tarmac and Madone, have smaller tubes and don’t pick up and amplify as much noise. The SCSN is even a bit louder than the Canyon Aeroad CFR, another bike in the same full-aero category.

It was loudest with the HED 62mm wheels that came with my test bike, but I fitted several other wheelsets to the SCSN and it remained louder than average.

I am a fan of quiet bikes, so I’m admittedly more persnickety about noise than many riders. Take that for what it’s worth.

Other than the noise, I have few complaints about the SCSN. In a very competitive category, it holds its own admirably well.

I was particularly impressed by its cornering performance and high-speed handling. One of my favorite test loops ends with a descent that starts with a steep, dead-straight section alongside a reservoir before diving into a canyon with several turns of varying radius and not-great pavement. Between the reservoir and canyon, the wind is usually unpredictably gusty and swirly.

I take all my test bikes down this road at least once, so I’ve built up a good mental basis for comparison. The SCSN was one of the best I’ve ever taken down it.

Despite its aero profile, it was rock solid when I was supertucked at more than 50 mph. When I returned to the saddle for the canyon turns, I immediately noticed how smoothly the SCSN initiated each turn, finding that intuitive balance between too quick and twitchy and too stable and sluggish.

But what really impressed me was how well it tracked through the turns. Between the wind and changing pavement, there is a lot here that can upset a bike, which is unsettling when speeds are above 40 mph, cars are climbing in the other lane, and I’m trying to navigate around potholes and sand washed onto the road. It’s a complex and potentially dangerous section of pavement, which isn’t helped by my need to try to set a PR every time.

And damn, did the SCSN carve these corners and prove itself a worthy companion in the chaos like few bikes I’ve ridden.

If you are wondering, no, I did not PR the segment on the SCSN. After so many attempts, getting that PR now feels as much a matter of catching the right wind and traffic as it does of riding a great-handling bike. Still, I do feel a new PR is within reach on the SCSN if the conditions line up, which is more than I can say about some bikes.

Rider comfort and compliance were also better than I anticipated, with the front and rear feeling well balanced. I will note, however, that because of my fit and Quintana Roo’s sizing, I was riding the size small frame with the seatpost at its extension limit.

So, yes, I found the SCSN to be a very good bike. Above average, for sure.

And I want to again shout out QR for the no-fuss customizability it offers. I also love the little details the Studio NEXT program brings, like painting the handlebar and transition spacers to match the frame. That is an uncommon and very premium touch.

But I get hung up when I try to answer the question: Why buy this bike instead of the many other great race bikes you can buy from brands big and small?

Certainly, the customization is a boon, but you can find that from Factor and Trek through Project One, among others. The SCSN rides and handles well, but I’d say the Tarmac is a more refined package on this front. It’s competitively aerodynamic, but I can almost guarantee the Cervélo S5 is markedly more slippery. It’s a good value, but brands like X-Lab, Winspace, and Quick Pro soundly beat it on that front. And Quintana Roo doesn’t have a vibe that lets you flex at the group-ride meetup.

I’m not trying to slam Quintana Roo here, and apologies to anyone there who feels like I am. It’s just that the market is awash in so many fast race bikes, many of them very good, that it becomes really difficult for me, as an objective—but human—reviewer and recommender of bikes, to arrive at the SCSN as the race bike someone should buy if they’re shopping right now.

And, to be totally fair to QR, I could say the same thing about many other bikes.

Ultimately, what you buy is your decision. So I’ll close with this: The SCSN is a great bike from a brand that knows a lot about building bikes made to go fast.
