Anyone with a backyard the size of the storied “back 40” has oodles of choices when it comes to flowering shrubs – because space isn't an issue. But those of us with smaller yards need to make every inch count.

And the truth is, you don't need a large yard to enjoy a spectacular display in every season. Planting compact flowering shrubs in fall gives roots time to establish before winter, rewarding gardeners with an explosion of color the following spring. What types of shrubs should you consider? You’ll want space-saving shrubs that punch well above their size.

Below is my shortlist of compact flowering shrubs to plant in fall for spring blooms. While you may want to add a few more bushes to fill up your space, these gems are a great way to get started.

The old saying goes that April showers bring May flowers, but in fact, the best spring flowers come from all that autumn rain. Fall is a wonderful time to plant shrubs, perhaps the very best time, if you want spring blooms.

First, the air grows cooler as October arrives while the soil is still warm. This combination supports root growth and, combined with shorter days, reduces heat stress and evaporation. Add the likelihood of fall rain, and you’ve got a wonderful window to plant shrubs.

These fall conditions help your plants establish root systems during the cooler months. That means the shrubs can invest their energy in foliage and flowers when spring arrives.

#2749. Des Linden’s Simple (and Somewhat Shocking) 100-Mile Fueling Plan

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If you buy through our links, we may earn an affiliate commission. This supports our mission to get more people active and outside.Learn about Outside Online's affiliate link policy

Boston Marathon champion Des Linden will toe the line at her first 100-miler on October 31, 2026, at the Javelina Jundred near Fountain Hills, Arizona. To prepare for the distance and hot desert conditions, she worked with a team of sports scientists, including Olympic-marathoner-turned-ultrarunner-champion Magda Boulet, to dial in her metabolic needs and a fueling plan. She will primarily rely on liquid calories, with the addition of some more out-of-the-box products like “cooling” menthol gels, while understanding that mid-race adjustments will be key.

For Boston Marathon champion-turned-competitive ultrarunner Des Linden, one question keeps driving her forward: “Can I do this?”

After years of racing some of the world’s most prestigious road marathons, the two-time Olympian is shifting her focus to off-road competitions. On October 31, she’ll attempt her most ambitious athletic endeavor yet when she toes the start line of the Javelina Jundred, a 100-mile trail race through Arizona’s Sonoran Desert that also serves as a Golden Ticket race into the 2027 Western States 100.

“I feel like part of me is just missing being afraid of something; the unknown,” Linden tells Trail Runner. “What is this going to pull out of me? What is this going to ask of me? That’s all really fascinating.”

In addition to the distance—nearly 40 miles more than Linden has ever covered in one go–Javelina presents a slew of unfamiliar challenges. One of the biggest is fueling and hydration over the course of 13-plus hours in desert conditions. Linden got a small taste, literally, at the Black Canyon 50K in February. It was her first ultramarathon and she acknowledged after the race that all of those years of fueling on the roads was only so helpful.

“I went into Black Canyon 50k with a loose fueling plan—to have a Gu at each aid station and sip on an electrolyte beverage between stations,” Linden said. “I wasn’t diligent about it during the race and certainly didn’t account for the time required for trail miles versus road miles. After the race, I realized it was a very easy place to make improvements, and getting it dialed before MDS would be important.”

It’s a relatable learning curve that Linden has taken seriously in the eight months since. Together with a team of sports scientists, including Olympic-marathoner-turned ultrarunning-champion Magda Boulet, Linden has tested her metabolic needs under hot ultramarathon conditions to develop a (somewhat surprising) fueling plan for October 31.

Linden retired from professional road running in 2025 after an illustrious career that included two Olympic Games, two runner-up finishes at both the Chicago and Boston Marathons, and an iconic win at the 2018 Boston Marathon—becoming the first American woman to do so in 33 years. Rather than hanging up her running shoes, Linden is targeting some of the world’s most prestigious trail races.

“I think a lot of this part of my career is getting away from comparing myself to my former self. So this is uncharted territory for me,” Linden says.

This past April, Linden, 43, competed in only her second ultra-trail event, finishing third at Marathon des Sables (MDS), a six-day stage race in the southern Moroccan Sahara often referred to as the “Toughest Footrace on Earth.” While Linden has spent her career refining how she fuels for peak performance and recovery, her MDS experience reinforced just how critical nutrition is to sustaining her endurance and rebounding after hours on her feet. She adds that ultrarunning has pushed her to reevaluate her approach to nutrition as she continues to take on longer and more demanding challenges.

“The distance itself makes nutrition so much more important,” Linden says. “You can’t cover that distance if you’re skimping or shortcutting getting the calories in throughout the day.”

The Javelina Jundred is an open and exposed course. The race is defined by heat: the average high is around 82 degrees Fahrenheit and the average low is roughly 58 degrees. Managing more time on her feet also creates more opportunities for things to go awry. Research has long suggested that heat stress can reduce appetite: A 2018 study in Nature found that exercise suppresses appetite by heating up a region of the brain responsible for regulating eating. Of course, this appetite suppression is counterproductive since you are expending lots of extra energy while running, and create a negative feedback loop that self-perpetuates.

Along with dehydration, racing in the heat creates a unique set of challenges. Linden has been working hard to prepare for them.

To prepare for the challenge, Linden enlisted experts from Gu Energy Labs, based in Berkeley, California, including Roxanne Vogel, Ph.D., nutrition scientist, exercise physiologist, and director of research and education, and Magda Boulet, Gu Energy Labs president and one of the world’s most accomplished ultrarunners. Boulet’s own evolution from Olympic marathoner to Western States 100, Leadville 100, and Marathon des Sables champion has made her a valuable resource for Linden’s own ultramarathon journey.

“I imagine most ultrarunners will look and be like ‘you’re not ready at all.’ But they don’t necessarily know that behind the scenes I have the ultrarunner,” Linden says.

Ahead of MDS this spring, Linden underwent laboratory testing at Gu’s Performance Lab with the goal of understanding her energy expenditure, hydration needs, and fueling tolerance during ultramarathon efforts. As part of the assessment, Vogel set out to measure Linden’s sweat rate and sodium losses under heat stress, her caloric demands at race pace, and how her body uses carbohydrate versus fat in simulated desert conditions.

The data helped Vogel develop an individualized metabolic and hydration profile for Linden. Vogel discovered that Linden isn’t a very salty sweater, and she also learned that Linden needs approximately 10 grams of carbohydrate per kilogram of body weight per day. (For a 120-pound person, that’s roughly 550 grams of carbs.)

Going into MDS, the team developed a precise nutrition strategy for Linden, which included Gu Roctane Drink Mix along with gels and chews for her on-course fueling. After each stage, Linden leaned on nut butter and Roctane Protein Recovery Drink Mix to supply the necessary carbohydrates, electrolytes, and 20 grams of whey protein. Her dinner included a savory dehydrated potato mash with added protein and sodium. Linden also integrated Gu Roctane BCAA capsules before and after the stages to support muscle repair and reduce soreness.

Vogel says those core principles will be part of Linden’s approach at Javelina, but the demands of a single day 100-mile race in the Arizona desert will require some adjustments. For Javelina, liquid calories will be the backbone of her nutrition strategy. The target is to consume approximately 16 ounces (500 milliliters) of Roctane Drink Mix per hour. Per 16-ounce serving, it offers 250 calories, 60 grams of carbs, and 320 milligrams of sodium. Linden also plans to integrate some products that remain under development, Vogel says, including Roctane MC-318, a patented menthol-based energy gel designed to help athletes feel perceptually cooler. The product is not yet commercially available, but has already been tested by athletes at races such as Badwater 135, Western States 100, and Marathon des Sables.

The Javelina course consists of five loops along rolling desert singletrack. It’s a format that allows for both Linden and her crew to accurately anticipate her arrival at aid stations, where she’ll be able to replenish supplies and, crucially, will allow for crew to frequently assess how she’s managing the nutrition and hydration plan.

The terrain will help determine when Linden takes in different forms of fuel. Uphill sections, when she’s moving more slowly, provide an opportunity to take gels or chews. On downhills, when she wants to let her legs unwind and rip down as fast as possible, she’ll focus more on liquids. Her approach also reflects a shift from road marathon racing, where fueling opportunities are often more structured and brief.

“In the marathon, [every] 5K you grab the bottle, you want eight to 10 ounces and you want to get rid of the bottle as quickly as possible. We have the luxury of carrying it and sipping on it, and also strategizing,” Linden says.

Temperature will be one of the most significant variables. Research has found that during prolonged exercise in the heat, gastrointestinal symptoms are greater.

“People think it has to be really hot for them to need to make changes to their nutrition or their electrolyte plan,” Vogel says. “The cutoff is somewhere around 75 degrees Fahrenheit. Above that, you’re certainly going to be at an increased level of needs in terms of your hydration and electrolytes, plus also potentially your carbohydrate needs. Even in somewhat moderately warm temperatures, athletes can be needing more than what they would normally take in.”

As the temperature rises throughout the day over the course of the race, Linden anticipates leaning more heavily on liquids. The drink mix can provide fluid and carbohydrates while helping her meet her electrolyte needs, and gels with water will fill any gaps.

“She’s never done a [single day] race this long. The longer you’re out there, the more likely your gut is going to have some issues,” Vogel says. “When you’re exercising, blood flow is going to your working muscles. Less of it is going to your digestive track. So that’s one thing that’s putting stress on the system that makes it harder to digest and absorb [nutrition]. Hence the whole reason for things like gels. They’re easy to digest.”

During the hottest part of the day, Vogel says the team may decrease Linden’s carbohydrate intake to account for changes in digestive tolerance. But as temperatures fall into the night, they’ll look to increase her carbs. Vogel says caffeine will require similar consideration.

“During very hot weather events, caffeine can actually be triggering to the gut in a bad way,” Vogel says. “Rather than following a fixed schedule regardless of conditions, we might actually pull back on caffeine during the heat of the day and start to strategically add it back in as it cools off later in the day.”

Another factor to consider: flavor fatigue, when sensory receptors on your tongue that detect flavor become less responsive with repeated consumption.

“Flavor fatigue is something we know and understand. In hot environments, you don’t generally want to have things that are what we call indulgent—chocolates and caramels and vanillas that are going to stay heavy on the palate. We want more things like citrus and light fruity or even sour, tart flavors that are going to clear the palate or stimulate some thirst,” Vogel says.

Ultimately, the goal is to make the strategy simple to execute, Vogel says. Linden’s success will depend not only on having a precise nutrition plan, but on adjusting on the fly when fatigue and discomfort begin to accumulate.

“I feel like it’s a race within itself. Did I try to execute as well as possible? Did I follow the plan? Did I make good decisions?” Linden says. “I want to finish, and I think there’s so many things that can go in so many different directions in that amount of time and that distance. So if I fueled enough to get myself through 100 miles, that’s a victory.”

The 2500 Series is a new collection that “reimagines the design DNA of the watch that started it all,” according to Casio. The brand is nodding towards the original G-Shock ref. DW-5000C from 1983, a watch designed to be tough enough to keep ticking even when dropped. The team famously tested prototypes by hurling them out of the third-floor men’s bathroom of the Casio offices. The new 2500 series is a close descendant of the shock-absorbent watch's original design.

The first G-Shock featured an octagonal digital display, four buttons on the case flanks, and an integrated rubber strap. The 2500 Series incorporates most of those features—but this time, the display is analog rather than digital, and a pair of case “ears” makes the model look a lot like Patek Philippe’s famous flagship luxury sports watch.

Designed by Gérald Genta and released in 1976, the Nautilus is based on a porthole—hence the “ears,” which mirror the hinges/securing mechanism used to open and close a ship’s window against the elements. You may also recall that this isn't the first time that Casio has taken inspiration from a famous Genta watch: the GA-2100 Series (aka ‘CasiOak’), with its octagonal bezel design, bears a remarkable similarity to the Audemars Piguet Royal Oak.

The watch community has been waiting impatiently for the announcement of the 2500 series since Casio’s Japanese website accidentally listed these pieces back in September. In the months since, collectors have already given the watch the “CasioNaut” nickname. (What good is a watch without a clever nickname, after all?) The wait isn’t over just yet, though. The line will officially go on sale through Casio’s website on November 6th.

The 2500 Series is much more than a quartz-powered Nautilus copy. At the center of the design is the Multi-Mission Date function that operates through a three-cell display at 3 o’clock. Besides indicating the date, it can also display the watch’s current mode, elapsed stopwatch minutes, and setting information. Meanwhile, a small-seconds display at 9 o’clock shows passing seconds, but can also be paused and restarted as necessary. (Pausing the function can extend the battery life up to five years.)

The timepiece's tech uses G-SHOCK’s familiar four-button arrangement and comes packed with all the features we've come to expect from the Japanese brand. For starters, the watch automatically keeps rigorous time through 2099 after you input the year, month, and date. LED illumination ensures everything is legible even in low light, while a magnetic system steadies the watch’s hands even if dropped, bumped, or roughed up. Water-resistant to 200m, the 2500 Series is just as capable as you’d expect from a hard-wearing G-SHOCK. Available in simple resin cases with matching rubber bracelets for $145; with metal accents and rubber bracelets for $280-$300; and with full-metal bracelets for $350, the handful of launch references come in a variety of versatile colors and weigh between 47 grams (resin) and 160-180 grams (metal).

Though Casio releases oodles of fresh colorways in existing G-SHOCK families each year, it’s not every day that the world gets an entirely new model family. Fun, streamlined, and much more affordable than the watch it's nicknamed after, the 2500 Series is certainly on its way to becoming a hit.

#1406. You Can Make Your Stairs Safer Without Sacrificing Style. Here Are 4 Tips Designers Always Recommend.

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Form and function can share the same staircase.

Pictured above: In this historic Pittsburgh home, designer Betsy Wentz installed a custom ombré stair runner.

A well-designed stairwell is more than just a pretty sight; it should be just as safe and dependable as it is beautiful.

You might not think twice about going up and down the stairs when your eyes and joints are in their prime. However, it's important to remember that aging adults, young children, and small pets are extra susceptible to falling down a hazardous set of stairs.

We asked a handful of designers to share a few stylish additions they recommend for any staircase to improve safety.

Terri Fiori is the founder of Fiori + Co. and a Certified Aging in Place (CAPS) specialist. When designing stairs in any home, one of the first changes she recommends is adding additional lighting. The reason is that as your eyes age, your retinas receive less light, which can make navigating dimly lit stairs a challenge.

But you don't need to opt for bright and harsh overhead lighting. In a recent renovation project, Fiori added recessed lighting to the stairwell and paired it with touch-activated LED lighting under the handrails. The end result ended up enhancing the stairwell's safety for family members of all ages.

"Recessed lighting plus handrail lighting ensured that shadows on the steps were eliminated and a trip to the ER avoided," she says. "Small children who may not be able to reach the light switch also benefit from the touch-sensitive illuminated handrail."

Caroline Aycock, the owner of Koukla Collective, often works on historic home projects. When dealing with narrow and steep original staircases, she always adds a stair runner to prevent feet from slipping. Not only do runners improve a staircase's safety, but they also offer a great chance to make a statement.

"I think of a patterned staircase runner similarly to wallpaper in a powder room," she says. "It's a place to let your hair down a little and make a small statement. I love stripes in particular. There is something so satisfying to the eye to see those vertical lines running up a staircase."

Fiori also notes that the installation style matters when it comes to safety. She recommends avoiding the "waterfall style," which is where the runner simply falls down to the next tread. Since the runner is not completely attached to the underside of the nose of the step, it increases the chances of slipping.

"To prevent this, we always specify a runner that has a 'Hollywood' wrap, meaning that the runner follows the contour of the step and is wrapped and stapled tightly underneath the nosing as well as secured to the riser below," Fiori says. "I think of handrails as one of the jewelry moments in a home," she says. "I love painting them with a high gloss, changing the metal hardware to something pretty or even using Lucite handrails with stairs that have wallpaper."

Cryptic snakes in Southeast Asia have evolved a remarkable trick for evading predators: Instead of slithering, they arch their long bodies and tumble away at high speeds.

The dwarf reed snakes (Pseudorabdion) pull off this acrobatic feat by keeping their head in a relatively fixed position while rotating the rest of their body, according to a new study published Monday (Oct. 19) in the journal Current Biology.

Researchers filmed the snakes from different angles to better understand the mechanics of the rolling, or cartwheeling, behavior. They found that the snakes were capable of reaching velocities of up to 12.1 feet per second (3.69 meters per second), which is much faster than they can slither (technically referred to as undulating locomotion).

"Rolling allows them to escape from danger very quickly," study first author Yosuke Kojima, a lecturer in the biology department at Toho University in Japan, told Live Science. "In our study, their peak forward velocity during rolling was, on average, four times that of normal undulating locomotion."

Dwarf reed snakes are secretive, often nocturnal species that live across Southeast Asia, where they are prey for birds and other snakes. Although the snakes are extremely cryptic and usually hidden beneath leaves or other natural objects, people occasionally observe the serpents' dazzling rolls.

In a 2023 study, researchers formally documented the rolling behavior for the first time in one dwarf reed snake species (P. longiceps), which researchers observed in northwestern Malaysia in 2019. Kojima's team started independently observing the behavior around the same time in the Malaysian state of Sarawak, on Borneo.

"I had never seen anything like it before, and I immediately wanted to learn more about it," Kojima said.

For the new study, researchers filmed the rolling behavior multiple times in three dwarf reed species ‪—‬ P. saravacense, P. collaris and P. longiceps — between 2019 and 2022. The team also studied three reed snakes from another genus (Calamaria), which didn't perform the behavior.

Kojima said their study builds on the 2023 research by quantifying the rolling movements, confirming the overall movement pattern that research described, and revealing further details, including how the snakes stabilize their heads.

Snakes aren't the only tumblers in nature. For example, some desert spiders can roll away from danger, as can mother-of-pearl moth caterpillars (Pleurotya ruralis or Patania ruralis). However, the snakes are unique in that they seem to have good control over their rolls.

Rolling movements typically destabilize the head and disrupt sensory-guided adjustment, especially at high speeds, according to the authors of the new study. In other words, it's hard for animals to control their bodies when they're rolling around. The snakes' technique of keeping their heads relatively fixed and often elevated off the ground likely counters what would otherwise be destabilizing effects.

Pseudorabdion saravacense is one of three dwarf reed snake species now known to perform the rolling behavior.

In addition to studying speed and form, the researchers found that the snakes could move in a relatively straight line, readily moving along a "racetrack" that was only 8 inches (20 centimeters) wide. The researchers noted that the snakes often ended their tumbling routine by diving underneath nearby objects, so they seem to remain aware of their surroundings.

The rolling is a behavioral innovation that allows the snakes to overcome their physical constraints and pull off an otherwise impossible movement, according to the researchers.

"Behavioral innovation can give rise to surprising new abilities that we would not expect from an animal's appearance alone," Kojima said.

Researchers have speculated that the bizarre behavior could surprise and confuse predators, in addition to being a rapid means of escape. The authors of the 2023 study also hypothesized that rolling would result in a patchy scent trail, and because king cobras (Ophiophagus) use scent cues to locate their prey, this could make it harder for the predators to hunt dwarf reed snakes.

As Cybersecurity Awareness Month continues, the GitHub Bug Bounty team spotlights @r31n, diving into their research approach, favorite bug classes, and advice for aspiring hackers.

As Cybersecurity Awareness Month continues, the GitHub Bug Bounty team is excited to spotlight another top-performing security researcher from the GitHub Security Bug Bounty Program: @r31n!

As home to the world’s developers, GitHub is dedicated to the security and reliability of the code behind millions of projects, and our Bug Bounty Program remains a cornerstone of that commitment. Collaboration with external researchers is one of the most effective ways to improve security, and every year, the community helps us identify and fix vulnerabilities that make the platform safer for everyone.

The security research landscape is changing rapidly. AI has lowered the barrier to entry and become a genuine force multiplier. We use it across our own internal security programs, and we welcome researchers who do the same. However, what separates great research has not changed: validation, a working proof of concept, and a clear demonstration of real security impact. The tools do not matter; the quality of the work does. The researchers we spotlight this month exemplify exactly that standard through findings that are verified, reproducible, and demonstrate that a security boundary has been crossed rather than simply theorizing that one exists.

Researchers who consistently work at that level can earn an invitation to our permanent VIP program, which offers higher payouts, faster response times, and early previews of beta products and features. Learn more about how to qualify on our public bounty site.

As we wrap up our Cybersecurity Awareness Month celebration, we interview @r31n, a researcher renowned for their work on GitHub Enterprise Server, where they have repeatedly uncovered high-impact issues. Their reports consistently include working proofs of concept and clear reproduction steps, helping our engineering teams move from triage to remediation more quickly.

How did you get started in security and bug bounty, and what keeps you coming back to GitHub’s program?

I got into bug bounty because I wanted a way to keep developing my security skills outside the environments I work with every day. It exposes me to different products, technologies, and architectures I probably wouldn’t run into otherwise. GitHub is one of those programs I keep coming back to because there’s just so much surface area. It’s large, complex, and constantly changing. New features ship all the time, so it never really feels like a finished target. They also work closely with researchers, the bounty structure is competitive, and the swag is a nice bonus. It’s simply a program I enjoy spending time on.

What are your favorite classes of bugs to research and why?

I don’t really have one favorite bug class. What interests me more are exploit chains, especially cases where a few things that seem relatively harmless on their own come together and create something much more serious. An open redirect is a simple example. By itself, the impact might be limited. But if you find an allowlisted-domain SSRF that follows redirects, suddenly that open redirect can become a useful part of a much bigger chain. That’s probably the part of vulnerability research I enjoy most: finding those connections, looking at two or three separate bugs and realizing they can be combined in a way that wasn’t intended. It takes some technical knowledge, but also quite a bit of creativity.

How do you keep your skills sharp and stay on top of new vulnerability trends?

It’s actually a mix of different things. Security blogs, vulnerability write-ups, conference talks, proof of concepts, research papers, courses. I try to learn from wherever useful work is being shared. But most of it only really sticks once I try it myself. I can read a vulnerability write-up, but working through it myself gives me a much deeper understanding. If I actually reproduce the technique, play around with it, break it, and figure out why it behaves the way it does, that’s when it really stays with me. And later, when I see a similar pattern on a real target, it’s much easier to recognize.

What tools or workflows have been gamechangers for your research? Any lesser-known utilities you’d recommend?

I tend to prefer manual investigation and small, purpose-built scripts over large automated toolchains. If I notice I’m repeating the same task during research, I’ll usually automate that specific part. AI has probably impacted my way of working more than any individual security tool.

Even then, I think mindset matters more than tooling. You can become extremely good with a particular tool and still miss interesting bugs because you’re only looking at the target through the lens that tool gives you. Sometimes relying too heavily on tooling actually narrows the way you think. Tools change. Techniques change. Curiosity and persistence tend to age much better. So, I don’t really have one tool I’d recommend to everyone. The most useful tool is usually the one that helps answer the specific question I’m investigating.

Walk us through your general approach. How do you pick a target area, and how do you go from “interesting feature” to a confirmed finding?

I usually start by mapping out the application and getting a feel for how its different parts fit together. I tend to spend more time on complex functionality, integrations, trust boundaries, and anything that behaves a little differently from the rest. Experience and intuition matter too. Some areas simply look more likely to expose interesting security behavior. From there, I often think in terms of sources and sinks rather than starting with a specific vulnerability class. A source is a point where attacker-controlled data enters the application.

Think of a request parameter, uploaded file, API input, or data coming from another service. A sink can be a security-sensitive operation that consumes this data, for example, a network request, file access, process execution, database query, or authorization decision. The interesting part is tracing the path between the two. I look at how the data is transformed, validated, or passed across trust boundaries, and whether those controls are sufficient before it reaches the sink.

I like this approach because it lets me reason about attack paths without forcing my investigation into a predefined bug class. Sometimes the vulnerability only becomes obvious once you understand how a particular source can influence a security-sensitive operation. I consider a finding confirmed once I understand the attack path, can produce a working PoC, and can explain the impact.

Do you use AI in your research? Where does it genuinely help and where does it fall short?

Yes. Quite a lot, actually, but mostly as an accelerator. I use it for navigating unfamiliar code, finding call sites, tracing data through the codebase, and understanding how a system is structured and how its components interact. I also find it helpful for writing PoC scripts once I’ve identified the vulnerability.

Where it works best is when the question is specific. “Is this application vulnerable?” usually isn’t a very useful prompt. Give it the right context, explain what you already know, and ask a narrow question, and the quality goes up dramatically. There’s a bit of a paradox there. AI becomes more useful as you understand the problem better yourself. And I never treat its conclusions as facts. Hallucinations, false positives, bad assumptions, and confidently wrong answers are all common. Everything should still be manually verified. AI makes my work faster. However, it doesn’t replace understanding the application, developing intuition, or proving that something actually works.

What’s your guidance on using AI responsibly in bug bounty?

Use it to improve your research, not to outsource responsibility for it. If AI tells you it found a vulnerability, that doesn’t mean you found a real vulnerability. You still need to reproduce it yourself. You should understand exactly what’s happening, which security boundary or control is being violated, and what the real impact is. Ideally, you should understand the issue well enough that you could write the report without AI at all. Build a clear PoC. Make sure the security team can follow it. Know why every step matters.

AI has lowered the barrier to doing security research, which is great in a lot of ways. But you can also see the downside in the number of unverified reports teams now have to deal with. AI should make research better. Not just make more reports.

As the industry ships more AI-powered features, does testing them require a different mindset than traditional web bugs?

Yes. Conventional vulnerabilities still matter, but attackers may also be able to influence a probabilistic decision-making component through the data, instructions, or context it receives. But getting a model to say or do something unexpected isn’t necessarily the interesting part. The more important question is what that influence allows an attacker to achieve. Can they access something they should not or cause the system to act with privileges the attacker does not have? That’s why I don’t think model-level guardrails should ever be treated as the only or primary security boundary. You still need deterministic controls around the model. This includes proper authorization, least-privilege access, isolation, and validation. The surrounding systems matter just as much as the model itself. And traditional application security doesn’t disappear just because AI is involved. Model output should still be treated as untrusted input. If that output ends up in a browser, database query, shell command, web request, or another downstream system without the right controls, you can still end up with very familiar vulnerabilities: XSS, SQL injection, command injection, SSRF, and so on. I think the model is new. A lot of the dangerous sinks aren’t.

What’s one thing you wish you’d known when you first started?

Learn systems, not checklists. When I started, I was tempted to collect huge lists of techniques, payloads, tools, and bypasses and then work through them one by one. Those lists can be useful.

I still use them. But understanding how a system works has been far more valuable. Its internal logic. The data flows. Trust relationships. Assumptions. Why one component is allowed to talk to another. Why a particular restriction exists in the first place. I also learned not to assume something is secure because it looks secure, or because plenty of researchers have already tested it.

A lot of interesting research begins with: “Why does this work this way?” rather than “Which payload should I try next?” There’s some luck involved too. Sometimes you spend days following something that goes absolutely nowhere. Then you notice one tiny detail almost by accident and it turns into a high-impact finding. You can’t control that part. You can control whether you keep questioning things long enough to notice it.

What advice or resources would you recommend to researchers trying to land their first valid report?

Get comfortable with the fundamentals, then pick one program and one target and stick with them for a while. Constantly jumping between targets makes it difficult to build a real mental model of any of them. Use the application. Explore weird flows. Test different roles and permission levels. Read the documentation. Watch the requests and responses. Look at the APIs. Labs, courses, public write-ups, disclosed bug bounty reports, and intentionally vulnerable applications are all useful too. They teach you where bugs tend to hide and, over time, help build the intuition to recognize similar situations elsewhere.

One mistake I see a lot is relying on tools too early. Another is giving up because the obvious tests didn’t find anything. Tools help. They don’t build the mental model for you. And when you finally do find something, spend the extra time validating it properly. Create a working PoC. Make reproduction easy for the security team. Understanding one target deeply will usually get you further than trying to find a bug as quickly as possible.

What do you enjoy doing when you aren’t hacking?

Endurance sports, mainly road cycling, especially in the mountains. I enjoy the physical challenge of a long climb and pushing myself over longer distances. It’s also a great way to get away from screens for a while and spend some time outside. There’s actually something about climbing that feels pretty similar to vulnerability research. You can put in a lot of effort without seeing much progress. Sometimes you have no idea whether what you’re doing is going to lead anywhere. You just keep going. And every now and then, it pays off.

The mini Wishbone is one recent example of adult favorites being remade for a younger (and smaller) customer, a practice that goes back decades but has gained fresh attention in recent years. Ligne Roset’s Mini Togo, introduced in 2007, shrinks Michel Ducaroy’s foam lounge chair for five-to-12-years old. Hamburg’s Museum für Kunst und Gewerbe features an exhibit called “Mini Furniture: Chairs for Children,” on view through the first of November, gathering more than 130 children’s chairs from two centuries.

A chair is one of the first objects that asks a child to arrange their restless body to fit adult architecture. And across two centuries, children’s chairs have been used as a means to carry out competing adult agendas: They encourage the toddler to sit properly, do things independently, or use their imaginations.

By the late 1800s, Thonet had added child-size cane seating and high chairs to its catalog. A 1931 English model in the MK&G exhibition bears the straightforward name Correction Chair. It was used to straighten posture and enforce discipline in homes and schools; a small chair meant to prepare children for the larger, meaner world.

On the other end of the spectrum we have Erich Dieckmann’s 1925 children’s ladder chair, also included at MK&G, which doubles as a set of steps for reaching higher shelves. Montessori philosophy, meanwhile, preaches the virtues of the “prepared environment”—a child-centered space designed to encourage independence. Here, low, lightweight furniture allows children to move objects and begin activities without asking an adult for help. n each case, the furniture changes scale not simply to accommodate a smaller body, but to give the child greater independence.

Designers eventually accounted for the reality that a seated child rarely stays seated for long. Charles and Ray Eames’s molded-plywood Elephant—as seen in this modular home outside Madrid—designed in 1945, could be sat on, ridden, or treated as an animal. The idea that a child’s chair can do more than offer a place to sit continues to shape new designs. When industrial designer Henry Julier developed his children’s chair for USM, the Swiss furniture maker known for its modular system of steel tubes and ball joints, he couldn’t simply shrink an existing design. He had to find child-friendly proportions using those same parts. "At that scale, sitting, climbing, leaning and playing can become pretty interchangeable,” he says. The finished chair behaves more like a stool, while its “back” doubles as a handle that makes it easier for adults to move.

For many of the adults buying them, miniature chairs are design objects as well as children’s furniture. Lora Appleton, founder of children’s design gallery Kinder Modern, has seen interest in collectible children’s furniture grow as design-conscious parents, influencers, actors, and other high-profile families embrace living stylishly with children. "Naturally, the purchasing decision is made by an adult, and many parents appreciate children’s furniture that integrates harmoniously into the overall aesthetic of the home,” says Knud Erik Hansen, CEO and third-generation owner of Carl Hansen & Søn. While children's furniture has "often been viewed as something transitional," to be discarded when the youngster grows, he says, the Children's Wishbone is designed to become an heirloom that can carry memories between generations.

A mini Jens Risom chair sits in front of custom laminate intarsia cabinetry in this San Francisco home by AD100 interiors firm The Archers.

Scaled down versions of adult designs, while beautiful, aren't always practical, Appleton says. Unlike furniture conceived around children’s needs, such as the simple bent-plywood pieces architects historically designed for schools, an adult icon made smaller can cost nearly as much as the original to produce, be outgrown quickly, or lack the stability and proportions needed for a child to use it independently. “Often, it’s just cute, but not functional,” she says. A miniature chair can succeed as an image while failing as a chair, eventually becoming, in Appleton’s experience, a catchall for clothes and miscellaneous bedroom debris.

Appleton evaluates children’s furniture according to safety, size, function, and play-ability: Can the child move it without help from a grownup? Can small fingers get caught? Will it remain useful as dexterity and interests change? She has watched children turn a miniature Panton chair on its side to support a fort, hide behind its curves, and wedge stuffed animals into its crevices. Every tiny chair begins as an adult proposal for what it should be. It may be meant to teach posture or independence, invite play, cultivate an appreciation of craft, or eventually become an heirloom. The child makes a counterproposal: Turn it sideways and build a fort. The best tiny chairs leave room for that possibility.

#977. What Are Those Black Dots on Your Microwave For?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

They're actually essential for the microwave to work properly.

For most of my adult life, I didn’t own a microwave. It wasn’t a deliberate stand against convenience; I just never got into the habit of using one. Leftovers went into a pan on the stove, frozen foods into the oven, and popcorn into my ancient air popper that looks like it came free with a 1978 station wagon. Even now that there’s a microwave in my kitchen, those are usually still my first moves.

I’ve found uses for it, though. I recently discovered that it’s surprisingly handy for caramelizing onions and making brie chips, and I’m certainly not dirtying a saucepan when I need a little melted butter for dipping artichokes. My microwave is slowly making a case for itself.

Which means I’m finally spending enough time in front of it to notice the door. More specifically, the field of tiny dots between me and whatever I’m heating. Turns out they’re actually doing one of the microwave’s most important jobs.

If you look closely, you’ll see that those dots are actually tiny holes in a metal screen inside the microwave door. That screen is there to keep microwave energy inside the oven while still letting you see your food.

The clever part is the size of those holes. They’re far too small for microwaves to pass through, so the energy stays contained inside. Light, however, can get through—which is why you can stand there watching your soup rotate without the door needing to be one solid piece of metal.

In other words, the dots aren’t really dots at all. They’re the little windows in a safety screen, and without them, keeping an eye on your favorite frozen dinner as it cooks would be considerably more difficult.

The dotted screen isn’t responsible for keeping microwaves contained all by itself. The interior is lined with metal, which keeps the microwave energy bouncing around where it can actually heat your food. The door is designed to seal that enclosure.

There are safeguards built in, too. Microwaves have safety switches that stop them from producing microwave energy when the door is opened—which is why opening the door instantly stops whatever is cooking, even if there’s still time left on the clock.

For a microwave that’s in good working order, there’s no reason to worry that those little holes are letting anything dangerous out along with the light. The FDA regulates how much microwave energy an appliance is allowed to give out, and the permitted amount is well below harmful levels.
