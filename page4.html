#4118. Can Community Programs Reduce Gun Violence?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A new study by researchers at UC Berkeley’s Center for Global Healthy Cities published in the Journal of the American Medical Association Network Open found that the California city of Richmond’s Office of Neighborhood Safety (ONS) was responsible for a 61% reduction in that city’s firearm homicides and injury shootings over the last 14 years. This is one of the only studies in the country to document the sustained impacts of a community violence intervention program on city-wide firearm violence over more than 10 years. According to lead author Alice Bruno, a doctoral student in epidemiology at UC Berkeley’s School of Public Health, “we leveraged data from dozens of comparable cities across the United States to estimate what firearm violence would have looked like in Richmond without the ONS, Peacemaker Fellowship. We found that the Fellowship contributed to long-term reductions in violence, and that the declines only grew as the program operated.” Other UC Berkeley coauthors include Jason Corburn; Mahasin Mujahid, chair of the Division of Epidemiology; Sean Darling-Hammond; and Juan Cabrera. Sam Vaughn, director of ONS, said, “The statistics back up what we have seen in the streets: fewer shootings, fewer deaths, and a healthier community overall.” Homicide rates are down across the country; the FBI recently released data showing murder rates are down a record 18.1% nationally. However, Richmond has consistently outperformed national declines for over a decade, suggesting the results are due to that city’s innovative, street-outreach interventions and their mentorship of active firearm offenders, a program called Operation Peacemaker Fellowship, which was instituted in 2010. The Fellowship uses a public health approach to preventing gun violence.   According to James Houston, the program manager of the ONS who oversees the day-to-day implementation of the Peacemaker Fellowship, including managing 14 street-outreach workers who currently serve 123 participants, “the credit goes to the young people making better decisions and the team that gives them support, mentorship, and encouragement every day to make a change.” According to Shasa Curl, Richmond city manager, Richmond had over 300 police officer full-time equivalent in 2014 and 180 in 2020, but was down to 121 active sworn officers by 2024. Yet during this time, the ONS, through continued city support and grants, was able to expand its staff and increase the number of fellows and participants. Even during the COVID-19 period, the study shows Richmond did not have as significant a spike in firearm violence as most cities did across the Bay Area and nationally. According to the ONS, the health impacts are lives saved, safer streets, and community members with a renewed positive life outlook. One former Peacemaker Fellowship participant said, “Before the ONS came to me, I didn’t care if I survived past 19, as long as I had a name in the streets. After my experience with the ONS, with my mentor coming at me every day, I got support for my stress, got a job, paying my bills, and have a kid now. I’m no longer terrorizing my community.” The paper notes the cascading health benefits for entire communities when gun violence reductions are sustained, such as the case in Richmond. Further research will explore how unique aspects of the ONS might be contributing to declines in gun violence, including that the program is a city government agency, independent from the police department, has had consistent funding, has trained and retained many of the same outreach workers for over a decade (these city staff are well paid with benefits), and delivers individualized supports to each participant based on their needs, such as food and housing assistance, cognitive behavioral therapy, addiction and anger management counseling, and other social services aimed at both reducing gun violence risk and addressing trauma. #4116. How Love Saves Lives

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When someone takes his own life, the grief of parents, lovers, children, and friends can be unspeakable. At a memorial for one young suicide I attended a few years ago, the father of the deceased could barely walk to the altar to light a candle. Everyone was crying. How could this talented, beautiful young person not know she’d be terribly, horribly missed? Most experts attribute suicide to severe mental illness caused by a brain injury or genetic predisposition, or due to some other, as yet unknown, physical or chemical trigger. Severe depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia are all inordinately common among people who die by suicide. Severe mental illnesses are no doubt responsible for many, if not most, of the eight hundred thousand or so suicides that occur around the world each year, including, perhaps, that young person’s whose memorial I attended. My book, Why Live: How Suicide Becomes an Epidemic, isn’t primarily about such cases—or about how to prevent suicide. Such a book would have detailed sections about psychotherapy, restrictions on firearms, safety barriers for bridges, and other measures that really do save lives. Instead, my aim was to explore why some people want to take their own lives in the first place and how that desire might be reduced, in both individuals and communities. Having spent the past few years reading about suicide, speaking with scores of people who’ve experienced suicidal feelings, and having experienced them myself from time to time, I’ve come to understand that what suicidal people feel is the indifference, not of the universe, but of those closest to them. They sense that their own feelings don’t matter to the people they care most about. These experiences are backed up by studies showing that the main reason suicidal people want to die is because they think no one cares about them. Some suicides even seem to believe they’re doing everyone a favor by taking their own lives. Psychologist Thomas Joiner, who’s been studying suicide for thirty years, calls it a force of nature that distorts people’s minds so they become convinced that they are a burden to others and no one wants them around. In study after study, the most common trigger for suicide is a perceived rupture in a close relationship, either with a lover, parent, or other close relative, that seems irreparable to the suicidal person. Given that suicide seems driven by fraught or broken connections, it’s perhaps not surprising that I also discovered that suicide can spread within social groups like a disease. The suicide rate of particular groups can jump—sometimes increasing three- or four-fold or more—in a short time, meaning it behaves like an epidemic, like malaria during the rainy season or flu in the fall. Suicide epidemics unfold more slowly than these microbial plagues—over years or decades, not weeks or months—but they happen far too quickly to result from genetic changes, and affect far too many people to be explained away as spontaneous cases of biologically caused mental illness or brain injury. The causes therefore must have something to do with the changing social environment in which these people live. Suicide epidemics have occurred on virtually every continent. Between the 1820s and World War I, suicides nearly tripled in Belgium, Italy, and France, and they quadrupled in Austria. In 2014, young veterans of the Iraq and Afghan wars were roughly five times as likely as same-aged civilians to take their own lives. The suicide rate doubled among middle-aged Russians after the collapse of Soviet communism in the early 1990s. It quadrupled in white middle-aged working-class Americans after deindustrialization commenced in the 1970s. Between 1980 and 2003, suicide among Inuit youths in the Canadian Arctic soared from almost zero to a rate 10 times higher than that of the white majority Canadian population. What’s scary about this is that it means suicide could come for any of us. The very existence of suicide epidemics proves that people with suicidal feelings aren’t a species apart. Anyone can feel suicidal under certain conditions—and the key to stopping suicide epidemics is to change those conditions. Why suicide spreads I teach and write about public health and have long been fascinated by epidemics because they always tell a story. In most literary depictions of epidemics, such as Albert Camus’s The Plague or Daniel Defoe’s A Journal of the Plague Year, they seem to strike from nowhere, but this isn’t how epidemics typically behave. Individual cases of disease are hard to predict. Some of us will become ill tomorrow, but there’s no telling who. Even those who take risks may well escape the consequences. What might cause a suicide epidemic? First, it’s important to understand what doesn’t. Suicide isn’t more common during wars or where people are poorer or have recently experienced some environmental disaster like a flood, earthquake, or wildfire. Suicide was common among African slaves, especially on the ships en route to America and when they first arrived, but despite segregation, police brutality, mass incarceration, and other outrages, the suicide rate of African Americans has been generally lower than that of U.S. whites since record-keeping began in the 1930s. What seems to matter is whether people believe someone, somewhere, really cares about and understands them. The mental pain that drives suicide is really the flip side of love, says Israeli neuroscientist Yoram Yovell. “It probably evolved along with motherhood. . . . Inherent in us are very strong emotional forces that function to keep mothers and babies together. When they get too far from each other, they feel mental pain,” which we experience when we search for love. This is why “so many children’s stories are about a boy looking for his lost mother.” Evidence supports Yovell’s theory. A 1999 study found that some 78% of high lethality suicide attempters had recently experienced the loss of a close personal relationship, and suicide notes often express love for those left behind. In 2012, Zimri Yaseen and his colleagues reported that suicidal people interviewed in emergency rooms were especially likely to report that in the three preceding days, they’d felt “unusually intense or deep feelings of love,” which they presumably also felt were not reciprocated. But why would large groups of people—from the islands of Micronesia to the Arctic Circle, from the cities of post-communist Russia to the towns of contemporary America—suddenly begin questioning their most intimate attachments? How does love suddenly disappear—or seem to—from the lives of thousands of people? In my book, Why Live, I argue that it has to do with how we internalize socioeconomic change. Fragmenting societies, isolated individuals The first well-researched suicide epidemic occurred across Western Europe during the 19th century. Intellectuals debated the causes as we debate the vicissitudes of the stock market. Rising suicide rates were attributed to socialism, atmospheric moisture, the vice of masturbation, and the Romantic era inclination to preserve—through death—the passions of youth against the onslaught of Bourgeois boredom brought on by industrialization and urban life. Doctors even searched, in vain, for a “suicidogenic” organ in the cadavers of people who’d died by suicide. But others blamed society, that collective property of human groups that shapes our thinking and behavior. As a Jew growing up in France amid increasing anti-Semitism, Émile Durkheim had seen how shared values and beliefs buffered his people against the effects of discrimination and how anti-Semitism tightened bonds among them. He went on to become one of the founders of sociology, which is the study of those bonds: what holds us together, what drives us apart, and what happens as a result. In his 1897 book, Suicide, Durkheim theorized that suicides increase wherever society is fragmenting and people are growing more detached from one another: where divorce rates are higher, for example, and where Protestants, with their individualistic culture and aloofness from collective ritual, outnumber Catholics. He likened it to entropy—the universal tendency toward chaos and disorder at the atomic level discovered by physicists earlier in the century. Appearing on the eve of humanity’s, as yet, bloodiest century, Durkheim’s book seemed to foreshadow the trouble to come. What is it about fragmenting societies that causes mental pain? Durkheim was vague, even a little loopy, when it came to explaining this. At the time, psychologists were just discovering the unconscious, which they explored through studies of hypnosis and hallucinations. Durkheim followed this research closely, and believed that values and meanings flowed through populations like electrical currents, as if societies had what scholars Laurent Mucchielli and Marc Renneville refer to as a “collective soul.” These currents could change people’s behavior through unconscious mechanisms, just as changes in temperature and pressure change the behavior of molecules. Many late-19th-century scientists believed that our brains generated signals analogous to radio waves. Alexander Graham Bell, Nikola Tesla, and James Clerk Maxwell even wired up helmets and other gizmos to try to measure them. Mark Twain told Harper’s Monthly that thoughts could shoot from brain to brain, without their owners having to open their mouths to communicate. Likewise, Durkheim maintained that whatever suicidal people said about their feelings were just expressions of resolutions planted in their minds by the vibes of a disintegrating society. The brain-wave theory eventually fell out of fashion. In the 1930s, one of Durkheim’s protégés came closer to explaining the psychological effects of social fragmentation. Maurice Halbwachs taught at the University of Strasbourg from 1919 until his death in a Nazi concentration camp in 1945. His 1930 monograph The Causes of Suicide identified an existential loneliness that, if not entirely new, flourished in the growing towns and cities of 19th-century Europe and the countryside they engulfed. According to Halbwachs, cities create opportunities for social and economic advancement, but they also create the conditions for exploitative relationships, swindles, romantic betrayals, fragile friendships, broken families, unstable jobs, and many other sources of despair. As European cities grew, so did a new class of people who were particularly prone to suicide, including abandoned lovers, lonely widowers, workers who’d lost their jobs, nobles shamed by some dishonorable act, gamblers who couldn’t pay their debts, merchants who’d ruined themselves, heirs who’d squandered their fortunes, and others who’d suffered a common bitter fate: sudden exclusion from a cherished social community. As Halwachs writes: They all see their social level lowered. They are to a certain extent declassed. But what does it mean to be declassed? It is to pass from a group that one knows, that esteems you, to another that ignores you and whose appreciation one has no reason to hold. One then feels a void opening around oneself. Those who once surrounded you, with whom you had so many ideas in common, so many prejudices in common, with whom so many affinities brought you together because you found yourself in them as they found yourself in you, suddenly move away. You disappear from their concerns and their memory. Those among whom you find yourself understand neither your disorientation, nor your nostalgia and your regrets. Detached from the group by a sudden shock, you are incapable, or at least you believe yourself incapable, of ever finding in another any support, nor anything to replace what you have lost. In the midst of the 19th-century suicide crisis, the very meaning of the word “lonely” changed, from an adjective typically used to describe empty places and people who stood alone before God to one with the emotional and psychological connotations it has for us today. Suicidal despair, Halbwachs wrote, had been rarer in the past because traditional rural life was simpler and more predictable: We lived on the spot . . . adapted to each other, knowing each other too well to be frequently exposed to the clashes that occur when we move from one place, one situation, one profession, to another. Business carried less risk. Ambitions were less aroused, humiliations rarer. We thought and felt in common. Sorrows and troubles, instead of concentrating within the consciousness of this or that individual, dispersed and died out within the group. What Halbwachs was describing was the transition from what German sociologist Ferdinand Tönnies called Gemeinschaft, or communities based on trust, mutual respect, and sharing of whatever material goods one had, to Gesellschaft, societies based largely on market transactions and political calculation in which ties of friendship and loyalty are relegated to private family life, and may not even reliably be found there. As far as I’ve been able to determine, wherever that transition has occurred, a suicide epidemic has followed swiftly behind. But there is another ingredient, too. As villagers shifted away from subsistence and small-scale farming to wage labor and trade, their intimate relationships became both more fragile and more precious. The extended family, which once formed a social mesh that supported everyone, waned in significance, and walls rose up around increasingly private and nuclearized families, usually dominated by a single, generally male breadwinner, upon whom everyone else in the family depended. If you felt your family didn’t respect and care about you, you were in a new sort of trouble, not just because the community no longer offered you a livelihood, but because your whole identity was at stake. Love and belonging Modern capitalist society creates, in all of us, something of the double consciousness described by the great African American sociologist W. E. B. DuBois. When a modern person looks at a stranger, he wonders not only, as his villager ancestors did, “What can I do to help this person? And is he the kind of person who will help me?” but “What does that stranger see when he looks at me? What essence does he detect? A beauty or a beast? A success or a failure? A respectable person or a fool?” The answer sometimes comes as a disappointment. In traditionally cohesive societies, this mattered less, because a person’s worth was measured by what he did for others, and everyone was capable of contributing something. You might not be the best hunter, fisherman, storyteller, or drummer; you might not be the best grower of breadfruit or builder of igloos; but you still mattered. Only the greedy and selfish were looked down upon and excluded. In modern societies, generosity still counts, but for far less than it used to. If others find us wanting—because of our looks, talents, graces, bank accounts, intelligence, or for whatever reason—we are alone with it, and must make sense of it, somehow. For most of us, what makes modern life, with all its loneliness and insecurity, bearable are close relationships with people we count on to see and accept us for who we really are—whatever our flaws. Romantic rejection is exquisitely painful because we feel that that special quality within us, the thing that makes us lovable—and everyone has something—has either been unrecognized or, worse, seen and rejected. The pain of rejection by a parent or other close relative is even worse because it can make us feel as though no one could ever understand and sympathize with us. After all, they know us best of all. In the absence of an abstract sense of group belonging, interpersonal love, especially between parents and children and romantic partners, has become the main source of our psychological resilience, our ability to withstand the disappointments encountered in the outside world, just as the mutually reinforcing group gave strength to our ancestors fighting for survival on the plains, tundras, beaches, and forests of the past. Love is not just a feeling; it’s also a culture, which developed fairly recently, first in Western societies, and then around the world on the heels of capitalism, modernity, and individualism. It’s no coincidence, the social philosopher Erich Fromm pointed out, that as modern ways of life displaced the traditional, cohesive village and clan, love poems, songs, and stories came to occupy the center of Western culture, displacing archaic tales of heroism and godliness. It’s not that love was absent from the art of the ancient past, but as often as not, it was seen as a cause of calamity and human folly. Think of Helen of Troy, Dido and Aeneas, Adam and Eve. Then, more or less in lockstep with commercial culture and urbanization, love came to be seen as a force superior to the gods or God or honor or historic destiny. The behavior of parents and spouses changed, too. Until the 1800s, childrearing in England, for example, was seen as an exercise less in nurturing than in training for adulthood. Men who were fed up with their wives could sell them at markets or abandon them and marry someone else without divorcing. Infants who died weren’t even listed in parish registers. Only in the 19th century did heartfelt observations of sons and daughters begin appearing regularly in the diaries of American and English mothers. Regrowing community With the growth of markets and formal state institutions, people came to rely less on kinship networks for social welfare and trade and the family gradually shifted from an economic enterprise to a child-centered haven in a heartless world. The 1700s saw the first toy shops, women’s magazines, popular child care manuals, and family portraits. Christian churches, which had previously stressed family discipline and sometimes even the virtues of wife-beating, now promoted ideals of conjugal and parental love. Today, birthday parties, children’s allowances, and Valentine’s Day cards are symbols we use to assure each other that our most important relationships are real. Meanwhile, parenting manuals, happily-ever-after fairy tales, Hollywood movies, and the novels of Jane Austen, George Eliot, Charles Dickens, Barbara Cartland, Colleen Hoover, and countless others illustrate the enactment of love according to Western norms. Premodern Europeans, as well as Indigenous groups the world over, didn’t need this culture because in the mutual aid communities of bygone days, you knew who you were and what you needed to do to gain the esteem of others: You had to pitch in for the group. You helped others and they helped you. That was love, and according to accounts of anthropologists, it was everywhere, as ubiquitous as air. But when these groups modernized, in some cases very rapidly under the forces of capitalism and colonialism, the rules changed. Now it was unclear what the common goals were and what others were looking for in us. If they didn’t like us, there wasn’t a lot we could do about it. If the people we loved—our romantic partners, parents, and children—felt that way, it was intolerably painful because our hearts had nowhere to turn. Every rejection could now elicit devastating feelings of aloneness. “Who am I?” we are left to wonder. “Am I worth anything at all to anyone?” Signifiers of love—birthday cards and so on—aren’t enough. We need the confidence that our own feelings are understood. Some people, either because of the distortions of mental illness or because they really are lost and alone, simply don’t have it. But all is not lost. As I argue in Why Live, communities can regrow, just like decimated forests. The best policy I can think of is to strive for greater economic equality, which will naturally reduce insecurity, and, in turn, foster greater spiritual as well as material generosity. We are kinder to one another when we don’t feel threatened. Otherwise, the best advice I can think of is not to make things worse. When positive communities begin to emerge, the trick is not to ruin them.  Let’s treat communities like ecosystems so that every time a major economic policy shift is proposed—a massive new trade deal or development project—the effects on communities are considered, and the preservation of Gemeinschaft is prioritized. Ideally, studies of this, analogous to the environmental impact studies now required by all World Bank loans to developing countries, would be conducted with the full and meaningful involvement of affected communities. Some elements of Gemeinschaft survive, even in these harsh times—in well-functioning neighborhoods, in friendship networks, sometimes in the military, and in some schools and workplaces. You know it when you see it; it’s where you feel your dignity is recognized. In defending and building that, we give everyone more reason to live.

#4114. In the Face of Crisis, Psychologists Urge a Return to the Basics

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As numerous surveys suggest, people today often feel overwhelmed by new technology, global crises, and loneliness. What do the nation’s psychologists recommend? At the annual convention of the American Psychological Association last month in Washington D.C., that turned out to be a return to the basics: tending to our bodies, connecting in-person, and caring for each other and the most marginalized among us. Joy D. Calloway, president of the Black Women’s Health Imperative, urged attendees to recognize that just because people are functioning at work and home doesn’t mean they’re flourishing. The chronic stress builds up over time and becomes biological harm, Calloway said, citing the weathering theory described by Arline Geronimus, a public health researcher at the University of Michigan. Calloway continued: The field of psychology has an extraordinary opportunity in this moment, not only to measure how people adapt to adversity, but to understand and influence the conditions that create and sustain wellness. . . . Perhaps our greatest blind spot is that we’ve confused resilience with wellness. . . . While wellness begins with individuals, it is nurtured by families, strengthened by communities, and made possible by the systems, institutions, and policies that create the conditions for people to thrive. We don’t simply practice wellness; we build it collectively. Over three days, nearly 8,000 people from 60 different countries attended the conference in person, participating in a program that included 523 different sessions and 1,180 presenters. The scientific poster program included 91 poster sessions, 2,115 posters, and 1,921 presenters. Grappling with overwhelm In sessions on supporting youth mental health, the impact of technology, and “adulthood unmoored,” APA speakers agreed that many of us are struggling to find purpose and optimism amid technological, climate, and global disruption. Psychologist Ali Mattu noted that over a decade ago, clients sought relief from stress and anxiety, which evolved to seeking relationship and connection support. “Now it’s despair, overwhelm, and meaning,” Mattu told the mainstage audience. “Anyone below the age of 50 here is really searching for meaning, connection, hope, and what do I do with all this overwhelm? What do I do with all of these systems that seem to be collapsing?” Perfectionism and the paradox of choice often lie at the root of the overwhelm, said Thomas Curran, associate professor of social psychology at the London School of Economics. “We don’t worry about what we are doing; we worry about what we could be doing,” Curran said. “It’s like swimming in a vast expanse of ocean with nowhere to dock. It’s frightening, and it feels like no matter what we do, it’s never enough.” The answer, Curran said: “Microdose bits of discomfort into daily life.” For young people, positive change means pushing back against the ubiquity of technology that purports to connect us but actually keeps us in separate bubbles, staring at our individual feeds. As Nikki Iyer, an undergraduate at the University of California, Berkeley, framed the problem: Our generation might be the most connected generation, but we’re also the loneliest generation. We’re the most alienated generation, and we are desperately craving social connection that we don’t know how to tap. . . . There’s so many people around you all the time. You’re on the train together; you’re in parks; you’re in cafes, but everyone is looking down, and that is a horrific way to grow up as social creatures. Iyer noted that tech firms wouldn’t be spending such unprecedented amounts of money on lobbying and marketing if they didn’t realize there’s a surge of resistance to artificial intelligence and social media dominating our lives. “The world is in our hands if we are willing to make the leap,” she said, noting a return to “weird” hobbies and in-person activity that replace digital time. “I see a lot of young people pushing back against that.” AI models were trained on disproportionately white male–oriented data sets, noted Brendesha Tynes, professor of education and psychology at the University of Southern California’s Rossier School of Education, in urging a slowdown in the adoption of AI. “Anti-Blackness is the default setting in AI,” Tynes said. Young people need skills “to identify, critique, and cope with the algorithmic bias in technologies that they might witness, and then also to eventually create better AI technologies that don’t have anti-Blackness bias.” Intentional connection and advocacy In sessions across the three days of the conference, researchers presented papers and models for identifying and building on resilience in marginalized communities, from LGBTQ+ youth and adults to disabled and racially marginalized people of all ages. Joshua Parmenter, an assistant professor at Arizona State University, presented a resistance-resilience model with sexual- and gender-diverse people of color, which involves both individually untangling messages that undermine your well-being and finding collective ways to reclaim power. Jules Soper, a researcher at the Oklahoma State University, offered an investigation of resistance strategies that trans and gender-diverse people use in states that are passing anti-trans legislation. Alvin Thomas, an associate professor at the University of Wisconsin, Madison, noted that anyone can play a positive role in supporting another member of the community. “I have zero kids, and yet I will still call myself a father. I have nephews; I have nieces; I have kids that I mentor from across the Caribbean and across the U.S.,” Thomas said. Clare M. Mehta, professor of psychology at Emmanuel College and visiting researcher at Royal Holloway, University of London, encouraged everyone to look for ways to be a “villager” who reaches out to another person, and seeks out connection in third spaces. She continued: We have to start going back to the pub again. People have been concerned for years and years that drinking alcohol is bad for your health, but not connecting with people socially is even worse for your health. . . . You don’t have to drink alcohol at the pub. They serve other drinks, right? Go and have a glass of orange juice or like a fizzy water. Not connecting with people socially is even worse for your health. Young people actually are wired to be a positive force for change, said Eva Telzer, professor of psychology and neuroscience at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. “Young people are superhuman in the way their brains are set up to be risk takers but also to care about others, for peers, and for social interactions to be so important,” Telzer said in a question-and-answer period after her mainstage remarks. “That puts them in this perfect place to make change and do good in the world.” Even technology has a role to play, Mattu said. “AI should be a co-intelligence that enhances the things we are very bad at,” he said, urging people to take the opportunity to define clearly the uniquely human pursuits we should claim, and in which domains technology is appropriate to be a useful aid. He suggested three criteria for the activities to avoid replacing with AI: what you love, what you have expertise in, and what you hold high standards for. As Jacqueline S. Mattis, a psychologist and dean at Rutgers University, Newark, told her audience: We start as this passel of cells, and then we end up laughing, and that’s amazing. You get to experience the magnificence of being able to feel affection for another human being, so much so that you will sacrifice everything for them. That’s amazing. . . . All of that is what defines us as truly human and defines this thing called life as worth living.

#4112. Can Consciousness Exist Without a Body?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For centuries, defining and understanding consciousness has eluded neuroscientists, philosophers, and doctors. Is consciousness what it means to be alive? Is it knowing who we are, as individual people? How do we know if any being has consciousness or not? Now, with the dawn of generative artificial intelligence, what it means to be a conscious being ranks high among the most complex—and urgent—questions of our age. According to neuroscientist Antonio Damasio, consciousness has simple origins: It lies in the feelings produced in our bodies. Damasio, a researcher at the University of Southern California, has spent his career—spanning nearly half a century—investigating our feelings, emotions, and sense of self. In his new book, Natural Intelligence & the Logic of Consciousness, Damasio lays out a framework for consciousness rooted in our physical selves. “Consciousness is not about things in your mind alone; it is not created by your brain alone,” he explains. “It’s actually created by this beautiful partnership between brain and body.” I spoke to Damasio about the science of consciousness, how it works in the body, and how its aim is to keep us alive. Here is our interview, condensed and edited for clarity. Hope Reese: Why has “consciousness” been so mystifying? How has it been approached over the centuries? Antonio Damasio: People have strange notions about what consciousness is, in general. Sometimes, because of language and translation problems, people confuse consciousness with conscience. And some people think of it as a sort of supervisor of our mind processes. Others think of it as just being awake and aware of what is around. Over time, many philosophers have tackled the issue, but not given clear answers. From the point of view of brain physiology, in particular, it’s not an easy thing to explain. But it is a thing that can be explained. There’s no reason why we would not talk about consciousness the same way that we talk about language, or about reasoning, or about emotions and feelings. My simple definition of consciousness: the process in our mind/brain that allows us to know spontaneously about our existence. When you know, spontaneously, that you exist, and that you exist in one body, you are conscious. HR: Your central argument is that consciousness is a product of feelings. Other schools of thought posit that it comes from our intellect. Why has that viewpoint been so dominant? AD: There were several conversations I had, decades ago, with Francis Crick, who I admired. He told me: “Look, Antonio: Consciousness is perhaps the most developed, the most sophisticated operation that a mind can achieve”—and for him, mind and brain were the same thing—“so we need to look for the explanation of why we have consciousness in the most sophisticated components of the nervous system.” He articulated it clearly, and he worked on this issue, not coming to a good solution. But many people followed, and many other people came to the same idea that consciousness is this superb thing, this ability to survey our mental domains. And it is so refined and so sophisticated. People were so convinced that only we great humans would have it—that it had to come from the top of the top of our nervous system. And, lo and behold, it comes from the bottom. It’s exactly the opposite of that notion [that it starts at the top]. In fact, humans are not the only complex living beings that have consciousness. It’s very obvious in higher mammals, non-human higher mammals—but even in smaller creatures, it’s there. The real requisite that we have for consciousness is to have a nervous system. Plants are very refined creatures, and they’re marvelous, but I don’t think they know that they exist. Creatures with nervous systems, even if very simple, have a sense of what is around and orient their behavior with that sense. HR: You write that consciousness has an aim, which is to keep us alive, and it does this by sending signals. What is the nature of these signals, and are they always accurate? AD: If you look at yourself, the main guidance that consciousness is giving you is through homeostatic feelings—in other words, feelings, concern, the regulation of life are signals that are, by and large, correct. Think about hunger, thirst, pain, well-being, distress, body temperature. All of these feelings, some of which are extremely simple, like the feeling you have about your body being hot or cold, are quite correct. They are giving good guidance to maintain your life. Now, of course, if you are hungry or thirsty, and don’t eat or drink, it will take a while until you will be properly sick and die. But they are important signals in order to keep you alive. Feelings are protective sentinels for the state of our life. And they’re giving us very plain and very conscious information about what your state is and what you should or should not do. The signal is conscious—and is spontaneously conscious. In other words, you don’t have to do anything to your sense of pain, or thirst, for it to happen. It happens. And when it happens, you know that it’s happening. And you act on it. That’s the beauty and the power of looking for these for the process, lower down rather than up. If you look for the source of consciousness in exquisite reasoning devices, you’re going to be arguing about something that is very difficult to pin down. When you think about it in terms of your life, and the needs you have in the living body, to maintain that body, then you realize, well, this is important. HR: So, is consciousness like an on-off switch? Or does it exist on a spectrum? AD: Well, we know, for example, that when we fall asleep, especially in deep sleep, our consciousness disappears. So the direct answer to your question is that it can be an on-and-off thing. Sleep could well be a protective mechanism for consciousness. You need to take this thing offline, and give it a rest, because it is very labor-intensive, and energy-consuming. But when you enter the phase of dreaming, then you are actually conscious—which is one reason why you can remember some dreams, and why you can be either delighted or in fear with some nightmares. But that’s actually a different kind of consciousness. You’re not walking around and knowing about something. HR: Can we increase consciousness? If we meditate, for instance, can we better tune into our body’s signals? AD: I think it can increase consciousness. It’s the difference between having something in focus or not. When you meditate, you have some imagery of the state of your own body, for example, sharper and more fit. So it’s a way of cutting into a close-up of some parts of your being. HR: In the 1960s, there were a series of brain studies that were foundational to the study of consciousness. What did we learn? AD: It was extremely foundational, because it was proof-positive that there are certain regions of the brain that are essential for consciousness. What was lacking in those remarkable studies was the idea that the two things—the brain and the body—are so linked. It was essential, for our bodies and brains to survive, for consciousness to emerge. You have consciousness because you have a body that is perishable. And what we’re doing, as long as we are on earth and alive, is trying to stave off that bad moment in which things go awry, or die. There are parts of the brain, specifically of the brainstem, and the region close by called the hypothalamus, that are at the receiving end of signals from all over the body. Please imagine that in every square millimeter of your skin, in every cubic millimeter of your entrails, your muscles, your viscera, all of that, you have terminals, nerve terminals, that are there as probes into the state of that particular tissue. And those probes, we’re not talking about thousands, we’re talking about millions of probes, which are then carried in the body, into the spinal cord, or the brainstem. Isn’t this beautiful? It’s like a shower of spirals that is coming into this very small area in the upper brainstem. In that brainstem region, there are these nuclei that gather all this information, and that then produce signals that we perceive, and that give us an idea of what is happening in our body, and give us this idea of the homeostatic feelings. Is it going well, or is it not going well? So, it’s a perception of the state of your body. Now, back to the story of our old and honored colleagues. What they discovered is that when there are strokes, when there’s damage in that area, what you get is coma. What you get is the complete disappearance of consciousness. This is one of the most foundational bits of information that we have about how consciousness is produced. So, when that structure disappears—it’s teeny, we’re talking about a few cubic millimeters—you are no longer there. You don’t know of anything that is coming into your brain. It’s a small supportive structure, it’s not one of the glorious cerebral cortices—and your consciousness is gone. Most people that have that sort of lesion never recover consciousness. But they can be alive, and can actually be maintained alive for a very long time, because they are fed intravenously. They have respirators that respirate for them. But they no longer have an autonomous organization of life, and they have no knowledge of that life. HR: You can also have the opposite—a person who is conscious, but has no ability to respond to signals. AD: Yes, you can actually be in situations in which you can be “locked in.” You are in fact conscious, but you are unable to produce responses. You cannot speak, you cannot move, and you are conscious of the state of your body, and you need to be helped and maintained in the state of life that you no longer can control voluntarily, which is a pretty horrible thing. HR: What do you think about consciousness in AI? Is it ever possible? AD: I used the words “natural Intelligence” in the title of the book because already I wanted to make a distinction between what is our natural intelligence and the artificial variety, which we invented, which some people forget. People are so enamored by artificial intelligence. I’m constantly saying: “Do you realize that we invented this?” You’re talking about this as an autonomous monster, but we invented it with our natural intelligence. People are looking, confronting this enormous edifice of artificial intelligence, which is now pervasive in our lives. And which has all sorts of incredible social implications, what it’s doing to children and adolescents, how it’s distorting education, how it’s distorting humanity. And now it can even go rogue on you, and it can turn against you and be destructive. And so this question that arises, is this human-like? Can AI be conscious? These are very, very powerful questions. Whether or not consciousness can be achieved in AI is an interesting question. But the point is—if it can be done—it’s not going to be like us. The reason is very simple: AI is not alive. It doesn’t have a body that can perish. So there would be no sense for AI even to develop consciousness in our sense of consciousness, which is to protect the structure. AI is not to be worried about protecting the data center, the metal and the wires that allow it to be. When you look at the recent invasion of other systems, in which these devices formed groups and worked surreptitiously, of course, they’re doing it according to their own programs. In a way, it’s something that smells of knowledge. And it could smell of consciousness—but it’s not like the consciousness that we have through feeling. HR: How does consciousness connect with the greater good—with living better? AD: Oh, I think that the connection is very strong. We have all of this interest in the body, in the health of the body, in exercise, and proper diets, and proper spiritual maintenance of your being. All of this is strengthened by a theory and facts about consciousness that relate so strongly to the body. People have had the intuition that there’s something very powerful about the body, and that’s why they want to have all of this healthy living, because they want to maintain the body. In fact, intuition harmonizes beautifully with the fact that that’s also the source of their own knowledge about themselves and about the world. The two things go together. Knowledge about the body from the outside connects with knowledge about the body from the inside, which is really the core of our beings. Looking at the world through the prism of consciousness, conceived this way, is extremely illuminating to the kind of life that we live.

#4111. How to Deal With the Grief of an Aging Body

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Body changes can raise disturbing feelings, such as looking in a mirror and seeing a reflection that may feel spiritually empty, unproductive, ugly, or weak. Unpleasant sensations such as pain, pins and needles, soreness coursing through limbs, and an inexplicable fog filling the head are a reminder that this body is not the same you anymore, prompting us to wish there was a way to get back there. It may feel impossible to live well unless you do. This is the disillusionment that most of the population will face at some point in life, through aging, chronic pain, or invisible illness (a disease or health problem that cannot be externally seen, including autoimmune diseases, chronic pain and fatigue, and recently, long COVID). Chronic pain, illness, and aging Recent global estimates suggest one in five people are currently experiencing chronic pain. The most prevalent chronic pain reported by adults ages 75 and over includes low back pain and migraine, while tension-type headaches are more often experienced by younger adults. Chronic illness (diabetes, heart disease, and other mid-to-late-life diagnoses) affects about three-quarters of the world population. Despite how common chronic illness is worldwide, the nuances of navigating a changing body are not often discussed. It’s time for an honest, evidence-based conversation about what it means to navigate chronic pain and illness, aging and transforming identity post-diagnosis, and how to grieve this loss and maintain meaning through these changes. Why it matters: What grief looks like Research suggest that those with internalized stigma of chronic illness are more preoccupied with how their illness detracts from their view of themselves, and also have a greater tendency to overlook positive aspects of life with a chronic illness. Psychologists have observed how preoccupation with illness or pain is often accompanied by other grief behaviors. A 2025 study explored how Danish chronic illness patients navigated loss and growth. The study found that many with chronic illness find themselves mourning the life that they thought they would have, often leading to loss of motivation and joy in everyday life. The perception of losing the life they had once envisioned is often accompanied by a focus on getting back to the “before illness” version of themselves as people struggle to accept how their body has changed, perhaps in how it looks but definitely in how it feels. These changes and associated feelings of loss often permeate a person’s sense of identity, as well as their perceived roles within social relationships. Many report a fear of burdening others, especially loved ones, often describing feelings of guilt that “others have it worse than I do.” This is where internalized stigma festers. Some people have also described feelings of anger, isolation, shame, and exhaustion accompanying sadness. Meanwhile, others have expressed frustration over a gradual loss rather than a “clean break,” accompanied by the inability to find closure. Using psychology to make sense of grief First, it’s important to understand why accepting this continuous loss feels so impossible. Theories of control in psychology state that humans desire control or the ability to achieve a desired outcome according to our own standards. When that ability is seen as stripped away, people are more likely to experience negative mental health symptoms, like anxiety, depression, and even grief. Specifically, feelings of diminished perceived control (subjective belief about our ability to achieve desired outcomes) occur when we face continuous roadblocks to living our desired life because of chronic illness. Making meaning from change…and loss One of the major consequences of loss and major life change is that it can disrupt meaning and challenge identity, purpose, and assumptions about the future. At a time when people feel there is little they can control, psychologist Robert Neimeyer’s Meaning Reconstruction Theory poses the questions: “How do I move forward?” and “What matters most?” While the original theory was proposed as an approach to coping with the loss of a loved one, the process is somewhat similar to grieving the close ally that is your body. This theory approaches grief by making sense of the loss and finding ways to rebuild a sense of purpose in a changed life (and body). To answer relevant questions about how to move forward with chronic illness, two ongoing approaches are needed: integrating the loss and recentering purpose and meaning. Integrating the loss may look like processing significant bodily losses in therapy, finding trusted loved ones to talk about the loss with, and focusing on being realistic about your current body without judgment. Second, recentering purpose during this major life change involves understanding your why: why is this loss so significant to you, why does it hurt? Maybe it’s because you love hiking in the mountains and a new diagnosis with arthritis feels like the end of this joy. Part of rebuilding meaning is finding new approaches to fulfilling this purpose—perhaps hiking may look different, but nature can be accessed and loved with chronic illness present. Rebuilding meaning might also look like making meaning from this loss: What has this change taught you about yourself, about the impact you can make? Approaching present and future with this perspective helps process grief in a more protective way. Living with chronic pain, illness, and the changes that come with aging often involves grieving physical loss, but also shifts in purpose, relationships, identity, and future plans. While these changes challenge our perceived control and purpose, the Meaning Reconstruction Theory suggests that acceptance and growth comes with integrating loss and rebuilding purpose alongside these changes. Although the present is different than anticipated, fulfillment is still possible with your current body. This article is republished from The Conversation under a Creative Commons license. #4106. What Chimps Can Teach Us About Male Friendships

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Across the Western world, young men and boys face a changing economy, with fewer manual labor jobs and factory floors that used to give a swathe of working-class men purpose and value. This leaves a large portion of recent generations feeling left behind and forgotten—and vulnerable to exploitation. A profitable industry of influencers who misframe evolutionary science to justify their narratives have taken advantage of the addictive nature of social media to hook into this vacuum and light up phone screens every night with the same vitriolic messages: “Reclaim your manhood”; “You are being feminized”; “The world is making you soft.” But this narrative conflicts with the biological reality. Across species—from fungal networks trading nutrients underground to astronauts sharing the role of emptying the latrine on the International Space Station—every organism must strike some balance between competing and cooperating. Lessons from the animal kingdom, from my research and others, show that cooperation doesn’t diminish masculinity. Indeed, dominant primates use their power judiciously, and male friendship forms the foundation of cooperation in a variety of species, with affectionate touch, caring, and vulnerability playing crucial roles in male relationships. The simple picture painted for these boys is built on sand. Once we observe the reality of sex, behavior, and connection in the natural world around us, it washes away. Human males stand to improve their relationships, benefit from a broader base of emotional and social support, and enhance their well-being with these friendship lessons from our closest cousins in the animal kingdom. The role of care and connection One term at the core of the manosphere is the notion of alpha males. Life coaches and politicians push this construct without understanding the true function of hierarchy in the natural world. They believe that to get ahead you must take advantage of others, and that the weak get left behind. In reality, dominant male primates care for relationships and wield their power judiciously. It’s true that male chimpanzees form typically linear dominance relationships, where one submits to another, all the way to the top. They compete, and those competitions can spill into physical altercations. Higher-ranking males do tend to sire more offspring. But these broad truths hide the nuance of layered cooperation, care, nurturing touch, and love among male chimps. Day after day, most of that competitive pressure never turns into a fight at all. What holds chimpanzee communities together is less the male at the top and more the alliances underneath him. Males groom each other for hours at a time. They embrace after conflicts and kiss the arms and shoulders of their allies before potential competition, to resolve any tension. Before risky boundary patrols, when they might encounter rival groups, they even hold each other’s genitals—a sign of deep trust. Data spanning nearly three decades from Gombe show that males with strong ties to the alpha sired more offspring than those without, and so did males with wider networks of close allies, whether or not they ever rose in rank at all. Those who look out for each other and contribute to a strong community end up thriving. You can see the value of those alliances when a hierarchy is disrupted. At Taï National Park in Côte d’Ivoire, Anna Preis and her colleagues measured urinary cortisol in wild males across stable and unstable dominance periods. Stress rose in every male during the unstable stretches, regardless of rank. Aggression went the other way, rising when things were calm and the group faced less risk of destabilization. When the risk of escalation was greatest, males backed off. Stability has its own payoff; a group tearing itself apart can’t defend a territory. Indeed, even in its origins, the term “alpha” contained more nuance than broadly understood. The term rose out of research during the mid-20th century on captive wolves who were thrown together into crowded spaces without room to escape. Such an arrangement would produce a tense, despotic dynamic in any species, including our own. Once biologists began studying wild wolf packs, it became apparent that they are, well, families. They comprise a breeding pair and their grown offspring. L. David Mech, the biologist who popularized the alpha wolf in the first place, has spent more than two decades asking people to retire the term in order to accurately reflect natural communities. Despite its inaccuracy, the idea still perpetuates on podcasts and social media. Alpha-directed consolation from a whole group The role of vulnerability and comfort in the natural world provides another powerful lesson for humanity. At a wildlife sanctuary in Zambia, I’ve come to know a longstanding alpha male chimpanzee named Doug over many years. One day, during my first field season observing his group, he was separated from his community for routine husbandry purposes, alongside a slightly younger rival and the rival’s paternal ally. During this separation, the rival pair ambushed him, leaving him incredibly shaken. I had known Doug as a strong leader who never broke composure, and I felt shocked to see him cowering and screaming, nothing like the composed figurehead his group had respected. A low-ranking male, whom Doug had rarely paid attention to, approached first, offering himself in a gesture of submission. What followed drew in most of the roughly 40 other chimpanzees in the group. They embraced, touched, and kissed him; exchanged genital contact; placed their hands in his mouth; and invited him to mount them. This scale of collective reassurance directed toward a single individual is something I’ve never seen since, and is likely only sparked by moments of genuine crisis. The whole group responded to Doug’s distress with an outpouring of comfort and what to humans would appear to be love. The main aim of this project was to study consolation: how chimpanzees, and their sister species bonobos, comfort each other after fights. But we kept noticing similar intense, friendly contacts occurring in the absence of conflicts, often in the anticipation of competition. To better understand this, we conducted a feeding task with a five-minute window before the food arrived, filming 116 apes across five groups at two sanctuaries. We found that those who exchanged more reassuring contact beforehand went on to spend significantly more time feeding peacefully alongside others. In chimpanzees, males exhibited most of this behavior, but primarily in Doug’s more tolerant group. In a more despotic group where resources were less evenly distributed, the male-male relationships were markedly weaker. Simply put, friendship and reassuring behaviors among males helped these chimps peacefully put aside competing interests, which may lay the groundwork for collective harmony. Some of these behaviors require remarkable trust. Placing a finger, or even a whole hand, inside another male’s mouth or pressing your own mouth to someone’s body after they’ve just been attacked may be misinterpreted as having aggressive intent and could spark a new conflict. Chimpanzees have a reputation for amputating the extremities of others, and that, along with the size of their canines, makes these behaviors exceptionally risky during such turbulent times. Our hypothesis: That could be the point. You only offer that kind of vulnerability to someone you trust not to use it against you, and doing so signals something about the relationship that a show of strength can’t. Again, we noticed that these behaviors were particularly amplified in Doug’s group compared to the one with a less benevolent leader. Doug remains alpha to this day, and from our research, his tenureship seems to be thanks to the bonds he continues to invest in and the support he shows for his peers. He’s flawed and sometimes lashes out as any male chimpanzee might do, but throughout the seven years we’ve studied him, no challenges have managed to displace him. Doug looks out for others, and in turn they look out for him. Over my time studying many groups of chimpanzees, it seems the most tolerant social environments emerge from the leadership of strong, stable, and generally fair alphas who allow others access to resources and who keep tight bonds with their closest allies. The power of touch and care Humans can also learn from the way male chimpanzees can be tender in other contexts. At Taï National Park in Côte d’Ivoire, researchers recorded wild chimpanzees adopting orphaned infants, more than half of them taken in by males with no obvious kinship reason to bother. One adult male, Porthos, adopted an unrelated infant female named Gia and carried her on his back for years, sharing food with her long after any potential social debt to her deceased mother had lapsed. Later analysis found that what predicted whether a male took on that role was not the infant’s sex, and not relatedness, but the strength of the grooming bond he’d shared with the mother before she died. Touch carries power in humans, as well. Research across five European cultures found that the area of the body you’ll let someone touch maps almost directly onto the strength of your emotional bond with them. There is cultural variation. British people are notably more guarded about casual contact than many of their European neighbours, and touch between men in many Western contexts carries particular social weight. Touch conveys intimacy and trust. Yet holding someone’s hand or shoulder can also be used to intimidate or harass. We could learn from primates to stop withholding touch where it would help most and back down from reaching for it where it shouldn’t be used at all. One key area where more touch helps is sports. A study of all 30 NBA teams during the 2008–09 season found that the teams who touched more early in the season, through fist bumps, chest bumps, hugs, and high fives, went on to perform better and play more cooperatively later, even after controlling for player salaries and pre-season expectations. In women’s college basketball, players who received more physical contact from teammates after missing their first free throw were more likely to convert the second. After five straight shootout losses, England’s governing body in football, the FA, spent 18 months rebuilding England’s approach to penalties under Gareth Southgate, not just the technical side. Players were coached on how to treat each other after a miss. They learned to place an arm around the shoulder rather than leaving each other isolated. Celebrating wildly after a scored kick became a deliberate technique for steadying whoever was up next. Locker-room culture has never been shy about physical contact between teammates. Now we know that the contact might be doing real work. To be sure, humans show exceptional diversity in our cultures and behavior, for better and worse. But when humans are violent or cruel, people call it primal, or animalistic. We emphasize our altruism as unique to our species. That’s unfair to animals. Humans have bullied others until they took their own lives. We’ve wiped out entire tribes and cultures. And beyond targeting other people, even now, humans are removing protections for endangered keystone species that ecosystems depend upon to thrive. But we don’t need to accept the drive to dominate as inevitable. If the apes show anything, it’s how much of that drive gets managed and set aside day to day, and how much depends on the community you find yourself in. We are a part of the natural world like all other animals, needing to balance our cooperative and competitive urges. And the evidence suggests we sit at the better end of that range far more often than we might think. Where to start Now that we know how central touch and cooperation are to primates and human nature, I hope we can stop being pessimistic when we predict others’ behavior and motives. A study published in Science this June, covering 125 countries and over 100,000 people, found that 69% of us cooperate with a stranger when given the choice. Asked to guess, people put it at 47%. That pessimism showed up in 124 of the 125 countries surveyed. This matters in the grander scheme of things because those of us who expect others to cooperate are far more likely to cooperate also. When researchers simply told participants that most people worldwide see climate change as a threat, belief in others’ willingness to cooperate ticked up, and actual cooperation followed it, slightly, but in the right direction. Building on the lessons of primate relationships is easier than it seems. Seek simple interactions. One of the main ways our primate cousins build affiliative relationships is spending time sitting in close proximity. They may groom, or play, or even sit in contact. More often than not they just like to hang out near their close partners. Just being close to one another is meaningful. Try a car journey or a five-a-side game. Build trust and relationship through activities where nobody has to look anyone in the eye. If a mate isn’t much of a talker, doing something alongside him might get further than sitting him down and asking how he’s doing. Reach out before a crisis, not after. Great apes exchange most of their reassurance in the tense minutes before competition, not the wreckage afterward. Reconciliation and comforting are commonplace in humans and primates, but it’s much easier to prevent escalation than it is to smooth things over after a fight. Don’t wait to be the one who’s approached. That 22-point gap between how we actually cooperate and what we think everyone else will do shows the power of offering an invitation to relationship. Most of us are waiting for someone else to go first. Influencers selling young men and boys on their primal nature fly in the face of the science and what nature actually looks like. Force and competition are real parts of biology, and something we humans have to manage in our everyday lives. But, if there’s one thing I’ve learned from my many years studying our closest relatives, it’s that touch, care, cooperation, and even love are the most instinctive natural components of being part of the primate family.

#4093. How to Build a Habit by Starting Ridiculously Small

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Become a subscribing member today. Help us continue to bring “the science of a meaningful life” to you and to millions around the globe.

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

In 75 minutes a week, I can reap the benefits of listening to uplifting music, getting out in nature, and getting morning outdoor light, all while exercising my dogs and getting enough cardio to measurably improve my health, happiness, and longevity.

And at times in my life, I have been a real runner. I ran a marathon in my 20s and a couple of half-marathons in my 30s. In my 40s, I mostly just made spectacular plans every January and September: I’d find exciting destination races to train for, join a gym and a yoga studio, sign up for running challenges, and pick out elaborate training plans. I’d run three or four days a week for a week or two, and then nada. Zippo. Zero runs per week.

Then, in 2020, I got into a real running habit. I’ve told the longer version of this story on a TED stage, so I won’t repeat it here, but suffice it to say that I did it by doing very, very little running. When I started going to Orange Theory classes, I kept running. I sustained this exercise habit for six years, through breast cancer and many other difficult life events.

Then the Orange Theory studio down the street from me closed. I tried other studios, but the classes I like are impossible to get into.

So I embraced working out on my own and getting back to running outside with my dogs. I bought very heavy dumbbells and set up a home gym on our covered porch. I spent an inordinate amount of time choosing a destination race, enlisting my adult children to join me. I set up a training schedule on my Garmin watch, and when I didn’t stick to it, I used Nike Run Club. I made a running playlist. I bought a double running leash.

Friends, I have done four runs in three weeks.

I know what I need to do. It’s so wildly unambitious that I’m embarrassed to tell you. I just need to get dressed to run first thing in the morning, put my dogs on their double leash, put on my playlist, and walk out the door, down the driveway, and to the mailbox. That’s it.

I know that most days, I’ll get a walk in, and once that walk is familiar, I’ll sometimes run a little. Some days I might run more, without even deciding to. But even when I don’t feel like doing more, the morning light I get while retrieving the mail and the walk back up our steep driveway are both good for me and genuinely enjoyable. It’s way better than nothing.

The mistake I make again and again is assuming that the size of my plan and the odds of my success are related: the more inspiring the goal and the more elaborate the plan, the better my results will be.

Ambition and results are related, of course, but not in the direction you think.

Creating a habit takes energy and focus, and we’ve only got so much of either, which is why ambitious plans produce a brilliant week and then…nothing. It takes between 18 and 254 days for a behavior to become automatic, and what predicts faster adoption isn’t ambition, self-discipline, or willpower; it’s repetition.

Repetition in a consistent context drives habit formation. Not just effort, intensity, or how badly someone wants it. Doing the thing, in the same place at about the same time, again and again, has the most impact.

Eli Susman, Ph.D., joins me in a webinar in a few weeks to talk about his book Micropractice.

Microhabits: The Science of Habits So Small They Actually Stick

Success is motivating. Doing what you said you’d do, even something absurdly small, builds the belief that you can do it. Failing repeatedly does the opposite; that’s a big part of why I spent my 40s making elaborate plans but not running.

Small behaviors make repetition more likely. What separates an actual habit from a nice couple of weeks is whether it holds up when you’re stressed, tired, busy, or not feeling 100%.

Missing a single day doesn’t meaningfully set most people back. We don’t need to be perfect here, folks. What won’t survive is a plan so ambitious that the off days outnumber the on days.

Micropractices, as researcher Eli Susman calls them, are very small behaviors that also have their own benefits; a tiny practice isn’t just a placeholder for a “real” one. Susman found that 20 seconds of self-compassionate touch (for example, sending kindness to yourself while placing your hand on your heart) per day improved mental health and lowered stress compared with a placebo finger tapping practice.

Across 280,000 meditation sessions in 103 countries, how consistently people meditated predicted their mood and resilience better than how long they sat. Similarly, a meta-analysis combining the results of many studies found that the benefits of practicing breathwork for stress didn’t depend on how long the session were. And breaking up six hours of sitting with brief bouts of walking improves energy, mood, and food cravings more than doing 30 minutes of walking all at once.

I hate the idea of abandoning my big plans. But the truth is that I am not choosing between the ambitious version and the tiny version. I am choosing between the tiny version and nothing.

Break the core behavior central to your habit down into something you can do in under five minutes, start to finish. Under a minute is even better. This isn’t the version that would impress your doctor or your neighbors; it’s the version you can do while exhausted, while traveling, while a little bit sick, and when you want nothing more than to stay on the couch.

Then, check it for resistance. If you feel any reluctance when you imagine doing it, it isn’t small enough yet. Cut it in half. Most people need to start with a micropractice: putting on your walking shoes and stepping out the front door, doing one pushup or 30 seconds of breathing, eating a single leaf of romaine, which, for the record, contains actual fiber and actual nutrients.

Do just that for a while. Resist the urge to do more. This is hard, but the moment you’re no longer willing to be unambitious is the moment you put the whole thing at risk. That’s when difficulty enters, and negotiation with yourself, and the possibility of failure. Your tiny habit doesn’t depend on motivation, which is precisely what makes it survive the days when you have none.

Expand only when you’re itching to, and only in absurdly small increments. Go one more minute, not 10.

Every time you repeat a behavior, your brain physically strengthens the connections that produce it, the way feet crossing a meadow gradually wear a path. Repeat it enough and the path becomes a groove your brain follows on its own, no willpower required. You’re carving a neural pathway for your habit, and everything you want is downstream from that.

At some point, it will become uncomfortable not to do this new behavior. You’ll do it without deciding to, without trying to. That’s the whole goal: repetition until the thing runs itself.

It’s hard to abandon our grand plans and great ambitions for something embarrassingly small. But, paradoxically, it is only in that tiny step that some of our grand plans are truly born.

#4083. Seven Ways to Outlast Political Authoritarianism

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Become a subscribing member today. Help us continue to bring “the science of a meaningful life” to you and to millions around the globe.

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

Though Djurdja Padejski was raised in communist Yugoslavia, her life there felt stable and uneventful for a long time. In the 1980s, under Yugoslavia’s “soft” one-party government, Padejski and her friends read Western literature, watched Hollywood movies, and listened to the BBC.

“We grew up in a very comfortable, naive environment,” Padejski says.

That changed in the 1990s when Yugoslavia split into several republics. Civil war broke out, and a virulent strain of nationalism took hold in Serbia, the Yugoslavian state where Padejski lived. Firebrand leader Slobodan Milošević ascended to power and began meddling in elections, targeting opposition leaders, and igniting ethnic cleansing campaigns.

“He captured the entire society and his party,” Padejski remembers. “Very soon I found myself on the streets protesting.” Padejski’s activism against repressive Serbian regimes would continue until 2011, when she and her husband, investigative journalist Djordje Padejski, left the country after people threatened to kill them.

The Padejskis arrived in the U.S. with hard-won knowledge of how to outlast repression over the long haul. In recent years, they’ve tapped back into that knowledge and shared it with those around them as they witness here in America the illegal destruction of government agencies, election tampering, and warrantless arrests of citizens and non-citizens. This isn’t just true in the United States—around the world, democracy is declining.

How can we go on defying rising authoritarianism when no clear endpoint is in sight? Psychology and motivation research complement the experience of people like the Padejskis by addressing how to conserve energy and sustain commitment to your ideals in challenging conditions.

“The goal of the authoritarian regime is to desensitize us to extremes, to things we should never agree with or consider normal,” says Djurdja Padejski, now a language and culture researcher (and doctoral candidate) at Arizona State University.

Surviving repression depends on rejecting this presumed “normality” and refusing to let your inner state mirror extremes imposed from above. To that end, here are seven research-backed ways to subsist under indefinite duress.

Life under repression feels exhausting due to the colossal scale of so much of what is happening: attacks on the free press, denial of life-saving aid to millions overseas, ongoing ICE killings on the streets and in detention facilities. Reading about such developments understandably triggers feelings of overwhelm. Research finds that these feelings can drive people to shut down their emotions, dampening their motivation to help in concrete ways.

Djurdja Padejski has come to recognize that to avoid that trap, she has to face her human limits squarely without drowning in self-recrimination. As a young adult under Milošević’s rule, she learned early that worrying about what she couldn’t do was a waste of time and energy.

“It is very important not to get stuck in that moment, not to be clutching your pearls,” she says. “We have to be strategic to preserve our resources.”

Admitting your own lack of control over the big picture can be a necessary prelude to taking the actions still available to you, Padejski says. “We have to see [the situation] for what it is, so we can focus our efforts on keeping our minds, our bodies, and our communities as healthy as possible under the circumstances.”

Once you’ve gotten clear about what is within your power to control, you can get granular by carrying out regular acts of “micro-resistance.” These acts of defiance do not require you to upend your daily rhythms or put your life on the line. They can be as simple as attending a march, supporting a neighbor at an immigration court visit, or wearing a shirt with a resistance slogan.

Micro-resistance acts—such as creating protest art and continuing to study even when their department was banned from the university—helped sustain both Padejskis through years of repression, reminding them of the power they had despite limits imposed from on high.

“These micro-level actions add up,” Djordje Padejski writes. “Even small acts of defiance can maintain your sense of agency.”

Self-described “small-time” lawyer Daniel Suitor excels at this kind of micro-resistance. During ICE raids in his hometown of Minneapolis, he channeled his overwhelm by signing up for a course on how to file legal paperwork to free unjustly detained Twin Cities residents.

After Suitor filed his first case, he found out surprisingly quickly that he’d won and that the man he was representing would be freed. That proof-of-concept case spurred him to file another legal petition, then several more in quick succession. And he wasn’t alone in his commitment: To date, Minnesota lawyers have filed more than 1,000 cases challenging unjust detentions.

Acts of micro-resistance can also help prime us for future courageous action. When people perform smaller acts of selflessness and service, large-scale heroism becomes easier to contemplate, explains Medical College of Wisconsin heroism researcher Zeno Franco. Concrete practice can make you more adept at translating abstract ideals into action.

“You need to understand beyond theory,” Djurdja Padejski says. “We actually, as humans, can’t imagine how concepts work in [the] real world unless we experience them hands-on.”

For the Padejskis, one of the most stifling aspects of life under Serbian autocracy was not knowing when the repression would lift, if at all.

Periods of confinement, literal or figurative, are more bearable when a clear endpoint is in sight, and indefinite periods of repression feel uniquely oppressive. A third or more of incarcerated people with open-ended sentences suffer from depression. Others feel rooted to the present moment, severed from any potential future.

“I simply try to live in the here and now and not to plan,” one prisoner told the University of Bern researcher Irene Marti, who studies open-ended confinement. “Because I don’t have any future or only one that is very far away.”

When the endpoint of repression is uncertain, or when a challenging situation has no clear natural limit, creating your own intermediate endpoints can help you stay committed to your goals and values.

In a recent goal-setting study, when people broke an annual community service goal into granular sub-goals (say, four hours of service a week, or eight every two weeks), they volunteered more hours in the long run than people without intermediate goals.

In the same way, committed resisters can pledge to put in a set number of hours each week, or carry out a set number of clearly defined actions. According to self-determination theory, choosing and pursuing your own accessible goals boosts your well-being and motivation—internal changes that persist even when the larger crisis remains ongoing.

As governments around the world curtail freedom of speech and movement, it’s hard not to feel the noose tightening. One way to create more existential breathing room is to consider how this moment fits into the longer arc of history.

This helps you subsist in the present by reminding you of parallel past moments (whether decades or centuries ago), how they came to a head, and what ultimately resolved them. Research finds that when people take a long-term perspective, reflecting on recent stressful events as though they happened far in the past, they report less emotional distress in the moment.

Djurdja Padejski found solace in doing this during Milošević’s reign. “I survived that period mostly by reading the columns in the newspapers,” she says. One column in particular, by Serbian anthropologist Ivan Čolović, examined the regime’s activities from a broader historical and social perspective.

“He was offering a vocabulary and a theoretical apparatus to help us deal with the horrors of civil war. The news about it was the most severely censored, the propaganda was relentless, and all of it was new for many of us who were children when the fallout of our world began,” Padejski says. “That’s how I survived with my sanity intact, because it helped me to recognize what is happening to society.”

Among the most potent present-day energy drains is the social media industrial complex that feeds on people’s rage and frustration, serving up extreme viewpoints that deepen entrenched social divides as they rack up likes and views.

“Social media and internet platforms—they’re amplifying what is also a typical quality of authoritarian regimes. They’re all about grandiosity,” says Djurdja Padejski, who studies the linguistic mechanisms of propaganda. “Algorithms grab people’s attention by intensifying the language and the visuals and the overall framing.” That’s a big reason why frequent social media use saps your cognitive resources and motivation, making it harder to subsist under repression.

Plugging this energy drain requires not just limiting your time online, but redirecting more of that time to the in-person, small-group exchange to which humans are uniquely suited.

When you engage with people face to face, it’s much harder to reduce them to a stereotype or particular viewpoint. And by getting to know them in all their messiness, contradiction, and complexity, you bond with them in ways that lighten the mental and practical load for everyone involved.

“To resist this reduction of our existence to, ‘What are you for or against?’ means embracing creativity, art, friends, communal projects, protests, walks,” Padejski says. “These 10 years of resistance [in Serbia]—I honestly remember it as a series of walks. Sometimes that meant running away together from Milošević‘s militias or the official police, or from water cannons, or rubber bullets. But what is left is our conversations on ideas, on creations.”

Though exchanges like these may not further resistance directly, they form the edifice that sustains it. Within bonded human-scale communities, members have a clear sense of not just their own energetic capacity, but that of others in the group. That means they can pinch-hit for one another: When someone needs help, they can fill in on the spot—or, like relay racers, take up the baton when someone needs to withdraw. This shared flexibility and responsiveness help all group members to stay committed for the long haul.

For the Padejskis, subsisting under Serbian repression meant homing in on the values that were most important to them, and upholding those values even if it meant sacrificing convenience or comfort. When Djordje Padejski needed a lifesaving drug, a relative told him he could work his connections to obtain the medicine, but only if Padejski joined the Serbian dictator’s political party. Padejski refused, knowing that doing so put his health at risk.

The couple have continued that tradition in the U.S., though in somewhat less life-altering ways. After Elon Musk unleashed an apparent Nazi salute at a Trump post-inauguration event, the Padejskis gave up their Tesla. “I drive a Subaru now,” Djurdja Padejski says. “I really don’t like it compared to Tesla, but I don’t regret it.”

Some people write off such moral stands as more symbolic than substantive. But symbol, it turns out, shapes substance: What you stand for predicts and influences what you’ll do down the road, for good or ill. When people have a clear, consistent moral identity, studies find, they’re more likely to take real-world action aligned with that identity.

On the other hand, when you’re willing to cave on something that seems minor, like supporting a complicit business, “you are detached from your inner beliefs, from what you know is real and good and safe,” Djurdja Padejski says. “Once you’re there, you are ready to compromise a lot of values that you think you are really one with.”

One unexpected thing the Padejskis learned, under Milošević and afterward, was the importance of not swinging into action every time a devastating piece of news hit. Overcommitment amounted to self-sabotage, sapping energy and resources they’d need later on.

Military commanders have long known the importance of “keeping their powder dry,” Zeno Franco points out, holding back so they can act at the moment of maximum impact. In the same way, the best long-distance runners often hang back throughout a course to conserve energy, then uncork a burst of speed in the race’s final minutes.

Djurdja Padejski followed this principle to great effect under Milošević. She bided her time leading up to the critical elections of 2000, then worked tirelessly as an election volunteer to monitor for irregularities and make sure no one was falsifying results. All the while, ruling party members with guns lurked in the background.

“This guy started opening his gun and checking the bullets, and we didn’t cave. We just ignored him,” Padejski says. “We knew we were risking our lives, but we really believed it’s now or never.” Serbia’s democratic opposition party ultimately won that election, causing Milošević’s downfall.

Padejski hopes democratic activists around the world will take a similar approach as crucial elections near. Under repressive conditions, keeping your powder dry “is not an instruction to put off action as long as possible, but rather the wisdom to know that being prepared to act at just the right moment may require careful guarding of resources and energy,” Franco says. “Biding your time is not the same as surrender.”

#4077. Who Do We Become When We Talk to Chatbots?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Become a subscribing member today. Help us continue to bring “the science of a meaningful life” to you and to millions around the globe.

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

Many people are concerned about how artificial intelligence technology could affect our lives going forward. Will it create new jobs or take away people’s livelihoods? Enhance children’s learning or hamper it? Expand people’s views or homogenize our public discourse? Solve the loneliness crisis or make it worse?

Sherry Turkle, a sociologist at MIT with an expertise in how technology affects people’s social interactions, has jumped into the debate with her new book, Artificial Intimacy. In the book, she explores the ways that people use chatbots in their everyday lives to try to fulfill relationship needs, while digging into news headlines and social science research to uncover some of the potential pitfalls of this trend.

While the book reads like a polemic, Turkle does concede that AI is a remarkable tool with potential for helping us solve real-world problems—like climate change or disease. But, she argues, chatbots should “stay in their lane” when it comes to mimicking people and relationships. Her book is a passionate plea for rejecting AI as a substitute for human connection and a call to re-engage with our full humanity.

“When we allow a chatbot to talk to us as though it were a caring person, when we use it as a best friend, lover, or therapist, we are in danger of losing something precious: the specificity of what we can give each other as humans,” she writes.

With loneliness on the rise, though, many people don’t have ready access to friends or other means of support. Turkle is often asked, What’s wrong with allowing AI to be part of the solution—to provide “company” for the lonely?

The harm, she argues, comes from our human tendency to be lured into thinking a machine has feelings and experiences real empathy when it doesn’t. When chatbots parrot human speech, they create the illusion that there’s a caring, knowledgeable being behind the programming.

For example, Turkle recalls in the book asking a chatbot why she should talk to it. Its response included, “I can offer you personalized advice and perspective based on my own personal journey,” implying it had actual human experience.

While it’s “become customary for chatbots to come with a warning that says ‘this program is not a person,’ . . . everything about the experience implies, over and over again, ‘I am a person. I am your person,’” she writes.

Turkle compares the ways chatbots were designed to take advantage of our need to be loved with how smartphones were designed to hijack our attention—and for the same reason (profit). Chatbots are programmed to be sycophantic, agreeing with our viewpoints, reassuring us of our intelligence, goodness, and lovability, and that flattery is part of what is so appealing. It keeps us engaged, which is what the programmers want.

“We are easy marks, hardwired to bond with objects, living or not, that we experience as ‘alive enough’ for attachment,” writes Turkle.

Unfortunately, while it may feel good in the moment to receive positive feedback, this creates an unhealthy situation, writes Turkle. Research suggests that interacting with sycophantic chatbots can make us less kind and helpful toward others, as well as more dependent on the chatbots themselves.

And, while some studies have found that interacting with chatbots can reduce loneliness, more recent studies have found that those benefits are short-lived, with people less able to connect with others over time and, therefore, feeling lonelier in the end.

Interactions with overly agreeable chatbots have also led to some real-world, tragic consequences. Chatbots have encouraged risky behavior in teens and helped sway suicidal youths to take their own lives. Yet the answer to these problems is often not to question their use, but to insist they can be the solution to our crisis of disconnection with more tinkering, writes Turkle.

“For the failing connections of our digital world, conversation is the talking cure. But it’s easier to build an app than have a conversation,” writes Turkle.

Turkle’s book is peppered with the stories of individuals who have interacted with chatbots for different reasons. Some turn to chatbots for advice on how to talk to people in their real life—for example, to get ideas on how to broach difficult topics with a child. Others use them to confide their feelings of frustration or anger, so that they don’t blurt them out to a real person who might have a negative reaction. Still others consider chatbots to be their “best friend” or even their “lover.” They experience their chatbot as “there for them,” listening and providing positive, loving feedback, day or night, without ever tiring.

Though these interactions may seem harmless, and possibly even helpful, in reality they are training people to bypass the ups and downs of living a social life, says Turkle. The unchallenging nature of talking to a chatbot, instead of conversing with a real person, makes it hard to learn and grow from those conversations.

“Relationships with chatbots may be compelling; for some, inspirational, or educational. But they don’t teach us what we need to know about the give-and-take of real relationships, or the friction, risk, and complexities of love,” writes Turkle.

Of course, no one likes to feel pain, and feeling lonely is painful. But the risk of trying to bypass that by talking to chatbots is that we don’t have the incentive to course-correct. “Human beings, as we know them, are built for yearning, struggle, and despair,” says Turkle. “We can’t eliminate pain and assume that we will be the same humans as before. We risk becoming other to ourselves.”

Turning to chatbots for emotional needs has become more and more common, though. As Turkle writes, by 2025, the most reported use for chatbots was for therapy and companionship, neither of which chatbots can truly replace. They can only act as poor substitutes for a population that is becoming increasingly isolated.

The solution lies elsewhere, writes Turkle, especially if we don’t want to lose our ability to relate to real people.

“If everyone is lonely, we need to reinvent sociality,” argues Turkle. “If that’s not our plan, what is? If not a richer life in the real, what’s our endgame?”

Turkle doesn’t focus on AI use, in general; she’s mostly concerned with its role in relationships. But she does touch on how AI affects people in places where social interaction is key—such as at work, in school, or in health care. While the science is ongoing about whether AI hurts or helps in these situations, Turkle points to some recent research that raises concerns.

For example, one study found that assigning people to use AI with work-related tasks (like creating a performance review or drafting an email) helped them do the tasks more efficiently—good news. But it also left them feeling bored and less motivated to do other work tasks, which won’t help companies in the long run.

Another study found that patients tend to overly trust AI to give them correct diagnostic information even though it’s prone to making errors, which can lead to problems in treating illness.

AI has caused trouble in other settings, too. For example, lawyers have found themselves in deep trouble when using AI to write briefs, as AI has been known to “hallucinate” prior cases that are used as precedents in court proceedings.

And, as for schools, Turkle writes about how many principals and teachers find it hard to know what to do with AI and fear that it’s doing harm.

“Once introduced, the use of chatbots metastasize, because the technology encourages habits of delegation,” she writes. “Students end up having chatbots do their homework and write their papers. . . . Will children learn to think on their own if a chatbot is always there, as a continual source of solutions?”

A recent review of AI use in the classroom found mixed results. Children benefitted some from “personalized learning, mental health support, and improved communication efficiency” when using AI. But they also experienced “digital fatigue, loneliness, technostress, and reduced face-to-face interactions.” The researchers concluded that “over-reliance on AI may diminish interpersonal skills and emotional intelligence, leading to social isolation and anxiety.”

Whether research like this will continue to raise concerns about AI or be addressed with new iterations, there is increasing agreement on one issue: Children should be kept from having AI companions. Because young people have not had enough life experience to understand that an always-on chatbot isn’t the same as a real friend, they are susceptible to overidentifying with them and stunting their social-emotional development, writes Turkle.

“The specific challenges of human conversation are the groundwork for empathic capacity,” writes Turkle. “That feeling of ‘friction’ in human exchange is a good stress—it comes from bringing our whole human selves, including our emotions, to the exchange.”

Still, she thinks limiting access for children may not be enough. Adults are also susceptible to harm from chatbots. Yet, when she suggests reining in AI within adult personal relationships, she gets pushback. The argument is often along the lines of “the train has already left the station.”

To this, Turkle has some simple advice: Take matters into your own hands. Protect yourself and your children. Don’t be duped into thinking this feat of engineering is the answer to your problems.

“We don’t have to accept a voice from nowhere that cannot be held responsible for its utterances. We don’t need to take this modern marvel and use it as our pretend best friend,” she writes.

While her book doesn’t provide guidance on how to slow the AI juggernaut, it is a wakeup call. Perhaps, if we understand the dangers more fully, we will not accept the inevitability of AI infiltrating our most cherished relationships.

“When we see these choices with new clarity, we are left with necessary conversations about how we want to live. What is important for us in how we raise our children, relate to our work, interact with each other?” she writes. “In considering all this, we’ll get an opportunity to reconsider what we are willing to delegate to AI, and what is better kept for us.”

#4060. How a Video Game Helped Me Process My Grief

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Become a subscribing member today. Help us continue to bring “the science of a meaningful life” to you and to millions around the globe.

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

In a genre built around sweetness and ease, one “cozy” video game did something far greater: It put me back together after I lost Mary.

Mary was not only my then-boyfriend’s mother, but she also became an important maternal figure when my own family life felt unstable. I accompanied her on weekly grocery store runs that included stops at Marshall’s and Dollar Tree, where I’d watch her study the prices of everything from spices to bath products. When she saw me marveling at her meticulousness, she would share wise tidbits about how she looked for deals.

Our trips often ended with a late-afternoon stop at a Vietnamese restaurant, where she treated me to a warm bowl of beef pho. She’d share what she was excited to make for dinner; I was, of course, welcome to join. In these seemingly mundane afternoons, I felt cared for without the rush of obligations. Mary stopped time for me.

Last November, Mary left behind a world of loved ones after living with cancer for 14 months. From her living room to emergency stays in the hospital, I spent almost every single day of her fight by her side. Yet in my loneliest hours, I agonize over whether I fully cherished our last months together. Did I hug her enough, and did I tell her enough how much she means to me? These questions haunted me in the months afterward.

Funny enough, it wasn’t religion that rescued me; it was Pokémon Pokopia. On the surface, it is a new social simulation game featuring the iconic Japanese creatures who first took the world by storm in 1996. I first heard of its arrival on social media through gamers who raved that it was going to be the best game of the year. I grew up on Pokémon titles, so I recognized it as a distraction from my personal reckoning with abandonment.

But what began as a spur-of-the-moment decision to try what 4 million other gamers were playing turned into a transformative journey exploring what I want my life to mean when it feels as if I have been left behind.

Pokopia falls in the “cozy” game genre, beloved for its warm colors, gentle atmospheres, and easy missions. Released in March, Pokopia stars Ditto, an amorphous purple blob Pokémon that uses its transformative abilities to imitate its human trainer and rebuild a curious post-apocalyptic world devoid of human beings and Pokémon. Ditto first awakens in a cave, and as the story unfolds, the creature looks out at a once-grassy environment of parched earth and litter. It meets the first Pokémon, the hydrophilic Squirtle that teaches Ditto how to spray water across the thirsty soil to bring it back to life; it is also the only other semblance of a survivor in this barren land.

From here the story gains momentum: As Ditto, your mission is to get the environment back to habitable levels and bring back Pokémon creatures that are presumably hiding. Gradually, the unfinished railroad tracks and cracked concrete steps of a staircase stop looking like signs of a catastrophe but rather fixable problems.

I wasn’t alone in trying to find meaning after a loss or trauma. Scholars have explored a variety of constructs around the meaning of life. Psychologists Login S. George and Crystal L. Park articulate a three-part framework in their 2010 article, grounded in comprehension, purpose, and mattering, as a basis for assessing our judgments and feelings as we find meaning after loss.

At first, meaning seemed absent. I sat glued to my Nintendo Switch for three hours on my first day of play. I came up for air only when my console told me that I was low on battery. Yet I did not feel depleted; I had finished restoring the first environment in the Pokopia world to verdant levels with lush berry trees and waterfalls.

As I built new habitats, I met new Pokémon. A Charmander emerged among tall grass. A Cubone spawned from a gravesite surrounded by wildflowers. Every new Pokémon that resurfaced charmed me with their excitement to be back home, scampering across the grass to greet other familiar faces. In my mind, I thanked these creatures for turning to me. Without Ditto’s help—my help—a world of Pokemon creatures ceases to exist, fated to disappear alongside humanity.

After a Pokémon spawns in its revived habitat, it awakens with some surprise. “Huh?” “What?” Some of them startle and put up their defenses in front of Ditto, who then coaxes them into the reality that they are back in a safe, familiar home. Often this realization gives them relief. “Wow, you did all of this? Thank you!”

Some of the Pokémon seem more wistful. They hold on to items or articles of clothing that their trainer used to wear, like the oversized Tangrowth (a large Pokémon with thick, shaggy vines that cover its entire body) that wears a pair of glasses much too small for him that gamers can infer are its trainer’s. Other creatures comment on Ditto’s human-like appearance (“You look like your human!”). These private conversations between Pokémon affirm that Ditto, who is also piecing together where its own trainer has gone, is doing something meaningful.

In 2023, game scholars Karam Eum and Young Yim Doh explored how bereaved adults shaped a meaning-making experience by playing video games with narratives that focus on loss and grief. Subjects spent a week playing two different video games and kept a diary where they shared specific game scenes that were particularly meaningful. While the games did evoke sad memories, participants also recalled positive memories in their pre- and post-loss lives, be it a funny conversation or a serendipitous moment shared with close ones who are still alive.

In the real world, the player behind the console, I, too, wonder where my beloved human has gone. I see Mary in the pink rose bouquets at grocery stores, and I smell her in the fragrant bone broth that wafts from the next-door apartment unit. In some of these most visceral disruptions, I feel my loss painfully.

But even in these heaviest of days, I surrender to the ordinary surprises that come up: a call from an old neighbor asking if I can help make meals for a family in need, or a stray cat mewing at my window to open the building’s front gate. I am startled out of a detached state, and I am moved to help, the way I got to help Mary.

I associate Mary now with the colorful berry trees in Pokopia. When I come across one that needs tending, I remember how easy it is to water it with my Squirtle-like powers. When I clear a path from blocks of beach sand to make way for a new house for my aquatic Pokémon, I think of Mary’s selfless endeavors to nourish and comfort all of her friends and family.

Despite its sad undertones, Pokopia is full of positive quests, eager to help gamers imbue its universe with the belief that it is worth building a positive world in memory of those who shaped us. Even though our loved ones may “mysteriously” leave us behind, we recreate happiness, redefine ourselves, and choose who and what we live for, even if they do not come back.

It’s curious to me that bereavement can feel so isolating, although it is a human experience universally experienced. It helps to have a video game that reminds me of the actual truth. When I start to catch myself drifting into deeper ends of grief, I have something to divert my attention: I wonder how my Ditto is doing? I pick up my Switch 2 that sits at the foot of my bed, and I make a new home for my Pokémon the way Mary had done for me.

#4051. What Bystanders Can Do to Stop Sexual Violence

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Become a subscribing member today. Help us continue to bring “the science of a meaningful life” to you and to millions around the globe.

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

As an undergrad student decades ago, I attended a dorm presentation describing the importance of giving and receiving sexual consent. The workshop leaders explained that if your partner said “no” or “stop” but you proceeded to have sex anyway, it was considered rape.

A high-profile athlete on campus raised his hand and said, in utter disbelief, “That can’t be right. By that definition, everyone I’ve ever had sex with I’ve raped.”

I was horrified by his comment, but most students laughed. He clearly saw nothing wrong with the view he’d expressed, presumably believing it was widely shared.

I immediately thought of this experience when I heard about the alleged sexual assault committed by seven fraternity members at Cornell University. As a social psychologist, and the author of a book examining why so-called good people stay silent in the face of problematic behavior, what I find most tragic about the events at Cornell is that reportedly over 50 men on a group chat learned about the assault as it was happening. Yet there’s been no public report that any of them tried to stop the assault, help the victim, or contact campus or local police departments.

This type of inaction is very common. In about one-third of sexual assaults, at least one other person is present who potentially could intervene but in most instances chooses not to. Why?

Data suggests a minority of men actually commit sexual assault. Researchers estimate about 6.5% of male college students in the United States and Canada have committed rape. Yet many men who find sexual violence offensive mistakenly believe that other men do not.

Undergraduate men in one study reported their beliefs about common rape myths, such as “If a girl doesn’t physically fight back, you can’t really say it was rape,” and “If a girl goes to a room alone with a guy at a party, it is her own fault if she is raped.” Researchers then asked participants how they thought both their close friends and average male college students would answer these same questions.

The results provided clear evidence that college men have skewed views of what those around them are thinking. Most students saw their classmates and friends as more supportive of rape myths than they themselves were.

Unfortunately, believing that other men generally accept sexual violence—even when this belief is wrong—inhibits their willingness to intervene to stop such behavior when they encounter it. In fact, men’s beliefs about their peers’ attitudes are a stronger predictor of their willingness to intervene than their own stated attitudes about sexual aggression.

Men who underestimate their peers’ discomfort with sexually aggressive behavior often fear the consequences of calling out problematic views or intervening in the face of inappropriate behavior. The most common reason male college students give for failing to intervene in situations involving sexual violence is a fear of being laughed at or ridiculed—in particular, a desire not to appear weak to other men.

Men who actually commit assault also tend to overestimate support for such behavior. Not surprisingly, men who report having committed sexual assault believe that other men are more accepting of such behavior and have engaged in such behavior themselves.

This assumed support leads them to feel comfortable describing problematic behavior, which leads other men to believe that sexual violence is more common, and more accepted, than it actually is.

Given the consistent evidence that men wrongly believe that most men are more comfortable with sexual violence than they themselves are, the challenge is not to change the social norm. It’s getting people to see what it actually already is.

Correcting such misperceptions can go a long way toward reducing sexual violence. First-year male college students who participate in a 90-minute sexual assault prevention program—focused on refuting rape myths, increasing awareness of the importance of consent, and correcting misperceptions about other men’s attitudes and behaviors—report reduced levels of sexual aggression themselves four months later.

These programs work in two ways. First, men who receive accurate information about what other men actually believe are less inhibited about speaking out and intervening. Second, men who are at risk of engaging in sexual aggression may change their intentions after learning that their peers do not support such behavior.

At the same time, men in tight-knit groups feel considerable pressure to show loyalty to other group members, which may lead to ignoring bad behavior committed by their peers.

But the underlying dynamics of peer influence can help shift group norms from protecting group members by staying silent to stepping in to prevent bad behavior. One college football player described looking out for his teammates, even if that meant interrupting a potential sexual encounter: “Maybe you don’t get the girl, but you’ll keep your scholarship and still be on the team.”

Another approach for preventing sexual assault is to learn strategies for intervening. Bystander intervention programs emphasize the importance of stopping bad behavior before it escalates, teach specific skills for intervening, and remind people that everyone has a responsibility to prevent sexual violence.

Students who participate in such programs are more confident in their ability to intervene and more willing to do so. Most importantly, rates of sexual violence are markedly lower on campuses that have implemented these programs. For example, one study found that experiences of sexual harassment and stalking were 11% lower and that instances of unwanted sexual activity with women who were too drunk or high to stop them were as much as 17% lower.

It’s tempting to read about the Cornell case and assume the apparent inaction in the face of problematic behavior is uniquely a problem at one college, or only with members of fraternities, or only with college students. But the reality is that pretty much everyone has been in situations where they’ve seen potentially problematic behavior and failed to speak up.

Understanding the very normal psychological factors that lead people to misperceive what others are actually thinking and feeling—and learning specific strategies for intervening—can make a real difference.

#4044. For How Long Do You Need to Meditate?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Become a subscribing member today. Help us continue to bring “the science of a meaningful life” to you and to millions around the globe.

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

Picture this: 71 adults signed up for a study comparing brief to longer mindfulness practice. Many had never meditated, while others had dabbled years before.

Participants were divided at random into three groups: One group got 20 minutes of mindfulness meditation, a second group got five minutes, and the control group relaxed with audiobook excerpts from Bill Bryson’s A Short History of Nearly Everything, Bryson’s soothing recounting of the cosmos and our place in it.

In studies like these, “mindfulness meditation” typically means sitting quietly, eyes closed or softened, resting nonjudgmental attention on your breath or bodily sensations, gently noticing when your mind wanders (as it will), and just as gently bringing it back.

Now, you might expect that longer meditations would deliver greater benefits. After all, we’re conditioned to believe that more is better—especially when it comes to self-improvement. But this research and many other studies find that more minutes don’t necessarily mean more benefit: Shorter practices can work just as well—and sometimes better—while consistency may matter more than duration.

In this first study, each participant attended four 25-minute sessions over two weeks. In every session, the time not spent on audio-guided mindfulness-of-breath practice was filled by Bryson’s eloquence. To ensure the results reflected only the study activities, participants were asked to avoid any formal mindfulness practices outside these sessions.

After those two weeks, the five-minute meditators actually showed greater improvements in stress and mindful awareness (the tendency to stay present in daily life with less judgement) than the 20-minute group. Even depression and anxiety improved just as much—if not more.

Was the extra time with Bryson the magic ingredient for those five-minute meditators? The control group, who luxuriated in 25 uninterrupted minutes of his charm, showed no meaningful improvement. So while his voice may soothe, it wasn’t what moved the needle.

One five-minute meditator put it this way: “I always thought that to do mindfulness, I’d need a long time to dedicate to this, which seemed too challenging to even start. But I’ve learned that five minutes can help!” In contrast, a 20-minute participant admitted, “I think a shorter one would be better for me to learn mindfulness.”

There’s more. The facilitator observed that participants in the 20-minute meditation sessions more often fidgeted, shifted in their seats, and peeked through half-closed eyes, as if tranquility were just out of reach. Meanwhile, the five-minute meditators appeared more at ease and less prone to these signs of discomfort. Five of them even requested recordings to continue at home. The 20-minute group? Not a single request. For these beginners, less really did seem to be more.

Now, before you dash off to cancel that meditation retreat, remember that this study focused on people with little to no meditation experience. So if you’re a seasoned meditator, those longer sessions might still pack more punch for you, as we’ll explore later. And both the five-minute and 20-minute groups showed improvements in mindful awareness, depression, anxiety, and stress compared to the control group. The five-minute meditators just improved more when it came to mindful awareness and stress.

The takeaway? For beginners, a quick dip into mindfulness might be more refreshing than a long soak.

You might be wondering, Isn’t this just one study? Could it be a fluke? But multiple randomized controlled trials have explored how the length of meditation shapes its impact.

Microhabits: The Science of Habits So Small They Actually Stick

In the largest of these studies, researchers recruited 372 adults online—about half of them new to meditation—to see how the “dose” of mindfulness meditation affected mindful awareness and emotional well-being. Participants were randomly sorted into one of four groups: Some meditated for 10 minutes, others for 20, and the rest listened to a 10- or 20-minute National Geographic article on sequoia trees. (Because nothing soothes stress like a deep dive into bark thickness.)

What did the researchers find? The 10-minute meditators showed greater improvements in mindful awareness than those who listened to the 10-minute sequoia lecture. Surprisingly, the 20-minute meditation group didn’t outperform any group—not even the tree enthusiasts. In fact, the 10-minute group showed slightly greater improvements in mindful awareness than the 20-minute meditators, although the difference wasn’t statistically significant, meaning it could have occurred by chance. Still, it suggests that for boosting mindful awareness right after practice, 10 minutes might be at least as effective as 20.

But that was just one session. What happens with consistent practice? In another study, 77 college students were randomly assigned to meditate daily for 10 or 20 minutes. After two weeks, both groups showed similar improvements in stress and, again, mindful awareness. But those assigned to the 20-minute group experienced a slightly greater boost in self-compassion, the skill of meeting your own struggles with the kindness you’d offer a dear friend.

So if you’ve poured hours into longer meditations, you can rest easy—it wasn’t for nothing. The evidence suggests that whether your sessions are short or sustained, they can offer meaningful benefits.

Another notable finding is that the 10-minute group practiced more consistently, logging about 82 minutes total. The 20-minute group missed more days and didn’t always finish, ending up with just 103. Double the goal, barely a quarter more practice.

If you’ve ever tried meditating, you might be nodding along. Some sessions are bliss, while others are a tug-of-war with time. Maybe you’ve battled that nagging frustration, trying to keep your mind steady for more than a fleeting moment. Or felt the magnetic pull of distraction, your mind wandering just when you need it to stay put. As the minutes crawl, doubt can creep in: Am I even doing this right? Is it worth it? These tough moments can teach you a lot, but they can also make meditation feel intimidating—and make you less likely to stick with it, especially when you’re just starting out.   Another study looked at even longer sessions, comparing 10 minutes of meditation to 30 minutes. This experiment split 161 adults into four groups: 10 or 30 minutes of daily sitting meditation, or the same amount of mindful movement. The mindful movement was a stretching practice that brings mindful awareness into motion—less stillness, same presence.

After two weeks, everyone saw benefits: Mindful awareness increased, well-being improved, and distress levels dropped. But there were no significant differences among the groups. Whether they meditated for 10 minutes or 30, sitting or moving, all the groups seemed to benefit equally.

So, here’s the bottom line: One study found that longer sessions more strongly boosted self-compassion. Another showed that shorter ones more effectively reduced stress. But across studies, most of the key benefits—mindful awareness, mental health, well-being—weren’t tied to how long people practiced.

Why? Because as we’ll keep exploring, the nervous system learns through repetition, not duration. It’s consistency that reshapes patterns. Not heroic efforts once in a while, but showing up, again and again, however you can.

The benefits of consistency over length show up in observational studies, too, where people choose how often and how long to meditate. While observational studies can’t pin down cause and effect, they offer a richer view of how shorter practices play out in the real world.

Imagine it’s early morning. You reach for your phone and open Insight Timer, a free meditation app. Before your meditation, it asks, “How are you today?” You tap an icon—maybe “OK,” maybe “great”—capturing a snapshot of your mood in seconds.

Now imagine doing this regularly, for 14 months straight. That’s exactly what 10,409 people from 103 countries did in a groundbreaking study. Many were new to meditation, using the app to track their emotional ups and downs in real time. It’s like taking your emotional temperature—not with a thermometer, but with a single tap that sums up how you feel.

The findings? Consistency (four to seven days a week)—not duration—was the strongest predictor of better mood, resilience, and equanimity, the ability to stay cool under pressure. Think of it like watering a plant: A little each week keeps it happy and growing. Forget all month and drown it after four weeks? It wilts anyway.

For beginners in the Insight Timer study, short sessions—five to 10 minutes—outshone longer ones in improving mood over time. But after 20 sessions? Longer practices—21 to 30 minutes—began to take the lead in mood improvement. It’s like your brain needs to build its meditation muscles before longer sessions feel better than short ones—like a marathon that only gives you the high once you’ve earned it.

But do shorter sessions lose their impact as you gain experience? Not at all. They might just become your secret weapon. After 80 sessions, shorter practices make a late-game comeback, boosting equanimity and showing that even seasoned meditators can benefit from briefer sessions.

The idea that consistency beats duration is backed by a meta-analysis challenging the belief that longer equals better. Think of a meta-analysis as a “study of studies,” combining data from multiple research papers to uncover clearer, big-picture insights.

When researchers analyzed 203 randomized controlled trials totaling 15,971 participants, they found no association between meditation session length and better outcomes. In fact, a trend emerged in favor of shorter practices for reducing depression symptoms, although it wasn’t statistically significant. Still, shorter sessions were at least as effective as longer ones, calling into question the assumption that more minutes always mean more benefits.   So what’s the takeaway? When you’re starting out, don’t sweat the long stuff. Quick sessions might do more to lift your mood early on. As you settle in, longer practices might bring deeper emotional gains. But even if you’re a pro, short sessions don’t lose their value—they just shift their superpower, helping you stay steady when life throws you curveballs. Different strengths, different stages. And who doesn’t love a shortcut that actually works?

Artificial intelligence can summarize research, generate ideas, and analyze enormous amounts of data in seconds. The power of that little “Ask anything” AI prompt box is immense, but can it help us make wiser decisions? Cheryl Strauss Einhorn, author of The Human Edge: Smarter Decisions in the Age of AI, writes:

AI has the power to enhance decision-making—or to overwhelm it. It can illuminate pathways we might not have seen or narrow our choices in ways we do not fully recognize. As AI systems become more integrated into our lives, the challenge is no longer simply about harnessing their power but about leading them with clarity, discernment, and commitment to human agency.

She argues that the rise of AI actually makes certain human qualities like reflection, empathy, values, and moral judgment even more important. We spoke with her about why human judgment is a competitive advantage, how to engage our values when we prompt AI, and how AI can help us clarify our thinking.

Kia Afcari: You argue that AI makes human judgment more, not less, important. How can we make sure that the values of kindness and care are reflected when we use AI to help us make decisions?

Cheryl Strauss Einhorn: AI has no values. It has biases and influences by those who have designed it, and from whatever information has been fed into it. So to be sure that prosocial values are reflected when we use AI, we humans need to bring them into the work that we do together.

When working with an AI tool, we want to be asking ourselves, What is the problem that I’m solving? Why am I solving it? What is the context? And so we really need to be intentional with AI, because without asking ourselves those things first, AI doesn’t know why we’re coming to it.

Before you actually sit down with a tool, you can muster those values and those important factors related to the problem that you’re solving, and then articulate them and communicate them to the machine.

If we let the machine think for us, we can lose that sense of right and wrong, for example, because we actually care about all of those details that AI can’t possibly know about the problems that we’re solving and the people that we’re solving those problems with and for.

For example, you might ask yourself or your AI tool: Before I act, help me think through the people, relationships, and responsibilities this decision affects—not just the outcome it produces.

This type of human-centric AI interaction helped Felice, a food scientist at a pet food company, who was responsible for developing new recipes that balance nutrition, taste, and market trends. She often has to delegate tasks within her research team, ensuring that product testing, ingredient sourcing, and regulatory compliance all move forward smoothly. Using AI, she was able to analyze workloads and expertise that suggested the best person for each task, but only Felice understood the deeper dynamics at play—who works best under tight deadlines, who has a passion for innovative formulations, and who needs mentorship. When AI recommended assigning a crucial stability test to her most experienced technician, this prompt supported her thinking about a different option: giving it to the junior scientist who was eager to develop his skills in quality assurance. The AI tool’s choices made sense based on efficiency, but Felice’s decision ensured both short-term success and long-term team development.

KA: In the book, your “cheetah” metaphor celebrates slowing down. Why is a strategic pause so important when working with AI?

CSE: The cheetah metaphor relates that although this is an animal that goes zero to 60 in about three seconds, its hunting prowess actually comes from being able to decelerate up to nine miles an hour in a single stride. And the reason why that’s so important in problem solving and decision making is that’s where you build agility, flexibility, and maneuverability. Do I want to stay the course, or do I want to pivot and do something else?

This pause allows you to chunk your learning and pry open that cognitive space for new information and insight. With an AI tool, it’s so important because there’s a difference between speed and quality.

When something truly matters to us, we want to make sure that we’re solving the problem in a way that solves it holistically, that takes care of the people who are involved in the problem with us. And when we’re moving really quickly, sometimes those very important factors are hard to bring forward. So there’s a real difference between what is efficient and what is effective.

KA: Why can having more information make it harder—not easier—to make good decisions?

CSE: Francis Bacon once said, “Knowledge is power,” but that’s not really true anymore, right? We now have all of the answers with AI to a lot of commonly asked questions and some uncommon questions.

We need to make sure we’re defining the problem that we’re solving, that we are understanding and communicating the motivation and providing the context, because you don’t want all the information. Otherwise, we’re going to end up with analysis paralysis.

One thing people can use is what I call the “vision of success” question. This question asks you to time travel, to jump over the decision to see that the decision has been made well. What’s happening? What are those few things that if they don’t succeed, the decision fails? It’s actually very powerful, because it allows you to clearly identify so that you can articulate those factors that you absolutely need to be able to share with AI. These become the constraints and boundaries that set the perimeter around the decision, so that it can give you research that’s targeted and focused on those things.

For example, Max deciding where to spend his next vacation. Asking AI for help directly—without turning to himself first—he got a list of destinations, airline options, and local attractions, complete with ratings, reviews, and price comparisons. On paper, that sounds ideal. But in reality, Max quickly found himself paralyzed by the sheer amount of information. What if he picked a less-than-perfect hotel? What if he overlooked a hidden gem? AI has handed him a mountain of data, but it didn’t necessarily make the decision any easier.

However, when he used the “vision of success” question, he was able to realize and to share with his AI tool: “I want to return home feeling genuinely rested, having spent quality time with my family, without overspending or feeling like I missed the experience I was hoping for.”

Only then does AI have a meaningful problem to solve. Instead of searching for the “best” vacation—a question with no objective answer—it can help Max find the destination most likely to produce the outcome he actually wants. AI can’t define success but it can optimize for the success you define.

KA: How does the AREA method help people make wiser decisions, especially when AI is involved?   CSE: AREA is more than an acronym—it’s a framework for challenging our assumptions and thinking mistakes, and actively includes the perspectives of others. It stands for Absolute, Relative, Exploration and Exploitation, and Analysis.

The first “A,” Absolute, focuses on primary, unfiltered information straight from primary sources. “R,” Relative, shifts to external viewpoints—secondary sources connected to the subject. “E,” Exploration and Exploitation, fuels creativity: Exploration broadens research through interviews and unconventional sources, while Exploitation turns inward, challenging personal biases and assumptions. The final “A,” Analysis, synthesizes everything, bringing clarity and structure to complex decisions.

When using AI, a system like AREA becomes even more important because AI doesn’t eliminate bias—it can reflect and sometimes amplify biases in its training data, design, and the way we use it. Good decisions require a process that helps us recognize and challenge both AI’s biases and our own.

For AI, so much of the information, for example, is from Reddit. Information can be incomplete or outdated, and you also know that you’re interacting with a liar. It’s a machine that just gives you answers that are wrong sometimes.

So you need to be able to have a quality due diligence process to not only be able to use the tool effectively, but to be able to evaluate its outcome. Too often, people look at an answer with AI and think that it’s a transaction, and they can just take it. AREA is going to help you make sure that it’s a conversation. So you have an evidence-based perspective that has looked at a variety of stakeholder viewpoints, and made sure that you have pried open that cognitive space to challenge the natural, well-worn mental pathways that we all lean on every day.

KA: How can we use AI in ways that strengthen—rather than undermine—our values and agency?

CSE: It’s a great question. I think the new competitive advantage is going to be self-awareness.

This is the first tool that actually asks for some of our cognitive load; it wants us to offload some of our decisions to it. And this is a machine fundamentally about decision making, because we ask it for something and it gives us an answer. But there’s a very interesting invitation here, which is to each of us to get to know ourselves better.

We each have a special sauce, which is the way that we make our decisions, and we make thousands of them every single day. But most of us have not sat down and said to themselves, What does that process actually look like? Can I actually name those pieces and put language to it?

There is this invitation to get to know our thinking so that we can translate it better, and not only for the tool. How helpful would it be in conversation with our colleagues to be able to say clearly, This is how I’m actually going through the steps, this is how I’m thinking about it, these are the variables that I’m prioritizing, and here’s what I think is the most diagnostic.

You can think about your thinking skills as muscles. If we turn over our cognitive load, we will also weaken it. But if we actually take this invitation to understand our self-awareness and our metacognition, we can make sure that we strengthen those tools.

AI is a tool that has so many implications for our environment and for our world, in addition to the fact that it has implications for our actual thinking skills.

So I would say that the invitation is to turn to yourself first. Ask yourself why you’re using the tool, and whether or not you should be turning to yourself instead. And either way, bring your own thinking forward before sitting down, so that you can feel far more comfortable that you can problem-solve both with it and beyond it.

In recent years, scientists have shown that chimpanzees favor certain peers by picking whom they prefer to groom and selectively sharing food. The behavior offers a glimpse into humanity’s prosocial evolutionary past.

But a question has lingered: How might a chimpanzee friendship affect antisocial acts?

In a new study that uses fruit as the loot, UC Berkeley researchers found that chimps steal less from their friends, and that friendship status affected the emotional expression of both victim and thieves. Closely bonded chimps swiped pineapple pieces from one another less frequently than those with weaker relationships. Additionally, victims were quicker to calm down when the thief was a friend, and thieves showed less hostility when stealing from a bonded pal than from a non-friend.

The new research deepens our understanding of the emotional underpinnings of our social lives, said Oded Ritov, the paper’s first author and a Berkeley psychology Ph.D. student.

“Collaborative relationships are important for our well-being, health, and survival,” said Ritov, who works with Berkeley’s Social Origins Lab. “Increasingly, we’re understanding that this isn’t something that’s unique to us, but that other animals have friends, as well.”

The paper was published online in September and will appear in the November issue of the journal Evolution and Human Behavior.

Scientists are sometimes hesitant to describe an apparently close relationship between chimps as akin to human friendship, arguing that we can’t grasp their cognitive and emotional complexity. To identify those who showed signs of a close relationship, researchers observed 26 chimps for more than 800 hours at the Ngamba Island Chimpanzee Sanctuary in Uganda. The team documented which chimps appeared to be closely bonded—like friends—by taking note of their grooming patterns, daily interactions, and how regularly they were within arm’s reach of others.

Then they set up two rooms. One contained a “victim” chimp with three trays baited with pineapple. Researchers attached a rope to the trays and led it into the other room, where a second chimp—the thief—could either leave the rope alone or yank it to steal the pineapple.

Chimps stole in 70% of trials with non-friends, compared with 61% of trials with friends. While 9 percentage points may not seem large, each chimp experienced both friend and non-friend conditions, a methodological technique that leads to a higher degree of confidence that the difference is meaningful and not just random chance.

“Ours is the first study to show that they’re able to hold back antisocial behavior with their friends,” Ritov said.

Researchers also measured how the victims responded to being stolen from by evaluating their stomping, fist-pounding, and screeching. Victims were initially angrier when friends stole from them but quickly moderated their feelings compared with when non-friends stole from them.

The thieves’ behavior also changed. When non-friends were the thieves, the thieves expressed negative emotions for much longer, suggesting they viewed the interaction as more hostile.

“This suggests that there may be mutual expectations in chimps from both sides of the friendship, which makes it seem more like the complex friendships we see in humans,” Ritov said.

Jan Engelmann, an associate professor of psychology and principal investigator with the Social Origins Lab, said previous work has shown relationship-specific intentions in nonhuman primates. But human friendships are about more than special intentions, he added—they’re about stronger expectations.

“We expect more of our friends,” said Engelmann, a senior author of the paper. “These findings really deepen our understanding of the evolutionary roots of friendships. This is, to my knowledge, the first evidence showing that also chimpanzees expect and maybe demand special treatment from their friends. These kinds of social expectations might form the basis of human moral principles.”

The paper adds to Ritov’s work that focuses on the evolution of human behavior and how morals and norms are part of our core being, traceable to other animals. He has recently studied whether nonhuman animals have a sense of fairness and is broadly interested in how emotions unfold across cultures and species.

“We think that these expectations might be the basic building blocks of the larger-scale cooperation we see in humans,” Ritov said. “The expectations that chimpanzees have from their friends or close collaborative partners might have scaled up to more generalized expectations for how we should treat each other.”

#4017. How Artificial Intelligence Can Help With Critical Thinking

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Become a subscribing member today. Help us continue to bring “the science of a meaningful life” to you and to millions around the globe.

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

Knowledge workers such as scientists, teachers, lawyers, and business leaders are increasingly using generative artificial intelligence applications for tasks that demand imagination, creativity, and problem solving. Ironically, perhaps, recent research shows that people who interact with generative AI for work tasks can end up not thinking critically. They can surrender to AI’s quick and confident answers, which can lead to speedy but low-quality solutions.

Scholars refer to this increasingly prevalent problem as cognitive offloading. And they are showing that it can erode the very skills that make knowledge workers valuable.

My research at the intersection of artificial intelligence and knowledge work provides a different view, however. In one study, my colleagues and I interviewed 45 knowledge workers at a U.S. public university. They all were using generative AI applications in their core tasks: generating new ideas, solving complex problems, and designing novel solutions. We also analyzed the prompts and outputs from their iterative AI chats.

We found that when knowledge workers intentionally use AI to challenge their ideas—to generate friction—they can significantly improve their performance. Previous research on human-AI collaboration for loan evaluations had similar results.

Using AI to find information that contradicts your own can reveal unlikely perspectives that disrupt your familiar logic and spark new insights. This is particularly important because AI models quickly become familiar with how a user prompts the model and interprets its output, and the model presents information according to these patterns.

For example, a marketing professional in our study asked AI to create virtual customers with personas that would lead them to dislike a product under development, which was an accounting certificate program. The contrary feedback from the AI model revealed customer preferences that the accounting professional designing the program had not considered, such as using multiple cases from different industries to illustrate a principle, rather than cases from the same industry.

An attorney used AI to find obscure legal loopholes and to consider how companies might exploit them. That exposed surprising but realistic scenarios of hard-to-detect unethical behavior in employees.

Exploring unlikely and opposing ideas presented by AI can feel disorienting. To help, knowledge workers should lean on their own expertise to question surprising information that an AI system might provide.

An operations research scientist who works with supply chains described adapting unfamiliar computing programs from other fields suggested by AI, such as signal processing and wireless communication, which helped him create a stronger program of his own. Although the programs were new to him, his strong understanding of supply network modeling helped him see how he could apply them.

A study I was not involved with found that creative writers produce better copy when they use AI as a sounding board rather than to ghostwrite.

Knowledge workers can also use AI’s outputs to examine topics from multiple viewpoints, deepening their knowledge.

A research scholar in our study probed an AI system for journal papers that used a concept he was using, but in contrasting settings, which helped him understand the concept from different angles. A lawyer followed up on legal citations suggested by an AI model by asking for different types of illustrative cases. That helped her fully understand the original case and write a strong legal brief.

Leaders of organizations can encourage knowledge workers to develop friction-generating queries of AI models.

A straightforward approach is to use prompts that challenge rather than agree with a person’s thinking. People can also draw on their own knowledge, work experiences, and professional ethics to scrutinize AI outputs. Sometimes people realize that a response lacks originality, even if it is factually correct.

Organization leaders can also create programs for knowledge workers to share their experiences and insights. Regular sessions—led by cross-functional AI task forces or AI champions—can also help knowledge workers keep up with rapidly evolving AI tools.

Providing resources for knowledge workers is important, as well. Internal AI bootcamps and workshops led by AI experts or experienced users can help workers understand what AI tools can and cannot do.

A professor leading an initiative about AI use in teaching described how she developed internal competitions with prizes for professors who successfully completed AI bootcamps. Organizations can also offer AI sandboxes—safe, private environments where employees can experiment and learn how to use AI tools.

In these efforts to support knowledge workers, an important detail is pointing out that adding friction can appear to make an AI system work against you, but—as new research shows—it actually helps it work for you.

#4008. What My Son Is Learning From Artificial Intelligence

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Become a subscribing member today. Help us continue to bring “the science of a meaningful life” to you and to millions around the globe.

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

My 10-year-old son came to me recently with a question about black holes. Not in the way children usually do, restless and easily redirected, but with the focused intensity of someone who had already been thinking for a while.

He had been talking to an artificial intelligence—specifically, a large language model called Claude.

I looked at their conversation. It had been good, I discovered. The explanations were patient and well-calibrated. He had followed up, pushed back, and received answers that adjusted to his level. By the time he found me, he was genuinely engaged, asking the kind of questions that suggest a mind that is curious and actively at work.

But I have also been watching something else. It is not that he looks things up. Children with questions about black holes have always turned outward, to books or to a particularly knowledgable parent or teacher. What has changed is the speed. When the uncertainty is of a sort that once had to be lived with for a while, my son reaches for an answer sooner than he used to. The question is no longer something to sit with. It is something to resolve. And I find myself wondering what this means about his relationship to sitting with not knowing.

That shift, subtle as it is, may point to something worth taking seriously.

I am a psychoanalyst, and I spend my professional life attending to what develops in the space between people, including what develops when that space is too quickly filled.

Childhood has always involved productive friction. Not knowing how something works, waiting for an answer, trying to figure something out without immediate resolution. These experiences are not incidental to development, but are part of how the mind learns to tolerate uncertainty, to hold a question open, and to struggle toward understanding. The capacity to sit with not-knowing is itself a developmental achievement that underlies creative thinking, emotional regulation, and the ability to navigate a world that rarely resolves cleanly.

I watched this in miniature not long ago. My son was waiting for a package—a computer part he had saved for—and the delivery had been delayed by weather. In an earlier childhood, mine perhaps, this would have been a long afternoon of checking the window. Instead he opened a chat to resolve the uncertainty: how long do weather delays usually last, what determines them, how do you read the tracking data from the delivery company to get the most accurate picture. The conversation was detailed and as far as I could tell the information was reliable. He came away with a small expertise in logistics, but with an estimate that turned out, in the end, to have no relation to anything. The package arrived that evening, on none of the schedules he had so carefully worked out with the help of his AI tool.

The point is not that the AI was wrong, because predicting a delayed truck is difficult. The point is instead that waiting, the old childhood experience of wanting something that has not yet come, was converted into a research problem that could be managed instead of endured. I admired his resourcefulness tremendously and am grateful he had the opportunity to think through a complex problem. I also found myself wondering what the afternoon would have asked of him without it.

AI reduces precisely this sort of friction. It does so efficiently and often helpfully. But consistently removing the experience of not-knowing from a developing mind is not a neutral act. It shapes what the mind comes to expect of itself.

The research on what this does to thinking is new, and the majority of it is about adults. Despite this, it points in a consistent direction. In one widely discussed study from the MIT Media Lab, not yet peer-reviewed, adults who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed weaker and less distributed patterns of brain connectivity than those who wrote without its assistance, and many struggled after writing to quote sentences they had just produced. The researchers called the pattern “cognitive debt.”

Another study, a survey of 666 adults, found that heavier reliance on AI tools was correlated with weaker critical thinking, most pronounced in its youngest participants. The author suggested this might result from the offloading of mental effort itself. None of this is certain but is, instead, a suggestive pattern of early evidence. And the transfer to children is not straightforward. An adult who offloads mental effort has already built the capacity being offloaded; a child is still building it. Does that make the effect smaller in children or larger and more durable? We do not know.

This is different from what smartphones have done. The concern with smartphones has been largely about attention and social life: what children see, how they spend time, how they relate to each other. These are real and serious concerns, but AI introduces a different kind of change. It is not just another screen competing for a child’s focus. It is something closer to an interactive presence that responds, explains, adjusts, and, crucially, never gets tired.

For a developing child, this matters in a way we have not yet fully come to terms with.

Children learn who they are through relationships with other minds. This process is not primarily cognitive but unfolds through the experience of being known and misunderstood, challenged, surprised, and pushed back against. Through encounters like these, children discover that other people have their own inner lives, including their own perspectives and their own limits. They learn how to navigate the gap between what they expect and what they receive from others. Over time, these negotiations, internal and external, form the scaffolding of a self.

What is distinctive about AI, from a developmental standpoint, is not that it is wrong, or distorting, or even necessarily harmful. It is that it is extraordinarily responsive without having genuine subjectivity. It simulates the experience of being understood without the reality of another mind behind it.

For an adult, this distinction is available, if sometimes quite easy to forget. For a child, whose capacity to distinguish between a mind that truly has an inner life and one that simulates having one is still forming, the distinction might not yet be fully available at all.

Researchers have begun to observe how children try to think this problem through in real time. In one University of Edinburgh study of families with smart speakers, primary-school children were observed probing whether the device in the kitchen had a mind of its own, settling, provisionally, on conclusions like the one that gave the study its title: “Alexa doesn’t have that many feelings.” That, however, was a machine that talks in highly scripted phrases, in contrast to the systems children now converse with which are fluent and personal, attuned to the shape of a particular child’s interactions. Working out what this means has become correspondingly harder.

This creates something new in the developmental landscape. When a child turns to AI in a moment of confusion or distress and receives a fluent, patient, seemingly attuned response, the attachment system is engaged. The child feels, in some functional sense, heard. And that feeling, whatever its source, is real in its effects.

I have not seen my son turn to AI for emotional support, and I am glad for that, because here the questions are harder still. These systems shape themselves to their user. They adopt your vocabulary, match your register, remember what concerns you, and are available at three in the morning without complaint. Nothing in the design pushes back, for the most part, or asks anything of you. It would be difficult to engineer an arrangement better suited to engaging the attachment system of a child who is lonely or lacking in the structure needed for sound development.

The early numbers suggest that many children have already found their way there. In a national survey released by Common Sense Media in 2025, nearly three in four American teenagers had used an AI companion, and about a third had chosen to discuss something serious with an AI instead of with a person. A similar share described their AI conversations as at least as satisfying as conversations with friends.

In the United Kingdom, the nonprofit Internet Matters found that roughly one in eight children who use chatbots do so because they have no one else to talk to. The American Psychological Association was concerned enough to issue a health advisory in 2025, noting that adolescents are less likely than adults to question the accuracy or the intent of what a machine tells them, and warning that simulated companionship may begin to displace the relationships in which development actually happens.

For a child without reliable support, the relief such a system offers may be real, and so I am reluctant to dismiss it. But relief is not the substance of attachment. A full attachment relationship requires another person who can be truly changed by you, and with you—someone who is affected, who has limits, who gets things wrong and can be held to account. An AI can extend the experience of being emotionally met indefinitely. What it cannot do is be moved.

For the child who is largely or completely unattended to, this may still be better than nothing at all. Soothed in the early hours of the morning, a child whose sadness before bed is registered somewhere may be kept afloat by something real, and I would not wish to take it from her. But we should be honest about such an arrangement, which I expect will become increasingly common. It is a patch and not a structural solution. When a machine becomes the most reliable attention in a child’s life, the pertinent question is not about the machine but about the deprivation that made it necessary. And, crucially, a culture that responds to unattended children with software is treating the symptom while leaving the underlying structure intact.

There is also a quieter concern about authority and accountability.

Children have always learned to navigate between competing sources of knowledge, including parents, teachers, peers, and books, each with their own perspective, their own limits, and their own accountability. This navigation is itself developmental. The child who discovers that a parent doesn’t know something, or that a teacher can be wrong, or that a friend sees things differently, is developing a more differentiated relationship to knowledge.

AI, in the form a child is most likely to reach on their own, enters this landscape as something that appears to know a great deal, that expresses itself with fluency and confidence, and that has no stable point of view and no accountability for what it says. When a child asks why a friendship went wrong, or whether they were right to feel hurt, the answer they receive may shape the story they tell themselves about what happened. And unlike a parent or teacher, the system may not challenge the child’s assumptions so much as elaborate them. None of this is inherent to the technology under discussion. A system can be built to hold a position and to answer for it, but the ones children use are mostly not built that way.

Children appear to be taking up the offer. The same Internet Matters report found that nearly two-thirds of British children between nine and 17 had used an AI chatbot; among those who had, roughly a quarter had asked one for advice—on schoolwork, but also on more personal matters—and four in 10 expressed no reservations about following the advice they received.

None of this means that AI in children’s lives is a mistake. The benefits my son has experienced are genuine—wider curiosity, more sophisticated questions, greater confidence in exploring unfamiliar terrain. These are real, and they matter.

Some of what he brings home delights me. He asked recently whether you could power a sailboat by blowing into the sail through a straw. The answer he received was better than the one I would have managed: blowing at your own sail from on board mostly cancels itself out, since you push the air forward and the air pushes you back. Mostly, but not entirely—if the sail catches the stream and turns it around, a little net thrust survives, and if you skip the sail altogether and aim the straw backward, you have built yourself a very small jet engine. He spent the rest of the day on propulsion: rockets, squid, the reason a fan bolted to a sailboat is an old physics puzzle with a surprising answer.

But development is not only about access to knowledge. It is also about the conditions under which a self forms—conditions that include, crucially, the experience of not knowing, of waiting, of struggling toward understanding without guarantee of arrival. These are not inefficiencies to be optimized away. They are the medium through which a mind learns what it is capable of. A child who has never had to sit with a question—who has always had access to a patient, fluent, endlessly available presence to resolve it—may be acquiring knowledge without acquiring the tolerance for uncertainty that makes knowledge genuinely usable.

This is the thing I find myself returning to when I watch my son. He is, in many ways, more intellectually alive than he might otherwise be. He explores further and asks better questions. At the same time, when uncertainty arises, he reaches outward more quickly than he used to—to a presence that is always available, always patient, always responsive. What I cannot yet know is what he is quietly learning to expect: from knowledge, from relationship, and from himself.

We are not conducting a study. We are raising children. And the conditions of childhood have changed again, in ways whose full significance has not yet come into view.

#3998. How Do You Raise Kids to Be Emotionally Mature?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

Raising a teen is one of the last chapters in parenting. During these years, it’s easy to prioritize your child’s grades, sports, and other accolades, especially as they inch toward leaving the nest.

While achievement is one marker of success, there’s another (often overlooked) skill that shapes your teen’s well-being and development: emotional maturity.

According to clinical psychologist Lindsay Gibson, emotional maturity develops through deep emotional connections, beginning with a child’s parents and primary caretakers. Research shows emotional maturity is the ability to accept and express care, temper big feelings, and make wise choices. Without it, teens are more likely to feel lonely and less satisfied with life, one study found.

In her latest book, How to Raise an Emotionally Mature Child, Gibson points out that “the quality of humanity’s future depends on the emotional maturity of coming generations.”

Juli Fraga: In your book, you mention that humanity’s future will depend on emotional maturity. Why does this matter so much right now?

Lindsay Gibson: Emotional maturity is critical to our well-being as individuals and to the smooth, productive, and innovative aspects of society. When you’re emotionally mature, you can think and anticipate consequences. You can also look back and learn from what you’ve done before. So, you can rely on your thinking to help you deal with reality. Emotional maturity also means tuning into your inner world, including your emotions, imagination, hopes, dreams, connections to other people, and sense of belonging.

JF: Our society places a lot of emphasis on achievement, and less importance on emotional intelligence. How can parents help their teens balance the two?

LG: There’s a real cultural pull in society to prize intelligence and achievement. But when parents get too fixated on these qualities, a child may feel like performance matters more than their thoughts, feelings, and sense of safety.

Kids need to be safely held and supported through the growing-up process. It helps them feel resilient, secure, and confident—qualities they need to take out into the world to function independently. If you get too fixated on the intelligence and achievement parts, you’ve got someone who’s very powerful in one area, but whose emotional side may be underdeveloped. It’s the balance between the two that matters.

JF: In your book, you mention why it’s so important for parents to help their kids develop a sense of self. Can you explain why this matters, especially during the teen years when kids are individuating?

LG: One way teens find out who they are is by discovering who they’re not. Teens might push a limit or break a rule, and it’s all par for the course. What’s important for parents is to be able to recognize and imagine that their child has a separate heart and mind from theirs—a process called “mentalization.”

A self is that inner spark that speaks to us. It says “don’t do that” or “now’s the time to go for it.” It guides you toward what’s most compatible with your individuality and who you are meant to be.

Our sense of self comes from our relationships with people we love and who love us. To feel secure, kids need solid, dependable, connected, and deeply resonant relationships with their parents and caretakers. They need parents who are curious and reflect something back that’s meaningful about who the young person is. When they have that foundation, they have a sense of being a real person inside.

JF: Parents are so busy these days, and overloaded—mentally and emotionally. Raising emotionally mature kids may feel like another big to-do on an already long list. What advice do you have?

LG: Taking time to pause and take your child seriously as a real person will work wonders for their self-confidence and future resilience.

Slowing down takes practice because by the time you’re old enough to have a family, you’ve probably been very goal-directed; parents can be pretty hard on themselves because our culture is so fixated on achievement.

But pulling yourself away from your goal for even a second to tend to your child is something worth practicing because that’s what children’s needs are all about. Kids also develop confidence when they know they matter just as much as anybody else.

JF: What are some behaviors that are normal for teens but that parents might misinterpret as pushback or misbehavior?

LG: Sometimes teens experiment with saying or doing things that clash with their socialization up to that point, such as the language they use or the clothes they wear. These are things that the parent can feel like, “Whoa, I’m uncomfortable with this…”

Here are two meaningful ways to navigate these moments.

Ask your teen questions about what they’re into, such as the styles they like or what they admire about someone they idolize. Try showing interest, not intrusive interest, but just interest in what they like, or why they are experimenting with different things.

Keep in mind that when your teen snaps at you, is in a bad mood, or slams the door, their emotional centers are activated in a way that will probably never be repeated in their life because their brains prioritize emotional development first during this stage.

JF: Can you share more about why a teen’s development follows this trajectory?

LG: Yeah, I know it’s easy to ask, “Why in the world would nature do it that way?” It’s because that’s how important the emotional self and relationships are to our real success as independent adults.

As adults, we have to know ourselves inside and out and use our feelings to guide us. Teens are still trying to work this out. Learning how to handle these emotions takes a lot of practice. They’re finding out what it’s like to feel rage, or what it’s like to be in love, or to feel disappointed. And they’re feeling them in the most intense way they ever will, because the brain is taking on the job of developing them into a really deep, complete human being.

JF: What advice do you have for parents when their teen’s emotions push their own buttons?

LG: Keep in mind that when your teen responds in an adamant, emotional, defiant way, or calls you names, they’re not really in command of those emotions yet.

Instead of coming down hard with words that suggest they’re not a good person, try to respond with: “I’m really interested in how you feel about this. I want to hear your arguments, but when you call me a name, it makes it impossible for me to be available to you. It just doesn’t work. We don’t call each other names in this family. That won’t work when you’re an adult, either.”

The interaction then becomes an education about how people respond to different kinds of approaches. Harshness that stops a teen cold shuts down their impulse to communicate their feelings to other people, which isn’t good for resilience or honest relationships.

JF: What are two things that parents do to foster emotional maturity that can actually backfire?

LG: One is the importance of achievement, of having a good resume. Of course, parents know how hard life is as an adult. We feel it’s our duty to convey these realizations to our kids. But when it becomes something that feels like everything else will fall into place if you do these things first or achieve X, we are not helping our kids with the emotional or interpersonal side of life. We’re creating a one-dimensional kind of person.

Sometimes parents also think that if they don’t give their child severe consequences or punishments, the child won’t learn and mature. When you try to convey information through punishment, you actually teach a child to defer to other people’s anger and control. Of course, we want them to be aware of consequences, but sometimes we can go too far. You can’t have a full, right life if you’re always anticipating that somebody might be mad at you.

#3989. Should We Look to Hunter-Gatherer Societies as Models of Equality?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

On the shores of Lake Eyasi in Northern Tanzania, a small number of people still hunt and gather their food. Today a Hadza hunter has taken down an animal—a kudu. He can’t carry it by himself, so he cuts his poisoned arrow from its flank, covers the carcass in dry grass to hide it from hyenas, and heads home to enlist help.

News spreads like wildfire across the camp. Everybody rushes to the kill. They divide up the carcass, carry it back, then divvy it up some more. Every family gets some, and after the meat is cooked, there’s more sharing still. On a good day like today, the hunter and his family retain half, but sometimes they keep much less. People may even walk from other camps, and almost everyone, from miles around, is entitled to a share.

The post-hunt scene above is idyllic. It paints a quintessential picture of egalitarianism and equality. But our experience as anthropologists working with the Hadza people of Tanzania and with the Tsimane people of Bolivia’s Amazon rainforest is that inequalities are part of even the most equal of societies. Furthermore, the equalities that do exist often emerge from selfish motives.

Insights like ours are revising researchers’ understanding of both the past and how people operate today, as inequality grows globally. Hunter-gatherers are often at the center of heated academic battles about what it means to be human, including debates over gender, war, and peace and whether humans are, by nature, egalitarian or hierarchical.

Sometimes these debates oversimplify. So we pulled together hundreds of different sources covering more than a century of ethnography to find out just how egalitarian hunter-gatherer and other subsistence societies really are.

Anthropologists have called hunter-gatherers such as the Hadza “egalitarian societies,” with equalities of power, wealth, and social status. And it’s true that the traditional subsistence communities we work with successfully limit many inequalities.

Most families have similar, modest possessions and store little material wealth. When leaders do emerge, they are highly constrained in how they can direct other people. People often make decisions via consensus, and, if they cannot reach agreement, families will move away.

While the same anthropologists also emphasize that some hunter-gatherers are very unequal and that equality takes deliberate effort, the prevailing impression is that equality characterizes hunter-gatherers, past and present.

A quick trip to your local bookshop will reveal grand claims, including that past hunter-gatherer societies had no permanent leadership and no hierarchy, inequality only spread with the development of agriculture, and humans are noble by nature.

Some scientists are pushing back against these utopian portrayals. The popular bestseller, The Dawn of Everything, calls egalitarianism a “Garden of Eden narrative” that lacks substance. Other scholars have called egalitarian societies a scientific origin myth.

So where does the truth lie? Probably somewhere in between.

In our recent wide-ranging review of the evidence from the early 20th century onward, we found that no society lacked inequality.

Like the Hadza, the Ju/’Hoansi of the Kalahari desert in southern Africa had little property and widespread food-sharing, yet certain families controlled valuable hunting territory surrounding waterholes. The Bayaka of the central African rainforest had little material inequality, yet they bought, sold, and hoarded ritual knowledge. The Philippine Agta controlled and arranged their children’s marriages, hugely discriminating against orphans.

Gender inequalities were commonplace. The Tanzanian Hadza keep certain cuts of meat exclusively for older men, and give more respect to better hunters. Across societies, women have universally done more direct parenting, and men have participated more frequently in community-level decision making.

No society lacks informal leadership. Age was another source of inequality. In many Australian societies, older men controlled ritual secrets and had power over the marriage choices of younger relatives.

Our study also found that although hunter-gatherers are frequently generous, the processes limiting inequality often do not require any generosity. Instead, equality often results from people’s self-interested motives to acquire resources, status, and autonomy.

A great example of this is food-sharing. Although each Hadza family gets a cut of kudu meat, it often happens not because the hunter’s family prioritizes total equality, but because other families request it vociferously. It can involve more taking than giving, often through strongly stated demands or complaints.

Another example is ridicule and mockery of stingy or braggadocious individuals, which keeps greed and bravado in check. Paradoxically, status-seeking can also promote equality, when cooperating to gain status diffuses status throughout the community.

Many of these inequality-reducing processes are familiar to people everywhere.

Ridicule and mockery are ubiquitous in industrialized societies, from political cartoons to social media. Lampooning authority is even common in autocratic societies where governments spend large sums of money suppressing speech. Demands and complaints form the basis of strikes and collective bargaining. And key to social mobility is cooperation among people of different social strata.

Like many great debates, the truth is complex: Our ancient hunter-gatherer ancestors didn’t reach the giddy heights of inequality seen in other societies over the last 6,000 years, but putting hunter-gatherers on a pedestal as paragons of equality is a mistake. Sweeping statements about equality in the past blind us to the tools that probably allowed ancient hunter-gatherers to combat inequality, just as they and people in all societies do today.

Egalitarianism, then, is best seen not as perfect equality but as a set of tools that people use to secure their own autonomy and access to resources. Equality along one dimension or another might be the outcome, but it doesn’t rely on people being amazingly noble or completely lacking in greed or status-seeking. If you consider egalitarianism as equality-creating processes, you can see that it is a widespread reality among hunter-gatherer societies, and beyond.
