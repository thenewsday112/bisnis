#555. Unexpected cold water from the north delayed squid arrivals and surprised fishers in the northeastern US

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Study links a mysterious pulse of cold water off the northeastern U.S. coast to major fishing disruptions, including delayed squid arrivals and the return of cold-water mackerel.

Commercial fishers in the northeastern United States started seeing odd catches in the summer of 2023. Squid catches dropped off, while mackerel, a cold-water fish that had declined for years, suddenly returned. A cold pulse of fresh water was moving onto the continental shelf.

Longfin squid usually reach southern New England in May and stay through July. In 2024 they did not arrive until August.

Scientists at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (WHOI) and NOAA now tie the delay to that cold pulse. The water came down from the Scotian Shelf and the Grand Banks, but the team can’t yet say what pushed it south.

The paper credits the fishing industry with spotting the event. Industry representatives and scientists held weekly informal calls, known as the Squid Squad, and Rhode Island’s Commercial Fisheries Research Foundation (CFRF) ran regular ocean update meetings.

By March 2024 several commercially important fish species were reaching New Jersey four to five weeks late. Adult shortfin squid tolerate 40 to 58°F (4.5 to 14.5°C) but prefer a salinity of 34.5 to 36.5. In May and June they were missing from the shelf break, the outer edge of the continental shelf.

Noelle Olsen, a senior research biologist at CFRF, said several captains told her in spring 2024 that their target species “were up to five weeks late or missed their usual spring run altogether.”

The R/V Roger Revelle sailed from Morehead City, North Carolina, on June 6, 2024, and worked until June 11. Magdalena Andres was chief scientist. The plan drew on the Squid Squad calls and a May meeting in Rhode Island.

The ship recovered moorings off Cape Hatteras and took CTD casts to 3,300 feet (1,000 meters) with a SeaBird 911+ instrument. It finished with a line north across the shelf along 71 degrees west.

Scientists on board traded notes with four squid companies on water conditions and on squid seen at the surface at night.

Fishing boats out of Rhode Island and nearby states have taken ocean profiles south of New England since 2014. The crews lower an RBR Concerto CTD, a probe that records conductivity, temperature and depth six or eight times a second.

CFRF and WHOI run it as the Shelf Research Fleet. More than 1,000 profiles have been logged, and the data are open to the public.

The study used 11 years of casts from Zone 2, the second of six rectangular zones south of New England. The team also pulled in NASA’s SMAP salinity satellite, NOAA satellite temperatures and the Revelle data.

In May 2024 the boats’ average surface salinity was 30.7, against an open-ocean average of about 35. No May in the 11-year record had been fresher.

NOAA’s ECOMON survey, running since 1977, ranks 2024 as the second-largest freshening of the Northeast shelf, after 1998.

Satellite maps first show the fresh water in July 2023. It lay along the shelf edge from the Tail of the Grand Banks to the eastern tip of Cape Cod. It spread southwest and toward shore and got to Cape Hatteras in December 2023.

By January 2024 it covered the Gulf of Maine, Georges Bank and the shelf down to Cape Hatteras. At the June 2024 peak, much of the shelf and slope was 1.5 to 2.0 salinity units fresher than normal. WHOI says surface water during the pulse ran 5 to 9°F (3 to 5°C) below the 20-year average.

South of Cape Hatteras, the Revelle read surface salinity of 35 to 36, normal there. Northeast of the cape it dropped as low as 29. The track average came out at 31.8, against about 33 for typical mid-shelf water.

On June 10 and 11, the surface along 71 degrees west, south of New England, was about 63°F (17°C). The R/V Neil Armstrong had measured close to 72°F (22°C) in the same area in June 2021. Over the slope, the fresh layer was 165 to 230 feet (50 to 70 meters) thick.

Longfin squid do best in water of 50 to 57°F (10 to 14°C). Few turned up in southern New England in June and July 2024. In late August, boats finally caught them southwest of Hudson Canyon.

The NOAA Fisheries landings database puts the Northeast longfin harvest at 39 million pounds in 2023 and 29 million in 2024. Its value dropped from $39 million to $32.5 million.

Shortfin squid did a little better. Their catch went up from 6.4 million to 7 million pounds in 2024.

Final 2025 numbers haven’t been tallied. Preliminary quota monitoring by NOAA’s Greater Atlantic Regional Fisheries Office shows the catch more than doubled by weight.

The pulse followed years of fast warming. The Gulf of Maine has been transformed by warming waters. Lobster fishers have faced an unprecedented decline in southern New England.

The authors looked upstream at the Labrador Sea and the Tail of the Grand Banks, off Newfoundland. They used daily sea surface height maps from the Copernicus Marine Service, with the 30-year trend removed. The 2023 maps point to a Gulf Stream lying farther south there than during 2009 to 2018.

Earlier studies proposed that a northward shift of the Gulf Stream can block the Labrador Current. That keeps its cold water off the U.S. shelf. The authors write that the 2023 southward shift fits that mechanism.

Monthly JRA55 wind records from the Japan Meteorological Agency showed a change over the Grand Banks in 2023 too. The 2009 to 2018 pattern vanished. In its place came one that earlier studies linked to a fresher Northeast shelf.

Glen Gawarkiewicz, a WHOI senior scientist, led the study. He said that “the coastal ocean in the northeastern U.S. has a very complicated seesaw between flow from the north bringing cold fresh water and flow offshore from the Gulf Stream bringing warm, salty water, and those influences swing back and forth. It’s always in flux.”

Paula Fratantoni leads the oceanography branch at NOAA’s Northeast Fisheries Science Center and co-wrote the paper. “A relatively small shift in the trajectory of a large-scale current like the Gulf Stream can lead to dramatic changes locally,” she said.

Because the Gulf Stream and the winds are linked, the data can’t show which one drove the event. The authors write that targeted model experiments will be needed.

Argo floats are free-drifting robots that dive and resurface while recording temperature and salinity. One of them, float 4902601, took five profiles over the slope in June 2024.

From those casts, the team estimated 200 to 460 cubic miles (840 to 1,933 cubic kilometers) of extra fresh water, a rough figure.

A 2024 model study had predicted a pause in the region’s warming. The cold of May and June 2024 was stronger than that, though the model averaged over larger areas.

By October 2024 a warm core ring, an eddy shed by the Gulf Stream, had ended the pulse. The authors add that the event differed from the cold blob south of Greenland in location, duration and salinity.

How each species responded still needs study, the authors say. In 2024 the first signs came from fishing crews, through the Squid Squad calls and the CTD casts made from their boats.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans.

#3905. In Life, as in the Navy, He Knew How to Change Course

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

The following is an excerpt from my memoir, And Now, Back to Me: Stories from an Empty Nest. How this man responds when he finds out he needs to stay in the rehabilitation facility, when his heart had been set on coming home, is all you need to know about the man I am so lucky to call my father.

Ten minutes or so after it’s been decided that he needs to stay in the rehabilitation facility for another two weeks instead of going home as scheduled, my father leaves a long message on my phone. He makes a point of speaking slowly so that I can take notes. “Reets,” he says. “I need you to get some things from my house. My cell phone is in the little cubby in my car. It should be fully charged. But just in case, the charger is in your bedroom.” I love that he calls it my bedroom, even after all these years. “Also, I need stamps, address labels, and note cards, which you’ll find in the drawers of my desk. And don’t forget the thumbtacks.” He must mean paper clips. Yes, I’m sure he does. “I really appreciate you doing this for me. I don’t know what I’d do without you girls.”

When I arrive at the rehabilitation facility the next morning, his favorite Dunkin’ Donuts coffee in one hand, a bag filled with his requested supplies in the other, I watch him go about the business of setting up shop. Here, in the corner of his room, the nightstand has become his filing cabinet. His bedside table is now his desk. The rocker is his chair. And with that, he’s ready to go about conducting the daily routine of his life, keeping himself busy, making his days productive, no matter where he is or what circumstances he might find himself in.

Maybe his ability to make do with what he has, to make his home wherever he happens to find himself, was born out of necessity. Growing up the youngest of twenty-one children (not a typo!), he and his brothers and sisters ate dinner in shifts and slept three to a bed. If his childhood didn’t teach him enough about how to make the best of what was available, then joining the Navy at age eighteen was all the education he needed. He quickly learned to turn a mug of black Navy-issue coffee into a morning ritual; to express a genuine interest in other people’s lives, total strangers though they might be; and to appreciate beauty, even tucked away in a little sanctuary he made for himself on the deck of a ship while waiting for the sun to rise on an early-morning watch. That is how he made his way in the world from World War II to Korea to Vietnam, and all the places in between.

This way of his, this uncanny ability to accept what comes, to adapt and change course at a moment’s notice, lies at the core, in my opinion, of why he recently celebrated his ninety-second birthday. It’s not as though it’s been smooth sailing. It hasn’t. In the time since we lost Mom, his wife of sixty-three years, there have been heart ailments, a hip replacement, a broken wrist, a small stroke, a blood infection, and a stubborn gallstone. Yet always, there he was, rising to the occasion and finding a silver lining.

As we’ve traveled through these challenges together, he’s said to me many times, “I am where I am. I don’t know why. I’m not sure what’s coming at me next. But I’m grateful to be here, with all the love and care that surrounds me. Might as well make the best of it.”

His words—which echo through my head along with the vision of my dad sitting at his “desk,” the bedside table in his rehabilitation room—are instructive as I go forward, arranging and rearranging the bits and pieces of my new way of empty-nest living. His inspiration reminds me that our attitude means more than any of our circumstances. Our mindset possesses the awesome power to change the course of our moments, our days, and, yes, our lives.

Lucky for me, I have his example to lead the way. I see him calling his relatives and friends to find out how they’re doing, going out to lunch whenever he gets the chance, sending notes of appreciation to all those who help make his days brighter. He's always showing genuine interest in the lives of those around him and finding ways to help however he can.

That acceptance of his, that grace of embracing whatever moment he finds himself in—perhaps shared mostly by example—that’s Dad’s best advice of all.

#2149. Hasan Piker Sues Scott Jennings

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From the complaint in Piker v. Jennings; of course, this is just Piker's side of the story—I expect we'll get Jennings' in a forthcoming motion to dismiss:

On August 11, 2026, Scott Jennings falsely told a nationwide podcast audience that Hasan Piker was "out there calling for Black voters to be lynched." He falsely told his listeners that Mr. Piker had a "plan of returning our country to lynchings." And then he falsely told his social media followers that Mr. Piker "says the USA should resume lynchings against black people."

All of Mr. Jennings' accusations are false. Mr. Piker has never advocated the lynching of Black Americans, or anyone else. He has never called for a return to lynchings. And he has never proposed anything remotely resembling the supposed plan that Mr. Jennings falsely attributed to him.

Mr. Jennings did not merely mischaracterize something Mr. Piker said. He took a false, fabricated, and manipulated video of Mr. Piker, added a made-up quotation that Mr. Piker never uttered, and then made accusations that went beyond even what the manipulated footage purported to show. Mr. Jennings presented those accusations as fact, telling his viewers to "[l]isten for yourself" about Mr. Piker, knowing that he was playing a falsified video of Mr. Piker for those viewers, and completely disregarding the truth of Mr. Piker's actual words.

The underlying footage came from a broadcast nearly two years earlier on Twitch, an online streaming service owned by Amazon. On October 29, 2024, Mr. Piker was reacting to a debate between two Black political commentators, one liberal and one conservative. When the conservative commentator blamed "woke DAs" for policies supposedly harming Black Americans, Mr. Piker mocked him by sarcastically portraying that comment as nostalgia for an era before the civil rights movement. Laughing, Mr. Piker said:

He said, "We need more Klansmen, okay? Can we go back to a better time when lynchings were happening? This is what I like as a Black man. Also, there's no real racism in America." My man said, "woke DAs," dude. What are you saying?

Mr. Piker was plainly ridiculing the commentator's position. He was not endorsing it. The debate video remained visible behind him. Mr. Piker introduced the remarks with "He said," laughed as he delivered them, and concluded by questioning the commentator's argument. His viewers understood the sarcasm. Some laughed along. Others expressly described his comments as hyperbole. Still others predicted that someone would eventually remove the context and circulate the resulting footage as though Mr. Piker had been speaking sincerely.

That is precisely what happened. In August 2026, a 42-second video began circulating on social media that combined fragments of three separate Twitch broadcasts. The portions taken from Mr. Piker's October 2024 stream had been cropped, shortened, and rearranged. The underlying debate was removed from view. So was the contemporaneous audience commentary.

The words "He said," which identified the object of Mr. Piker's ridicule, were omitted. Mr. Piker's laughter and subsequent explanation were also removed. The resulting video portrayed sarcastic criticism of another as though it were an expression of Mr. Piker's own beliefs.

On August 11, 2026, Mr. Jennings broadcast that false video on The Scott Jennings Show, misrepresenting the manipulated video as authentic. But Mr. Jennings did not stop at repeating someone else's misleading edit. He added his own caption to the video, presented as a direct quotation from Mr. Piker:

PIKER: "LET'S GO BACK TO A BETTER TIME WHEN LYNCHINGS WERE HAPPENING" …

Mr. Piker never said those words. The original broadcast does not contain that statement. Neither does the manipulated video that Mr. Jennings chose to air. Mr. Jennings converted a sarcastic question, expressly attributed to and directed toward someone else, into a patently false first-person statement of Mr. Piker's own supposed beliefs, and then relayed that false statement to his viewers.

Mr. Jennings then went even further. After warning Black listeners to pay particular attention, Mr. Jennings accused Mr. Piker of "calling for Black voters to be lynched" and referred to "the Piker plan of returning our country to lynchings." Later that day, Mr. Jennings published the same false accusation on social media, telling his followers that Mr. Piker believed the United States "should resume lynchings against black people."

These were not expressions of disagreement with Mr. Piker's politics. Nor were they rhetorical characterizations of an admittedly controversial commentator. They were factual assertions about what Mr. Piker had said and advocated in his work. Each statement was demonstrably false.

The distinction matters. Mr. Jennings was free to criticize Mr. Piker, to condemn his political positions, or to argue that his commentary was offensive. Mr. Jennings was also free to play Mr. Piker's actual words, and provide his own commentary on what Mr. Piker actually said. Mr. Jennings was not free to do what he did: knowingly attribute to Mr. Piker statements that Mr. Piker never made, and then present those invented statements as evidence that Mr. Piker supports the racially motivated murder of Black Americans….

#541. A Routine Blood Test Could Predict Dementia Risk 15 Years in Advance

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A large study tracking more than 316,000 adults for up to 15 years suggests that a routine blood test may reveal important clues about future dementia risk.

A standard blood test taken during a routine health check could offer clues about dementia risk more than a decade later. In a study of more than 316,000 adults, researchers found that people with unusually low or high levels of hemoglobin were more likely to develop dementia, although the risks varied depending on the type of disease.

Hemoglobin is the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells. Low levels are commonly associated with anemia, while unusually high levels can result from smoking, dehydration, or certain heart and lung conditions. The findings suggest that this familiar blood measurement may also reflect broader aspects of physical health associated with brain aging.

Researchers analyzed health records from 316,542 Korean adults aged 40 and older who underwent national health screenings in 2004 and 2005. Participants were divided into five groups based on their hemoglobin levels, from the lowest 20% to the highest 20%. National health insurance records were then used to track new dementia diagnoses through the end of 2019.

Compared with the group that had the lowest dementia rate, participants in the lowest hemoglobin category had a 16% higher risk of developing dementia from any cause. Those in the highest category had a 7% greater risk.

The findings, published in the Journal of Alzheimer’s Disease and presented at the 39th ECNP Congress in Munich, also identified differences between Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia.

For Alzheimer’s disease, the increased risk was concentrated among people with the lowest hemoglobin levels. Participants in this group were 14% more likely to develop the disease than those in the reference group.

The relationship was different for vascular dementia (caused by reduced blood flow to the brain). People in the lowest hemoglobin category had a 19% higher risk, while those in the highest category had an 18% higher risk. However, the association did not follow a consistent pattern across all five hemoglobin groups. The researchers also observed clearer and more consistent relationships among women than men.

Lead researcher Dr. Hyun Soo Kim, an assistant professor at Dong-A University College of Medicine in Busan, South Korea, emphasized that the results do not establish a direct causal relationship.

“The findings do not show that hemoglobin itself causes dementia, but suggest that abnormal hemoglobin levels may be a visible marker of wider physical vulnerability relevant to brain health.”

Kim added, “Dementia is one of the fastest-growing health challenges in aging societies, and there are not many treatments and no cure. Identifying simple, accessible markers of future risk could help researchers and clinicians better understand how whole-body health relates to brain health over time.”

Hemoglobin is already measured in standard blood tests around the world, making it considerably more accessible than specialized brain imaging or newer dementia biomarkers. The researchers suggest that existing blood test results could potentially help scientists investigate connections between general physical health and long-term cognitive decline.

Kim explained, “A blood test most people already get at a routine check-up may say something about their brain health many years later. In this large Korean cohort, people with unusually low hemoglobin, and, to a smaller degree, those with unusually high hemoglobin, were more likely to develop dementia. This does not prove that hemoglobin causes dementia, but it supports the idea that general physical health, including the body’s ability to carry oxygen, matters for long-term brain health.”

Because the study was observational, it cannot determine whether abnormal hemoglobin levels contribute directly to dementia. Other medical conditions could influence both hemoglobin measurements and brain health. Early dementia processes might also affect nutrition, body weight, frailty, or anemia before the disease is formally diagnosed.

“We want to emphasize that the findings should not be interpreted as advice to take iron supplements, to raise hemoglobin levels, or to lower high hemoglobin levels in order to prevent dementia. An abnormal hemoglobin result should instead be discussed with a doctor, because it may point to underlying health issues that deserve attention,” Kim said.

The study drew on health records from South Korea, leaving questions about whether the same relationships apply to people from other ethnic, geographic, and medical backgrounds. Further research is also needed to establish whether identifying abnormal hemoglobin levels can meaningfully improve dementia risk assessment or prevention.

Dr. Lindsey Sinclair (University of Southampton), who was not involved in the research, commented:

“There is a growing body of evidence that physical health and brain health are intertwined. We are also increasingly aware that changes supporting better brain health, be they medical or lifestyle changes, are important in mid-life to reduce the risk of later dementia. While this study shows a possible link between hemoglobin levels and dementia risk, more work is required to understand if this link is present in people from a wider range of backgrounds; and what can best be done to reduce dementia risk in people with the lowest and highest levels of hemoglobin.”
