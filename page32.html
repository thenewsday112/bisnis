#817. Deadly train disaster deepens political crisis in Spain

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A deadly high-speed train collision in Spain has deepened a political crisis engulfing Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez’s government ahead of next month’s general election, as opposition parties demand accountability. The disaster, which killed at least 69 people, has sparked angry protests and calls for resignations.

The collision occurred near Urda in Toledo province when a high-speed train operated by Spain’s state railway company Renfe struck a stationary train. Audio recordings obtained by El Pais suggest controllers breached safety protocols despite repeated warnings from the stationary train’s driver. It was Spain’s second major railway disaster this year, following a collision near Adamuz in January that killed 46 people.

The tragedy has become a major issue in Spain’s election campaign, with the right-wing Vox party blaming Sanchez and the left-wing Frente Amplio coalition demanding political accountability.

“Beyond technical responsibilities, we demand the assumption of political responsibilities. Out of responsibility and humanity,” Frente Amplio said in a statement on Wednesday.

The coalition, which includes parties allied with Sanchez’s government, called for an independent audit of the railway network, describing two deadly high-speed train accidents in one year as “unacceptable.”

The leader of the main opposition People’s Party, Alberto Nunez Feijoo, also demanded an immediate investigation into the state of Spain’s railways, but stopped short of calling for resignations.

“Spain has the right to know what is happening with its infrastructure, what condition the tracks and safety systems are in,” Feijoo said.

Public anger spilled onto the streets of Madrid on Wednesday, where protesters clashed with police outside the Transport Ministry while demanding the resignations of Sanchez and Transport Minister Oscar Puente. Some demonstrators hurled flares and other objects at officers, who responded with tear gas.

Vox and its affiliated trade union Solidaridad staged a separate rally in central Madrid, where protesters carried a banner reading “Corruption kills.”

Sanchez has promised a thorough investigation, calling two such disasters in one year unacceptable. He has also pledged to strengthen railway safety while standing by Puente, according to El Pais.

The tragedy has added to the Socialist-led government’s troubles following parliamentary defeats and mass protests over soaring housing costs. Sanchez called early elections after parliament rejected key housing measures in September, with the vote scheduled for November 29.

#173. Study of 100,000 Australians seeks causes of mental health disorders and early onset cancer

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Sax Institute is recruiting for 18 and Up, an intergenerational study of wellbeing and ageing that follows its landmark 45 and Up study

Get our breaking news email, free app or daily news podcast

Researchers hope to better understand the drivers of major health challenges facing younger people, such as mental illness and rising cancer rates, through the recruitment of 100,000 people for Australia’s largest intergenerational study of wellbeing and ageing.

The independent and not-for-profit research institute the Sax Institute on Thursday launched the 18 and Up study, which will follow the health of those aged 18 to 45 throughout their lives.

Two decades ago, the institute started 45 and Up, now the largest population-based cohort study in the southern hemisphere, with more than 267,000 taking part ranging in age from 45 to older than 100.

Since then, participants have been repeatedly surveyed and their responses linked to other datasets, leading to significant discoveries about smoking, dementia prevention and differences in cardiovascular outcomes between men and women.

18 and Up’s scientific director, Adjunct Prof Justine Gatt, said there was a lack of long-term data for younger generations, with existing studies often only giving researchers information from a snapshot in time.

“Almost two in five young Australians aged 16 to 24 experienced a mental disorder in the past year, and that number has been rising,” Gatt, a neuroscientist and mental health researcher said. “Yet we still lack the long-term evidence needed to understand why.”

Suicide is the leading cause of death among Australians aged 15 to 44, and researchers have said there is an urgent need to understand and address driving factors to improve prevention efforts and mental health treatments.

Children and grandchildren of 45 and Up participants will be invited to participate, which Gatt said will help researchers understand how health is influenced by a complex mix of family history, lived experiences and social conditions, and how these factors interact across generations.

The chief executive of the Sax Institute, Dr Martin McNamara, said along with mental health, questions around cancer will also be an important part of the initial round of surveys.

Since 2000, there has been an increase in early-onset cancers – defined as those diagnosed beyond adolescence but before the age of 50 – in Australia and other parts of the world. This includes bowel, thyroid, breast and other cancers.

While scientists think increased screening, changing lifestyles and different exposures to risk factors in early life and young adulthood may all be factors, there have been calls for urgent research to better understand these and other influences.

Participants will be asked about health, lifestyle, social circumstances and background and, with consent, this information will be linked to other health records, such as cancer registries and Medicare.

“It’s important we follow people over time … so that we can figure out ways to fix and re-organise our health and care systems,” McNamara said.

“We’ll have a really good picture of their lives, the things that are important to them, their education, their employment, where they live.”

The study will evolve with participants to receive age-relevant questions, and emerging health conditions or pandemics will be factored in. It is being funded by a $4.5m National Health and Medical Research Council grant, with recruitment beginning in November.

A professor of epidemiology and public health at the Australia National University, Emily Banks, is a former scientific director at the Sax Institute who used 45 and Up study data to reveal in 2025 that smoking-related illnesses kill 66 Australians every day – more than previously thought.

Banks said large-scale cohort studies are powerful as they give researchers a window into the population and help detail what people are exposed to during their lives. She said the 45 and Up data has become an important collaborative resource.

It has been used by more than 800 researchers worldwide.

“We’ve had hundreds of researchers using the data. We also have around 10% of the population of New South Wales in that age [45+] group in the study,” she said.

“Some of the biggest advances in health have come from using this kind of information.”

The Marist Brothers, a Catholic order, holds a shocking clergy abuse record and was found by the royal commission to have shuffled religious brothers across its network of Australian schools when complaints arose, allowing them continued access to children.

The order continues to face a string of civil claims from survivors and is represented by major law firm Carroll & O’Dea, which has represented Catholic orders such as the Marist and Christian Brothers for decades.

Now, one survivor has turned their scrutiny on to the conduct of Carroll & O’Dea itself, asking the ACT supreme court to issue subpoenas to individual lawyers to explain an explosive document in which the firm advised the head of the Marist order that a known offender should be kept away from legal processes because he has “high honesty and integrity levels” and might implicate senior church officials.

The case involves a survivor known as CQ who alleges he was abused by Brother Kostka Chute, or John William Chute, in 1985 and 1986 at Marist College Canberra. Chute is a serial offender who has attracted abuse complaints across Marist schools in Randwick, Bondi, Hunters Hill, Lismore, Coogee and Canberra.

CQ is alleging the Marist Brothers knew of abuse complaints against Chute but concealed them, something the order denies.

CQ’s lawyers this week resurfaced a once-confidential letter from Carroll & O’Dea to the then head of the Marist Brothers, Br Jeffrey Crowe, in 2009, penned after the lawyers visited Chute in Canberra’s jail.

The lawyers had gone to see Chute to better understand whether the Marist Brothers would be on the hook for civil claims relating to his abuse or whether it would be indemnified by the Catholic church’s insurance arm, a question that partly depended on whether it had prior knowledge of Chute’s offending.

The letter from Carroll & O’Dea advised Crowe that Chute was in “good form” but that he was: “Regrettably quite up front about various discussions with Provincials over the years including Br Charles, Br Quentin, Br Weldon. I think we will have some problems with indemnity in due course but not immediately.

“Trouble is fundamentally he has high honesty and integrity levels and all of my ‘suggestions’ about not dumping on others excessively go out the window pretty quickly once there is a conversation under way.”

The lawyers advised the Marists that Chute – at that stage in jail for abusing six children – was not a “classic predator” and was a “good man with a fair bit of self-discernment”.

“We would need to keep him well away from the legal arena upon release,” the lawyers advised.

The airing of the contents of that letter caused significant issues in CQ’s case this week.

Porters Lawyers, the firm representing CQ, have subpoenaed two lawyers from Carroll & O’Dea to give evidence, and the court heard the move may raise potential issues alleging professional misconduct.

That required Carroll & O’Dea lawyers to themselves have legal representation and made its ongoing representation of the Marist Brothers impossible, because it would create a conflict of interest.

On Wednesday, Carroll & O’Dea told the court it would have to cease representing the Marist Brothers, causing a sudden halt to the trial and delays that will potentially stretch into next year.

The court also heard another matter against the Marist Brothers, also involving allegations against Chute, might be delayed.

Guardian Australia understands there are currently 15 cases against the Marist Brothers, represented by Carroll & O’Dea, involving Chute.

In a statement, the Marist Brothers said it could not comment because the proceedings were ongoing. A spokesperson said it expects the trial will resume “either later this year or in early 2027”.

Senior Marist Brothers have previously said that letter was sent as part of a chain of correspondence and was penned in the context of “serious doubts” about what Chute was claiming. The letter was examined in 2014 by the child abuse royal commission, which decided not to call its author to give evidence.

Crowe was asked about the letter at the royal commission, including whether it showed Chute was “seen as a liability that may impact upon the Marist Brothers and one that they need to sweep under the carpet”.

“I agree that he does give the impression there of some liability,” Crowe said. “However, I would also believe – because this, once again, is all part of another series of communications – that there were serious doubts as to the evidence being given by John Chute.”

Carroll & O’Dea was approached for comment.

ROME, Oct 29 - Italy's government will allow motorists to personalise the number plates of their vehicles, in a revenue-raising move that follows the example of the Unites States, Britain and other countries.

Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni announced the measure on Wednesday as she unveiled Italy's 2027 budget law, the last before a general election due next year, describing the number plate initiative as a "colourful note".

"One will be able to indulge oneself more or less as one sees fit, obviously at a cost. Something tells me that Italians' creativity could shine here," Meloni said.

Personalised plates will cost about €600 ($669) for cars and €360 for motor-bikes, according to a decree.

A Treasury official declined to estimate how much revenue the government expects the measure to generate.

Italian car number plates are currently made of a string of two letters, three numbers and two letters. The extent to which motorists will be allowed to stray from the standard formula is unclear.

The reform has triggered a wave of memes and jokes on social media, amid concerns that personalized license plates could open the flood-gates to insults and profanities.

One meme showed Meloni dressed up as Marie Antoniette, holding a platter of licence plates and saying, "Let them eat personalised plates" - a play on the famous phrase by the 18th century French queen "Let them eat cake".

Opposition parties on the centre-left were less enthusiastic, saying the licence-plate initiative was a distraction from more pressing issues that budget failed to address.

#520. Young Iranians turn from opium to cannabis and meth

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Young Iranians are turning from opium, their parents’ drug, to cannabis, methamphetamine and a widening range of synthetic substances, treatment specialists and officials say, as unemployment, hardship and uncertainty about the future reshape the country’s drug use.

Opium is still common among older users, Tejarat News reported. The younger generation’s drugs go by other names: cannabis, known locally as gol (bud), and methamphetamine, known as shisheh (glass).

In July, the head of prevention at Iran's Drug Control Headquarters, Zahra Abedini, said the highest prevalence of drug use was among people aged 24 to 35.

She estimated the prevalence of drug use among university students at between 2.5 and 2.7 percent, although the figures were based on existing studies. A new nationwide survey is underway to provide updated data on addiction in Iran.

Kamran Fateh, an addiction specialist, told Tejarat News that drug consumption should not be viewed simply as a matter of personal choice.

Behind many cases of drug use lies a combination of social, economic and psychological factors, he said.

"A young person facing anxiety, unemployment, a sense of having no future, emotional setbacks, economic pressure or family tensions may look for a quick way to temporarily escape those pressures," Fateh added.

Cannabis use and the changing drug market

The Drug Control Headquarters' director of treatment has identified cannabis and hashish as among the country's most commonly consumed substances, warning about the rapid emergence of synthetic drugs and new psychoactive compounds.

One concern highlighted in the report is the growing normalization of cannabis use among young people, some of whom regard it as less dangerous than traditional narcotics or believe occasional consumption carries little risk of dependence.

Fateh warned that misconceptions about the safety of certain substances were among the biggest obstacles to prevention.

"When a substance is described as 'recreational,' 'herbal' or 'less harmful,' people's awareness of the risk of dependence decreases," he said.

Addiction treatment specialists in several provinces have reported cases of young people beginning with cannabis before experimenting with other substances, although such progression is not inevitable.

According to prices reported by addiction treatment specialists, a cannabis cigarette costs around 1.5 million rials ($0.55), while methamphetamine sells for approximately 2.5 million to 7 million rials ($0.92 to $2.59) per gram.

Heroin prices range from 2 million to 7 million rials ($0.74 to $2.59) per gram, compared with 1.21 million to 3 million rials ($0.45 to $1.11) for opium. The figures are estimates rather than official market rates.

Experts have also warned that newly emerging substances are increasingly marketed with claims of reducing stress, boosting energy, improving concentration or promoting weight loss.

Economic hardship and uncertainty fuel vulnerability

Fateh said the expanding illegal drug market was making prevention increasingly difficult as suppliers introduced new substances and combinations to meet changing demand.

He also said that families sometimes discover drug use only after occasional consumption has developed into a more serious problem, adding that punishment and intimidation are not necessarily effective responses.

"If society focuses only on 'saying no to drugs' but offers no solutions to problems such as unemployment, social isolation, depression, identity crises, economic pressure and a lack of healthy recreational activities, many of the underlying causes of drug use will remain," he said.

Fateh highlighted the relationship between economic hardship and addiction, saying that rising drug prices could drive dependent users toward cheaper, unfamiliar or more dangerous substances.

"If a dependent person does not have access to effective, affordable and accessible treatment, higher prices may even push them toward more dangerous alternatives," he said.

#833. Western Europe is falling apart – we should prepare for what comes next

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

By Timofey Bordachev, Program Director of the Valdai Club

For Russia, developments in the rest of Europe matter primarily insofar as they affect our national interests. Above all, this concerns security given that, for centuries, Russia has faced its greatest challenges from the West, and there’s little reason to assume that this historical pattern has disappeared.

Western Europe’s leading countries now stand at a crossroads, yet the choice confronting them isn’t simply between competing models of domestic development, it’s between war and peace with their neighbors, foremost among them Russia, whose size and historical role have repeatedly made it the principal obstacle to Western European expansion.

The unrest sweeping several European countries this autumn marks a new phase in the continent’s systemic crisis as it reflects the convergence of two long-term problems that much of Europe’s political establishment has proved incapable of resolving.

The first is the exhaustion of the socioeconomic model that sustained Western European prosperity throughout the Cold War and its aftermath. That model depended on Europe’s privileged position in the global economy and the absence of serious competitors beyond the Western world, but China’s rise, combined with increasingly limited resources, has undermined those advantages.

Closer economic integration with Russia, and access to its enormous natural wealth, might have offered Western Europe a final opportunity to preserve its exceptional position, but instead regional leaders allowed their own arrogance to prevent an accommodation with Moscow because they couldn’t accept that Russia wished to live peacefully with its neighbors, but on terms consistent with its own interests.

The second problem is Western Europe’s longstanding absence of genuine sovereignty.

Integration into the American-led NATO system has deprived even major powers such as Germany, France, and Britain of the freedom to pursue fundamental political change and one of the alliance’s practical functions has been to preserve the existing political order within its member states.

Any serious attempt to transform that order would require a reassessment of relations with Washington and such independence is something these European governments have long been unwilling, or unable, to contemplate.

Together, these structural weaknesses have left Western Europe’s political and economic establishments effectively paralyzed.

For nearly two decades, governments have substituted the appearance of activity for meaningful reform. Successive emergencies, involving migration, the pandemic, hostility towards Russia, and preparations for military confrontation, have allowed leaders to avoid addressing deeper problem and this helps explain the temptation to interpret today’s unrest as another exercise in political manipulation. Emmanuel Macron, for example, has repeatedly demonstrated an ability to survive the consequences of disastrous decisions through tactical maneuvering.

Yet it would be a mistake to dismiss the protests as artificial or orchestrated.

In France, the initial demonstrations were driven by schoolchildren and university students demanding more teachers and improvements to an education system suffering from declining standards, but these grievances are hardly unique to France and they reflect broader weaknesses in modern education, including funding arrangements that encourage institutions to compete for pupils rather than concentrate on teaching.

By early October, the French protests had expanded as other groups affected by Macron’s liberal economic policies joined the demonstrations. The authorities blamed the political left, while unrest escalated into rioting and looting.

Similar discontent emerged in Spain and Portugal, where soaring housing costs became the immediate trigger. Both countries have spent years occupying subordinate positions within the European Union, with little influence over its economic priorities, even as their former politicians find comfortable positions in Brussels.

Western Europe has consequently entered a period in which conventional political competition is increasingly accompanied by mass protest and the participation of young people is particularly significant.

In France, the EU’s most important military power and its only nuclear-armed member, it was students who provided the initial momentum. While much of the adult electorate has descended into political apathy under Macron, younger generations retain aspirations for a better future and their grievances are genuine. So, too, is the wider crisis that has brought people onto the streets.

There are therefore grounds to believe that public dissatisfaction may eventually bring new political forces to power, which from Russia’s perspective raises an obvious possibility in that perhaps Western Europe’s next generation of leaders will prove more reasonable on questions of security and relations with Moscow.

Such hopes, however, may be premature because they reflect less our actual experience of European politics than our desire to imagine a Western Europe capable of recovering its political judgement.

Indeed, the objective nature of the present crisis makes its possible consequences especially troubling and European history offers little reassurance that profound domestic upheaval produces peaceful relations with neighbouring countries. On the contrary, revolutionary transformations have repeatedly generated external aggression, and the French Revolution provides one example.

The upheavals of the late 18th century were followed by wars against neighboring states and, eventually, Napoleon’s attempt to dominate Europe.

The extraordinary energy released by revolution demanded an outlet, while new rulers also needed resources and opportunities for enrichment beyond their borders.

As Henry Kissinger observed in his early historical work, the internal foundations of Napoleon’s expansionism helped prevent a lasting accommodation with Russia after the disastrous campaign of 1812.

Germany offers an even more destructive precedent, where the political upheavals of the 20th century ultimately brought to power a regime whose ambitions extended far beyond Germany’s borders. The conquest of neighboring countries was followed by an attack on the Soviet Union and the most devastating war in human history.

Again, internal political transformation was accompanied by an effort to direct national energies towards external expansion and the consequences of that war fundamentally altered Europe’s position as many of its leading states lost much of their effective sovereignty and became dependent on the United States for their security.

Paradoxically, that dependence may now offer some protection against the most dangerous consequences of Europe’s internal crisis because governments that lack genuine strategic independence may also lack the freedom to embark upon adventures of their own choosing.

Nevertheless, Russia can’t base its security on the assumption that European political upheaval will produce more sensible leaders even if the temptation to hope for a peaceful transformation of Europe is understandable. Hope isn’t a strategy, and history provides ample grounds for caution.

Russia must therefore prepare not for the miracle of a Western Europe reconciled to peaceful coexistence, but for the possibility that its internal crisis will once again find expression in confrontation abroad.

Whatever political forces eventually emerge from the present turmoil, preparing for a military confrontation with our European neighbors must remain one of Russia’s central strategic priorities.
