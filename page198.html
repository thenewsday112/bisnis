The country’s most prestigious journalism school has initiated “a critical evaluation” of the Master of Arts program and is pausing admissions to it.

The Columbia Journalism School will pause admissions for its Master of Arts program, dean Jelani Cobb announced on Thursday. The country’s most prestigious journalism school also announced a new online, part-time degree.

In a note to alumni of the M.A. program, Cobb wrote that the administration has “initiat[ed] a critical evaluation” of the M.A. curriculum. “To give this review the focus it deserves, we are temporarily pausing admissions for the upcoming admissions cycle.”

The school’s admissions page has been updated with a similar message.

Columbia will continue to offer its Master of Science programs in Journalism and Data Journalism. Current M.A. students are not affected by the pause. The estimated cost of attendance for the nine-month M.A. for the 2026-2027 year is roughly $120,000. The M.S. costs around $130,000 and the year-long M.S. in Data Journalism costs about $180,000. According to Columbia, around 80% of students receive some sort of financial aid.

The M.A. program is a newer degree — created in 2005 — and was designed for working journalists with three or more years of professional experience “to go deeper into a specialized subject.” The M.S. degree, described as “the foundational program of the Journalism School,” is for students with “zero to three years” of experience.

The school also announced it will, for the first time in its history, offer an online degree. The new online Master of Science in Journalism program will be part-time with students earning a degree over two years.

“This new program brings an Ivy League J-School education directly to students across the United States,” reads one post from the Columbia Journalism School on X. “With guidance from CJS’s faculty of working journalists, you can develop your reporting skills while continuing to live and work in your community.”

Roughly half of enrolled M.A. candidates at Columbia Journalism School are international students, Aditya Soni and Edmund Lee reported for Reuters. Uncertainty in immigration policies and visa challenges under the Trump administration caused “a sharp drop” in international applications to Columbia, according to Reuters, and contributed to the program’s pause.

#1011. Salt Bread (Shio Pan)

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Welcome to the Bon Appétit Bake Club, a community of curious bakers. Each month senior Test Kitchen editors Jesse Szewczyk and Shilpa Uskokovic share a must-make recipe and dive deep into why it works. This month we welcome deputy food editor Hana Asbrink to be our guest baker. Come bake and learn with us—and don’t forget to join the Bake Club group chat over on Substack.

The version of this recipe that appeared in our November 2026 print issue has the incorrect bake time listed. The recipe that follows features the correct total bake times of 15–18 minutes (please see step 8).

Salt bread is a wondrous buttery roll known as shio pan in Japanese or sogeum ppang in Korean. It originated in Japan in 2014 and quickly became popularized in Korea soon thereafter, taking off in the post-Covid era around 2021. It’s formed by rolling each ball of dough around a nub of salted butter. As it bakes, the butter melts and oozes out of the bun, “frying” and crisping up the bottom. If you can imagine a warm dinner roll meeting a flaky croissant, you’re getting closer.

You’ll often find a range of salt bread, some leaning closer to sturdy bread with a tighter crumb and others resembling a softer milk bread. In all salt bread cases, there are a few key identifiers: (1) a deeply golden exterior, with the aforementioned crisp bottom; (2) the signature hollow in the middle (where the pat of butter once stood) when you tear the bun in half; and (3) a sprinkle of flaky or coarse salt atop the bun.

In this riff, I use an enriched dough, bringing in qualities of a soft milk bread into the mix. With the bread flour, the end result is a lovely, crowd-pleasing bun that’s great for snacking on its own or enjoying alongside a special holiday meal. If there’s one note to highlight: Ensure you are starting with a fresh pack of instant (or “rapid-rise”) yeast. This will make all the difference in the rise and the eventual yield.

1 hour 30 minutes (plus 3 hours 30 minutes for resting)

Stir 2 Tbsp. (16 g) flour and ½ cup water in a small skillet, preferably nonstick, over medium heat with a heatproof rubber spatula to combine and cook, stirring constantly, until thickened to the texture of mayonnaise, about 2 minutes; remove from heat. Add 4 Tbsp. unsalted butter, cut into small pieces and stir until melted. Transfer tangzhong to the bowl of a stand mixer.

Add 2 large eggs, ½ cup whole milk, ¼ cup (50 g) sugar, 1 Tbsp. Diamond Crystal or 1¾ tsp. Morton kosher salt, and 1 ¼-oz. envelope instant yeast (about 2¼ tsp.) to tangzhong; stir vigorously by hand with rubber spatula until incorporated. Add remaining 4 cups (500 g) flour, attach bowl to stand mixer fitted with a dough hook or paddle, and mix on low speed until dough is smooth and soft (it may stick to bottom of bowl but should mostly clear sides), 20–25 minutes. Pinch off a small piece of dough and stretch it between your thumbs and index fingers on both hands. The dough should be able to stretch thin enough to let light through without breaking. If it tears, the gluten is not yet developed enough. Keep mixing until it can stretch; it may take up to 30 minutes.

Transfer dough to a work surface (no need to flour) and shape into a ball. Lightly grease stand mixer bowl with vegetable oil. Return dough to bowl, cover tightly with a plate or plastic wrap, and let rise in a warm, draft-free spot until nearly doubled in size, about 2 hours. (For optimal flavor, let dough sit at room temperature 30 minutes, then cover tightly or transfer to an airtight container and chill at least 8 hours and up to 2 days to proof slowly.)

Meanwhile, cut 1 cup (2 sticks) salted butter into 1-Tbsp. pieces and arrange in a single layer on a large plate or small baking sheet. Chill in freezer until ready to use.

Transfer dough to a surface (no need to bring to room temperature before shaping if you chilled it). Using a bench scraper or paring knife, cut dough into 70–75 g portions (you’ll get 12–16 portions depending on how much your dough has risen). Working with 1 portion at a time, cup your hand over dough and drag across surface, moving your hand in a rapid circular motion to form into a tight ball (cold dough is easier to work with; rolling into balls on an unfloured, ungreased counter creates friction and helps the shaping process). Place on a baking sheet and cover with plastic wrap; let sit 15 minutes.

Uncover dough (save plastic wrap) and, working one at a time, carefully roll each ball into a cone shape with your hands (you want them about 4" on the wider end). Return to baking sheet and re-cover with reserved plastic wrap. Let dough rest 15 minutes.

Place a cone in front of you on surface with wider end facing toward you (keep remaining cones covered in plastic wrap). Lightly flatten with your palm, then roll out dough lengthwise to make an isosceles triangle about 12" long with a 3½"–4"-wide base (short side of triangle). Place 1 Tbsp. frozen salted butter at base of dough and roll up (not too tightly) like a croissant. Arrange, seam side down, back on baking sheet. Repeat with remaining cones and salted butter (you may not need all of the butter; save it for another use). Let rolls sit at room temperature until slightly puffed, about 1 hour.

Place a rack in lowest position in oven; preheat to 425°. Remove plastic from rolls. Lightly mist rolls with water from spray bottle; sprinkle with flaky sea salt. Bake 10 minutes. Rotate baking sheet front to back and continue baking rolls until golden brown on top and bottom, 5–8 minutes more (keep an eye that they don’t burn). Remove from oven (there will be melted butter on baking sheet, so be careful) and, using a heatproof pastry brush, brush butter on baking sheet over rolls. Let rolls cool at least 10 minutes before serving.

Do Ahead: Rolls can be baked 2 hours ahead. Store at room temperature.

#4183. Could quitting ultra-processed food boost your mental health?

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A small preliminary study from UC San Francisco adds to mounting evidence that eating fewer ultra-processed foods may help reduce symptoms of depression.

Published Sept. 23 in JAMA Psychiatry, the study was funded by the National Institutes of Health. Researchers believe it is the first study to compare depression symptoms in the same participants before and after they moved from their usual ultra-processed diet to healthier foods.

The pilot study included only 20 people, but senior author Andrew Krystal, MD, Ray and Dagmar Dolby Distinguished Professor in UCSF's departments of Psychiatry and Behavioral Sciences and Neurology, said it "successfully established the viability of carrying out this research in a larger-scale trial."

Depression Symptoms Improved After a Major Diet Shift

Participants cut their intake of ultra-processed foods by more than 85%. After doing so, they showed moderate to large improvements across three different measures of depression. Anxiety scores also improved modestly.

Ultra-processed foods include products such as packaged snacks, fast food, sugary drinks, and other industrially manufactured foods. These products may contain ingredients including preservatives, artificial coloring, flavor enhancers, and emulsifiers.

"Reducing the amount of ultra-processed food in our diet can be challenging, because it's highly palatable, cheap, and convenient," said corresponding author Nyasha Chagwedera, MD, PhD, assistant professor in the UCSF Department of Psychiatry and Behavioral Sciences. "Ultra-processed food triggers dopamine, the pleasure hormone, which promotes craving. Food companies work with scientists to find just the right amount of sugar, salt, and fat that make you want to eat more and more."

The participants had an average age of 44, and 70% were female. Along with depression, 14 participants also had anxiety. Everyone in the study had a high BMI and at least one metabolic disorder -- such as hypertension, diabetes, or elevated LDL cholesterol.

Researchers divided the participants into two groups. One group stayed on its usual diet for four weeks, while the other switched to a diet low in ultra-processed foods. Participants selected and bought their own food with guidance from the research team. After four weeks, the groups switched diets.

Chagwedera, who founded the UCSF Metabolic and Mental Health Program, said one possible explanation for the connection between ultra-processed food and depression involves what she described as a "leaky, out-of-balance gut." According to this idea, ultra-processed foods may damage the protective lining of the gut.

"This gut damage can create low-grade, chronic inflammation throughout the body that either directly causes depression or makes people more vulnerable to it."

"In other words, junky processing may harm your gut in ways that ripple up to your brain."

Because the study was small, the findings are still preliminary. The researchers now plan to investigate the effect in a larger group, with enrollment for phase 2 expected to begin in 2028.

OmVed Gardens in North London is rather chic. The brainchild of World Health Organization activists who wanted to create a space that provided food education with an emphasis on diverting food waste, they asked their friend Paul Gazerwitz, a Brooklynite by birth whose international portfolio is decidedly upscale, to help them develop a pocket of post-industrial land. The result is miraculously successful: OmVed is not only gorgeous, but it has soul. With views overlooking London, yet quiet and tucked away, it feels like a very nice bit of countryside, in microcosm. Let’s take a closer look.

Photography via OmVed Gardens unless otherwise noted.

The garden is in Highgate Village and easy to reach by bus. Hidden down an alleyway off Highgate High Street and perched on a hilltop, it has the fairytale look of a small village inside a forest clearing, with shingled wooden buildings of varying roof pitches, surrounded by gardens and paths that meander through the steep site.

Built in collaboration with Gazerwitz by Smerin Architects, the glorified huts (or “pavilions”) are for seed-saving, growing, cooking, and teaching; in addition, there’s a roof garden over the kitchen and an outdoor amphitheater. They are all connected, and once you have found OmVed, you will want to linger.

Entry to the garden requires a ticket to one of the frequent courses and dinners (at various price points) or via the free art show in summer. OmVed is a community interest company which feels like a well-oiled private enterprise, staffed by people who love their job.

Visitors come from far and wide, with a steady flow of loyal regulars from London. At the heart of the enterprise is human connection—and health that is both environmental and plant-based, communicated through food.

Activity in the kitchen never stops: There is always something to pickle or preserve, and discarded offcuts, such as strawberry tops or tomato cores, make their way into fruit leather or shrubs for drinks and dressings. At the end of the season, there are talks, demonstrations, and hands-on making; the upcoming Fermentation Celebration explores the “Four K’s”: kimchi, kombucha, kefir, and kraut. More of the harvest is preserved in an underground fridge—which is really a large hole in the ground like an old-fashioned root cellar, cooled by its subterranean position rather than external power. Lined with plywood, it’s as chic and efficient as the buildings above-ground.

The site has had many iterations: It was once a market garden for growing flowers for Highgate Cemetery (just down the hill); a drover’s yard, providing pasture for cattle on the way to market at Smithfield; and the worst kind of garden center, concreted over and no stranger to chemicals. From a developer’s point of view, it was an awkward site, which saved it from housing construction and allowed mature trees to thrive around the edges. A lower story of native trees and shrubs has been added slowly; cherry shows up in two ways, as a wild tree around the periphery and as an espaliered culinary tree by the kitchen garden.

Seed-saving is also a public-facing endeavor, with free seeds sent out to participants through the Seed Saving Network, of which this writer is one (I am currently growing and monitoring tomatoes, both orange and black, white cucumber, “giant” parsley, and stripey eggplant, all grown from the network’s seeds).

All of the materials at OmVed are top-notch, from wooden cobblestones by the kitchen, to a better class of cinderblock arranged as a holding wall by a sunny patio, for displaying rare edible plants and providing habitat. The aesthetic could be called industrial-rustic.

Up to 400 people can pass through the garden in a day, with concurrent events spilling out into different spaces, as well as quieter areas over the two acres. The genius is in the design. The steep site has lent itself to a contoured approach; the main path is based on a squiggly line that Paul made on a piece of paper during his first visit.

The lack of cars or parking adds to the garden’s feeling of enchantment. Replacing the former parking lot at the bottom of the site are two ponds that are replenished with rainwater as well as the overflow from several springs, which occur naturally here (a chemist on Highgate High Street used to sell bottled Highgate spring water).
