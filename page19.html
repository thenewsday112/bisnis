#2692. “When They Say Go, Just Go.” How a Colorado Couple Survived 11 Hours Inside a Flooded Motorhome.

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Three dogs belonging to Hank and Stacey Wills sit inside a Colorado National Guard helicopter after being hoisted from the couple’s flooded motorhome near Pagosa Springs, Colorado, on October 23. (Photo: Photo: Courtesy of Colorado National Guard)

Hank and Stacey Wills tried to evacuate their property ten miles southeast of Pagosa Springs, Colorado, on October 22, when their motorhome stalled in rising floodwaters. Heavy rain had saturated southwestern Colorado and sent rivers and streams across Archuleta County into roads and low-lying areas.

As the Rio Blanco River continued to swell, the current swept the motorhome downstream and onto its side, trapping the couple, their three dogs, and two cats for roughly 11 hours before a Colorado National Guard helicopter crew hoisted them to safety.

“As we’re going down the middle of the channel, water just kept pouring into the motorhome,” Hank said during an October 26 press conference. “There’s only so long it can do that before it starts to sink.”

When floodwaters overtook an RV the CONG responded, performing a hoist rescue from a UH-60 Blackhawk. Two people, three dogs, and two cats were saved from the trapped vehicle. pic.twitter.com/rvEoDV09Tf

During the press conference, the Willses and Archuleta County responders reconstructed the night-long ordeal and the rescue effort that followed.

The emergency began with a storm the couple believed they could handle, Hank said. A year earlier, southwestern Colorado endured historic flooding. The Wills’ property flooded then, too, but the water never reached the inside of their house. Expecting this storm to be less severe, they set out sandbags, covered structures with tarps, and moved their chickens and rabbits out of harm’s way.

Early on the afternoon of October 22, Archuleta County officials issued mandatory evacuation orders for low-lying areas along four river corridors. A sheriff’s deputy also came to the Wills property and told them to evacuate, Hank said.

The couple did not leave immediately. Hank said they were still trying to secure the property and animals, while monitoring water levels at an upstream diversion dam. Based on what they had seen during the 2025 flooding, they believed they had roughly five hours after the water peaked upstream before it reached them.

“We thought we could probably still ride this out,” Hank said.

By late evening, floodwater had breached their sandbag barrier. The couple gathered their pets, climbed into the motorhome, and tried to evacuate.

Around 10:30 P.M., Hank drove onto a flooded road, and the engine stalled. They called for a tow truck, but authorities closed nearby bridges before it could reach rescuers said in the press conference.

Archuleta County Sheriff’s Deputy Craig Tamburelli said he arrived around 2:15 A.M. on October 23. He tossed Hank a rescue line, which Hank secured to a table leg inside the RV. Tamburelli anchored the other end on shore and called for search and rescue backup.

Within about 20 minutes, the current undercut the motorhome and tilted it toward the driver’s side, Tamburelli said.

“We started whistling and yelling, ‘Hey, this is going to go over soon,’” Hank said.

Tamburelli shouted for them to get out when the RV suddenly lurched, pulling the rescue line hard enough to rip the table loose and strike Hank and Stacey on its way out the window. Swept downstream, the motorhome tipped onto its side and crashed into the couple’s fence, about 75 yards from their house. Stacey fell through the bathroom doorway with two dogs landing on top of her, but the couple moved the animals above the rising water and huddled together on a narrow bathroom ledge.

Rescuers, meanwhile, had temporarily lost track of the motorhome, according to Tamburelli.

Tamburelli drove downstream, spotted the motorhome from the highway, and saw Hank signaling with a flashlight, but swiftwater teams could not safely reach the vehicle. Archuleta County Office of Emergency Management Deputy Director Stanley Okazaki arrived around 6 A.M. and deployed a drone equipped with thermal imaging, a light, and a microphone. Responders used the drone to relay updates and monitor Stacey for hypothermia while arranging a helicopter rescue.

During the wait, the couple realized Gomer, one of their two cats, had disappeared.

“We thought maybe he’d crawled out the window because we were so close to our home, and there was a tree within jumping distance. We couldn’t find him,” Hank said.

Roughly 30 minutes before the helicopter extraction, Hank tried to close one of the RV’s drawers, but it wouldn’t shut. He pushed again and heard a meow. Gomer had been inside the drawer the entire time.

The couple’s three dogs handled the wait surprisingly well, Hank said. They whined and wanted to go outside, but “there wasn’t much panic.”

A rescue technician descended to the motorhome to rig evacuation hoists. With only one harness for the dogs, the crew hauled them up one by one, then lowered the gear back down. Rescuers improvised for the cats, tucking one into a jacket and—to Gomer’s chagrin—zipping him into a backpack.

“Gomer did panic in the bag,” Hank said. “He kind of looked like a little alien trying to escape from my body, but everybody else did exceptionally well.”

All seven people and animals reached Stevens Field near Pagosa Springs safely around 1 P.M. on October 23.

The Wills family are now staying with family while assessing damage to their property. Water never entered their house, though mud and debris damaged several outbuildings. Their chickens and rabbits also survived.

After spending roughly 11 hours inside an overturned RV waiting for rescuers to find a safe way to reach them, Hank had a straightforward message for anyone facing an evacuation order.

“Know what you can get out, what you can’t get out, and limit yourself to the things that you can,” he said. “When they say go, just go.”

#4318. Why roaming packages have become an anachronism in the age of app-first travel

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

PARTNER CONTENT: Connectivity at all times, abroad

Travelling in the modern day and age differs significantly from what it looked like a few short decades ago. Today, it is all about apps: you need applications for navigation, booking accommodation, buying tickets for events, following the local news, staying in touch with friends and family, and more. That also means that you depend on connectivity at all times while abroad.

The problem is that a lot of people still rely on traditional roaming, while the roaming model is out of date. You cannot choose a specific mobile internet plan that suits your travel plans. Instead, operators have split the world into zones, and each zone has its own pricing, limits, and permissions.

That is why many have turned to alternative ways of staying connected, and so far, the travel eSIM has emerged as the most practical solution. Companies like the Swiss eSIM pioneer, Yesim, have recognized the problem that travelers are facing and have come up with a solution. They let you pick a specific plan that will fit your trip. That way, you get to tailor your connectivity experience in advance. Furthermore, you even get to see the price in advance and know exactly what you will have to pay.

The problem with roaming is that your home operator decides what your internet will be like abroad. When you try to book a flight or a hotel room, you will try to get the best deal possible. That means comparing providers, prices, any potential benefits that different providers offer, and the like. However, with roaming, you can’t do any of that. The terms of your existing home contract still apply, and connectivity abroad just gets added to it.

That means that your operator decides what zones are available, what partner networks you can access, and, most importantly, how much they will charge you. You, as the user, can only accept their terms or choose to look for a different way to secure an internet connection. In other words, there is no flexibility and no participation on your end, you can only take it or leave it.

Take EE as an example - the official terms for their EE Travel Data pass for Canada and the US say that the user gets 500 MB of data per day. This is not nearly enough for someone who will depend on their phone for everything, from paying for food to comparing ticket prices and booking accommodation. The limit is the same for countless other countries, including Australia, China, India, Mexico, New Zealand, South Africa, Thailand, Turkey, and the UAE.

It gets even worse for other countries, including Japan, Singapore, South Korea, and Indonesia. For these, the daily allowance is only 150 MB. That essentially forces you to ration your data use very carefully and pick what you are going to use it for.

Some providers have introduced daily packages as a secondary roaming option. O2 Travel Bolt On roaming option for Pay Monthly customers charges £7 for every 24 hours of use. They start charging you as soon as you make a call, send a text, or use data for anything. It happens even if your phone updates an app automatically in the background, without you even knowing about it.

As a result, you get barely any connectivity, but still have to pay the full price of this deal. For some, that might be fine, but if you aren’t aware of it, you can return home to a surprise bill that you now have to pay.

As mentioned, travel eSIM emerged as an alternative to the roaming model. The first thing to note about it is the difference between an eSIM and a travel eSIM.

An eSIM is a chip that is built into most modern smartphones, tablets, and similar devices. This chip allows users to install operator profiles offered by travel eSIM providers. This can be done remotely, without having to go to the provider’s shop. It’s as simple as downloading an app.

Travel eSIM uses this chip to create a digital service. That typically includes the provider’s app, tariffs, partnered mobile networks, full control of your data, and even top-ups and customer support, should you need them.

The system is also quite practical, as you get to set up everything in advance. You don’t have to wait to enter another country to secure your connectivity, but can instead do it from home before you leave on your trip. Start by checking your device’s compatibility with eSIM technology. If it’s compatible, you can download the operator’s app, pick a package for the region you plan to visit, and install the appropriate profile. Then, sit on the plane, travel to your destination, and activate the profile when you get there. You get instant connectivity without having to stand in line at a SIM kiosk, rely on Wi-Fi coverage, or pay high roaming prices.

If you are still not sure if a travel eSIM is the right option, consider the following: A one-week trip from the UK to the US will have you paying £7 per day if you are an O2 Travel user. For seven days abroad, that will end up being £49. Alternatively, if you use Yesim’s unlimited package for the US for seven days, the price is only £18.27 as of the time of writing. You buy it upfront, and it won’t activate until you start using it after arriving at your destination.

When selecting a plan, you start with the trip itself. In other words, you click on the country or region that you plan to visit. You can also enter the length of your stay there, and how much data you expect you will need. Then, select a tariff to match the parameters.

All terms of the deal will be known to you in advance, including the country that is covered by the plan, how long the plan will last, how much data you get, and how much it all costs. Best of all, everything is managed in the app, so you don’t really have to get in contact with anyone.

A package can be selected for a single country, an entire region, or a route that includes several countries. You prepare all of this at home, and activate it after arrival. You even get to keep your home number active, since you don’t have to replace your SIM card. That way, any banking messages, service notifications, important calls, 2-Factor Authentication (2FA) texts, and the like can come through and reach you at any time.

If you need a travel eSIM provider, consider the Swiss company Yesim. The firm has been in the travel eSIM market since 2019, becoming one of the pioneers in this sector. Today, it provides connectivity to over 4 million users across 200+ destinations. For travelers, this means that they can rely on one service that they are familiar with regardless of whether they are going on a short holiday in one country or a lengthy, complex trip that will include multiple nations.

Yesim’s position as a global provider of connectivity services is also reflected in the fact that the company has become an official partner of the World Players Super League - the largest golf tour in the world. The League holds over 1,800 tournaments in more than 50 countries every year, and is one of the largest events of this kind.

The same global scale is evident in Yesim’s pricing options. The firm offers a flexible tariff range, especially when it comes to its most popular packages. For example, the Global Package covers 80+ countries, while the Global Plus Package covers 140+ destinations. These will likely suit those who tend to travel a lot and want to pay a fixed budget. But, if your data needs are unpredictable, you can always pick the Pay & Fly tariff - Yesim’s pay-as-you-go model. This is a single eSIM for 170+ countries, and you only pay for the data that you actually used.

Yesim’s offer is known for the ease and simplicity of connection. The firm has created an intuitive app where you simply pick a country or a region, install the profile, and activate it in a few taps after getting there. Plus, the app uses automatic network selection. This is possible thanks to SwitchLess Networks technology, which switches the user to the strongest available network at any given time. This is especially important for remote locations, where connectivity is usually unstable, but it can also come in handy for mass events, when the network may struggle to cope with tens of thousands of connections at once.

Yesim also offers the Multiple eSIMs feature, which helps you manage several profiles from a single account. This is convenient for those traveling with their family or a small group. You can even test its service with a trial plan that gives you 500 MB for a symbolic price of £0.45. That way, you can see how it all works for yourself before committing any real money. Lastly, Yesim offers a 15 percent discount to new users who use promocode GETYESIM15, so make sure to use it.

Since modern travel has evolved and is now an app-first experience, the old roaming model is no longer a good fit for an average traveler. Instead, travel eSIMs give tourists the freedom to choose their location, amount of data, and even to know the cost upfront. Yesim is a prime example of the fact that the travel eSIM model works and that it can satisfy the needs of modern-day travellers.

"For Yesim, freedom of connectivity is a fundamental human right that helps people stay close, communicate, and share meaningful moments in spite of distance," emphasizes the company's founder and CEO, Dmitri Verbovski.

#3504. US and China share a love of "new things and innovation" says Ma Yansong

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Chinese architect Ma Yansong has completed one of the highest-profile US buildings of 2026 – the Lucas Museum of Narrative Art, in California. In this interview, he explains his architecture’s international appeal as a “bridge between imagination and reality”.

Speaking over video call on the occasion of the Lucas Museum’s opening, Ma told Dezeen that the building has acted as a stable point in a rapidly changing world, with trade wars, AI races and a US federal government that has enshrined classical architecture as a national norm.

“This project took 12 years, across two countries, and the world changed around it. The building didn’t,” he said.

The long-anticipated project, funded by Star Wars creator George Lucas, was first planned for San Francisco, then Chicago, before landing in Los Angeles.

“What made it possible is that George and I come from different roots, but both love the same kind of thing – stories that belong to ordinary people, not to high culture,” said Ma.

Ma believes this shared tendency toward exploration between himself and the American filmmaker applies more widely, and the 51-year-old architect spoke positively about the potential for architecture to illuminate the similarities between China and the United States.

“I see a lot in common between the two countries,” he said. “They both like innovation and new things, and they focus on young people, on education.”

“I think architecture is a bridge between the imagination and reality”

His own life mirrors this outlook. Attracted by innovation as a youth in Beijing, he travelled to Yale, where he graduated with a master’s degree in 2002.

At Yale, Ma became interested in Zaha Hadid’s parametric architecture and eventually worked at her firm in London.

Despite teaching and working mostly in the United States and Europe, Hadid cultivated her signature parametric style working in China’s early 2000s building boom.

The completion of the Lucas Museum represents a Chinese architect taking these principles abroad, a full-circle moment as Ma moved from the US to the UK, back to China, where he has completed dozens of projects, bringing his honed sensibility to California.

Buildings created in the parametric style, or design based on the specific geometries allowed by computer calculations, have been criticised for their lack of relation to sites, and for being self-referential.

The museum’s curved exterior is covered in thousands of white polymer panels, giving it a futuristic look.

Nevertheless, Ma pushes back on Dezeen’s description of the building as “spaceship-like”. Instead, he compares it to a cloud, as an enduring symbol for the imagination – an aspect of consciousness that the architect hopes is provoked by his buildings.

He worked with landscape studio SLA to design an expansive park to serve as an extension of the museum, and the building itself, according to him, features organic curves. He called the effect of these combined elements “surreal nature” and said he aims to bring the natural into the urban environment.

The suggestion of the surreal also points to his aim of provoking the imagination in his design – something the architect said he shares with Lucas and science fiction storytelling.

“I think architecture is a bridge between the imagination and reality,” he said, adding that he hopes the Lucas Museum functions like the narrative art that it holds.

The relationship between Ma and Lucas developed around the question of narrative art since the filmmaker first tapped the architect for the project in 2014.

Ma had actually referenced a scene from Star Wars in his bulbous 2005 museum for Ordos, though he didn’t advertise the influence in his early meetings with Lucas.

“On the personal level, me and George have very easy communication,” said Ma, who famously wanted to become a science fiction director before his career in architecture.

“[George and I] are always talking about ideas, and he’s always supportive. We were constantly discussing what is narrative art,” he continued, adding that the question has led Ma to ask a question that has helped clarify his own practice – “what is narrative architecture?”

Ma said that the project has allowed him to “kind of achieve my goal as a movie maker”.

“You create a dream,” said Ma. “That’s important.”

“It’s why I like science fiction movies – because you put your imagination on the screen, and when people watch it, they believe in something.”

For Ma, this narrative approach to architecture is about opening up to different perspectives, and like science fiction and fantasy, this bridge to the imagination requires a certain suspension of disbelief.

“I’m not interested in everyone receiving the same message,” he said. “I think good public space lets everyone reflect their own experience or knowledge or thinking and share with other people.”

“So I’ve had to find a balance between abstraction and emotion.”

At the Lucas Museum, this intersection of abstraction and emotion is most evident in the massive open-air dome, where visitors can stand on the ground and look up through the panelled apex at the sky.

The space has almost a cathedral-like quality in its grandeur, but it is without ornament and other traditional figurative elements of storytelling in architecture, such as statues or text.

“The architectural space is a trigger for different people to have their own stories. I think that’s a little different from the classic buildings.”

“When you frame it and bring the light into the space, people start to look up, and they will see this connection between individuals and the universe beyond the building, so I think that’s something narrative to me.”

“I like the future, but not the cold future”

The building was also designed to evoke well-proportioned natural elements and bring nature into the city, literally in the case of the green roofs and surrounding park.

Other projects by Ma’s studio have had similar ambitions, such as the Shenzhen Bay Culture Square and One River North in Denver.

And if his architecture is futuristic, Ma hopes that a greener version of the city is the future it evokes, he said.

“I like the future, but not the cold future,” said Ma. “It’s more important that the building is warm and welcoming.”

Ma hopes that Lucas Museum will be inspiring to people, just as his younger self was inspired by the innovative architecture coming out of Yale when he was starting.

“When people see a building that’s so idealistic, that is pushing the boundary, everyone will be encouraged,” he said.

InfoQ Homepage Articles Building an Offline-First AI Coding Tutor for Low-End Android Phones

Oct 28, 2026 17 min read

I spent a year watching students disappear. I was running an academy where they would join a live class, participate actively, and then vanish mid-session. When I followed up, almost none of them had chosen to leave. Their connections could not hold long enough to finish a lesson. Out of one cohort of 80 enrolled students, 6 graduated.

That experience exposed four assumptions underneath most coding education platforms, so normalised they barely register as assumptions: the learner has a laptop, the learner has broadband, the learner reads English comfortably, and the learner can pay for a subscription or belongs to an institution that already has.

The platform I built, Efiwe, inverts every one of those assumptions. It runs in a smartphone browser, works offline after a single setup, executes learner-submitted code locally, and does not require a data bundle after the initial load. It has been used by more than 22,000 learners across 160 countries, and it has been deployed in structured school pilots in Lagos and Ogun State in Nigeria, and in Uttarakhand, India.

Equal Experts helps organizations harness AI, modernize technology, and build better software through expert engineering, collaboration, and innovative delivery practices. Learn more.

This article is not about pedagogy. It is about engineering, and specifically about the parts that broke. The interesting problems in this space are not model quality. They are storage, quota, cold start, and failure-path design. Those problems generalise well beyond education, to any product whose users are on constrained devices and intermittent connections.

The first decision was distribution. A native Android app is the obvious route, yet we did not take it for three reasons.

First, install friction. An APK from a store is tens of megabytes before any content is available, consuming a data bundle that the learner may be rationing. A progressive web app lets a learner start on the first tap and pay the caching cost incrementally, in the background, while they are already learning.

Second, update cadence. We ship changes continuously. A service worker update propagates on the next visit. A store release does not, and in markets where users defer app updates to save data, a store release can leave a long tail of clients running code you fixed months ago.

Third, the web platform gave us a single runtime for code execution. We needed learners to write Python, SQL, JavaScript and HTML and see results immediately, without a server. WebAssembly in the browser gave us that on every platform at once.

A native shell wrapping a WebView would have given us the same execution model, and we considered it. Loading remote content, it would also have solved the update problem. What it would not have solved is install friction: the APK is still a download before any learning happens, on a data bundle the learner may be rationing. For a product whose entire premise is removing barriers to starting, that was enough to decide it.

The cost of this choice is that we inherit the browser's storage model, including its quota behaviour, and that is where most of our production pain has come from.

Grading a coding challenge normally means shipping the submission to a server, running it in a sandbox, and returning a verdict. That model requires a round trip per attempt. In a classroom in Ijebu-Ode, Nigeria, with unreliable connectivity and learners who attempt a challenge ten or twelve times before solving it, that model is unusable.

So execution happens on the device. For SQL we use sql.js, which is SQLite compiled to WebAssembly. For Python we use Pyodide, which is CPython compiled to WebAssembly. Both are large, both are slow to initialise, and both will freeze the main thread if you let them.

The rule we settled on is that no runtime initialises on the main thread and no runtime initialises before it is needed. Course selection triggers a lazy worker spin-up, and the first paint of the challenge screen never waits on it. This is exemplified in the following snippet:

Inside the worker, initialisation is guarded so a second init message is a no-op, and the module payload is fetched from the cache first so that a repeat session on a device that has been offline for a week still starts.

The distinction in that last block is worth dwelling on. In a normal application, a thrown exception from executed code is a bug. Here it is the expected case most of the time, because the learner is a beginner and the entire point is that they will get it wrong. Every layer of the execution path has to treat failure as ordinary traffic.

For SQL grading we do not compare output strings. We compare result shape and content, which lets a learner write a correct query with different formatting, aliasing or column ordering and still pass where the challenge permits it.

The reason field is not decoration. It is the input to the feedback system described later, and it lets us tell a learner that their columns are right but their filter is wrong, rather than telling them only that they failed.

Efiwe presents content in 246 languages. Translated challenge text is cached on the device so that a learner who selected Yoruba or Hindi does not re-fetch the same strings on every session, and so that translation survives going offline.

We started that cache in localStorage. It was the wrong choice and it failed in two ways at once.

The first failure is capacity. localStorage is typically capped at around 5MB per origin, though the exact limit varies by browser and platform. Cached translated content for a multi-hundred-challenge course in even a handful of languages exceeds that. When it does, writes throw QuotaExceededError, and if you have not wrapped every write, that throw lands somewhere unhelpful.

The second failure is worse and quieter. localStorage is synchronous. Every read and every write blocks the main thread. On a mid-range laptop that is a few milliseconds nobody notices. On an entry-level Android device with a slow flash controller and a saturated CPU, a large synchronous read at startup is a visible stall on exactly the hardware that can least afford one. We had optimised our JavaScript payload carefully and then hidden a main-thread block inside the storage layer.

We moved to IndexedDB. IndexedDB is asynchronous, its quota is a percentage of available disk rather than a fixed few megabytes, and it stores structured values without a JSON round trip.

Choosing IndexedDB was the easy part. Moving existing users onto it without losing their data was the problem that actually took the work. We had live users holding data in the old store, many of them offline for days at a stretch and therefore reaching the new code at unpredictable times. A hard cutover would have silently discarded their cached translations and their in-progress state. Instead we ran a dual-read window: read the new store first, fall back to the legacy store once, promote whatever we find, and delete the legacy key.

Two details in that function are the whole lesson. The legacy read is wrapped, because a partially written localStorage value from an interrupted session will throw on JSON.parse and you do not want a parse error on a cache to take down a page. And the promotion write is wrapped separately, because the write can fail on a full device while the read succeeded, and in that case the correct behaviour is to hand the learner their content anyway and try again next time.

This is the incident I would most want another engineer to learn from this article.

During the Nigerian pilot, an educator reported that on some students' phones the learning page did not display after account creation. The account was created, the login succeeded, and then the course simply did not appear. Blank.

Our first hypothesis was hardware compatibility at the low end of the Android range. It was a reasonable guess but it was wrong, and we lost time on it because the reports clustered on cheaper devices. They clustered there for a correlated reason rather than a causal one: cheaper devices have smaller storage, and smaller storage fills up.

The root cause was a single await in the course startup sequence. On boot, we persisted an initial course manifest to the cache and then continued. On a device with no free space, that write could be rejected. The rejection was not caught, which made the promise chain halt before the render call. No error surfaced to the learner because nothing had crashed in a way the UI could detect. The course was simply never asked to draw itself.

The course now starts normally even when it cannot save data to the device. Offline availability is degraded and the learner is told so, but learning is not blocked. We verified the fix across a matrix of storage conditions, including a device deliberately filled up to its capacity, before closing it. The whole cycle from report to verified fix ran within the same day.

The generalisable rule we adopted afterwards: persistence calls in a startup path are fire-and-forget with an explicit catch, never awaited. If your render depends on a successful write, your users' full phones are a blank screen waiting to happen.

We also added a preflight so that we can degrade deliberately rather than discover the problem at write time.

navigator.storage.estimate() returns padded and approximate values by design, for privacy reasons, so it is a planning signal and not a guarantee. It is enough to decide whether to attempt a full course precache or to fetch lazily, and it is enough to tell a learner in advance that they should free space if they want offline mode.

Efiwe gives every learner personalised feedback on wrong answers. That is the feature that our field data rated highest, at 4.3 out of 5 across the Nigerian pilot, with nine of ten educators scoring it 4 or 5.

It is also the feature most at odds with the offline premise, because the strongest feedback comes from a hosted model behind an API, and the whole point of the product is that the network is not there.

We resolved this with two tiers and an explicit contract between them. Tier one is the hosted model, used when connectivity is available and within a hard timeout. Tier two is a smaller on-device path that combines deterministic analysis of the submission with a local model. Tier two is always available and always correct about facts, because the deterministic part does the factual work.

Three design points here that took us longer to learn than they should have.

The local analysis runs first and unconditionally, not as a fallback. It is cheap, it is deterministic, and it is also passed as context into the hosted request. This means the hosted model is reasoning about a pre-diagnosed submission rather than starting from scratch, which produces more consistent output and a smaller prompt.

navigator.onLine is checked but never trusted. It reports whether a network interface exists, not whether anything is reachable, and on a phone attached to a captive portal or a stalled mobile connection it will happily report true. It is used as a fast negative signal only. The timeout is what actually protects the learner.

And the tier is recorded on the response. We do not hide which path gave an answer. That matters for our own telemetry, and it matters because feedback that arrives from the weaker tier is written differently.

Our early feedback was binary and flat. A learner who submitted consolee.log("hi") and a learner who had fundamentally misunderstood what a function is both received a variant of "this is wrong, it should be this instead."

An educator in the pilot flagged this precisely. Her students were receiving corrections they could not act on without her sitting beside them, which defeats the point of a tutor that scales.

The fix was to measure how close the submission is to correct before deciding what kind of feedback to give. A near-miss gets a character-level nudge. A structural miss gets a conceptual explanation.

The analogy I use internally is two teachers marking the same page. The less experienced one puts a red cross next to the wrong answer and writes the correct one beside it. The more experienced one circles exactly what is wrong and writes a note about the specific thing that needs to change. This change moved our feedback from the first kind of marking to the second, and it is entirely deterministic, which means it works identically offline.

In the Nigerian pilot, eight of ten educators cited students not owning a personal device as the dominant obstacle. Phones were shared between siblings, borrowed from teachers, or passed around a group during a session.

Our authentication model at the time assumed one device, one learner. Two students on one phone meant logging out and back in, which on a slow connection is a minute of lost session time each way and, worse, risks a learner losing local progress that had not synced.

Account switching therefore is not a convenience feature, it is a consequence of the deployment context. A device now holds up to twenty saved profiles, and switching is one tap with no full logout. Architecturally this meant namespacing every local store by profile, so that progress, translation cache and runtime state are partitioned rather than global.

If you are building for a market where devices are shared, retrofit this early. Unpicking a globally scoped local store after launch is considerably more expensive than namespacing it on day one.

Two deployments gave us the evidence base for most of the above. In Uttarakhand, India, four institutions ran the platform with structured, managed device access. In Lagos and Ogun State, both in Nigeria, ten educators across ten schools ran it over eight weeks relying almost entirely on students' personal phones.

The contrast isolated platform problems from context problems, which is the single most useful thing a pilot can do for an engineering roadmap. Offline reliability scored 4.0 out of 5 in Nigeria, and every low score traced to the same condition: connectivity dropped before the first caching cycle completed. That is not a reliability failure, it is a first-run sequencing failure, and it is fixable in code by prioritising the minimum viable offline payload ahead of everything optional. Device access, by contrast, is not fixable in code at all, and pretending otherwise wastes engineering time.

The finding that stung most was about language. Supporting 246 languages was one of the reasons the platform exists. In the pilot, four of ten educators never tried a local-language interface, and one tried and could not work out how. Every one of them had been shown the feature in onboarding.

The button was in the page footer. A learner moving through challenges has no reason ever to scroll to a footer. The feature was present, documented and demonstrated, and it was functionally invisible.

Discoverability and usability are not the same property. A feature that requires a user to already know where to look is, for most users, a feature that does not exist. We are moving the control into the active learning line of sight, but the more valuable outcome is the check we now run before shipping anything: not "can a user find this if told where it is" but "will a user encounter this in the path they actually walk."

Three things remain unsolved and I would rather say so than write a tidy ending.

Cold start on WebAssembly runtimes is still worse than I want on the weakest devices, and streaming compilation only partly helps when the module has to come from cache rather than the network.

On-device model quality is the real ceiling on offline feedback. Deterministic analysis carries more of the load than I would like, and it does well only because coding challenges have checkable answers. A softer domain would not have that fallback.

Storage remains adversarial. Quota is padded, eviction is opaque, and persist() is granted inconsistently across browsers. The defensive posture described here is a workaround for a platform gap, not a solution to it.

None of these are reasons not to build this way. The alternative, which is assuming a laptop and broadband, excludes the majority of the people who would benefit. The engineering is harder, but the addressable population is several billion larger.

#1592. New York eases part of Native American imagery ban

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

A core part of the mascot ban will remain in place, however, despite pressure from the Trump administration to overturn it as a form of racial discrimination.

New York is continuing its fight against Native American mascot imagery despite pressure from the Trump administration and local districts suing to keep their logos, which have in some cases been unsuccessful so far.

However, the state is backing down from implementing a portion of its ban that it put in place in 2023 amid those legal crosshairs, according to court documents.

The state in 2023 prohibited the use of Indigenous names, mascots and logos by public schools, saying in its proposal at the time that it was trying to "promote positive learning environments in schools" and that research has found such imagery could create a hostile environment for Indigenous students. Under the policy change, most districts were required to transition away from the prohibited mascots by 2025.

Some districts sued the state as early as 2023, with Massapequa Union Free School District saying in its lawsuit that its mascot references the "rich history" of its Long Island community and is "meant to honor the area's Native American roots."  Changing mascots can also come with hefty price tags for school districts, with one Minnesota school district affected by policies similar to New York's estimating last year that the change could cost $1 million.

As part of Massapequa's and another district's lawsuit against the state, brought by Wantagh Union Free School District, the New York State Attorney General Letitia James’ office told the courts in an Oct. 16 update that it was repealing a section of the state mascot ban that prohibits school employees on school property or at school events from wearing or promoting any Indigenous names, logos or mascots.

However, the broader ban on Indigenous school mascots will remain, while some claims brought by the Wantagh school district lawsuit and others are still ongoing in the appeals circuit.

New York State Department of Education spokesperson JP O'Hare, in an Oct. 21 email to K-12 Dive, described legal challenges brought against the state by Long Island school boards as "unsuccessful.”

"As schools complete the transition away from stereotypical Indigenous mascots, the Department will recommend that the Board of Regents leave decisions regarding employee clothing, including apparel displaying offensive Indigenous imagery, to local school districts, consistent with how other employee attire and personnel decisions are handled," O'Hare said.

The Massapequa district celebrated the change as a positive step forward, but called on New York Gov. Kathy Hochul and the Board of Regents to repeal the entire ban.

"This is a significant victory for Massapequa and for the principle of local control. From the beginning, we have maintained that the state’s Native American mascot regulation represents an unconstitutional overreach," said the Massapequa School District Board of Education in an Oct. 23 statement to K-12 Dive. "The state’s recommendation to return authority over employee apparel to local school districts is an important acknowledgment that these decisions should be made locally, not dictated by Albany."

New York's decision to walk back a portion of its mascot law while remaining staunch in its decision to prohibit Native American mascots comes despite pressure from the Trump administration, which has repeatedly backed the logos and said that prohibiting them is racial discrimination.

Last year, the U.S. Department of Education launched an investigation into the New York Department of Education for alleged Title VI violations. About a month later, the U.S. Education Department found the state agency in violation of the civil rights law protecting students from race-based discrimination.

“The U.S. Department of Education will not stand by as the state of New York attempts to rewrite history and deny the town of Massapequa the right to celebrate its heritage in its schools,” said U.S. Education Secretary Linda McMahon in an April 2025 statement. “While New York chooses to prioritize erasing Native Americans, their rich history, and their deep connection to the state, it is requiring schools to divert time and resources away from what really matters: educating our students.”

Within two months of the original investigation, the agency referred the case to the U.S. Department of Justice for enforcement proceedings, after the state refused to change its policies as requested by the administration. There has been no recent public update in that referral proceeding.

New York has been on the frontlines of the issue, joined by a handful of other states with similar restrictions. In 2019, Maine became one of the first to enact such a ban, according to Lutzker & Lutzker, which provides trademark and branding law services.

As of April 2023, more than 21 states had acted to or were working to address the use of Native mascots in their public schools, according to the firm.
