#192. Seven oil tankers carrying 288,000 tonnes of Russian diesel sail towards US

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Tankers, tracked by Greenpeace, are first phase of Trump-Putin deal – but there is uncertainty over whether they will unload

Seven oil tankers carrying an estimated 288,000 tonnes of Russian diesel are now sailing towards the US, though it is uncertain if the vessels will be able to unload amid a political row about the legality of the shipments.

The transport, tracked by the pressure group Greenpeace, is the first phase of a contentious supply deal negotiated between presidents Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin earlier this month, reversing years of sanctions on Russia oil.

Previous reports had counted three tankers that had departed Russian oil terminals in the Baltic Sea, but using a range of sources, including shipping tracking websites, Greenpeace believes its monitoring exercise is the most complete yet.

Shaun Burnie, an energy specialist with Greenpeace Ukraine, argued that now the location of the tankers could be plotted on a map, the shipments should be halted by Congress or local authorities in the US.

“Instead of rewarding Russia for its vile war against Ukraine, there needs to be more punitive sanctions against Russia,” he said. “These shipments can be stopped”.

One of the seven tankers, Taurus, is relatively close to the US, but at the time the map was first published, five of the others were close to the UK, and three were sailing through or into the English Channel.

In Ukraine, 14 people were reported killed on Thursday morning when a Russian drone struck a crowded train platform in the central city of Dnipro. Five others were reported killed in daytime strikes in two other cities, Chernihiv and Kremenchuk.

Ukrzaliznytsia, Ukraine’s state railway company, said a high speed jet-powered drone had struck as a commuter train was preparing to depart Dnipro’s main station. Bodies lay scattered after the relatively unusual daytime attack, which took place at around 11am.

Putin agreed to supply the US with 1.8m tonnes of diesel in a surprise deal announced on 9 October, starting with 300,000 tonnes immediately. Trump announced a six-month waiver on existing sanctions to allow the shipments to proceed.

Russia had been hit by waves of economic sanctions from the US, EU, UK and other leading western economies since Putin launched the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. But this autumn, Trump has altered course and tried to work more closely with Putin, even though Russia has not stopped attacking its neighbour.

Earlier this week, a group of US lawmakers, led by a handful of Democratic senators, warned banks and port authorities that they could face congressional investigations and other legal action if they handled the Russian diesel.

Mikie Sherrill, the Democratic governor of New Jersey, said she was looking at how to prevent the diesel from being unloaded, if an attempt was made to do so in her state. She described the deal as “corrupt and illegal”.

There have been reports that the White House has been trying to encourage US firms to take the oil, though it is not clear buyers are forthcoming, given the legal risks so close to next Tuesday’s midterm elections.

Trump’s popularity has waned as fuel prices in the US have soared since he attacked Iran in the spring. Polling suggests a high likelihood that control of the House of Representatives could pass to the Democrats in the elections, while the battle over the Senate is considered too close to call.

The US president had argued that the Russia deal would help lower diesel prices, though the price per gallon has risen from $6.28 at the time the agreement was announced to $6.33, according to the American Automobile Association.

Ukraine’s president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy, denounced the agreement because the money raised would help the Kremlin fund the ongoing war. “Backroom deals” gave Putin more chances on the battlefield, he said.

The Kremlin has been trying to keep information about the latest oil exports secret. A presidential decree from the end of September restricted the publication of information about oil exports in an effort to evade international sanctions.

#330. ‘Arab voice doesn’t count’: Jewish Israeli blocs reject Palestinian support

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Palestinian voters could decide Israel’s future, but its political class refuses to accept their mandate.

Few certainties have emerged from Israel’s parliamentary elections. The diverse coalition of Israel’s self-styled ‘Zionist’ opposition remains short of a majority. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud appears to be the largest party, but its electoral bloc has not secured enough seats to form a government.

What is not in doubt, however, is the surge in turnout from Palestinian citizens of Israel, which has established Arab parties as a pivotal bloc in the parliament, potentially one able to determine which of the rival camps forms a government, if either is willing to work with them.

Anger over Israel’s genocidal war on Gaza, and what analysts have described as the overt racism of its governing coalition helped drive Palestinian citizens of Israel to the polls in large numbers.

An estimated 80 percent of eligible Palestinian voters in Israel turned out for Tuesday’s election, marking a surge in participation by a community that accounts for about 21 percent of the population.

The turnout has given the two main electoral lists representing Palestinian citizens of Israel – the United Arab List (Ra’am) and the Joint List – a combined 19 seats in the 120-member parliament, potentially giving them a decisive role in determining who forms the next government.

However, rather than acknowledge that groundswell of support, Israel’s leading politicians have sought to either delegitimise their vote – attributing it to foreign interference – or somehow cast Palestinians exercising their democratic right as an “attempt to steal the election”.

Framing the result as a bid to secure the “Islamisation of Israel”, Netanyahu’s ruling Likud party has gone so far as to call on Israel’s internal security service, the Shin Bet, to investigate the possibility of foreign election interference into the large-scale Palestinian turnout. In doing so, they appear to have thrown the legitimacy of the Arab vote into question and tainted any future attempt by the opposition to ally with the Arab parties.

Despite denouncing Netanyahu’s government as corrupt, incompetent and damaging to Israel’s international standing, much of Israel’s Jewish-led opposition has remained reluctant to embrace the support of Palestinian parties to remove him from power.

The left-wing Democrats have been the likeliest to cooperate with the Palestinian parties, while other opposition figures have either resisted such an alliance or stopped short of rejecting allegations that foreign actors helped engineer the surge in Palestinian voter turnout.

On election night, former prime ministers Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid ruled out sitting alongside Palestinian parties in government, despite having relied on their support during their brief 2021–22 government.

On Thursday, another leading member of the opposition, Avigdor Lieberman, the hard-right leader of Yisrael Beytenu, also ruled out forming a coalition with Palestinian parties. He said he would sit alongside “neither Bibi nor Tibi”, using Netanyahu’s established nickname and invoking Ahmad Tibi, leader of the Ta’al party – part of the Joint List – as a stand-in for the wider Palestinian bloc.

“It was clear from campaigning that there was going to be a large turnout,” said Orly Noy, who stood for the Palestinian-dominated Balad party. “The [governing] coalition had shown there were no red lines in Gaza and Palestinians in Israel sensed they might be next. People also just couldn’t face another term of soaring crime and a police force headed by [far-right National Security Minister] Itamar Ben-Gvir.”

“What few rights Palestinians had have been slipping away since October 7. The last tool they had in their toolbox – the only thing they had that was equal to everyone else – was their vote, so they used that in massive numbers, but even that now seems to be being undermined and delegitimised,” she said.

With all ballots counted, the pro-Netanyahu bloc has 53 seats, while the anti-Netanyahu Jewish opposition bloc has 48. However, with the 19 seats held by Palestinian parties, the majority in parliament is against Netanyahu.

“Why are the opposition and the press even thinking of this as a three-way split? In any other rational democracy, people would be framing this as an overwhelming victory for the opposition and a rejection of Netanyahu, but we’re not doing that,” Noy said.

“Instead, we’re hearing elements of the opposition talking about joining with Netanyahu in some kind of National Unity government. That’s both a measure of how racist these parties are, when they won’t align with Arabs to form a majority, as well as how profoundly undemocratic they are,” she said.

Political differences do exist between an opposition that deliberately frames itself as Zionist and a Palestinian minority whose claim to equal national and civic rights is seen by some as a rejection of that. But underlying that disagreement is a fundamentally different vision of Israel’s future, according to political analyst Nimrod Flaschenberg.

“Given that much of Israel’s mainstream politics rests on the assumption of Jewish supremacy, I think it’s understandable that the Arab vote is seen as a challenge,” he said of a sentiment that many argue has been state policy since the 2018 Nation State Law, which enshrined the right to national self-determination as exclusive to Jewish people.

“There are very different ideas at play of what it means to be a citizen of Israel and of how Israel should be governed and for whose benefit,” he said. “These are fundamental differences, of course they are, but they can’t disguise the racism underpinning all.”

“They want to form a government with only Jewish voices,” said Mauricio Lapchik, Hebrew campaign director for the Joint List. “If the numbers of these results were in a different country, in a democratic country, I think it would be crazy to think that they will not be able to say that they won the elections, having this majority. But only in Israel, because the voice of the Arabs do not count as the voice of the Jews, we cannot talk about a victory.”

The move comes amid reports that the US had warned its allies of a possible renewal of strikes against Iran.

A Department of Defense official told Al Jazeera on Thursday that Sylvia had been “meeting with journalists outside the Pentagon in order to leak classified information”.

“Sylvia is the subject of an investigation for leaking classified information about the Iran war, the war between Russia and Ukraine, and more,” the official added.

The investigation into Sylvia remains ongoing and has been referred to the Department of Justice for further action, according to the Pentagon.

Sylvia’s firing comes as Trump has repeatedly floated starting new military operations against Iran.

He is the second person to be ousted from the post, known in military circles as the J5, under Hegseth. His predecessor was forced to retire in October last year.

“Sylvia failed three polygraphs which questioned him about leaking classified information,” the Pentagon official said while accusing Sylvia of putting national security at risk.

“Urgent action was needed and could not wait another second,” he concluded.

There is no immediate replacement for Sylvia, according to an official who spoke on the condition of anonymity to The Associated Press news agency.

Before arriving at the Joint Chiefs of Staff, Sylvia served as commander of the 101st Airborne Division at Fort Campbell, Kentucky, until May 2025, according to his official biography, which has now been taken offline. He also previously served as the vice director for the office he recently led.

His military awards include six Bronze Star Medals, including one for valour, and a Purple Heart, indicating he was wounded in combat.

Sean Parnel, assistant to the defense secretary for public affairs, said earlier on Thursday in a post on X that “the Department of War has zero tolerance for the intentional, unauthorized disclosure of classified information.” He did not refer specifically to Sylvia.

Sylvia is the latest senior Pentagon official whom Hegseth has accused of leaking to the media. He fired three aides from his inner circle last year as part of a broader leak investigation that included polygraph tests.

President Donald Trump has promised to “finish the job” in Iran after launching the war with Israel eight months ago and said he would do so after the midterm elections on Tuesday.

#681. Leaked recordings reveal final moments before the Spanish train disaster

London: Leaked recordings from a Spanish train controller have shed new light on the horrific train crash that killed at least 69 people, while raising key questions about the ultimate blame for the disaster.

The recordings reveal the final messages between the rail control centre in Madrid and the two train drivers operating on the same line south of the Spanish capital, moments before one train smashed into the rear of the other.

Spanish media outlets report that a controller in Madrid told the driver of a Renfe AVE high-speed train to “resume normal operations” after being stopped due to a signalling malfunction.

The instruction in Spanish, according to national newspaper El País, was “la marcha normal” – a message that led the driver to accelerate rapidly without knowing another train was stationary on the line ahead.

But the disclosures are fiercely contested, with union leaders angry about the leaks, and the Spanish people are yet to learn the full facts about the catastrophic errors that led to the tragedy.

Five questions stand out in the search for answers about the crash on Wednesday morning (Wednesday evening AEDT). Finding the facts will take time, however, because Spain goes to a national election on November 29. Political arguments are clouding the debate about who bears the blame for the tragedy.

The first question is about the failure of the signalling and control system on the high-speed line from Madrid to Seville.

The control centre in Madrid experienced a disruption to the system called LZB, which is also used in Germany and Austria to monitor train speeds and relay traffic instructions.

The failure of the automated system brought eight trains to a halt along the line – a standard safety precaution. Operations then reverted to manual control.

One expert, transport professor Carlos Gutiérrez Hita, has expressed concern about a series of LZB outages in the weeks before the crash. Why did the safety system fail on Wednesday morning?

The second question is about the instructions from the control centre to the Renfe AVE train – run by Spain’s state-owned railway company – after the halt.

The leaked recordings obtained by Spanish media outlets suggest the controller in Madrid told the Renfe AVE driver to “resume normal operations”.

The driver asked: “Normal operations?” The controller replied: “Yes.” (Bear in mind, this is sourced from leaks to the media, not official testimony.)

The definition of “normal operations” includes speeds of up to 200km/h rather than the more cautious 40km/h during a signal outage. Why did the controller allow a speed so fast without being sure that other trains were also moving?

The third question: What did the control centre tell the other train involved in the crash, run by private operator Iryo?

The leaks suggest the instruction was to resume normal operations, but it is not clear if the Iryo driver knew that the Renfe AVE had begun accelerating at 8.36am and was only about 25 kilometres behind on the same line. Was anything done to make this known?

The fourth question is the most crucial. What happened in the final minutes before the crash? The driver of the Iryo train told the control centre of a problem: the system restart had failed and the Iryo remained stationary.

The leaked recordings give her actual words. “I have had a loss of transmission and I am stopped at the Urda entrance signal,” she said, according to the transcript published by Spanish state broadcaster RTVE. This was at 8.41am. The control centre told the driver to keep trying to resume normal operation.

Minutes later, the two sides spoke again. “The train is not allowing me to restart the LZB system,” the Iryo driver said. “And that is why I am still stopped before the entrance signal.”

The response from the controller was to ask her to begin moving as soon as she could. “And if you have a problem, call us,” the controller said. The driver replied: “OK, perfect. I’ll let you know.”

This was at 8.48am, according to Spanish newspaper El País. The speeding train smashed into the stationary train at 8.53am.

If the leaked recordings are accurate, it is already clear that human errors led to tragic deaths.

The rail unions, however, have reacted angrily to the idea that one of their own will bear the blame for the disaster. “Human errors occur when you don’t have the equipment you need to do your job,” one union leader told El País.

Getting straight answers will be challenging during an election campaign. Union leaders are calling on Transport Minister Óscar Puente to resign because they believe he should be held responsible. This pits the Socialist Party government against its union support base.

Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez is promising a full investigation and a solution, given the death of 46 people in a derailment at Adamuz in January and the death of at least 69 in the Urda crash on Wednesday.

“If we need to further strengthen safety in order to rebuild confidence in our high-speed rail system, we will do so, because what is unacceptable for a ​country like Spain is to suffer two high-speed rail accidents in the same year,” he said on Wednesday.

The contender to replace Sánchez at the election, People’s Party chief Alberto Núñez Feijóo, has suspended his campaign during the three-day mourning period for the dead, but he is pointed about the onus on the government. “Spain has the right to know what is happening with its infrastructure,” he said.

And that leads to the fifth question. Did the national government put lives at risk by running down the high-speed train system?

About 25 million passengers used the country’s high-speed services last year, according to a Reuters analysis. Spain has spent billions of dollars on a network that is one of the largest in the world – but has the investment kept up with the demand?

When the election campaign began a few weeks ago, the dominant issue was housing. Sanchez led in the polls.

The tragedy at Urda now shapes a debate about whether the Spanish people can rely on their essential services. And that, in turn, may determine who runs the country.

When the summit closes on Friday, both sides will issue a revised joint statement that reads: “We will create an even closer association between Canada and the EU... and work to define by the 2027 EU-Canada Summit the concept of an ever closer association of Canada to the EU, short of full membership itself,” according to a draft seen by Policito.

Von der Leyen reportedly gave neither the EU’s vast bureaucracy, nor its members’ wary leaders, any warning before she told Carney in front of the European Parliament that she “would like to work with you on opening the door for Canada to being the first associate member of the European Union.”

Unilateral decisions and boundary-overstepping are von der Leyen’s calling cards, as her fellow Eurocrats have repeatedly complained to the press. “Nobody was consulted about associate membership apart from [von der Leyen’s] closest staff, it would seem,” an unnamed diplomat told The Guardian on Wednesday. The widespread anonymous briefing to various mainstream European media made it clear that France, Germany, Greece and Italy had forced von der Leyen to adopt the new, watered-down terminology.

In her first speech in Montreal on Thursday, von der Leyen refused to adopt the new, sanitized wording. After noting that “Canada cannot become a full-fledged member of the EU because of geography,” she insisted that “we are discussing the idea of Canada becoming the first associate member of the European Union. Our idea is simple: it is an ‘association without accession’.”

Whatever form Friday’s communique will take, von der Leyen has evidently chosen to disregard the concerns of the EU’s largest member states for the sake of her own personal project. However, the difference between both statements is purely rhetorical.

Neither “Associate member” nor “ever closer association” are defined anywhere in EU law.

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz attempted to define “associate membership” in May when he suggested that it be granted to Ukraine. Under his proposal, Ukraine would participate in European Council meetings without voting rights, send representatives to the European Parliament, receive more EU funding, and become party to the EU’s mutual defense clause.

The proposal was rejected by Kiev, with Ukrainian leader Vladimir Zelensky arguing that this kind of ‘membership-lite’ would leave his country “present in the European Union, but voiceless.”

“Ever closer association” is an even more meaningless term, lifted straight from ‘Ever Closer Union’, a hagiography on EU integration by the bloc’s court historian, Desmond Dinan. Writing in 1994, Dinan argued that “European integration is a good thing,” and the phrases he used – “ever closer union” and “pooling sovereignty,” for example – have since become staples of EU speeches and press releases.

US President Donald Trump ridiculed von der Leyen’s word games last month, calling the concept of associate membership “laughable,” and threatening to “put very serious tariffs or stop trading with Europe on many things” if the EU-Canada proto-alliance ever harmed US interests.

Carney – who plainly insisted last month that Canada is “not looking to become a member of the European Union” – responded diplomatically to the intra-EU squabbling. “The good news is it doesn’t matter about ⁠specific names or nomenclature,” he said ahead of the summit. “What matters is the difference it will ​make to the lives ​of Canadians.”

However, Von der Leyen’s “membership” promise and the subsequent retraction has undermined Carney in front of his opponents. In a social media post on Thursday, Conservative leader Pierre Poilievre described the concept of “associate membership” as “theater,” and dismissed the summit as “yet another grand theatrical meeting to sign a bunch of meaningless declarations filled with big words (catalyze, anyone?) and promises to meet again to talk more."

How long will this theatre go on? While Canadians can’t afford food and homes, Mr. Carney has distracted for months with big talk about “Associate Membership” with Europe. Now, Europe has rejected it. For the next two days, we will be distracted again from the cost-of-living crisis with wall-to-wall media coverage on yet another grand theatrical meeting to sign a bunch of meaningless declarations filled with big words (catalyze, anyone?) and promises to meet again to talk more. No one’s mortgage or grocery bill will come down. We already have a free trade deal with Europe, Mr. Carney. Get the 9 countries who have not ratified it to do so. And get EU countries to let our beef into the continent and cancel their planned carbon tariff on Canada. Otherwise stop wasting your time and our money. Both are running out.

Von der Leyen has far more riding on this summit than Carney. While the EU needs energy, minerals, and Canada’s geographic clout in the Arctic, Canada only needs to diversify some of its trade away from the US – which accounts for around 72% of its exports and 46% of its imports – in order to hedge against Trump’s tariffs and trade restrictions. At present, the EU accounts for less than 6% of Canada’s exports and 12% of its imports.

🛬 Touchdown in Montréal 🇨🇦 Rarely have I looked forward to a Summit this much. An ocean separates Europe and Canada. Yet we have never felt closer. We have a new ambition: bringing Canada closer to us than ever before, as Associate Member of the EU. And we want to take our unique partnership even further. With an Alliance for the Future, we will stand together and respond to a world of growing rivalries. Montréal, we’re about to write a new chapter in our shared history.

Although the agenda is wrapped up in the language of “shared values” and “respect for the rules-based international order,” Brussels’ priorities in Montreal are clear: the energy-starved EU needs access to Canada’s resources.

According to Euronews, the latest draft of the joint declaration includes a promise to “assess opportunities for long-term energy connectivity,” including the supply of Canadian liquefied natural gas to the EU. Remember that in July 2025 von der Leyen personally pledged to Trump that bloc members would buy $750 billion worth of US energy by the end of 2028, in a trade deal that was widely seen as one-sided.

Canadian Finance Minister Francois-Philippe Champagne has also announced that Ottawa and Brussels intend to sign an agreement on the supply of critical minerals including lithium, nickel, and cobalt. The EU currently controls less than 4% of the world’s supply of these minerals, which are vital in the production of electric vehicles and missiles – both of which the EU intends to increase.

The Europeans also intend to sign defense production and technology-sharing deals, to squeeze cash out of Canada for the bloc’s increasingly stretched €90 billion loan package for Ukraine, and deepen involvement in the Arctic, according to the summit agenda. As the EU set out in its updated Arctic Strategy’ earlier this month, Brussels intends to increase its military presence to challenge Russia in the north, and plans to literally redraw the map of the region to increase its meager Arctic claims, and to pursue closer ties “with like-minded partners,” including Canada.

To challenge Russia in the Arctic, the EU likely has its eyes on Canada’s polar-trained military and its 20-strong icebreaker fleet, compared to the ten such vessels spread across the 27-member bloc. None of these icebreakers are nuclear powered, and even if they were to operate on joint Arctic missions, they would be outnumbered and outclassed by Russia’s fleet of 43, eight of which are nuclear powered

However, any hope von der Leyen may have had of securing preferential single market-style trade deals with Carney have apparently been dashed. According to AFP, the “associate member” version of the joint statement included an “explicit goal of deeper financial market integration.” The new, “ever closer association” communique has replaced this with a promise by both sides to “limit potential negative effects on each other.”

Whether von der Leyen will, by virtue of repetition and against the will of four EU member states, invent an “associate membership” is immaterial. The concept is a legal fiction, and by insisting on the word “membership,” the European Commission president set herself up for an entirely avoidable clash with the French, Germans, Greeks, and Italians.

It’s very interesting to see the Commission president goes so defiantly against the position of Germany — the biggest member state and her home country — on an issue that, as of now, remains strictly theoretical.

The argument over the statement’s wording has overshadowed the summit, but the geopolitical reality remains unchanged: the EU needs Canada’s energy supplies to keep its economy afloat, Canada’s money to prop up its Ukraine project, and Canada’s geography to legitimize its Arctic expansion. For these, it doesn’t matter how the deal is dressed up.

#623. EU pressure on Antigua's 'golden passport' trade threatens budget cushion

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Antigua and Barbuda’s Prime Minister Gaston Browne addresses the 80th United Nations General Assembly at U.N. headquarters in New York, U.S., September 26, 2025. REUTERS/Eduardo Munoz

CASTRIES, St Lucia, Oct 29 - Antigua and Barbuda's likely move to end its citizenship-by-investment program under European Union pressure threatens a vital revenue stream for the island nation and could test the future of Caribbean "golden passport" schemes, analysts said.

The issue exposes a vulnerability across the Caribbean, where several small economies have built government revenues and luxury developments around demand for passports valued partly for the access to Europe they provide.

Prime Minister Gaston Browne said this month Antigua would “very likely” commit to phasing out its citizenship-by-investment program after the EU gave Antigua and four other Eastern Caribbean nations until November 12 to agree to close their schemes by June 1, 2028.

The EU says the programs pose security risks by allowing foreigners with few ties to a country to obtain citizenship and visa-free entry to the Schengen area, a key selling point for passport buyers. Countries that keep the schemes risk losing that access.

The EU had initially requested the June 2028 phase-out in letters sent to Antigua and Barbuda, Dominica, Grenada, Saint Kitts and Nevis and Saint Lucia in June. Under a revised EU visa-suspension mechanism, operating an investor-citizenship program can itself provide grounds for suspending visa-free access, though the suspension is not automatic.

The programs, which require investments starting at $200,000, grant foreign applicants citizenship and a passport, rather than temporary residency, typically in exchange for contributions to state funds or investments in approved projects.

Buyers have included nationals of China, Russia and Middle Eastern countries who would normally need visas to enter the EU, and applicants generally have not been required to live in the Caribbean to obtain citizenship. Demand from Americans has grown since 2020, according to Kristin Surak, a London School of Economics professor who researches investment migration.

Citizenship-by-investment inflows in 2023 equaled about a fifth of the combined GDP of the Eastern Caribbean Currency Union, a group of eight Caribbean economies that share a currency and central bank, according to a 2025 International Monetary Fund paper.

The IMF estimated in a paper published in 2025 that direct investment in projects tied to the passport programs was so large that it represented between 100% to 170% of all foreign direct investment officially recorded across the five countries with such schemes.

Schengen access has traditionally been an important attraction for buyers of Caribbean citizenship-by-investment passports, and losing it could reduce their value while affecting ordinary citizens too, said Surak.

A suspension could force hotels and other developments financed through passport sales to seek fresh capital, delay construction or abandon projects altogether, analysts said.

The immediate stakes are high for Antigua, where citizenship sales are the government’s largest source of non-tax revenue, according to C. Justin Robinson, director of the University of the West Indies Five Islands Campus.

The government collected about $58 million from the program in 2025 and budgeted $44.4 million for 2026, or roughly 7% to 11% of recurrent revenue, he said.

Ending the program would erase more than half of Antigua’s projected primary surplus this year, Robinson estimated.

The consequences could be even greater elsewhere.

In St Kitts and Nevis and Dominica, where such inflows have at times exceeded 10% of GDP, suspension could pose what Robinson called an “existential fiscal question.”

Antigua's experience could also test how aggressively Brussels is prepared to use access to Europe as leverage over citizenship policies beyond its borders. The EU also withdrew Vanuatu’s visa-free access in 2024 over concerns surrounding its investor-citizenship scheme. REUTERS
