Your credit card is interest-free only for purchases you pay off in full by each due date. Leave part of a bill unpaid, take cash from it, or accept store financing, and the card becomes a loan, often at over 20% a year. Before you do any of those, check when interest starts and what fees apply.

Each mistake here turns a useful card into that loan, by starting interest sooner than you’d expect, keeping it running longer, or adding a fee on top. The everyday habits that keep a card free to use head off most of them, but not all.

At the 22% average rate the Federal Reserve reports for card accounts charged interest, a $2,000 balance works out to about $37 in interest a month.

Your statement shows how long minimum-only payments would take to clear today’s balance. If the answer is years, your card is already a long-term loan.

Paying only your credit card’s minimum keeps the account current but barely reduces what you owe, since most of a typical minimum goes to interest. The minimum also shrinks as the balance falls, which adds years to the payoff. Pick a fixed monthly payment at least as large as this month’s minimum and stick to it.

Late fees cost cardholders $17 billion in 2024, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau found, and the fee is the least of what a missed payment can cost.

Missing your credit card’s minimum payment starts a clock. A late fee can hit right away, a credit report mark at 30 days, a penalty rate at 60, and collections near 180. Each stage is easier to stop than the one after it, so call your issuer before the due date and pay the missed minimum before day 30.

This one involves no fee, no cash, and no new rate, only everyday spending on a card that already carries a balance.

If you carry a credit card balance most months, new purchases collect interest from the day you make them, and that interest soon costs more than any rewards they earn. Switch everyday spending to debit until the balance is gone, then go back to credit once you can pay in full every month.

Read more: Carrying a Card Balance? Switch to Debit Until It's Paid Off

Some cardholders leave part of each bill unpaid deliberately, because they believe lenders want to see a balance carried from month to month.

Paying credit card interest never raises your credit score. Scores see the balance on your statement and whether you paid on time, and newer ones count a balance you roll over against you. Use your card, then pay the full statement balance by the due date every month to build credit without paying interest.

In a cash crunch, the quickest money in reach is often the credit line on the card already in your wallet.

A credit card cash advance is the costliest way to use your card. You pay a fee up front, usually 5%, and interest builds from day one, often at a higher rate than purchases. Money orders and some person-to-person transfers can count too, so keep your credit card out of anything that works like cash.

These checks look most useful for a bill that won’t take a card, such as rent paid to a landlord or a contractor’s invoice.

Convenience checks, the blank checks your card issuer mails you, draw on your credit line. Unless they carry a promotional rate, most are treated as cash advances, with a 3% to 5% fee and interest from day one, often at a higher rate than purchases. The next few weeks can make or break some of your favorite bulbs and tubers so don’t leave things to chance and lose next year’s blooms to extreme winter cold. Lift these 7 gorgeous garden classics to safeguard your future flowers

Planting bulbs is a beautiful act of faith – and a classic act of delayed gratification. Tucked in cool soil, they are packed with the promise of spectacular blooms months down the road. Yet, while spring-flowering hardy bulbs like tulips, daffodils, and crocuses require freezing winter chill to trigger their bloom cycles, many summer bloomers originate in tropical and subtropical climates and won’t survive the cold.

When left in cold, sodden winter earth, tender bulbs, corms, tubers, and rhizomes face catastrophic cell damage, and wet winter soil causes rot. Combined with cold winter rain and melting snow, unlifted tender bulbs can quickly succumb to fungal rots long before spring arrives. Knowing how to store bulbs over winter, and knowing which need storage, can save your time, effort, and flowers.

Depending on your hardiness zone and bulb variety, lifting and overwintering these tender beauties is a rewarding fall ritual. Southern growers might be able to get away with leaving their bulbs in the ground beneath a thick mulch, but if you live in zones 7 and colder, you are strongly advised to dig them up and store them indoors. By acting now, you can protect your summer favorites from winter freezes, saving money while ensuring your yard is more floriferous next summer.

Before grabbing your spade, it’s worth identifying whether you are handling bulbs, tubers, corms or rhizomes. These are all often lumped together under the umbrella term of bulbs, but the following summer bloomers represent a fascinating variety. True bulbs like lilies consist of layered scales. Dahlias grow from swollen tuberous roots or tubers. Gladiolus form solid, disc-shaped corms, while cannas and callas expand via fleshy horizontal rhizomes. That said, all tender summer rootstocks share identical winter vulnerabilities and require careful fall lifting.

Timing is key to lifting successfully. For most tender varieties, the ideal window opens immediately after your region's first light frost blackens top foliage, signaling the plant to transfer energy into underground storage. However, don’t delay until the ground freezes solid, as frozen crusts make excavation tough and expose tender root crowns to sub-surface chill. In hardiness zones 3-5, lifting typically peaks around mid-to-late October. In mid-Atlantic and transitional zones 6-7, late October through early November provides the ideal window.

Use a garden fork, like the Trooper D-Handle Garden Fork from Walmart, to lift your bulbs, tubers and corms cleanly. Insert the tines 8-12 inches (20-25cm) from the main stems. Gently lever the clump out, brush away loose dirt, and allow them to cure in a dry, ventilated, frost-free location for several days to a few weeks.

Once outer skins toughen, pack them in breathable cardboard boxes or plastic crates cushioned with dry vermiculite, perlite, wood shavings, or peat-free coir. Store in a cool, dark space maintaining 40-50°F (4-10°C), such as an unheated basement or insulated garage. Check them monthly for signs of rot or shriveling.

Dahlias (Dahlia spp.) can be the jewels of a fall border. However, their fleshy tuberous root systems originate from the warm mountain highlands of Mexico and cannot tolerate freezing soil. Leaving prized varieties like Café au Lait or Kelvin Floodlight in the ground in zones 7 or colder can be a death sentence. Wait until the first frost nips the plant, turning the leaves brown or black. This shock signals the plant to go dormant and pull its final starches into the roots.

Using sharp bypass pruners, cut the stems down to 4-6 inches (10-15cm) above the soil. Carefully fork beneath the clump, lifting the tubers whole. Gently wash off heavy clinging soil and let the tubers dry in an airy, shaded spot for 2-3 days. Dust cut stem ends with sulfur powder or cinnamon to prevent fungal rot, then pack the tubers in crates of coir or dry vermiculite like Sta-Green Vermiculite from Lowe’s, storing them at 45-50°F (7-10°C). Check periodically and if tubers wrinkle, mist lightly with water.

Loved for their vertical flower spikes, gladioli (Gladiolus × hortulanus) grow from corms. While miniature Byzantine gladiolus can tolerate mild chills, large-flowered modern hybrids lack cold hardiness and turn into watery rot when left in frozen ground across zones 7 and below. You can begin lifting gladiolus corms once the foliage turns yellow, or immediately after the first light frost – whichever comes first.

Use a hand fork to loosen the soil around the base of the stems, gently pulling the corms upward by their leaf bases. Cut the foliage back cleanly with some shears or snips such as Fiskars Micro-Tip Pruning Snips, available from Amazon, which can trim stalks without tearing skin. Leave an inch (2.5cm) of stem attached to the corm.

Curing is essential, so place the corms in a warm, dry, well-ventilated room (70-75°F / 21-24°C) for 2-3 weeks. Once cured, the new, plump corm separates easily from the spent mother corm. Snap off and discard the old bottom corm along with loose, papery tunics, saving tiny cormels for propagation. Store clean corms in breathable mesh bags or open cardboard cartons in a cool, dark room at 40-45°F (4-7°C).

With their paddle-shaped leaves and fiery blossoms, cannas (C. indica) bring lush drama to patios and borders. Striped purple-and-gold Tropicanna or dark-leafed Australia make striking centerpieces. However, the rhizomes cannot endure freezing temperatures. In zones 7 and below, frost turns them into mush if left unprotected. Allow the first killing frost to blacken the foliage. Cut the thick stalks back to 3-4 inches (8-10cm) above ground level. Cannas produce dense roots, so dig wide around the clump using a sturdy fork, gently levering the rhizome mass from the soil.

Unlike other bulbs, canna rhizomes benefit from keeping a light layer of dry soil clinging to the outer skin, which cushions dormant eyes and prevents moisture loss during storage. Allow the lifted clumps to dry in a protected, frost-free shed or garage for a couple of days. Pack them snugly into cardboard boxes lined with damp peat moss, shredded paper, or vermiculite, maintaining temperatures around 45-55°F (7-13°C). Nourish the planting site with a little mushroom compost once cannas are lifted. Some Espoma Organic Mushroom Blend Compost from Walmart can help to replenish the ground ahead of next year’s plantings.

Tuberous begonias (B. × tuberhybrida) produce dazzling, rose-like double blossoms through summer and early fall. But under their succulent stems lie cup-shaped tubers that are sensitive to cold, wet conditions. Leaving them in freezing soil causes swift rot. Begin the overwintering process when nighttime temperatures dip toward 45°F (7°C), before heavy frosts. Withhold watering to encourage the plant to wind down naturally. As moisture declines, the succulent stems will loosen. Gently cut or snap the stems back to an inch (2.5cm) above the tuber. Carefully lift the round tubers using a trowel, taking care not to gouge the skin.

Spread the tubers out in a single layer on newspaper in a warm, dry, frost-free room for 2-3 weeks until the stem stubs shrivel and drop. Once cured, brush away dried soil and store the tubers hollow-side up in shallow trays filled with dry peat moss, perlite, or sawdust. Keep them in a dark, dry space at 45°F to 55°F (7°C to 13°C), checking monthly to discard any soft or rotting tubers. A 10x stand magnifier such as the Carson LumiLoupe 10x Power Stand Magnifier from Walmart is ideal for closely inspecting delicate tuber indentations for early mold or soft rot before storage.

With their heart-shaped leaves splashed in luminous shades of white, pink, rose, and emerald green, caladiums (C. × hortulanum) are native to South America. Evolved in warm tropical soils, their tubers are more sensitive to chilling than dahlias or cannas. Exposing their tubers to soil temperatures below 55°F (13°C) can prevent them from sprouting again. Don’t wait for frost to lift your caladiums: lift in early fall as soon as night temperatures consistently drop below 60°F (15°C) and leaves lose their hues. Lift the tubers gently using a fork, keeping the foliage attached to the tuber clumps.

Curing with the foliage intact allows your caladium to draw remaining moisture and vital nutrients back into the tuber. Place plants in a warm, dry, shaded room (70°F or 21°C) for 2-3 weeks. Once the leaves are crisp, snip them off at the tuber neck. Store the knobby tubers in dry peat moss or vermiculite, keeping them warmer than other bulbs, between 60-65°F (15-18°C), in an interior closet or heated utility room. Add a sprinkle of Down to Earth Crab Meal, from Amazon to the planting site before replanting caladiums to boost disease resistance against soil-borne nematodes.

Nothing creates a jungle-like aesthetic quite like elephant ears. Whether you grow wetland-loving Colocasia esculenta (such as near-black Black Magic) or upright alocasia, these foliage giants form thick corms that hate winter mud. Hardy in zones 8-11, gardeners in colder regions must lift them before freezing weather strikes. Lift either immediately after your first light frost blackens the leaf margins or as soon as fall soil temperatures drop below 50°F (10°C). Use sharp loppers or bypass pruners to cut the stalks to 3-6 inches (815cm) above the crown. Using a fork, dig around the base of the plant to pry the heavy corm out of the earth without severing offsets.

Wash away excess soil using a hose and trim stringy roots with snips. Allow the corms to cure in a warm, ventilated spot out of direct sunlight. Cure for 2 weeks until the outer surface feels dry. Wrap individual corms in newspaper or store in crates of dry wood shavings, such as CZ Grain Wood Shavings from Walmart, or peat-free coir. Keep them in a cool, dark location between 45-55°F (7-13°C), checking occasionally to ensure they remain dry. As a side note, hardy garden bergenia shares the nickname elephant's ears. While pink-flowering bergenia (B. cordifolia) is winter-hardy down to zone 3 and stays safe in the ground, tender tropical colocasia and alocasia must be lifted before freezing temperatures arrive.

Adored for their sculptural spathes and speckled foliage, calla lilies (Zantedeschia spp.) bring sultry elegance to patio containers and garden borders. While common white callas display modest cold tolerance in mild coastal climates, colorful hybrid callas (yellow, flame-orange, lavender, and deep burgundy) grow from tender, flat rhizomes that rot when subjected to prolonged freeze-thaw cycles. Lift rhizomes once the foliage turns yellow and withers, or immediately following the first light frost. Gently lift with a hand trowel or small fork, taking care not to scratch or bruise the surface. Cut away remaining foliage stems 1-2 inches (2.5-5cm) above the crown.

Lay the rhizomes out in a single layer on slatted trays or cardboard. Leave in a warm, dry, well-ventilated space (65-70°F / 18-21°C) to cure for 2 weeks. Once cured, pack the rhizomes in dry peat moss, vermiculite, or shredded newspaper inside breathable cardboard boxes. Store in a dark, dry space between 50-55°F (10-13°C), keeping the packing material dry to prevent premature sprouting. Next spring when potting up cured calla rhizomes, add a diluted seaweed or kelp drench such as GS Plant Foods Organic Liquid Kelp from Walmart to jumpstart root growth.

Taking a little time in October to lift and store your tender summer bulbs is one of the most rewarding investments you can make for next year’s blooms. By lifting these tender classics before winter freezes hit, you protect them from cold and ensure an even more spectacular return next summer. Always label them clearly with the plant variety and flower color as you pack them away: this will make mapping out your dream borders in spring a breeze.

#2185. South Korea: Medication Abortion Rules Too Restrictive

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image A box of Mifepristone combined with Misoprostol tablets, May 2026. © 2026 Soumyabrata Roy/NurPhoto via AP Photo

(Tokyo) – A South Korean government proposal would allow medication abortion in South Korea in 2027, but overly restrictive regulations will continue to impair the right of women and girls to safe and affordable abortion services, Human Rights Watch said today. National Assembly members have questioned government officials about the plan during a parliamentary audit. 

Seven years after South Korea’s Constitutional Court ruled the country’s decades-long abortion ban unconstitutional, effectively decriminalizing abortion and ordering legal reform by the end of 2020, the government has yet to amend the law to explicitly guarantee abortion rights. The government’s new proposal would finally establish a regulatory framework for medication abortion using Mifegymiso, a combination of Misoprostol and Mifepristone recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the safest and most effective regimen. But access to the medication will remain unnecessarily impaired. 

“Allowing use of abortion pills is an important step, but South Korean lawmakers need to ensure that regulations aren’t so restrictive that abortions are inaccessible and unaffordable,” said Susanne Bergsten, acting women’s rights research assistant at Human Rights Watch. “Access to safe abortion is essential to protecting the autonomy, health, and rights of women and girls.” 

The draft rules would, for the first two years, permit medication abortions only up to nine weeks of pregnancy and specify that only doctors in large hospitals can prescribe and dispense the medication. If no side effects are found, it would likely be distributed more widely after the trial period. 

These restrictions are inconsistent with best practices, which endorse use of these medications up to 12 weeks, and with WHO recommendations that a broad range of health workers be authorized to manage medical abortion. Blocking many qualified providers from prescribing and dispensing the medication could hinder patients from receiving timely and critical care. Denying or delaying abortion access violates the rights to life, health, privacy, and freedom from discrimination. 

Mifepristone is approved by around 100 countries worldwide, and has been used safely in France and China for nearly 40 years. Major complications requiring hospitalization following medication abortion are very rare and occur in less than 1 percent of cases. The WHO also recommends telemedecine models of medical abortion care, in which prescriptions can be sent directly to pharmacies for self-administration at home. It considers this approach as safe for pregnancies of up to 12 weeks, provided the pregnant person has “accurate information, quality medicines and support from a trained health worker (if she needs or wants it during the process).” 

South Korea is obligated under international human rights law to ensure that all women and girls in the country can readily access timely, affordable, and safe abortion services. 

To meet these obligations, the National Assembly urgently needs to enact legislation that clarifies the decriminalization of abortion, and amends South Korea’s Mother and Child Health Act to ensure that the rights of pregnant people are protected in all areas relevant to safe abortion care, including health care, counseling, and support services.

Abortions should be available at a pregnant person’s request, without requirements for parental or partner consent, notification or involvement, or accompaniment for adolescents or women with disabilities. Lawmakers should ensure that abortion services are covered by the National Health Insurance plan, to avoid costs becoming a barrier that prevents people from accessing abortion services. 

The government currently operates a crisis counseling system under the Special Act on Crisis Pregnancy and Protected Childbirth, enacted in 2023 for women and girls with unwanted or unintended pregnancies. However, the system has been criticized for not providing neutral advice and failing to provide adequate information about abortion, delaying access to abortion care. 

The law, which was intended to address unregistered births, also failed to introduce a comprehensive framework to ensure that all women in South Korea can register their child's birth, access pre- and postnatal care, receive financial support and services, and be free from the stigma and discrimination often faced by single mothers. The government should take genuine measures to support all pregnant people, whether they choose to continue or terminate a pregnancy by ensuring access to healthcare services throughout their pregnancy, at childbirth, and for childcare support. 

The government should also improve support and access to information for women and girls experiencing unintended or unwanted pregnancies. A 2023 report by Moimnet (Network to Assure Rights for Safe Abortion for All) found that many people face difficulties obtaining reliable abortion information. The government should provide unbiased information about reproductive health and abortion care, and ensure that schools deliver comprehensive, age-appropriate sexuality education. People seeking abortions should be able to make informed and autonomous decisions with support from healthcare providers who offer unbiased and respectful care. 

“South Korea’s National Assembly should swiftly align abortion legislation with international human rights standards to protect patients and providers,” Bergsten said. “Without safe and affordable access to abortion services, women and girls cannot fully experience their autonomy and reproductive rights.”

In early September 1948, a year after she rewilded the landscape of literature with Goodnight Moon, Margaret Wise Brown watched the love of her life fade to black.

Michael Strange, born Blanche Oelrichs, had cast an instant spell on Margaret — outspoken, sophisticated, and self-possessed, so tall Margaret had to lift her grey-blue eyes to meet the black of Michael’s, her tall frame clad in masculine clothing she herself had designed to cling to her curves, with a musical voice unspooling from her haunting dark beauty, a deep velvet laugh, and a reputation for rarely keeping a promise. When her wealthy family of Austrian royal lineage had found her erotic poetry embarrassing, Blanche had emancipated herself under the male nom de plume, which soon became a stage name as she strode into the theater world as playwright and actress, and eventually swelled into a total persona — the name with which she signed her letters, the name by which her intimates addressed her, the name of her self-image.

In her youth, Blanche had been named the most beautiful woman in Paris. Now, about to turn fifty-eight, Michael Strange was a ghost on a New York stage, her skin sallow, her body emaciated to the size of a child’s after refusing to let her aggressive leukemia keep her from performing.

Margaret and Michael had met seven years earlier. One day on Vinalhaven — the Maine island where Margaret would spend much of her life and write most of her books — she had rowed to a lover’s cottage and found the luscious stranger sunbathing there with her lover. Soon, back in New York, she was surprised to receive a lunch invitation from Michael, who had shown up dressed in fur from head to toe, asking bold questions about her love life while sipping sherry. Margaret was thirty, Michael fifty and on her third unhappy marriage; her latest husband had never read her poetry. Both women were born in the wrong century, bent on bending it to their will; both were accidental radicals, just by living unselfconsciously; both had had affairs with Thomas Wolfe; both were at heart poets more than anything else.

By the middle of the World War, they were lovers; Michael had declared that she had never loved anyone the way she loved Margaret and never would; she had promised to love her until her dying day.

Late one night, Margaret’s phone rang. Michael’s voice poured in, sped up with alarm, imploring her to get into a taxi right away. Her husband had found out about their relationship and, in an era when the diagnostic manual of psychiatry classified same-sex love as a mental illness, was threatening to have her locked away in an asylum. A doctor was on his way to “diagnose” her. With her maid’s help, Michael managed to slip out through the back staircase and into the taxi as Margaret was pulling up.

On the disorienting ride through the New York nocturne, they weighed their options and decided to head to the high-society women’s club Michael frequented. There, she collected herself, phoned her husband to demand a formal apology, then set the wheels in motion for a legal separation.

From this point on, Michael became — to use the modern term, hard-won and ahistorical — Margaret’s partner. Soon, they were living across the hallway from each other, in a pair of twin apartments on the East End, with Margaret part nominal tenant and part unnamed wife as she was quickly becoming one of the country’s most original and beloved children’s book authors.

It was a stormy love that pushed and pulled, but grafted itself onto Margaret’s being. Michael wrote adoring letters and criticized Margaret’s diction at dinner parties. She gave her a golden wishbone necklace and a ring, made her feel like she was too needy, and derided her children’s books as unsophisticated, “silly furry stories,” not Real Literature: an actress and socialite who had not published a poem in a decade and was feeling abandoned by her own muse, deriding one of the most vibrantly creative people of the past century — poet, songwriter, progressive education reformer, author of more than a hundred singularly wondrous books for the young, with which she would earn herself a little red house, a yellow convertible, and the love of millions of children; the author whom the visionary Ursula Nordstrom had no qualms calling her favorite author, despite also publishing Maurice Sendak, Shel Silverstein, and E.B. White. Even Michael’s pet name for Margaret was laced with this ambivalent mixture of affection and disdain: Bunny-no-good.

And yet nobody ever knows what electrifies the infinite sky between two people, what magnetizes them together, what roils deep beneath the faint surface trails left in letters and diaries and the recollections of bystanders, what animates the long days between the islanded moments crashed by emotion and frozen in time. Margaret loved Michael with unassailable devotion, and Michael was dying. He agreed out of love for Margaret, but he resented being rushed and did not condone her relationship with Michael. For an artist of such staggering talent and versatility, the unfeeling two-tone illustrations he produced for this layered, lovely book were by far his weakest. A $200 or $300 credit for booking the same fare you would find elsewhere is free money, and having one point of contact at AMEX Travel beats calling a cruise line call center when something goes sideways. That said, many agencies have similar (sometimes better benefits) without adding to the cost of the sailing and most agencies don’t charge for their services.

It is a weaker play at the top of the market. On a luxury yacht suite that runs well into five figures per person, a $300 stateroom credit is a rounding error, and the 2x earn is modest. Chase Sapphire Reserve, by comparison, earns 8x on Chase Travel purchases. The difference is structural. AMEX Travel is the agency of record on the booking, so you cannot pair the AMEX amenity with the perks an independent advisor would have negotiated for the same cabin. You pick one or the other, and on expensive sailings the advisor package is often the bigger number. An example is below in the agency section.

Chase competes in the space. Voyages by Chase Travel attaches perks to designated sailings booked through Chase Travel, including up to $100 in onboard credit plus a specialty dining experience on Royal Caribbean, up to $200 in onboard credit plus free laundry on Virgin Voyages, and a $250 per person Destination Experience Credit on Explora Journeys. Credits are typically added to the reservation automatically. Chase does not market it with anywhere near the polish of the AMEX rebrand, but on Explora a per person credit can beat a per stateroom one, that said, Destination Experience credits must be applied toward an excursion offered by the brand vs a Journey Experience credit which can be used on anything (and is offered by an approved travel agency.)

Beyond those two, I could not find a comparable cruise program from the other premium issuers. That makes two obvious targets. Capital One already runs the Premier Collection for luxury hotels on the $395 Venture X, with a $100 experience credit on those stays, as CNBC notes. Bolting cruise lines onto that framework would be a natural next step. Citi is the second. The $595 Strata Elite built The Reserve by Citi Travel for 4.5 to 5 star hotels, per the same CNBC comparison, and Citi has the American Airlines relationship to pair air with sailings. Either bank could launch a credible rival with partners AMEX has already shown will sign.

The truth of card travel programs is that they are travel agencies with a bank logo. A luxury agency in a consortium like Virtuoso can often deliver more. Virtuoso advisors regularly secure onboard credits ranging from about $100 to more than $1,000, complimentary shore excursions, upgrades when available, and on Virtuoso Voyages sailings a private reception and an onboard host.

As you know, I own Scott & Thomas, a Virtuoso agency that sells many of the same lines in the AMEX collection, so weigh my view accordingly. My advice is simple. Before booking a five-figure sailing through any card portal, get a quote from a luxury advisor for the same cabin and compare the full package, not just the fare.

One thing that AMEX and Chase can’t do that only specific agencies can is offer unpublished fares unavailable to the public. We are one of those agencies and have access to unpublished rates from 16 different premium, luxury, and expedition cruise lines (over 1,200 at last check.) They could offer this in spots but don’t have the right market shape to sell targeted sailings in the way an agency can. The card programs are more of a generalist approach whereas a qualified agency offers something more.

The Cruise & Yacht Collection is a smart rebrand of a perk that was already decent, and for a Platinum cardholder booking without an advisor it is worth using every time. It is not the best deal available at the luxury end, and Chase has a credible alternative on some lines. The fact that Capital One and Citi have not answered yet feels temporary. Expect at least one of them to follow AMEX onto the water within the next year.
