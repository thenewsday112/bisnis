#450. The asteroid that killed the dinosaurs may have helped ants conquer the world

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Around 66 million years ago, an asteroid struck Earth and brought the age of the dinosaurs to an end. The mass extinction that followed created opportunities for countless other animals to evolve and spread. Ants were among the groups that eventually became remarkably successful, and scientists have now identified a surprising genetic factor that may have helped fuel their rise.

Today, ants are the largest family of social insects, with more than 15,000 species found around the world. New research published in Science Advances suggests that their extraordinary evolutionary success was partly driven by mobile segments of DNA known as transposable elements, or "jumping genes."

The international study was led by Dr. Lukas Schrader of the Institute for Evolution and Biodiversity (IEB) at the University of Münster (Germany).

Jumping genes are pieces of DNA that can move to different locations within a genome and produce additional copies of themselves. Scientists once viewed them primarily as "genomic parasites" because, much like viruses, they can multiply without providing any clear benefit to their host. In some cases, their activity can even contribute to disease.

More recently, however, researchers have begun recognizing that these mobile genetic elements can also create opportunities for evolutionary change. The new findings suggest they played an important role in the remarkable diversification of ants.

When the scientists compared different groups of ants, they discovered that lineages containing the greatest numbers of jumping genes also tended to have the most species today.

Even more revealing was the timing of these genetic changes. The researchers found that several major ant lineages experienced separate surges in jumping gene activity during the early Paleogene (about 66 million years ago). These bursts occurred shortly before the groups began expanding into the thousands of species that exist today.

"The asteroid impact had dramatic consequences for the environment. We have now found the genomic mechanism that connects these ecological upheavals to the subsequent rapid diversification of the ants: transposable elements," explains Lukas Schrader.

Ants are not the only animals showing this evolutionary pattern. Previous studies have also identified periods of increased jumping gene activity associated with the emergence of new species in other groups, including primates and bats.

The researchers also discovered a connection between jumping genes and the expansion of gene families that allow ants to communicate through chemical signals.

Of particular importance were genes responsible for odorant receptors, which enable ants to detect and respond to smells. These receptors are essential to nearly every aspect of ant social behavior. Ants depend heavily on chemical cues to find their way through the environment, identify members of their own colonies, and coordinate activities with other ants.

The findings suggest that jumping genes may have contributed to the expansion of these sensory systems, potentially improving the ability of ants to detect and interpret the chemical messages that make their complex societies possible.

Tracing 100 Million Years of Ant Evolution

To investigate how jumping genes influenced ant evolution, the international research team examined and compared the genomes of 163 ant species. These represented twelve of the 16 ant subfamilies that survive today, providing a broad picture of the genetic diversity found across modern ants.

Using these genomic comparisons, the scientists reconstructed how transposable elements evolved and changed in activity over the past 100 million years.

Their results reveal a striking connection between ancient bursts of jumping gene activity and the later diversification of ants, suggesting that DNA sequences once dismissed as genetic parasites may have helped one of Earth's most successful insect groups flourish.

#366. Curiosity Blog, Sols 5043-5049: Preparing to Drill at ‘Nazca’

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Mars Science Laboratory Mission Team Members

Written by Lucy Lim, Planetary Scientist at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center

Earth planning date: Friday, Oct. 16, 2026

Following the Basque Lakes drill campaign, Curiosity has returned to a spot just a few meters away from the "Chiu Chiu" block to conduct our next drill campaign. "Chiu Chiu" itself wasn't a good candidate for drilling but the block just beyond it was much more promising, so it became our drive target during Monday's planning. I was on planning Friday as Geology and Mineralogy Science Theme Lead when we received our close-up views of the neighbor block from the post-drive imaging and our team was happy to see that it was jam-packed with the "disc- and needle-" shaped lumps, just like "Chiu Chiu." We quickly gave the science go-ahead to proceed with preparations for drilling at the newly named "Nazca" site.

One major hypothesis about how the "discs and needles" texture formed is that the characteristic shapes were originally created during the growth of crystals, or "crystal forms." Minerals will tend to grow into specific shapes depending on their microscopic crystal structure, similar to the salt crystals that you can grow in your kitchen as a home science project. (Maybe you have grown one of those for a science class in school!) The relatively large sizes of the crystal forms at "Nazca" and "Chiu Chiu" are providing clues about the temperature at the time when they grew and the availability of salts and fluids. However, we can't necessarily assume that the modern-day composition represents the original mineral because sometimes mineral crystals are replaced by other minerals some time after original crystallization. The new minerals are then known as "pseudomorphs" because they have the same shape as the first minerals but not the same composition or molecular structure.

As we prepared for the drill preload test, we also planned several special observations aimed at highly detailed characterization of the crystal forms. MAHLI will execute a Quantitative Relief Model ("QRM") observation consisting of five overlapping images at slightly different geometries. The combination of these will enable a detailed three-dimensional relief model to be constructed. In addition, MAHLI will also add extra frames to its highest-resolution imaging by doubling the number of focus mechanism steps. Together, these observations will increase the resolution of the 3D models we will have of the crystal forms.

Meanwhile, the ChemCam LIBS will conduct a "depth profile" experiment to obtain compositional information from slightly deeper layers within a nearby crystal-form-rich target, "Toucan". Normally the 150 LIBS laser shots from each sol's observation are spaced out over a five-point raster at 30 shots per point, but this time we are concentrating all 150 laser shots on the same spot on the rock. Since each laser shot vaporizes a very small amount of the rock to form a plasma, the 150-shot profile should penetrate approximately five times deeper than a normal 30-shot point observation. We will thus be able to observe the composition deeper inside the exposed crystal form and see whether it's different from the surface-level composition. There are several reasons this could be the case including exposure of the surface to the modern Martian environment and also the circumstances in which the crystal form developed originally, so it's an experiment worth pursuing.

The LIBS, DRT and APXS will also be applied to the "Nazca" spot before the preload test to characterize its surface composition as thoroughly as possible before drilling. The third LIBS of the plan will go to "Condor," a nearby block in which a finely layered texture is exposed.

We also continued to observe the modern Martian environment as twilight cloud season begins. In this plan we included some nighttime imaging of the larger and brighter of Mars' two satellites, Phobos. Capturing images of Phobos and the nearby sky when Phobos is full can show sunlight reflecting off the moon and scattering in the Martian atmosphere, providing a good way to estimate the particle sizes in the Martian clouds. We can do similar measurements sometimes with solar observations, but the Phobos data allow us to measure cloud particle sizes later in the night. We expect to be measuring high-altitude carbon dioxide (CO2) clouds in this observation.

#2876. Day 13 part2: The Final Resting Place for Tomás the Templar Knight

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

We followed the path away from Cruz de Ferro somewhat less burdened. Suzanne placed her stone and the clay brick, I’d tossed my stone. We’d each left something significant and now I wanted to find out what had become of Tomás , the last Templar Knight and his “kingdom”.

I’d been walking with Peter in 2017 when he asked if I wouldn’t mind staying with Tomás , or should we try out a new albergue in the next town with a swimming pool. Adventure or comfort? Is this really a choice?

In 1993, Tomás had moved to Manjarín and set a refugio in its abandoned schoolhouse. He was part of the early wave of simple refugios of the 1990s. In 2017, there were five of us who stayed there with no common language, but Peter spoke some of each, so we got bits of the story Tomás told at dinner, as Peter dropped a few words in English and then moved on to the next language:

First night… big storm… howling wind… candle in window… lost pilgrims… darkness… door blown open… pilgrims enter and Tomás now stands and shouts: Una luz en el Camino as he raises his hand — “A light on the Way”.

For years, I’d wondered what had become of Tomás and his A light on the Way. The refugio was primitive—no running water or electricity, cooking and heat by woodstoves, and outside flags flapping in the mountain winds. To all who stayed there, it is a grateful memory, men upstairs in a dorm of floor futons, women below on beds (if I remember correctly).

And that haunting bell he rang after dark to guide lost pilgrims from the fog, it became our wake-up call at dawn. I still remember Tomás grumbling about pilgrims who left before dawn insisting that the trail belonged to the night until the sun rose.

We saw the sign for Manjarín, then stopped for coffee at the food truck. The place looked reminiscent, but not how I remember it. Given my experience at Cruz de Ferro, where I had remembered a different look to the place, I was shaken, had these memories changed also?

Charlie and Sarah are there, and I told them about my stay at Manjarín, and Tomás. I get the impression they are private room pilgrims—Charlie booked their stays 9 months ago—so I saw a bit of horror in their eyes as I described the place. Finally, I asked the food truck lady, and she pointed down the road. Phew, so this wasn’t the place.

Back to the road, accompanied by a herd of cowbells, there it was, Manjarín, the castle of Tomás , The light of the Way. The place looks like I remember it, the flags flying, and its eclectic nature, continuing.

Where Peter and I stayed in 2017, men on the top floor, women on ground level

I asked about Tomás , and they pointed to his grave. They were making donativo wax stamps, something I had seen at Carnival de Ferro. Sarah and Charlie walk up, and watch as they make wax seals in our credentials.

The grave of the last Templar Knight, Tomas

It turns out Charlie and Sarah are staying at the then new Albergue, the one with the pool about two hours away. We decide to land there too, if possible.

Cruz de Ferro had left me questioning my memories, they had become so different from reality. Malcolm Gladwell calls these flashbulb memories — the vivid, emotionally charged memories we form during major events. Right after 9/11, a large group of people were surveyed about who they were with and what they were doing when they found out about the attacks. Years later 40% remember quite differently from what they reported then, and yet their confidence in the accuracy of their memories remains almost 100%. The same certainty I felt about Cruz de Ferro, even when confronted with the pictures I took nine years ago, still haunts me. I can still see it as it only exists in my mind. Unmoored, it feels. How could my memories be so different from objective reality?

Following the Way downhill and away from Manjarín I felt restored, maybe even vindicated. I knew the rest of the story, Tomás had passed in January 2026, and his funeral was held in Ponferrada, where we should be tomorrow after the long scramble down. I was thankful the place wasn’t abandoned, even if it was just a couple of guys melting donativo wax stamps. It felt alive, and seemed to honor this man who was one of the first generation of hospitalerios. Those memories were intact, for now.

Pilgrims who follow will never know this Templar Knight who gave so much to the Camino. It is a gentle reminder that the Way we walk today is never the same Way others walk, or even the one I walked years ago.

Tomas, circa 2017, stamping my credentials

Its four miles to El Acebo, where we expect to meet up with Charlie and Sarah. We do meet up with them, but they are dragging their suitcases up the long hill of the village from the place with the pool downhill, where their luggage was delivered, uphill to the other hostel with the same name. The pool one is 60 euros a night, but they don’t have a reservation, and the other one 16, where they do. They look tired and discouraged.

On a whim, we decide to check out La Trucha del Arco Iris Casa Rural, or the Rainbow Trout Country Lodge. We walk into the garden, the door is locked, and as we find the number and are fixing to WhatsApp it, when Jamie, the owner walks up.

He takes a long thinking pause. He had just come from the field, where there is a small hut. A dog circles us, smelling our feet, as if to check us out. Jamie looks at the dog, then at us, the dog wagged its tail, and he says “We have … room. Welcome”. He explains someone had made a reservation six months ago, and he was expecting their luggage to arrive, but no luggage and no pilgrims, so the room is ours!

Suzanne is excited for a self-contained private room!

It’s a beautiful place, once his home, but now he lives in the hut of the field with his wife of 25 years. Today is their anniversary. This house is a home without corners, a labor of love, and tribute to the Camino. Jamie says “The Camino gave me this home, it gave me my wife, and now gives me meaning.” It’s a fascinating home appointed with several admirable craftmanship details, the most prominent are curves doors and edges where corners should be.

Top of the door showing how curved it is, and above the door center, see there is no corner to the room?

Jamie directed us to a bar in town, the only place open for a pilgrim meal. For dinner they serve stewed horse, surprisingly delicious and tender. As we look around the communal tables of gathered pilgrims, we realize we do not know a single soul. I try making small talk with a mother and two adult daughters who are travel agents, from Texas and Aggies. Suzanne does a better job at engaging the Scandinavian sitting across from her, but for me, my conversation feels thin, shallow, void of any connection, and it wasn’t because we’re Longhorns.

I sat there wondering if we were even on the same Camino as any of them. Did we intersect another Camino? Maybe they don’t stay in the kind of places we do, or maybe they are just part of the collection of NPCs non-player/pilgrim-characters who quickly change their dinner orders to Spanish Tortilla as soon as they learn the meat is horse; they have no sense of adventure.

We never see any of them again, NPCs for sure.

#668. Unveiling Starbirth Mysteries

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

In 1985, the physicist Heinz R. Pagels wrote, "Stars are like animals in the wild. We may see the young, but never the actual birth, which is a veiled and secret event." In the decades since then, astronomers have drawn closer and closer to figuring out what actually goes on in the veiled stellar nurseries throughout the Universe. The tale of starbirth we know today invokes the collapse of clouds of gas and dust into prestellar cores, but it still has many unknowns, such as the role of magnetic fields in the clouds where stars are born.

Researchers from Kyushu University and the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics have used radio astronomy techniques to study the magnetic field influence in prestellar cores. Lead researcher Doris Arzoumanian at Kyushu explained her team's findings using the Institute for Radio Astronomy in the Millimetre Range (IRAM) to study a prestellar core called L1544. It lies in a region of the Milky Way called the Taurus Molecular Complex. At a distance of 140 parsecs, it's one of the closest star-forming regions to Earth.

A graphical representation of ion-neutral drift in the L1544 prestellar core. The blue lines represent the magnetic field lines, which are bent due to the gravitational contraction of the core. The red and green dots depict the ion and neutral molecular species, respectively, and the arrows trace their inflow motion towards the core center (the faster they travel, the longer the arrows). While in the outer part of the core both the ions and neutrals are attached to the magnetic field lines, within the inner part of the core the neutrals decouple from the magnetic field lines and infall faster compared to the ions, which remain attached to the field lines. This ion-neutral decoupling, known as ambipolar diffusion is required for the onset of the gravitational collapse of the prestellar core, which will produce a protostar in its center and ultimately a stellar system similar to our own Solar System. Credit: Arzoumanian, et al.

âPrestellar cores are fascinating stellar bodies," said Arzoumanian. "They are dense and cold, and a source of a lot of complex chemistry. The cold environment allows for molecules to assemble into more complex ones like precursors of prebiotic organic molecules. One of the questions we are investigating is the role of magnetic fields in star formation. Strong magnetic fields permeate prestellar cores. If that field is too strong, it can delay gravitational collapse and therefore star formation. We wanted to investigate how prestellar cores reduce the strength of their magnetic field.â

The result of the team's research is the detection of a phenomenon called "ambipolar diffusion". This happens when neutral particles in a plasma decouple from the plasma. In the molecular clouds that are the most likely homes to starbirth creches, there are both neutral particles and some amount of partially ionized gas where the ions are strongly coupled to magnetic fields that thread through the cloud. Neutral particles interact with the embedded magnetic field indirectly through collisions, so their velocities can be measured. Studies such as Arzoumanian's give key insights into the activity of molecules in the core and with its magnetic fields.

Prestellar cores are cold regions, despite the fact that they're coalescing to form future stars. They can be anywhere from 10 to 30 degrees Kelvin (about -263 C to -243 C). That's cold enough to freeze molecules to dust grains, essentially veiling them from deeper analysis. So, the team searched around for other molecules to act as "tracers" of activity. âWe selected Diazenylium-d1 (N2D+), an ion, and para-monodeuterated ammonia (para-NH2D), a neutral molecule, as our tracers because they are generally located in similar high-density regions within prestellar cores,â said team member Silvia Spezzano, group leader at the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics. âWe therefore collected spectral data of the core and modeled the velocity of the two molecules.â

The result was a very clear velocity difference between those molecules of about 0.05 km/second, which the team interpreted as "ion-neutral drift". This happens as the density of the prestellar core increases during accretion. It gets protected from radiation, and that puts a damper on ionization. The end result is that the coupling between the molecules and the embedded magnetic field grows weaker. As the neutral particles fall towards the center of the prestellar core, they speed up. In the meantime, the ions remain coupled to the magnetic field, causing the velocity difference. This process is ambipolar diffusion, and it has been difficult to observe. "Until now, observing this phenomenon in a prestellar core was a major challenge,â said Arzoumanian. âAs ambipolar diffusion continues, the strength of the magnetic field decreases. Eventually, gravity becomes the primary driving force in the core, resulting in its gravitational collapse into a protostar.â

To confirm their finding, the team hopes to observe other prestellar cores using techniques of radio astronomy, as well as principles of dust physics and astrochemistry, according to Arzoumanian. A larger sample of prestellar cores should be examined to study ion-neutral pairs in cores at different stages of evolution. Statistical studies will also provide a way to assess the impact of the magnetic field geometry and density structures in these cores, along with other effects. The team also suggests that future studies could focus on ion-neutral drift velocities in other structures in star-forming regions, such as dense filaments.

Such observations should give new insights, essentially pulling aside the veil on other aspects of star formation and an understanding of the 3D magnetic field strength in cores, and more insights into the evolution of dust size distribution within prestellar cores as they continue on the road to forming stars. âUnderstanding star formation addresses a fundamental question about the origin of life in planetary systems and helps us better understand the universe as a whole," said Arzoumanian.

Staged within the domestic interior of VILLA tbh in Shanghai, ‘it’s raining outside, it’s raining inside’ is a solo exhibition by designer and artist Duyi Han. Developed following a multi-week residency in the residence, the site-specific installation configures a ‘Listening Garden’ composed of textile sculptures, hand embroidery, objects, and acoustic elements. Drawing visual and atmospheric references from rainfall, cinema, fashion, and pop music, the project investigates how soft furnishings and narrative materiality alter spatial perception within a historic home.

At the core of the installation by artist Duyi Han, a custom carpet printed with concentric rain-ripple motifs grounds a cluster of pleated, translucent organza sculptures. The domestic rooms feature varied contextual interventions: a sheer, tailored garment suspended within a timber vitrine, embroidered sneakers arranged as static sculptural accents, resin water droplets suspended over the kitchen sink, and backlit textiles softening the bathroom surfaces. Graphic fragments of song lyrics and film dialogues are embroidered across the woven fabrics, embedding typographic narratives directly into the decorative textiles to link sound, light, and interior form throughout the residence.

#6. VCs will make billions on Anthropic’s IPO, but Amazon is set to make much (much) more

Many investors stand to win big when Anthropic goes public. Stakes held by firms including Menlo Ventures, Sequoia, Sound Ventures, and Spark Capital are expected to return billions.

Yet according to Anthropic’s S-1, none of those VC firms owns 5%, the threshold for being included in the principal stockholders’ table. That’s highly unusual. In a typical tech IPO, lead venture investors hold double-digit-percentage stakes in the startups they backed. The reason is Anthropic’s size. At its $965 billion private valuation, a 1% stake is worth nearly $10 billion, so, for example, Menlo’s roughly $14 billion position is only about 1.5% of the company, according to recently reported Bloomberg figures.

While the venture firms may lack sizable percentage stakes, Anthropic sold large stakes to strategic partners, companies that are also its business partners. Indeed, Amazon is likely the single largest financial beneficiary of the IPO.

Amazon first backed Anthropic in 2023 with a commitment to invest up to $4 billion in the AI company. Since then, Amazon has poured billions more into Anthropic. Instead of taking stock upfront, Amazon structured much of its investment as convertible notes, a type of debt that converts into shares upon an IPO or sale.

According to the S-1, Amazon holds convertible notes with $6.2 billion in principal outstanding as of June 30. That is Anthropic’s entire debt load. But as Anthropic’s valuation soared across successive funding rounds, the shares those notes will convert into became far more valuable. Amazon’s own filings valued its note holdings at $97.9 billion as of June 30.

As the value of Amazon’s convertible notes surged, Anthropic had to record the growing value of what it owes on its books. It reported the adjustments on its income statement under “change in fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities, and SAFEs,” a line that totaled $72.4 billion for the first half of 2026. The charge is non-cash, meaning no money left the company.

Amazon’s notes could convert into as many as 1.84 billion shares, according to the S-1. But a cap limits Amazon to 12% of Anthropic’s “partially diluted” share count, a figure that counts some shares not yet issued, such as stock options. Amazon can waive the cap. Notes that can’t convert will stay outstanding and convert in later quarters, subject to the same limit.

But the notes are only part of Amazon’s holdings. It further invested $10 billion in nonvoting preferred stock (a type of equity), in the second quarter, according to Amazon’s quarterly report for the period ended June 30. Amazon did this by investing $5 billion in each of Anthropic’s last two funding rounds. Combined with the $8 billion it invested in the notes from 2023 through 2025, that brings Amazon’s total investment to about $18 billion.

Beyond its stock gains, Amazon will also continue to earn significant revenue from Anthropic, which has committed to spend at least $110 billion on AWS over the next 10 years.

Google, too, is set for a major windfall from both the listing and its cloud computing deals with Anthropic. The search giant held about 14% of Anthropic as of early last year, according to court filings that were reported by The New York Times, and has invested more since, including $10 billion in April. (Google was likely diluted in subsequent and massive funding rounds, with PitchBook analyst Harrison Rolfes telling TechCrunch he estimates its current stake at closer to 5.6%.) It has also secured at least $114 billion in cloud commitments from the Anthropic through 2033.

While VCs set for massive paydays have little reason to complain, the S-1 makes it clear that the biggest winners of Anthropic’s IPO may well be tech giants like Amazon, which backed a would-be rival early on and now sell it much of the computing power it runs on.

The S-1 itself warns that some of those cloud providers also build rival AI models and that they may not always want the same things that Anthropic does.

#2694. Vanja Matijevec Gave Up His Own Everest Summit to Save Three People

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Vanja Matijevec on the Lhotse wall during his 1981 Himalayan expedition (Photo: Aleš Kunaver, kept by the National Museum of Contemporary History of Slovenia)

Vanja Matijevec belonged to a generation of Slovenian alpinists who pushed onto some of the biggest walls in the Himalayas, but one of the defining moments of his climbing life came when he chose not to summit.

On his legendary 1979 expedition to Everest, Matijevec gave up his own planned summit attempt to help Stipe Božić, Stane Belak and Ang Phu, who had become stranded above 8,000 meters during their descent. Božić later remembered Matijevec’s selflessness as helping him survive after he lost a crampon high on the mountain.

Born in Ljubljana on October 14, 1946, Matijevec first encountered the Slovenian mountains as a child, tending cattle on pastures above Tržič. He came to alpinism later, after meeting climber Jože Hočevar through his field work, and began training as a mountain guide in 1975. In 1979, Matijevec, Lado Vidmar and Blaž Oblak made the first ascent of Lucifer (WI4+), a roughly 200-meter frozen waterfall in Gozd Martuljek. The climb, completed with equipment Matijevec later remembered as rudimentary, helped usher in the era of modern ice climbing in Slovenia.

But Matijevec’s ambitions stretched far beyond Slovenia. In 1981, Matijevec and Franček Knez climbed to the rim of Lhotse’s immense South Face, then considered one of the great unsolved problems of Himalayan alpinism. Over the course of his life, Matijevec accumulated more than 30 first ascents, including a direct new route up the West Face of Trisul in 1987. Despite his achievements, his peers remember his kindness more than anything. Fellow Slovenian alpinist Viki Grošelj, who had known Matijevec since their early climbing days, remembered him after his death as a distinctive personality and, simply, “the right man in the right place,” he told Slovenian newspaper Delo.

#440. Scientists discover the immune system’s hidden sense of touch

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Scientists at McGill University have discovered a surprising ability in immune cells that could reshape our understanding of how the body develops lasting protection against infections.

For years, researchers believed that tissue-resident memory T cells, specialized immune cells that remain in the body's tissues after an infection and respond quickly if the same threat returns, were guided mainly by chemical signals.

However, a new study published in Nature Immunology challenges that assumption. Researchers found that T cells can detect how stiff their surrounding tissues are and use this physical information to influence whether they develop into long-lasting memory cells. The findings suggest that adjusting to the stronger mechanical forces within tissues may be important for these protective cells to survive over time.

"This is an exciting fundamental finding that challenges the way we think about immune cells," said lead author Judith Mandl, a professor in McGill's Department of Physiology. "It suggests T cells can 'feel' their surroundings and adapt to them, making the physical environment an active force in shaping their behavior."

The research brought together scientists from McGill's Department of Physiology, the Alan Edwards Centre for Research on Pain, the Department of Bioengineering, and the McGill University Health Centre, along with collaborating institutions in the Netherlands and the United States.

How the Discovery Could Influence Cancer and Immune Treatments

Understanding how T cells respond to physical conditions could eventually open new possibilities for treating diseases in which the immune system mistakenly targets healthy tissue. These include autoimmune disorders, allergies, and complications involving transplant rejection.

The discovery could also have important implications for cancer research, particularly efforts to improve treatments that use the immune system to fight tumors.

"Tumors are often dense and physically difficult for immune cells to penetrate. Understanding how T cells adapt to these environments could help researchers design cancer immunotherapies that are better able to reach and attack tumors," said Mandl.

How Scientists Discovered the Immune Cells' Hidden Sense of Touch

To investigate whether physical surroundings influence T cell behavior, the researchers studied immune cells collected from humans and mice. They placed the cells inside specially designed collagen gels that mimicked tissues with different levels of stiffness.

The experiments revealed a clear pattern. As the gels became stiffer, T cells were increasingly likely to acquire characteristics associated with tissue-resident memory cells, which help provide long-term immune protection.

These results indicate that the physical properties of surrounding tissues can help shape how immune cells develop, adding an important new dimension to scientists' understanding of immune memory.

The researchers now hope to identify the specific molecular sensor that enables T cells to detect their physical environment. Determining how this sensor converts mechanical forces into changes in cell behavior could help explain how the immune system establishes lasting protection.

The study received funding from the Human Frontier Science Program, the Canadian Institutes of Health Research, the Canadian Foundation for Innovation, the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada, and the U.S. National Institutes of Health.
