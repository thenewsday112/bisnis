#1020. In <i><em>A Court of Splintered Harmony,</em></i> Sarah J. Maas Dismantles the Romantic Fantasy of Breastfeeding

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Not even the High Fae of Prythian are spared from chapped nipples in the latest installment of the global sensation

“Breastfeeding is the most natural thing in the world,” they say while you stare blankly at 300,000 plastic pump and bottle pieces, holding cold metal caps to your chapped, raw nipples so that they don’t fall off. It took four appointments with lactation consultants before my son and I got the hang of it, this supposedly natural thing. But I’m stubborn. Persistent. I went on to breastfeed and pump for over 14 months. I pumped on New Jersey Transit and at my desk. I pumped in Paris, carrying bags of warm milk down to the concierge to freeze so I could fly it home, this precious hard-earned gold.

Breastfeeding was, by far, my most triumphant accomplishment. And while it may come easily to some mothers, for so many others, the fantasy of breastfeeding is swiftly killed by the cold, hard reality of it.

In the sleep-deprived months after I had my son, I turned to books for an emotional escape. I wanted to spend time in worlds so very different from my own, where I could read inspiring stories of women fighting gods and demons to find love and happily-ever-afters. And also hot sex between hot Fae, obviously. The romantic-fantasy genre, or romantasy, as the industry calls it, has been the biggest publishing success story of the last five or six years, with sales reaching $1 billion in the United States alone last year. The genre’s shining star is Sarah J. Maas and her A Court of Thorns and Roses (ACOTAR) series, which first launched in 2015. As of the most recent sales data, the 40-year-old author has sold more than 100 million copies of her books.

After a five-year wait, fans of ACOTAR, myself included, are once again falling through the pages into the magical world of Prythian, as the sixth book, A Court of Splintered Harmony, hits shelves today. The gang is back together, so to speak: There’s Nesta Archeron and her mate Cassian. Sister Elain Archeron and the mate she doesn’t want, Lucien Vanserra. There’s broody batboy Azriel, Amren, Mor, and Emerie, as well as priestess Gwyneth Berdara (and a few other surprise appearances). And of course, there’s no ACOTAR without Feyre Archeron and her mate, Rhysand, High Lord and Lady of the Night Court, and their six-month-old miracle baby, Nyx.

Plot details were scant before the highly anticipated book was released, so I went into my read not knowing what to expect. While some part of me thought we’d get cute details of Feyre’s journey into motherhood, as Maas herself is a mom of two and has spoken openly about her experience, the one thing I never expected to find in ACOSH was one of the best, most realistic depictions of breastfeeding I’ve ever read—in a fantasy novel, no less.

Texts from friends who were also reading the book came quickly: “Why is she talking so much about her boobs?” “Leaky boobs are the new watery bowels.” “Going from a sweet breastfeeding scene to Rhysand wanting to suck Feyre’s nipples is giving me whiplash.”

For fans expecting nothing but playful banter and graphic sex, the book’s frequent mentions of painfully engorged breasts, dresses lined with nursing pads, partners sexually turned on by the aforementioned engorged breasts, and the delicate dance that is timing your life around feeding your child might strike some as bizarre. Gross, even. Too freaky. But if the mentions of breastfeeding feel like a lot … it’s because breastfeeding is a lot. I came across one early review from a fan who felt like Feyre had been reduced to nothing but a milk machine. That’s not an entirely fair take, but it’s also not not true: Anyone who has exclusively breastfed can tell you that’s what you feel like, for months on end.

Plus, Maas has never shied away from the physicality of the human experience. We’ve read her female main characters literally piss themselves in front of love interests. Nearly bleed out in childbirth. Vomit. Get their period out in the wild without sanitary precautions. Describe how their anxiety manifests as “watery bowels.” This is standard practice for Maas, who masterfully blends the humbling realities of womanhood with Fae lore, magic, and political fantasy.

In ACOSH, Feyre is High Lady of the Night Court, nobility, armed with the magic of all seven courts. Her Fae body is stronger and more resilient than her human body ever was—but that still isn’t enough to protect her from the hardships of breastfeeding. She winces watching Nyx teethe on a toy, thinking of her boobs. That’s more reality than fantasy. She mentions her chapped, sore nipples and how she wonders what message her leaking boobs might send to her political rivals. If it might negate the seriousness of her speech. That, too, struck me as reality. She mentions having to hand-express while away from her son and the delightful rush of the milk being released. I’ve been there too. And in one of the more beautiful moments on the page, Feyre takes out her bare breast and feeds him in front of all her family and friends, and not one of them—not the loudmouthed warrior Cassian or grumpy old Amren—makes a snarky comment. For many women, this is a fantasy. In real life, we have far more work to do in dismantling a patriarchal society that shouts loudly about how breastfeeding is best and yet only wants it done in secret. Away from the eyes of men who find the act inherently sexual.

Ah, but here is where things get interesting. Can breastfeeding and sexuality coexist? Maas says yes. I’ve been ruminating on a scene in this book where Rhysand, after just witnessing Feyre feed their son to sleep, tells her not to bother buttoning up her nightgown. Moments later, they tumble together onto the bed.

It’s perhaps one of the last remaining taboos: the notion that parents, that mothers, want to have passionate sex. That they can—and do—often code-switch from a moment of maternal love to an act of sexual intimacy. It’s not an easy mental jump, but it happens, and it can be learned.

In Maas’s fantasy world, where hot men have wings and fated mates exist, not even Fae are spared from the realities of breastfeeding or new motherhood. Working moms are working moms, whether they’re commuting to an office job in New York City or meeting with political rivals to stave off an invasion of the human lands. In bringing the reality of breastfeeding to the pages of A Court of Splintered Harmony, Maas aims to disarm our culture of silence around the physical toll it takes and perhaps show a new generation of women that even when you have access to untold wealth and magical spells, it’s still an all-consuming labor of love. If it’s as empowering for you as it is for Feyre, and for me, then by all means, breastfeed away. Otherwise, in our modern world, we have access to something more powerful and world-changing than a gods-blessed sword: infant formula.

#4367. Apple takes powerful M6 chip to the MacBook Pro, iMac

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

6th-gen M-series silicon promises more cores, faster memory, a fatter NPU, and better connectivity, now in more form factors

If you were waiting to upgrade your Mac until you could get one with the latest CPU, the wait is over. Apple’s 6th-gen M-series processors are finally trickling down to its 14-inch MacBook Pro and iMac family early next month.

Available in either “space black” or silver, Apple’s base-model MacBook Pro is largely unchanged from last gen. This is a mid-cycle refresh and so most of the improvements are under the hood. It sports the same high-res mini-LED display, chiclet keyboard, and port selection as its predecessor.

Most of the improvements come from the silicon itself. Announced alongside a refreshed Mac mini in August, and fabbed on TSMC’s 2nm process tech, the M6 features a refreshed CPU with 12 CPU and 12 GPU cores, and is now complemented by a dual 16-core Neural Engine (what Apple calls its NPUs) which promises better performance for the slew of Apple Intelligence features now baked into macOS Golden Gate.

The M6 MacBook Pro comes standard with 16 GB of unified memory, good for 153 GB/s of bandwidth. Memory can be specced up to 24 GB or 32 GB, boosting bandwidth to 170 GB/s for those with more demanding workloads or, as is more often the case, an aversion to closing Chrome tabs.

Compared to the outgoing M5, which for the moment is still available in the MacBook Air, Apple says the M6 delivers significant performance gains thanks to the addition of two more cores. In Xcode, Apple boasts up to 30 percent higher performance versus a similarly equipped M5 MacBook Pro.

The GPU enjoys similar gains in Blender ray tracing, local AI inference, and image processing, delivering 1.3x faster prompt processing and 1.3x faster rendering, and 1.2x faster performance in the Affinity creative suite.

Despite higher performance, Apple boasts the same 24-hour battery life as the outgoing M5 model. Pricing also remains unchanged. A 16 GB model with 1 TB of solid state storage will set you back $1,999.

One area of improvement not related to performance is connectivity. The M6 finally brings Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6 connectivity to the 14-inch MacBook Pro.

The majority of these improvements also come to Apple’s most colorful of all-in-one desktops: the iMac. Here the performance jump is even larger, owing to the fact the machine never got the M5. Apple claims 1.5x faster performance in productivity apps like Excel, 2x faster graphics performance and 5x faster in local AI.

Continuing the trend of better connectivity, along with Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6, certain iMac models also gain 2.5 Gbps Ethernet. As with the MacBook Pro, pricing remains unchanged, with iMacs starting at $1,499.

Both the M6 MacBook Pro and iMac are available for preorder starting tomorrow with availability beginning Friday, Nov. 6. ®

#4437. The next circle of security hell

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A few years back I wrote “The modern packager’s security nightmare”. Have things improved since? On the contrary, I feel like we’re slipping down a well-oiled slope with no end in sight. Vulnerabilities are reported at unprecedented rates. A lot of people are doing everything they can to patch them up, which doesn’t necessarily mean they’re not making things even worse. Complex software involves complex trees of dependencies, and every branch on that tree needs tending to. When they stop being reliable, people further down the tree start taking more weight. And there’s a limit of how much a single person can do, so what started as an effort to resolve an issue upstream may quickly become a weakness. Is software less secure these days? It’s tempting to say yes. I’d love to blame the corporate sector for egotistically dumping low quality software on us. I’d love to blame marketing-oriented people developing stuff with the sole goal of making a profit. I’d love to blame Rust for getting people obsessed about memory safety, and ignoring the kinds of vulnerabilities that aren’t caught by the compiler. Finally, I’d really love to blame slop. But the truth is, I don’t know. That’s really beside the point.

The point is that nowadays everyone and their chatbot became security researchers. Projects are being flooded with vulnerability reports. Even though pretty much every bug bounty was discontinued because of slop, people aren’t stopping. And even if we disregard truly invalid junk reports, there’s a lot left, and you have to deal with them somehow. You have to figure out how important they are, and how to fix them (or more likely, whether the fixes submitted are truly valid and don’t introduce regressions), or if you should reject them and why.

In the end, security is tricky. We’re seeing lots of bugs like REDoS, or other kinds of “performance bugs”. Are they really vulnerabilities? Well, it depends on the attack vector. And that in turn depends on how the software is used. Some scenarios are really conceived, and feel like “shell can execute dangerous commands if you type them in”. But then, you also have really serious issues such as “timing attack can be used to guess user’s secret”. You need to know a lot to make that kind of judgment, and sometimes your judgment doesn’t really match users’ expectations.

In the end, what can project maintainers do? Even if they have the expertise and the energy to judge, if they can “know better”, there’s likely a CVE assigned already. It may have absurdly high score. All security scanners will complain unless you fix it, and users will come asking or complaining, or attacking, or forking. I mean, technology can’t handle nuances. More than once I’ve been doing unnecessary security stabilizations over vulnerabilities that didn’t affect Gentoo (for example, because we already unbundled the vulnerable dependencies), to avoid false positives from security scanners. I’ve been also doing meaningless version bumps (with no real changes or no changes that applied to Gentoo) so that our packages don’t get reported as being “outdated”. It’s a hassle and it sucks, but it saves people from worrying.

I’ve said this before and I’ll say it again: most projects aren’t really prepared to handle security issues. And it’s not just about triage. It’s about having LTS or security branches, it’s about making proper security releases with minimal changes. Unfortunately, it’s quite common for security fixes to be combined with new features, deprecations, API changes. In extreme cases, people are caught with their metaphorical pants down, and suddenly need to make a security release in the middle of a major rewrite, or along with barely tested new code. And then, people downstream discover that they need to quickly deal with major API changes to upgrade past a vulnerable version. These things keep piling up.

What happens next? The community reaches a breaking point. People can’t keep up. You discover you can’t rely on others, so you work around them. You start bundling more stuff. Soon enough your workload goes beyond your ability. Software development becomes “every chatbot for itself”. The nightmare becomes worse, and the wheel keeps turning.

Kai Cenat had barely gotten his hands on Apple's new iPhone Duo before another foldable caught his attention. The surprise? It was a Samsung.

During a livestream from Japan that accumulated millions of views, the streamer spotted a fan's folding phone and asked, "Is that the iPhone Duo?" When the owner explained that it was a Samsung, Cenat responded with a startled "What!"

"Wait, they been had this? I might have to switch to Samsung," he says in a viral clip.

For Samsung users watching, the answer to that first question was an emphatic yes. And they were more than happy to catch him up.

"Apple fans watching Kai discover Samsung features that have existed for years are about to have a rough day" one user wrote on X. "It’s crazy how ignorant normies are," another wrote, while one user said, "everyone starting to realize Apple is washed and overpriced; Samsung actually feels like an upgrade worth the value and the features alone make it worth it."

"Just imagine when he finds out they have a trifold," another user pointed out.

The snark comes with some history. Samsung unveiled its original Galaxy Fold in February 2019 and put it on sale that September, seven years before Apple’s first foldable arrived. Both follow the same basic idea — a phone with an outer screen that opens like a book to reveal a larger, tablet-like display, with room to run apps side by side. Samsung’s first Fold could already display three apps at once, while the iPhone Duo supports two side by side, with picture-in-picture video available too.

Samsung has released several generations since then, with its current lineup including the Galaxy Z Fold 8 and Z Fold 8 Ultra.

Comparing the Duo with the standard Fold 8, Mashable Tech Editor Timothy Beck Werth noted that Samsung's phone is thinner, lighter, and $100 cheaper at its starting price. Cameras were his biggest complaint about the Samsung, particularly its 10MP selfie camera. But after testing the Duo, Senior Editor Stan Schroeder found that Apple’s under-display FaceTime camera produced heavily processed, smudgy selfies, too.

Apple introducing more people to foldables could actually benefit Samsung, and Cenat's viral interaction is a prime example of how. Samsung says European sales of its Fold 8 and Fold 8 Ultra rose 45 percent compared with a year earlier, while Google search interest in Samsung foldables jumped 260 percent between Sept. 6 and 12 — the week Apple announced the Duo.

Whether Cenat actually switches remains to be seen. For now, he’s given Samsung fans something almost as satisfying as a new customer: an iPhone owner asking what he's been missing.

Azaleas and rhododendrons paint the spring garden in gorgeous shades of pink, red, orange, and other vibrant colors. But the work of protecting those future flowers starts now. Fall care before winter is essential for keeping these shrubs healthy and ready to bloom abundantly in spring.

Fall azalea care and rhododendron care are all about preparing them for winter dormancy, maintaining healthy roots, and protecting flower buds. Did you know that these shrubs form their buds in summer? Next year’s flowers are already set in buds, so now is the time to preserve and protect them.

Azaleas and rhododendrons are very similar plants with similar care needs in fall. However, keep in mind that there are deciduous and evergreen types with slightly different needs. We’ll point these out here, along with the essential tasks you should be tackling this fall for all your Rhododendron species.

Fall is not the time for heavy annual pruning of rhododendrons or azaleas. Most spring-blooming members of this plant flower on old growth. This means they set buds over the summer. Trimming too hard in the fall will prune away many of the buds that become next spring’s flowers.

The best time for heavy shaping or pruning is right after spring flowering. But this doesn’t mean you can’t or shouldn’t make any cuts in fall. Inspect your shrubs now for dead, damaged, or diseased branches. Remove these using sharp, clean bypass pruners, such as the Felco 8, available on Amazon, which can cut through woody stems up to 1 inch (2.5 cm) thick. For larger branches, switch to loppers or a pruning saw.

Fertilizing late in the season can trigger new growth that only gets damaged in winter conditions. That damage can introduce disease. It’s also a waste of energy that should be going into keeping roots healthy over winter.

Most fertilizing for azaleas and rhododendrons takes place in spring, after flowering. At that time, you can use a product formulated specifically for these shrubs or for any acid-loving shrub. Also, consider the fact that your shrubs might not need feeding at all if they are healthy or thriving.

If you suspect your plants need fertilizer, a soil test is the best way to determine their nutrient needs. The MySoil Soil Test Kit measures both soil pH and nutrient levels, helping you identify any deficiencies before deciding whether to feed your shrubs in spring.

You should keep watering your azaleas and rhododendrons right up until the ground freezes, especially if you’re having a dry autumn. Ideally, the ground will be moist before real winter weather sets in. Check the soil regularly using a moisture meter, such as the Yamron 4-in-1, to see whether water is reaching the root zone. Provide a deep soaking as needed, including one as late in the season as possible before the ground freezes.

This matters because many azaleas and rhododendrons are evergreen, making them susceptible to desiccation, or drying out, over winter. Cold, dry winds leech moisture from the leaves, but roots in frozen ground can’t take up any water. This can result in curled, brown leaves and even damaged buds. Ensuring there is moisture in the ground before winter is less important for deciduous types.

Mulch insulates the soil and roots of your flowering shrubs. It helps regulate soil temperature, retains more moisture around the roots, and prevents the root damage caused by soil heaving that occurs during cycles of freezing and thawing.

Organic mulches such as shredded pine bark or partially decomposed, shredded leaves are ideal for mulching azaleas and rhododendrons. If you don't have suitable materials to hand, Garden Pro's shredded pine bark mulch from Home Depot is a good alternative. Layer the mulch around the root zones of shrubs to a depth of 2 to 3 inches (5–8 cm). Keep it a few inches away from stems to prevent trapping moisture that can encourage rot and disease.

Moist soil is important, especially for evergreen shrubs, but too much moisture can also be damaging. Poorly drained soil can lead to root rot and fungal diseases. Eventually, these diseases can kill shrubs.

Look for signs your shrubs are getting waterlogged: standing water around plants, soggy soil long after rainfall, and wilting or yellowing foliage even when the plants have plenty of water. It’s important to try to remediate soggy conditions. You can clear off compacted mulch and other plant debris, divert runoff from other areas, or regrade the surrounding soil, for instance. If the problem isn’t easily fixed, consider relocating the plant in spring.

Evergreen azaleas and rhododendrons add beautiful color and texture to the winter garden, but they are also more vulnerable to winter damage. Winter burn happens when the leaves lose moisture faster than the roots can draw up water to replace it.

Cold and dry winter wind is a primary cause of winter burn on evergreen shrubs, so creating a windbreak can protect them from much of the damage. Set up a barrier on the side of the shrubs that get hit by winter wind, using natural burlap garden fabric, like this from Home Depot, attached to wooden stakes.

Don’t let the barrier actually touch the leaves. They need some airflow to stay healthy. Young shrubs are more likely to need this kind of winter protection. Established shrubs in more sheltered areas are probably fine without it.

Finally, it’s a good idea to look over your shrubs for any signs of problems caused by common azalea pests or diseases. Managing these issues now can prevent them from carrying over into next year’s growing season. Look for stippling or black specks on leaves, which can indicate lace bugs, a common pest of azaleas and rhododendrons. A 10x illuminated magnifying loupe, like this Carson loupe from Amazon, can help you inspect the undersides of leaves, where these tiny insects and their dark droppings are often found.

An unusual number of dying branches, persistent wilting, and many or unusual leaf spots can indicate fungal diseases and root rot. Keep in mind that some spots are normal and that leaf drop is expected in deciduous varieties.

If you do see issues or unusual damage, remove diseased plant material and dispose of it. If issues persist or you don’t know how to identify or treat a particular pest, check with your local extension office for advice.

Fall azalea and rhododendron care isn’t extensive, but it will help you protect next year’s flowers, safeguard vulnerable foliage, and keep roots healthy. Simple steps now help your shrubs enter winter in better condition and improve the chances of a healthy, colorful spring display.

From HVAC systems to roofs and paint cans, these simple maintenance tasks can prevent big-ticket repairs.

In years past, when I’ve failed to prep my home for the first frost, I’ve ended up with burst garden hoses, broken tree limbs and cars that won’t start. But sometimes, procrastinating on winterizing a home can lead to even more severe problems, including foundation damage and burst pipes.

“If you want to save money and protect your pocket book, then jump on the winterization of your home and yard,” says home improvement expert Todd Tripp. “Before the first frost is also an excellent time to put your yard and equipment to sleep, so that it will start fresh for you in the spring.”

Ahead, Tripp and other experts walk through exactly what to do before the first frost of the season.

Water that accumulates around a home during winter can cause foundation damage as it freezes and thaws. Before the first frost comes, clean leaves and other debris from the gutters and downspouts, check for leaks and extend downspouts so they carry water at least 10 feet away from the house.

Common mistake: “Don’t clean the gutters too early in the fall, before nearby trees have finished shedding their leaves,” says remodeling expert Michael DiMartino.

Many outdoor water systems fall victim to the first freeze, bursting as the water inside of them expands into ice. To prevent this:

“It’s also a good idea to put the hoses and other garden equipment in a shed, so they’re covered and out of the elements,” says Tripp.

Freezing water can split plumbing pipes. Then, once temperatures rise, the water leaking out can cause significant damage. To prevent this, use foam insulation sleeves, insulation tape or heat tape around pipes that run through unheated areas, such as garages, crawls spaces, basements, attics and exterior walls.

“Also, look for gaps around windows, doors, foundations and where pipes enter the home, and seal them properly, since cold drafts can make vulnerable plumbing even more susceptible to freezing,” says plumber Tim Fancher. As a bonus, this will also help keep mice and spiders out.

Pro-tip: know where your main water shutoff is, says Fancher. If a pipe does burst, quickly shutting off the water will help mitigate damage.

Frost can cause weak trees limbs to fall, potentially damaging your roof or car, so trim trees before the winter season begins. That will also keep dead leaves from accumulating on your roof.

While you’re at it, prune shrubbery and cut back perennial plants. “Before the frost comes is also a good time to put in your winter fertilizer,” says Tripp. “Blend on your lawn and in your flowerbeds so that it will percolate down into the soil over the winter.”

Cracks or damaged seals can let in moisture, which will cause further damage, plus invite cold drafts. Before the first frost, caulk any gaps and fix damaged weather stripping. “This will also save you a lot of money on your heating bill, plus keep moisture and mold out of your house,” says Tripp.

Also, fogging between your window panes is often a sign that the window seal has failed, and the window may not be insulating your house as well as it once did, says DiMartino.

Any damage to your roof will likely get worse once temperatures drop. Before the first frost, visually inspect your roof for missing, loose or damaged shingles and signs of water intrusion. Also, check the attic for moisture.

Pro-tip: “If you notice moisture in the attic or leaks in the home’s living spaces, it could be a sign that the roof needs to be repaired or replaced,” says DiMartino.

A freezing evening is no time to have the furnace fail. That could lead to burst pipes and even jeopardize your family’s safety. Home improvement expert Eric Goranson recommends hiring a pro to inspect your system every year, plus tune it for maximum efficiency.

While you’re at it, don’t forget to winterize your air conditioning system. “It’s not completely necessary to cover the outside condenser unit, but it’s a good practice to cover it or wrap it to protect it from frost,” says Tripp.

Safety check: Before using your furnace or fireplace in the fall, check your chimney for debris like spiderwebs and bird nests, which could cause it to not vent properly, or even catch on fire.

Cold weather can reduce your battery’s cold cranking amps, making it hard to start on the first cold morning, says Goranson. Before the first frost, test your battery, check your tires and windshield wipers and bring other neglected maintenance up to date.

Pro-tip: Run a Top Tier fuel through your vehicle at least once a month. “Those have cleaners in them that help keep your engine’s fuel system clean,” says Goranson.

Drain fuel or put fuel stabilizers in gas-powered lawnmowers, trimmers and blowers, before stowing them in the shed. “The best trick I was ever told, which has worked for over 30 years, is to drain the fuel out of your landscape equipment, then start it up and let it run until it’s completely out of gas,” says Tripp. “That gets all the fuel out of your carburetor so that it does not varnish or gum up, and it will start in the spring every time.”

For electric-powered equipment, charge the batteries to the manufacturer-recommended levels, then leave them in a temperature-controlled space.

If your garage isn’t insulated, chances are there are items in there that won’t fare well when temperatures drop. Especially check the storage instructions on paints and other liquids, plus battery-powered equipment. Also, don’t forget to check the seals on your garage door.

Pro-tip: Move shovels and ice melt to a place where they’re easy to access, leave a clear walking path through the garage and designate somewhere to put wet boots and tools, says garage expert Alan Bernau Jr. “You want to be able to use the garage in winter, not just cram everything into it,” he says.

#4023. Mental Illness During War Is Another Battle to Fight

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

By Sophie Wagner and Robert T. Muller, Ph.D.

According to the Geneva Academy, there are more than 114 ongoing armed conflicts across the globe. Many associate armed conflict with physical danger and death, but the impact on civilians’ mental health is an overlooked crisis.

This involves more than the traumas they are already facing, such as bombings and malnutrition, but also anxieties about things they might face—like family death, injury, or becoming disabled. These anxieties are especially dangerous, as they can persist across one’s lifetime.

The experience of war can result in a multitude of mental health diagnoses, the most common being post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, and depression. PTSD can develop after witnessing or experiencing a trauma related to a threat of death or injury.

Those with PTSD often relive the traumatic event through nightmares or flashbacks, constantly feel like they are in danger, and engage in destructive behavior. Although exposure to trauma does not guarantee the development of PTSD, citizens in war zones consistently develop it.

A 2025 study looked at PTSD rates among Palestinian civilians one year into the conflict with Israel. They found that of the 405 people surveyed, 85 percent met the criteria for a PTSD diagnosis. Additionally, 72.7 percent met the criteria for depression and 65 percent for severe anxiety. Similarly, a 2024 study found that 1 out of 6 civilians in Ukraine met the criteria for PTSD, and 88 percent reported poor sleep quality. The latter is attributed to frequent drone and rocket attacks that occur at night, forcing civilians to retreat into bomb shelters. PTSD and sleep disturbances are closely connected. Nightmares and PTSD symptoms can interfere with restful sleep, impacting mood stability and the ability to recover the next day.

Children experiencing armed conflict can develop mental health and developmental issues. They are more likely to develop behavioral issues such as increased anger, irritability, and difficulty maintaining social relationships. In Syria, years of ongoing conflict have had significant effects on children’s psychological well-being. Research involving 492 Syrian children found that 60 percent showed symptoms of at least one probable psychological disorder, with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) being the most common, followed by depression and anxiety.

Genocide can lead to intergenerational trauma, where the effects of trauma and ways survivors cope are passed down to future generations. This has been observed in families who survived Holodomor, the Holocaust, and in many other populations. Therefore, we must consider how future generations in places that are currently declared to be in the midst of a genocide, such as Palestine, will be affected.

Gazan doctors, in particular, struggle with mental illness as they often have to choose between mourning their own losses or caring for the wounded. Suppressing one’s personal suffering while treating others is a unique psychological burden that doctors report having.

In times of war, communities find new coping mechanisms, highlighting their resilience. Palestinians in the Gaza Strip gather over a shared meal, splitting their resources and telling stories, instilling hope. When a family loses their home, each neighbor offers whatever they can to the family in need: childcare, food, and household items.

Similarly, as schools have converted into overcrowded shelters, volunteer teachers will teach children math and writing skills. Civilians have also found strength in spiritual practices, using them to reframe their suffering as meaningful within the divine plan or as part of a divine test.

While these coping mechanisms are helpful, many do not address the long-term mental health effects that survivors experience. Currently, the focus of international aid is on food and medical care. However, it is crucial that we also address the mental health crisis faced by civilians to ensure that not only their futures are bright, but also those of their future families.

Sophie Wagner is a research student at York University.

#606. Aspen trees remember drought and strengthen their defenses a year later

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Aspen trees respond to past droughts by increasing their chemical defenses against insects, revealing a surprising form of environmental memory.

A dry summer shows up in a tree as a thin growth ring, and the next spring the tree starts fresh with new leaves.

But aspens may carry the effects of drought into the following year.

The leaves a quaking aspen grows the year after a drought carry about a tenth more of the compounds that keep insects from eating them. Those leaves never saw a dry day.

The finding comes from a three-year experiment at the University of Utah.

The study was co-led by Talia Karasov, an assistant professor in the university’s School of Biological Sciences who studies how plants and microbes interact.

Her team, including William Anderegg and graduate student Aubrey Hawks, grew 360 young aspens in a test plot and controlled how much water each block got.

The signal came from the prior year’s drought, not the current one, and researchers found no evidence of a growth penalty.

“It can basically be the difference between a plant being totally defoliated or not because the insects don’t like these phenol glycosides,” said Karasov.

In a dried leaf, she said, these compounds can make up as much as a fifth of its weight.

Quaking aspen is the only broadleaf tree that dominates forests in the mountain West, and it is in decline.

Insects, disease, a century of putting out fires and heavy grazing have let conifers take its ground.

On top of that, severe multi-year drought has been named the main driver of the die-offs foresters call sudden aspen decline.

Anderegg’s lab dug up root stock from aspen stands in five national forests in Utah and Colorado, at about 9,800 feet (3,000 meters) on south-facing slopes.

The clones grew in a garden in the Wasatch foothills east of campus. Aspens spread as clonal stands, so each genetic line could be split across different treatments.

From 2021 through 2023, some blocks got full water from June to September.

Drought blocks got half, then a quarter, enough to push the trees into moderate to severe stress. In the last two years the team pulled canopies over those blocks whenever it rained.

In late August 2022 and again in 2023, the team picked leaves and measured two families of defensive chemicals.

The first, called salicinoid phenolic glycosides, is what deters insects from eating aspen leaves. The second, condensed tannins, is better known for fending off microbes.

In 2022, trees in their first dry summer had the same level of insect-deterring compounds as watered trees.

Trees that had been dry the year before had 11% more, and the pattern held in 2023 with an 8% gap.

The tree’s own genetic line still explained more of the variation than the watering did.

Tannins went the other way. By 2023, trees in their second or third straight drought year had about a third less tannin than trees whose drought had been interrupted by a wet year.

Leaves richer in those compounds also fared better. For every extra percentage point of leaf weight given over to them, the share of the canopy showing damage fell by about 1.8%.

Plants usually pay for defense with growth, and the researchers expected a trade-off. They didn’t find one.

Trees that put more into the insect-deterring compounds grew at least as fast as the rest, though that result leaned on one unusually vigorous tree.

“Defenses against pests and pathogens likely matter a lot for aspen to be able to survive droughts and also normal conditions,” said Anderegg, a forest ecologist who co-led the work.

“And this study fills a key gap in understanding how drought and defense production change both during and after the initial droughts themselves.”

The team also sequenced the fungi living on the leaves and sorted them by whether they can act as plant pathogens.

Fungi with that capacity were more common on trees in a current drought and less common on leaves with more of the insect-deterring compounds.

The shift was uneven. A powdery mildew fungus became rarer in drought, while a wood-rotting fungus known to attack drought-weakened aspen became more common.

The authors cautioned that many of the fungi they counted as pathogen-capable can also live harmlessly on a leaf. A bigger share of them doesn’t prove the trees were sicker.

The study can’t say what carries the memory. Drought damage to fine roots could be starving the next year’s leaves, or the trees could be actively reprogramming how they build them, and the experiment didn’t test either.

Insects were seen feeding in the garden but never counted, and most of the trees belonged to one common genetic type.

Whether the extra defenses help an aspen recover or leave it worse off over the long run is also open, and Karasov said the study doesn’t settle it.

Tracking the same trees through the next drought and beyond, across more genetic lines, is what the authors wrote it would take.

The full study was published in the journal New Phytologist.
