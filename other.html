#940. 2026 Nobel Prize winner Anne Carson: Where to start reading

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The works of this year's Nobel Prize-winning poet and essayist Anne Carson can feel slightly daunting. Here are some keys to understanding her universe.

This quote is from the long, lyrical poem "The Glass Essay" published in Anne Carson's 1995 collection "Glass, Irony and God" — an entry point into the works of the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature laureate.

In the poem, the narrator explores the burden of memory and obsessive desire after a failed love affair, all while regularly reflecting on Emily Brontë — an author renowned for her intense literary depiction of destructive and passionate love in "Wuthering Heights."

The poem brings several of Carson's recurring obsessions together into one work. She has repeatedly written about desire, abandonment and jealousy, and has often used other literary figures as emotional doubles, as historical anchor points to her ideas.

In a way, "The Glass Essay" encapsulates Carson's method; the Canadian poet, essayist, translator and classicist has often intellectualized emotional themes by intertwining her personal reflections with analysis, etymology, translation, literary history and philosophical thought.

Another trademark of her work is the genre fluidity found in "The Glass Essay," which — as the title indicates — is partly a literary criticism essay, even though it is also a poem due to its form.

"Almost all her literary production has centered upon the intersection between the philologist's obsession with words and the poet's desire for new meaning," noted Anders Olsson, chair of the Nobel Committee, in his description of the Nobel laureate on October 8.

Born in the Canadian city of Toronto, Carson first developed her fascination for ancient Greek myths as a teenager. She told literary journal The Brick that she spotted a bilingual edition of "Sappho" by Willis Barnstone in the display of a bookstore of a shopping mall in Ontario, and that had led her to ask a high school Latin teacher to teach her ancient Greek.

She went on to publish her doctoral thesis in Classics, entitled "Odi et Amo Ergo Sum" (I hate and I love, therefore I am).

Carson's first book of criticism, "Eros the Bittersweet," was published in 1986. It lays the foundation of her approach, as she draws on ancient Greek writers such as Sappho and Plato to examine how desire — and the obstacles between the lover and the beloved — creates emotional tension.

The book suggests that the ache of wanting is not separate from love; it is one of the things that makes desire so powerful.

Because "Eros the Bittersweet" moves between Greek poetry, philosophy and literary criticism and personal interpretations, it is not considered a conventionally easy read. A more immediately engaging work and good starting point to discover Carson is the verse novel "Autobiography of Red," published in 1998.

"Autobiography of Red" was inspired by the ancient Greek myth of Herakles (or Hercules) and the red-winged monster Geryon, which the hero kills as his 10th task in his series of 12 monumental labors.

An otherwise minor figure from mythology, Geryon is turned into Herakles' lover in Carson's book. She reimagines the myth as a modern, queer coming-of-age story, with the monster experiencing love, jealousy, sexuality and heartbreak.

"We all feel we're monsters most of the time," explained Carson.

The work is accessible, funny and moving, and no deep knowledge of Greek mythology is required to enjoy it.

The key to reading Carson is not to expect every connection to be immediately clear. Her books reward curiosity rather than certainty, and her sprawling, non-linear web of knowledge can be compared to going down a hyperlinked rabbit hole of narrative ideas.

"Men in the Off Hours" (2000) is another intellectually playful collection of poems. Here Carson collides the modern with the ancient, with references to figures as diverse as Beethoven, Virginia Woolf, Emily Dickinson, Jean-Luc Godard, Sigmund Freud, Homer and Hokusai.

The poems bring together fragments of history, mythology and ordinary life, allowing unexpected emotional and intellectual connections to emerge.

In the spoken and sung performance piece "Norma Jeane Baker of Troy" (2019), Carson similarly brings together classical and pop culture, reimagining the story of Helen of Troy alongside the life and cultural image of Marilyn Monroe.

How do people become the victims of desires they cannot control? This is a core theme Carson explores in "The Beauty of the Husband: A Fictional Essay in 29 Tangos," published in 2001.

It features the Canadian author's wry humor in a powerful and moving way, with erotic, painful and heartbreaking scenes from a doomed marriage: a 30-year obsession with a pathological liar whose singular redeeming quality is his sheer physical radiance.

The collection of poetry, which won her the T.S. Eliot Prize, is dedicated to the English Romantic poet John Keats, challenging his famous aphorism, "Beauty is truth."

#878. Do non-German nationals commit more crimes than Germans?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

People without German passports appear more frequently in German police crime statistics than German nationals. A new study explains why.

Every year, when the Federal Criminal Police Office publishes the police crime statistics, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) — a party that is, in part, right-wing extremist — pays particularly close attention. They are looking for crimes committed by refugees, migrants and foreigners living in Germany.

But studies by media scholars show that 95% of the AfD's press releases focus on non-German suspects, while crimes involving German suspects are almost completely ignored.

However, the common narrative of the AfD — that non-Germans are inherently much more violent — is simply not true.

In a new research paper, Susann Prätor, a sociologist, criminologist and professor at the Lower Saxony Police Academy, has taken a very close look at crime data in Germany, together with her colleagues from the University of Zurich and the Lower Saxony Criminological Research Institute.

She looked not only at officially reported crime — that is, all offenses that appeared in police crime statistics — but also at unreported crime: These are all offenses that are not reported or detected, and therefore do not appear in any official statistics but are identified through representative surveys.

"According to police crime statistics, people who do not have German citizenship commit crimes more frequently than German citizens. However, studies into unreported cases show that non-German nationals are twice as likely to be reported. Differences between the two groups are also evident in crimes that go unreported, but they are significantly less pronounced," Prätor told DW.

Crimes committed by foreign nationals are therefore twice as likely to appear in police crime statistics as crimes committed by Germans.

These statistics record all reported cases and do not specify whether the allegations prove to be true.

When the statistics are adjusted for the bias in the reporting of crimes, foreigners are no longer 2.6 times more likely to commit crimes than Germans, but only 1.7 times more likely. This figure is also consistent with the results of studies on unreported juvenile violent crime. According to these studies, foreign youths are about 1.6 times as likely to commit violent crimes as German youths.

Other factors affecting crime include age and gender. Young men make up a larger proportion of the foreign-born population, and young men are the group most prone to criminal behavior across all societies.

According to Prätor, "categories such as being young, male, poorly educated and having a history of violence are factors that increase the likelihood of violent behavior. A certain concept of masculinity also plays a role, wherever that may come from. But we do not see culture as a factor, contrary to the prejudice that masculinity and violence play a greater role in other cultures."

Prätor points to a survey on attitudes toward violence. According to the survey, 5% of German youths view violence positively. Among those without a German passport, the figure is 10%.

The criminologist has identified a crucial factor: "Family upbringing plays an important role in shaping attitudes toward violence, because these attitudes are formed in the home. If I experience violence in my own home, I will have a different relationship to violence than if I hadn't experienced it," said Prätor.

Germany is, however, on the right track, says Prätor, even if it doesn't always feel that way. "Fifty or sixty years ago, parental violence was much more common in child-rearing than it is today. Acts of violence committed by young people are also far less frequent than they were in the 1990s or the early 2000s."

Prätor emphasizes that it is important to bring objectivity to the debate. A good illustration of this is a look at police records of serious violent crimes — such as aggravated assault, manslaughter, or murder. "Of every 100 German citizens, 0.2% commit violent crimes; among non-German nationals, the figure is 0.6%."

However, Prätor points out that "more than 99% of the people living here in Germany — whether of German or non-German origin — the overwhelming majority in this country do not commit any serious violent crimes at all."

Prätor also trains prospective police officers. She wants to send the message to policymakers that they should place greater emphasis on the positive aspects of migration. She argues that the debate on immigration is far too negative, largely due to an overly narrow focus on police crime statistics. The vast majority of non-Germans integrate exceptionally well, she says.

She wants the government to invest more money in prevention.

"By starting as early as possible with children and adolescents: strengthening social skills as early as preschool, building on that in school and responding when incidents of violence do occur. It's also important to invest in infrastructure and recreational activities in certain regions. After all, a key factor in youth violence — regardless of background — is that young people lack a meaningful daily structure," she said.

#563. Trump, Putin and Xi at one table, without Ukraine. What Moscow wants from the proposal and why Beijing might agree.

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Moscow is pushing for a summit with Donald Trump and Xi Jinping to discuss ending the war in Ukraine, without Kyiv or the EU. What does Russia hope to gain, and why might China be willing to take part?

The administration of US President Donald Trump has stepped up diplomatic efforts this autumn to revive stalled negotiations aimed at ending Russia's war in Ukraine.

Washington has increased pressure on Kyiv and its European partners while seeking to draw Moscow back to the negotiating table through a series of concessions, including a deal involving Russian diesel exports to the US and the temporary easing of some sanctions.

Yet the Kremlin appears in no hurry to resume meaningful peace talks. Moscow is ready to hold negotiations with Ukraine at a technical level next month, the Kremlin said after the meeting between US special envoy Steve Witkoff and Russian President Vladimir Putin in St. Petersburg on October 21.

Putin agreed to resume the peace talks mediated by Washington after November 10, his aide Yury Ushakov said after the meeting. He added that the next round of negotiations could take place in Abu Dhabi and would focus on "building the security system for Russia and Ukraine after the end of the war."

According to his aide, Putin also shared some thoughts with Witkoff on the possible trilateral summit involving US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping. Ushakov pointed out that Beijing is already preparing the meeting.

Moscow proposed to hold a three-way meeting on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit, scheduled for November 18-19 in Shenzhen, China, The Wall Street Journal reported earlier this week. According to WSJ sources, Putin hopes to reach an agreement with Trump and Xi on ending the war in Ukraine on terms favorable to Russia.

Moscow's proposal for the three-way summit involving Russia, the United States and China — but excluding Ukraine and the European Union — offers a glimpse of the negotiating format the Kremlin would prefer. Russia insists that Ukraine must be prepared to make territorial concessions before negotiations can move forward. Putin has also repeatedly stressed that he is willing to negotiate only if Russian interests are taken into account.

According to the WSJ, Putin views the proposed gathering in Shenzhen as a potential new Yalta Conference, referring to the 1945 meeting of the leaders of the United States, Great Britain and the Soviet Union to discuss the defeat of Nazi Germany and the postwar order in Europe.

The newspaper reported that Chinese officials had indicated through diplomatic channels that Beijing would not object to the three-way format if Trump agreed to participate. Trump, whose relations with Kyiv have deteriorated significantly in recent weeks, has also expressed openness to such talks.

Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy pointed out during his visit in Oslo on October 20 that talks concerning the future of his country will be acceptable only if Ukraine gets a seat at the negotiating table.

Temur Umarov, a researcher at the Carnegie Russia Eurasia Center in Berlin who specializes in China-Russia relations, is not surprised by the Kremlin's proposal. He told DW that Moscow wants to feel respected by Washington and treated as a major international power.

The previous US administration was unwilling to discuss questions affecting Ukraine's sovereignty with Russia without Kyiv's participation. Now, Umarov said, the Kremlin believes such negotiations may have become possible.

Moscow apparently hopes that any agreement reached at the proposed meeting would prompt the White House to pressure Ukraine and the EU into accepting its terms. But what role would China actually play?

Umarov believes Beijing would participate largely as a symbolic partner, rather than as an active mediator responsible for the outcome. "Beijing has no intention of taking responsibility for Ukraine's future," he said.

Simply attending the summit would not impose any additional obligations on China. "It is hardly surprising that the Kremlin insists on this format," Umarov explained. "Russia wants to demonstrate that it is on an equal footing not only with the United States, but also with China. Yet, objectively speaking, Moscow is not the senior partner in that relationship."

Umarov believes China has little direct interest in such negotiations. Even if the leaders issued a joint statement, Beijing would seek language that was as vague and noncommittal as possible, he said. That would allow China to present almost any subsequent development in the war as consistent with the understanding reached at the summit. For Umarov, such flexibility would be essential to Beijing's participation.

However, Yurii Poita, a senior associate researcher at the Mercator Institute for China Studies (MERICS), offers a different assessment. He believes China and Russia have overlapping strategic interests, particularly in their efforts to promote an alternative security architecture across Eurasia.

Both countries, he argues, would benefit from a reduction in Western military presence in Europe and Asia and from a weakening of NATO's influence. "If Russia and China manage to convince the United States to reduce its military presence in Europe and military assistance to Ukraine, Russia would gain additional advantages on the battlefield," Poita told DW.

He added that such a development would further undermine the EU's confidence in the United States as a NATO ally. Poita believes Moscow and Beijing would seek precisely these outcomes in any negotiations with Washington.

Michael Kimmage, director of the Kennan Institute in the United States, also doubts that Beijing is preparing to take a more active diplomatic role in ending the war.

Speaking to DW, Kimmage said China may see the conflict as destabilizing and would prefer to maintain trade relations with Ukraine and other countries in the region. But he believes another priority outweighs those concerns. "More important to China is the political survival of Vladimir Putin," he said.

According to Kimmage, Russia provides China with several geopolitical advantages, including low-cost energy and a foreign policy partner that rarely stands in Beijing's way.

He believes China will continue indirectly supporting Russia's military efforts to prevent a Russian defeat. An outright Russian victory may not necessarily be Beijing's objective, he said, but ensuring Putin does not lose is crucial. For that reason, Kimmage does not expect China to pressure the Russian president into ending the war if Putin is unwilling to do so.

Neither Ukraine nor the European Union would be part of the proposed talks in China.

Kateryna Krasina, a junior researcher at the Kyiv-based New Geopolitics Network, believes their exclusion could reveal how Beijing actually views their international influence.

In her assessment, China's approach suggests that it does not regard Ukraine as an independent player in the negotiations and considers Europe relatively weak.

Kimmage, however, argues that excluding Europe from the summit would not necessarily diminish its influence over the course of the negotiations.

He acknowledged that European governments may have little power to prevent a meeting between Trump, Putin and Xi. But he pointed to European efforts surrounding the 2025 Alaska summit between Trump and Putin, which he believes helped prevent outcomes that would have been unfavorable to Europe.

Kimmage said Europe's influence has grown through financial assistance to Ukraine and extensive defense industry cooperation, particularly involving Germany and other European countries.

He argued that European support has played an important role in Ukraine's ability to resist Russian advances. "And that support equals leverage," Kimmage said.

He believes Russia, China and the United States may all be interested in portraying Europe as a secondary power, but that such a portrayal would overlook Europe's actual influence.

"The optics and the reality are not quite the same," he said, drawing a distinction between the appearance of great-power diplomacy resembling the Yalta Conference and Europe's role in supporting Ukraine.

He believes that any diplomatic agreement between the US, Russia and China would face the reality on the ground.

"China understands that whatever the outcome of negotiations with the United States and Russia, the reality on the ground will be very different and will depend above all on Ukraine," he said.

"Whether the Kremlin likes it or not, Ukraine has agency of its own. Neither the United States nor Western countries can fully determine what happens on the battlefield through diplomatic pressure alone."

#504. Are the French police the most violent in the EU?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

As protests continue in France, the French police are under scrutiny after new allegations of police brutality. The force has a bigger arsenal than most in Europe.

Videos of alleged police violence are going viral on French social media as a wave of protests grips France. One video shows the police beating a man to the floor then dragging him away unconscious. Another shows an officer grabbing a young man from behind, seemingly unprovoked, and dragging him by the neck. Another shows a young woman being hauled off with a twisted arm, screaming in pain. These videos could not be independently verified.

The French police have drawn renewed criticism from rights groups, not only because of their methods but also because of their use of stun grenades and rubber bullets. France has one of the most heavily armed police forces in Europe. During the student protests of the past weeks, several teenagers sustained life-changing injuries as a result: One had a hand blown off, one was blinded in one eye, another had his jaw broken.

More than half a million people took to the streets in France on Saturday, most of whom protested peacefully, criticizing public sector cuts, the cost-of-living crisis and supporting students' demands for better conditions in classrooms.

Rioters took advantage, burning buildings, looting stores and lobbing projectiles at the police, creating a difficult situation for the officers and other emergency services.

The protests over the weekend followed a series of protests by high-school students, who barricaded school buildings to denounce a shortage of teachers and demand repairs to crumbling infrastructure.

More protests will be happening this weekend, with the French government saying it will even deploy military forces.

The French police have a bigger arsenal than most European countries. For instance, officers use the so-called LBD-40, a type of gun used to fire rubber bullets.

Many countries, like the UK and Germany, mostly do not allow the use of rubber bullets in protests.  In some European countries, like Spain, they are sometimes used but cause fewer injuries than in France.

"It is not a French specificity. But there are guidelines. There must be a certain distance," Anastassia Tsoukala, an associate professor of criminology at the University of Paris-Sud, told DW. "Even if you try to respect the guidelines, people may move and then all of a sudden they may lose one eye. So it's extremely dangerous."

Police in France routinely use tear gas, as do police in many other countries in Europe. In warfare, its use is actually forbidden under agreements like the Chemical Weapons Convention.

What makes the French police stand out from other forces is its use of stun grenades.

"These grenades contain a high level of explosive substances. So from this point of view, France is unique," said Tsoukala.

Minister of the Interior Laurent Nunez insists that weapons used by the police are "non-lethal." However, some of the equipment used by the force is classified by France's own internal security code as "war material."

The police's use of these weapons has received renewed criticism after it resulted in injuries during the protests. The teenager who lost use of an eye is suspected of being hit at close range with a rubber bullet from an LBD-40.

"When we look at crowd control, it is clear that if we measure police brutality by the number of deaths amongst demonstrators or members of the crowd, and if we also measure it by the number of cases of maiming, the French police are the most brutal in the whole of the European Union," says Sebastian Roche, a researcher at France's National Centre for Scientific Research who specializes in comparing police forces across Europe.

He points to the political context to understand the French police's equipment and how it is used today.

"The political authorities are free to decide on the police's equipment," the police expert says. "When LBDs were introduced into the [French] police force, they were intended for units like anti-organized crime units. This is the same weapon that is today used for crowd control. It gives an idea of how the way in which politicians in France think we can or should deal with political protest has evolved."

Another difference between the French police and forces elsewhere is the methods. German police, for example, are supposed to try to avoid escalation.

Roche compares how protestors were moved in Lützerath, Germany, in 2023 to the more aggressive methods in Sainte-Soline, France, in 2023. Both places saw environmental protests.

"In Lützerath, dogs were used, there were some injuries but far fewer seriously injured people on both sides. In Sainte-Soline, the military police was sent in and used LBDs and grenades intensively. There were serious injuries amongst both police officers and demonstrators. Two protestors were left in a coma."

According to international standards set by the United Nations, use of force by officers of the law "should be exceptional," in the case of "prevention of crime" or "lawful arrest." Any use of force should follow a principle of "proportionality," which means no more than necessary to achieve its objective.

French Interior Minister Nunez has insisted in radio interviews that officers have largely respected this principle, which is also enshrined in French law, during the current protests.

However, rights group Amnesty International describes the police response to the current protests as "disproportionate" and calls upon French authorities to "act upon their human rights obligations."

The French branch of the League of Human Rights condemns the "violent repression" of the high school protest movement.

DW contacted two police unions and the French Interior Ministry for comment, but none responded before time of publication.

"It was very complicated to contain all the different groups," Interior Minister Nunez said in Marseille on Tuesday. "The police did a great job and there were a great number of crimes that were prevented thanks to their commitment."

Experts are critical of the police's actions.

"Public order is subordinated to human life and safety," Tsoukala said. "As a jurist I should say that this is a non-negotiable value."

The French police have no independent watchdog, only an internal division called the IGPN (General Inspectorate of the National Police) that can investigate when problems occur. The IGPN says it has opened at least 40 cases since the beginning of the latest protests.

Countries like the UK and Denmark investigate incidents differently, with independent watchdogs. However, Tsoukala criticizes leniency and a lack of transparency across Europe when incidents with the police occur.

"They are acquitted or they receive extremely light penalties," she said. "And I'm afraid this is the standard method to deal with police brutality cases across Europe."

#475. Embarrassing name? How we live with names we don't like

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

What happens when your name evokes laughter or ridicule? Research points to negative life outcomes, but some people learn to embrace unfortunate names.

As a child growing up in Australia, Louise Pain hated her surname.

"People used to tease me; I was called things like 'Pain in the a*s,'" she told DW. "I remember actively hating my surname when I was a child and wanting to change it."

The jokes have followed her throughout her life, even after she moved to Germany. But one thing did eventually change: Pain's feelings about her own name.

Names are not only a fundamental part of how we identify ourselves — they can, beyond teasing, also influence how other people treat us. So, what happens if we dislike our own name, or if it is perceived negatively by those around us?

"A person's name is vital to their sense of identity," psychoanalyst Mikita Brottman told DW. "When someone has an embarrassing name, that embarrassment can be incorporated into their identity."

Research on name bias has linked negatively perceived names with romantic rejection,as well as lower educational outcomes, lower self-esteem and more frequent smoking.

This suggests that names could influence our life trajectory, namely through self-esteem and opportunity.

But a person's relationship with their name is not necessarily fixed. Pain's lived experience, and that of another woman with the first name Tiny, shows how people can find ways to reclaim, and even come to value, names that once triggered ridicule and rejection.

The planned corridor disregards Greek islands such as Crete, Karpathos and Rhodes. Greece's government insists that it possesses a comprehensive exclusive economic zone, or EEZ, around these islands and has already announced its opposition to the accord.

Whose claims are legitimate in the eastern Mediterranean is the central point of contention. Greece primarily cites the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, or UNCLOS. This, the government argues, must be given greater consideration when demarcating the boundary.

Unlike Greece, Turkey has never signed UNCLOS. Therefore, it does not agree to have the dispute heard by a UN arbitration tribunal. Moreover, even international experts on the law of the sea disagree on what a "fair division” might look like in this specific case.

When Turkey pushed for the agreement in 2019, a brutal civil war was still raging in Libya. At that time, Turkey intervened militarily in the west to support the government in Tripoli, thereby repelling the offensive by eastern Libyan General Khalifa Hifter.

The tradition began in the Zhou dynasty (1046–256 BC). On the first day of school, students wore blue gowns and brought their teachers wild vegetables they had gathered themselves.
