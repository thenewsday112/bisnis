Grace Nolan was supposed to be at the front of the women’s field at the Detroit Free Press Marathon on Sunday, October 18, returning to a race that had quickly become one of her favorites.

Sadly, she never made it to the start line, as Nolan, 25, died early Saturday following a car accident in Walled Lake, Michigan, the night before, according to the Detroit Free Press. Her boyfriend, Matt, was also in the car. Nolan’s family said in a Facebook post that he “sustained significant injuries [but] thankfully survived and is expected to recover.”

Her father, Jason Nolan, shared the news by describing just how many different parts of people’s lives his daughter occupied.

“If you knew Grace, I don’t need to tell you what an amazing human she was, truly extraordinary,” he wrote. “Daughter, sister, girlfriend, friend, mentor, scholar, athlete, comedian....those are just a few of the ways to begin describing Grace.”

The marathon community knew her especially well as an athlete on the rise. Nolan made her marathon debut in Detroit in 2025 and finished fourth, immediately giving her plenty of reason to see what she could do with another shot at the distance.

That first 26.2 came during her first semester of medical school, when Nolan later described herself as “stressed and severely sleep deprived” and admitted she didn’t really know how to train for a marathon. Apparently none of that spoiled the experience.

“I had the most fun I have ever had in a race that returning to this race wasn’t even a question!” she said in comments shared with race organizers before this year’s event.

By the time she was preparing to come back, Nolan had joined Michigan-based Gazelle Elite and was training with considerably more structure. Her parents told the Free Press that she quietly hoped to run an Olympic Trials qualifying time, even if she wasn’t exactly broadcasting that goal herself.

Their descriptions of Grace filled in the person behind all of her accomplishments.

“She was the most curious, angelic, intelligent, loyal, incredible person,” her mother, Wendy Nolan, told the Detroit Free Press, with her father saying “she painfully understated every part of her life.”

And there was plenty to understate. Not only did she earn first-team All-Horizon League honors in cross country three times and set a championship record while winning the conference 10,000-meter title in 2025, but Oakland University also selected Grace as its NCAA Woman of the Year nominee.

Running, meanwhile, was competing for space with a pretty full life off the course. Nolan was in her second year at Oakland University William Beaumont School of Medicine after already earning undergraduate and public health degrees from Oakland. She hoped to become a cardiologist, volunteered as a phlebotomist at free clinics, and worked with Girls on the Run to introduce younger girls to the sport.

“She wanted to be a doctor from a very young age, and she made it happen,” Wendy Nolan told the Free Press.

The Detroit Free Press Marathon called Nolan “a remarkable young woman” in its own tribute on Instagram and noted that her impact extended far beyond racing.

“We are honored she chose to share her running with us,” organizers wrote. “Grace, thank you for the light you brought to every start line. You will always be part of this race.”

Her family is planning a celebration at Oakland University on October 31, which Jason Nolan described in his post as a celebration of Grace’s “remarkable 25 years of life.”

#2061. Justices leave lower court order in place preventing Trump administration from implementing transgender inmate policy

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The Supreme Court on Wednesday left in place an order by a federal judge in Washington, D.C., that prohibits the Federal Bureau of Prisons from enforcing a new policy on healthcare for transgender inmates that would prevent such inmates from obtaining (among other things) hormone therapy and “social accommodations” (such as makeup, wigs, and padding). In a brief, unsigned order, the court turned down the BOP’s request to pause that decision.

Justices Samuel Alito and Clarence Thomas indicated, without any explanation, that they would have granted the Trump administration’s request.

The BOP adopted the policy in February, roughly one year after it issued two memoranda to implement an executive order signed by President Donald Trump on the first day of his second term. That order directed the attorney general to “ensure that no Federal funds are expended for any medical procedure, treatment, or drug for the purpose of conforming an inmate’s appearance to that of the opposite sex.”

The BOP emphasized that it adopted the February 2026 policy “independently of” the February 2025 executive order. In connection with the 2026 policy, the BOP also included an extensive administrative record and a 43-page memorandum in which it attributed the change from its prior policy to, among other things, “the latest scientific information.” Its earlier policies, the BOP said, had relied on recommendations by the World Professional Association for Transgender Health, but that group’s standards had been deemed “unreliable” and “unpersuasive” by prison administrators. Moreover, the BOP expressed concerns that providing access to procedures such as gender-transition surgeries could subject transgender prisoners to “attacks,” “raise fairness concerns,” and “breed resentment among other inmates.”

Three transgender inmates filed a class-action lawsuit in federal court in Washington, seeking to block the enforcement of the policy. On Aug. 26, Senior U.S. District Judge Royce Lamberth issued his most recent order prohibiting the BOP from implementing its 2026 policy; just over three weeks later, a divided panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit turned down the government’s request to freeze Lamberth’s order.

The BOP went to the Supreme Court on Sept. 28, asking the justices to pause Lamberth’s order. U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer told the Supreme Court that the district court had “substitut[ed] its own policy judgment for that of the” BOP, “rel[ied] on evidence outside the administrative record, and fault[ed] the agency for following presidential directions.” Moreover, he added, the district court “also denied prison administrators the substantial deference they are due.”

The inmates countered that the Trump administration is asking the justices “to overturn a three decades-long status quo.” They contended that the new policy fell short under the federal laws governing administrative agencies because the BOP had not provided a “reasoned explanation” for its decision. Moreover, the inmates added, they will be permanently harmed if the district court’s order is paused – one of the factors that courts consider in deciding whether to grant emergency relief. “Withdrawing care will cause significant changes to their bodies and put [them] and Class members at serious risk of worsening gender dysphoria as well as other mental health symptoms including depression, anxiety, self-harm, and suicidality,” they wrote.

In a one-sentence order on Wednesday, the court rejected the Trump administration’s request. It did not provide the reasoning behind its decision, nor did it explain why it took so long – 12 days after the government filed its reply brief – for the justices to act.

#3383. 7 Knee-Strengthening Exercises That Prevent Pesky Knee Pain

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Don’t let a twinge slow down your pedal stroke—or keep you out of the saddle.

Because cycling isn’t a high-impact sport, you’d be forgiven for thinking your knees are safe. But Tara Parsons, a USAC-certified cycling coach and Rapha Women’s Ambassador says she hears riders complaining about knee pain all the time. In fact, 23 percent of riders experience knee pain, according to a study of 116 professional cyclists—and you can bet that number is higher among amateurs.

The knee acts as a hinge between the hip and the ankle, so just think about how many times you flex and extend your knees during a ride. “If you’re missing 5 percent of your range or in a position that’s slightly less than ideal, a small issue can turn into a big problem for someone who's averaging 50,000 reps a weekend,” says Dane DeLozier, the biomechanics lab director at and co-founder of Revo Physiotherapy and Sports Performance in Boulder, CO.

“This, in combination with a lifestyle that involves sitting at desks all day, can cause the weakening of muscles of the trunk, such as the core, glutes, and hips,” adds Parsons.

From there, it’s a domino effect: “When those muscles become weak or underused, they can become inactive or underactive. And when that happens, the muscles that you use a lot on the bike, such as the quads, end up overcompensating. This overcompensation causes poor knee tracking and poor alignment throughout the pedal stroke, which can lead to knee pain,” says Parsons. And that’s not even considering the stress you’re putting on your knees if your bike isn’t fit correctly or if you’re positioning in the saddle is out of whack.

Pay attention to pain in the patellar tendon, the tendon right on the front of the knee, and the IT band, which goes from the hip down to the knee, says DeLozier—those are common areas for pain, irritation, and inflammation that can hamper knee movement. “If that irritation persists, it’s probably best to go see a PT or your physician to figure out what the root cause is,” he adds.

Whether you’re currently dealing with pain or not, it’s important to build up strength around the joint. Strength training off the bike is crucial to developing those muscles. Plus, “your weekly strengthening routine should involve not just strengthening and activation, but also foam rolling or other type of soft tissue manipulation and mobilization” to keep your muscles in peak condition, says Parsons.

Not sure where to start? These knee-strengthening exercises will help you pedal on pain-free.

How to do this knee-strengthening exercise: Perform 2 to 3 sets of each exercise two times per week. You will need a resistance band loop, a Bosu trainer, and a medium weight. An exercise mat is optional.

The exercises in this workout are demonstrated below by Noam Tamir, certified strength and conditioning specialist and founder of TS Fitness in New York City, so you can learn the proper form. Follow along in the video above with Brian Levine, USAT Level 1 coach and endurance athlete.

Place a resistance band loop around legs just above the knee. Lie on your left side with knees bent, and ankles, knees, and hips stacked. Rest your head on left hand to avoid straining your neck and rest right hand on mat in front of you. Keeping your heels together, rotate right knee up toward ceiling as much as possible. Return to starting position. Complete 15 reps, then repeat on right side.

Lie faceup on the mat with a resistance band loop around your legs just above the knees and knees bent, heels close to butt, arms at sides. Contract glutes, and press into heels to lift hips up toward ceiling so your body forms a straight line from shoulders to knees as you simultaneously press knees out to keep them in line with hips and maintain tension on the band. Pause. Lower and repeat for 10 to 15 reps.

Start standing on a Bosu trainer with hands on hips for balance. Take a big step back with left foot and lower into a lunge with right leg forming a 90-degree angle. Press through right heel to return left leg to start. Complete 10 reps, then repeat on other leg.

Place a band around ankles and stand with feet hip-width apart so band is taut. Lower into a micro squat, then step out to the left. Bring right foot in so feet are hip-width apart again; continue walking, taking 15 steps to the left before reversing to take 15 steps to the right, keeping the band taut the entire time.

Start standing and holding a kettlebell or medium weight in right hand. Shift weight onto left leg and micro bend left knee. Hinge at the hips as you lower weight to floor and extend right leg back behind you for balance. Continue lowering the weight until you are about parallel to the floor while keeping back straight. Return to the starting position. Repeat for 10 to 15 reps then switch sides.

Start on all fours with wrists under shoulders, knees under hips, toes tucked, and back flat. While keeping knee bent, lift right heel up as if to “stamp” your footprint on the ceiling. Return to starting position. Complete 15 reps then repeat on left side.

Start on all fours with wrists under shoulders, knees under hips, toes tucked, and back flat. Extend right arm and left leg straight out until they’re parallel to the floor. Maintain a flat back, level hips, and focus on pulling your belly button toward your spine. Return to all fours, then raise left arm and right leg. Continue alternating for 90 seconds.

Sailor Matt Rutherford has dreamed for several years of becoming the first person to circumnavigate the Arctic Ocean solo, nonstop and unassisted. He finally set out from western Greenland on June 25 to achieve this goal, but threatening encounters with Russia's security agency less than two months later forced him to reverse course.

Rutherford is the founder and executive director of the nonprofit environmental organization Ocean Research Project, which gathers and analyzes marine data from a sailboat. His route took him around the southern tip of Greenland, through the Denmark Strait and into the Greenland Sea. From there, he sailed through the Norwegian and Barents seas, and reached Russia's Novaya Zemlya archipelago in mid-August.

He was planning to continue to Canada and end his journey where it started, but Russian officials halted him in his tracks and ordered him to turn around.

On his way home, Rutherford told Live Science about his adventures and why he wants to complete this loop. In an interview on Sept. 23, he also revealed what he'll do differently on his next attempt to avoid another Russian interception.

Matt is the first solo sailor to complete a circumnavigation of North and South America nonstop. This achievement in 2011 inspired his new circumnavigation project in the Arctic Ocean.

Sascha Pare: Where are you currently, and what are the conditions like?

Matt Rutherford: I'm about halfway between Greenland and Newfoundland in the Labrador Sea. The conditions aren't too bad; I mean, it's close to a gale, but it's not quite a gale. [Editor's note: A gale is defined as an area of sustained surface winds of 34 to 47 knots (39 to 54 miles per hour).] It's just a little hard to do anything. I've got dishes in the sink that are all wedged in there that I really need to deal with ‪—‬ but I don't feel like it, because you're just getting tossed around. Waves are crashing on the cockpit. Normally, I do video calls under a dodger [a canvas screen on a boat or ship that provides protection from spray], and you can see the ocean in the background… But I'm going to get hit by a wave if I do that.

SP: You're sailing home to Maryland after a rather perilous expedition. How long have you been at sea for?

MR: I left in mid-May, and I've been basically underway for the most part since then. At this time, currently, I've only been at sea for a few days, because I left Greenland a few days ago. Essentially, I was attempting to do a nonstop, single-handed circumnavigation of the Arctic. That's where this all sort of came out of. I tried in 2018, but you couldn't get the Russian permits. And the Russians were the problem in the end [this year]. I got defeated by Russian bureaucracy, essentially. And there was some weird stuff going on with the ice up there this year. [Editor's note: Sections of the High Arctic were impassable due to a relatively cold summer and solid ice fields.]

Rutherford's route took him close to Russia's coast, where the FSB intercepted him.

SP: What happened with the Russians that forced you to turn back?

MR: The FSB got me. [Editor's note: The Federal Security Service (FSB) is Russia's main security agency and a successor to the Soviet Union's KGB.] They boarded me twice, actually. I've never been boarded in the ocean in my life, and I've been sailing 20 years, all over the place. I got about 1,000 miles [1,600 kilometers] into Russia, roughly. I got into the Northern Sea Route. If you just draw a line due north, I was due north of West China — it was way over there, about halfway around the world in the Arctic. I made it about halfway, because I started in Aasiaat, Greenland.

They boarded me once, and then they turned me around and boarded me again. It's a bit of a strange situation, I guess. Two days after I turned around, the ice that I was waiting to open opened, and I sent them an email — the NSRA, the Northern Sea Route Association in Moscow — and said, "Hey, it's open. Can I pretty please go?" And they just got mad at me, like, "You'll be in violation of your permit; you have to turn around."

SP: You're planning to try the circumnavigation again next year. Will you do anything differently to make sure you complete it?

MR: The boat's pretty good to go ‪—‬ which is great, because that's a huge time sink. Ideally, hopefully, if I can get north of [the Norwegian archipelago] Svalbard again like I did this year, then I'm going to stay north of [the Russian archipelago] Franz Josef Land, and I'm going to basically ride the polar ice cap all the way from Svalbard to the Northwest Passage — all the way to Alaska and beyond, essentially.

Because I've learned how the FSB operate, and I know where they're keeping their ships now. Even though I was north of Novaya Zemlya, they still were able to get me. But if I'm over there, north of Frank Josef, they don't go that far, so they won't be able to get me up there. I've figured out a couple of other things that I'm not even going to say, honestly ‪—‬ there are workarounds to get them to [leave me alone]. I'm going to go a little bit quiet.

The NSRA gives you the permit to do this, but the FSB are the people who patrol it. And the FSB don't like the NSRA. I learned that when they boarded me, because they think that they're the ones who should control the Northern Sea Route, not the NSRA. So there's [a] beef between them; they don't talk. There's a lack of communication, which benefits me. I can lean heavy onto the NSRA side, because they don't have boats; they're not going to board you. And they're the ones you have to check in with every 24 hours in Moscow; you've got to send them an email every 24 hours. So I'll do all of that stuff with those guys, but I think there's some ways I can avoid the FSB even knowing where I am, essentially, while still complying with my permit in the NSRA.

Basically, I've got to do the Northern Sea Route without getting within 12 miles [19 km] of any Russian land, period ‪—‬ even a little island, even a rock that sticks out that's 10 feet by 10 feet [3 by 3 m]. That's going to be real tricky, because there are a lot of little islands down there. But the FSB is mostly on the European side; they have almost no boats over on the east side or the middle. #3394. Stop Chain Skipping: How to Adjust Your Rear Derailleur for Smoother, Cleaner Shifts

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A step-by-step guide to cleaner shifts on mechanical and electronic rear derailleurs.

Many of us have been there—try to shift and the chain just does not want to get up into that next gear. That frustrating click click click as the chain can’t make the jump is like nails on a chalkboard.

The rear derailleur is a key piece of equipment on the bike—especially with the gaining popularity of single chainring setups. The good news is that adjusting a rear derailleur is a fairly straightforward process.

On bikes with cable-actuated derailleurs keep in mind that as you ride (especially after getting a new bike or a new rear derailleur cable installed) the rear derailleur cable will settle into the bike—you’ll hear shops call this cable stretch. Over time you’ll need to add a little cable tension to the system to make sure your shifting stays tight and crisp.

Below we’ll go through everything from how to do a quick cable tension adjustment to how to spot a bent derailleur hanger so you can keep your shifting smooth for your next ride.

Your rear derailleur moves your chain across cogs and maintains proper tension on the chain with every gear change. How? The chain runs around two jockey pulleys on the rear derailleur. When you shift to a larger chainring or cog, the shifter cable pulls the rear derailleur forward, causing the pulleys to push the chain onto the larger sprockets while keeping chain tension in check.

When you shift to a smaller cog, the shifter cable releases the rear derailleur, which swings outward, taking up chain slack and causing the pulleys to pull the chain onto a smaller sprocket. If everything is set up correctly, your rear derailleur should make one clean shift for every click of your shifter.

Before adjusting anything on the derailleur, first ensure that your rear derailleur hanger is straight. A bent hanger is often the cause of rear derailleur shifting woes.

The derailleur hanger is the part of the frame to which your rear derailleur mounts. Derailleur hangers are made to bend or break under impact and not destroy your expensive frame or drivetrain. On carbon and aluminum frames, this hanger is often replaceable. If bent inward, you can very gently align it. But your best remedy is to have your local shop use a special hanger alignment tool to fix it, or replace the hanger entirely. Universal derailleur hangers have gotten more popular, but different frames need different hangers. Pick up an extra hanger to keep at home, but make sure you have the right one for your bike. This small and relatively inexpensive part will come in handy when you need it in a pinch.

If your chain doesn’t move when you shift, or it skips cogs, your rear derailleur may be out of alignment. Hop off your bike and do this: Shift your chain into the biggest chainring and smallest cog, then loosen the cable clamp bolt (where the cable attaches to the derailleur).

Look for the high and low adjusters on the derailleur—often marked with an H and an L. Adjust the high-gear screw by turning it until the jockey pulleys line up with the smallest cog. Your chain should move smoothly around the cog and pulleys when you turn the pedals.

To check alignment with the largest cog, rotate the pedal forward and push the rear derailleur so that the chain shifts to the largest cog. If the pulleys take the chain beyond the largest cog, screw the low adjuster inward until this stops. Reattach the cable and tighten the clamp.

Fine-tune with the barrel adjuster, located where the cable housing enters the rear derailleur. For the best performance, position the guide pulley roughly 6 to 7 mm away from the cogs. If your chain skips when you shift to a lower gear, give the barrel adjuster a half-turn counterclockwise. (Tip: Think L for lower and for left turn.) A higher gear? Try a half-turn clockwise.

Sometimes the cause of a skipping-feeling of your shifting is a stiff chain link. This happens when one or more chain links doesn’t properly articulate, either from corrosion or damage to the chain. This can be mitigated by cleaning your chain and adding a few drops of lube to each of the rollers and pins. This creates a smooth and efficient chain and allows your rear derailleur to move through the cogs without hesitating. To check for a stiff link, gently backpedal the chain and pay attention to how the chain rolls off the derailleur pulleys. If any links skip, work some lube into the link and gently flex the link until the chain moves smoothly.

Also check your chain for excessive wear—an overly worn chain can also cause poor shifting. As a chain is ridden, the bushings and rollers in the chain wear out, causing slop to develop and the distance between the pin centers to increase. (This is commonly called “chain stretch,” though the metal in the chain hasn’t really stretched.) Generally, when a chain “stretches” by 0.5 percent, it is time to swap the chain.

The easiest way to test for wear is a chain checker tool. But you can also use a ruler. A fresh chain measures 12 inches from pin center to pin center. If it measures more than 12 ¹⁄₁₆ inches, change your chain.

After cleaning, add a few drops of lube to the pivot points. (there are eight) of the parallelogram linkage, cycle through a few shifts, then wipe off any excess lube. When you clean your chain, clear any muck from the inner and outer surfaces of both pulleys—it collects at the base of their teeth. Make sure to wipe away all the excess that may reside on your chain, pulleys, and pivot points.

First, remove the chain. Many chains use some sort of quick removal link to make this easy, so look for that first. If not, break your chain with a chain tool or, if your chain is joined by a connecting pin, by removing the pin (use a new pin to rejoin the chain). Loosen the cable-clamp bolt on the rear derailleur and pull the shifter cable out. Undo the hex bolt that attaches the derailleur hanger from the rear dropout.

Make sure your new derailleur is compatible with your bike’s shifters; eight- through 11-speed shifters must be matched with the same manufacturer’s equivalent derailleur. Attach the new derailleur to the hanger using its hex bolt. Reattach the cable and loop the chain through the jockey wheels before reconnecting the chain. Shift through all gear combinations to make sure everything is running smoothly. If it isn’t, turn the barrel adjuster on the derailleur in or out to fine-tune shifting.

While your electronic rear derailleur operates on the same principles as the mechanical version, the biggest difference is the electronic rear derailleur has a tiny motor doing the work of pushing and pulling rather than a cable. That motor is powered by a battery that sends and receives signals from either a wireless or wired connection to the rear derailleur.

With an electronic system, shifting is managed by firmware, so it shouldn’t go out of alignment once it’s set up—though it may be adjustable through an app or other software tool (and it may need firmware updates). If you’re having trouble with your electronic derailleur, first check to make sure the battery has enough charge. Make sure any cables are fully connected as well.

When electronic derailleurs are shifting poorly, one of the first things I check is the hardware. Bent derailleur cages, hangers, or damaged derailleurs can all cause poor shifting, so assess those parts while your firmware updates.

There are a few important steps to be aware of prior to adjusting or fixing your rear derailleur. Identify which brand of electronic drivetrain is on your bike. Each brand has a different procedure on how to troubleshoot or adjust your rear derailleur.

Once identified, confirm whether it is wired or wireless. Then, double-check that the battery or battery interface is connected or has power. The following steps are unique depending on which brand of drivetrain you have.

Step 1: Charge your battery and check the coin-cell batteries in the shift levers (as basic as this sounds, it’s often the issue when a SRAM AXS or Shimano Di2 drivetrain suddenly stops shifting).

Step 2: Download the manufacturer’s mobile app.

Step 3: Check for any derailleur or shifter firmware updates.

Step 4: Follow the manufacturer’s instructions for derailleur and shifting adjustment (often there are step-by-step instructions in the app or check the brand’s website for details).

Hornady’s 175-grain ELD-X is a staple hunting load for the 7 PRC and you should expect to see around 2,900 fps from a 24-inch barrel and close to 2,750 fps from a 20-inch tube. If we consider both those longer and shorter-barreled options, at 7,000 feet of elevation, they give us roughly 900- and 750-yard windows where our velocity remains above 2,000 fps.

The biggest complaint, from many shooters, is that most current 7mm PRC offerings don’t quite live up to their velocity potential. That’s because Reloader 26 is a key ingredient in maximizing performance, and it’s simply not widely available. Even so, the 7mm PRC is an extremely capable and accurate elk cartridge, and many find it to be the ideal balance between the large magnums and smaller options.

The 7mm Backcountry, recently introduced by Federal, is unique in that it’s the first high-pressure big-game-specific cartridge pushed out to the masses. While Sig’s .277 Fury, featuring a bi-metal case, would also be appropriate, the 7mm Backcountry was rolled out with the specific intent of driving heavy-for-caliber slugs at unmatched speeds. Specifically, the 80,000 psi cartridge sends these bullets from a 20-inch barrel at the same, or better, speeds than many 7mm PRC loads using 24-inch barrels.

The Backcountry, which roughly resembles a .280 Remington cartridge, uses a Peak steel alloy case to accomplish this. The steel case acts as a containment vessel, so that the rifle only experiences the effects of the pressures it would endure with brass cases. This results in very real gains. The Outdoor Life team fired more 7mm BC rifles and ammo than any other publication upon its introduction, and Federal delivered on its velocity promises.

In my testing, Federal’s flagship 170-grain Terminal Ascent load produced average velocities of 3,105 fps from two different 20-inch barrels and 2,955 fps from a 16-inch barrel. Shooting Editor John Snow took a nice bull elk at 540 yards, with the 175-grain Fusion Tipped ammo, which averaged 2,995 fps and 2,850 fps from 20-inch and 16-inch barrels respectively. This added velocity, compared to the 7 PRC and 7mm Rem. Mag. likely doesn’t make too much difference at closer ranges, but effectively extends the distance at which the bullet will work properly.

Recoil is comparable to that of the 7 PRC and, because cartridges are smaller in diameter, most magazines can hold four or five rounds. The downside is that the promises of reloadability have fallen a little flat. The cartridge can be reloaded, but we are finding out that it’s quite a different animal than reloading traditional brass cases. Fortunately, factory ammo is available and, in our testing, we’ve found it shoots on par with factory ammo using traditional brass casings.

The 6.5 PRC is one of the most versatile modern hunting cartridges we have. Like the 7mm PRC, it features modern case geometry, tight chamber tolerances, and is built for both accuracy and efficiency. It will fire a 140-grain bullet at about 2,950 fps from a 24-inch barrel, and roughly 2,800 fps from a 20-inch. Hunters who choose the 6.5 PRC have lots of quality bullets to choose from. Whether you prefer bonded, cup-and-core, or monolithic, many of these 6.5 bullets will buck the wind better than traditional .270, 7mm, and .30-caliber options — while producing less recoil than most of those.

A factory load like Hornady’s Precision Hunter, using a 143-grain ELD-X will stay above 2,000 fps for approximately 800 and 700 yards with 24- and 20-inch barrels respectively, at 7,000 feet of elevation. While many bullets will expand reliably below 2,000 fps, It’s a relatively safe demarcation at which you can expect consistent results. For most elk hunters, a shot that long isn’t realistic and the 6.5 PRC delivers plenty of performance at shorter distances too. In fact, that standard 143-grain Factory load from a 20-inch barrel maintains better impact velocity beyond 350 yards than many excellent factory .300 Win. Mag. loads fired from 24-inch barrels.

The 6.5 Creedmoor, which was designed for across-the-course high power competition, was just done right. Rather than a passing, overhyped fad, its rise was a slow burn, building for more than a decade to become the most prolific centerfire hunting cartridge in our nation — and across a lot of the world. There are few, except perhaps the .270 Winchester, that can rival the contentiousness it has produced too.

The Creed is a relatively mild-mannered short-action cartridge that fires the same heavy-for-caliber bullets as the 6.5 PRC. It has slightly lower powder capacity and, on average, lags behind the PRC by about 250 fps from 24-inch barrels. Most factory 140-grain offerings are around 2,700 to 2,750 fps. That gap narrows as barrels drop to 20-inches, but those standard 140-grain loads are typically in the 2,550 to 2,600 fps range. This results in a useful velocity window of around 550 to 650 yards with 20-, and 24-inch barrels respectively, at an elk-hunting elevation of 7,000 feet. Beyond that, velocities drop below 2,000 fps. Because it’s an easy and effective cartridge to shoot steel with at extended distances, hunters can make the mistake of not accounting for their impact velocity, which will produce poor results.

The Creed’s strengths are its inherent accuracy and ability to leverage efficient bullets that do well in the wind within its effective range. More importantly, it’s easy to shoot. Its low recoil, when coupled with good shooting positions and practices, can allow hunters to spot their own impacts and make quicker follow-up shots when necessary. Hunters also have the option to choose between many different brands and bullet styles in factory ammo. Magnum shooters often snub their nose at the 6.5 Creedmoor, but elk hunters who pick a good bullet and know the working limits of their system don’t seem to have a problem killing big bulls.

Something that makes the 6.5 Creed even more useful is the incorporation of the Peak steel alloy used in the 7mm Backcountry’s design. Labeled Plus Peak, this new line from Federal is high-pressure ammo that’s designed for standard 6.5 Creedmoor chambers. It’s supposed to meet or exceed the performance of the 6.5 PRC in barrels of equal length and, while we’ve found that results will vary slightly, it’s a big bump in velocity for the 6.5 Creedmoor, giving it a longer effective distance compared to standard offerings. The nice thing about this is that you can still use the plentiful brass-cased offerings for your 6.5, but have the option of using the Plus Peak ammo. The steel cases are also reloadable, but come with some caveats and quirks that we will cover after we finish our testing.

The 6mm Creedmoor is a wildcat rolled out as a collaboration between Hornady and OL shooting editor John Snow. A competition-minded cartridge like the 6.5, the 6mm Creedmoor has also been killing elk quite effectively. The 6 Creed fires .243-caliber bullets, but is designed with a 1:7.5-inch twist rate to handle heavy-for-caliber bullets in the 103- to 115-grain range, and it can work well with newer 116- to 120-grain options. It produces similar velocities as the classic .243 Win., but with more efficient bullets that maintain their downrange speeds better.

There are lots of elk hunters who will tell you that they haven’t seen much difference in the killing ability between .30-cal magnums and the 6mm using bullets like the 108-grain ELD-Match and the Sierra 116-grain Tipped MatchKing, and there are numerous hunting-specific bullets that work very well too. Proponents of the 6 Creed as an elk cartridge will point out that it produces better muzzle velocities than the 6.5 — about 2,900 fps with a 108-grain bullet from a 20-inch barrel — and maintains a longer usable distance than a similar rifle in 6.5 Creedmoor. The logic is that a 140-grain 6.5 bullet and 108-grain 6mm bullet of the same construction will produce pretty similar results at the same velocity — which lines up well with the gel testing results I have observed — so the 6 CM can effectively give the hunter a potentially longer effective distance than the 6.5 CM, with less recoil and better control.

Many elk hunters will always think of the 6 CM as a gopher cartridge, but it’s proven to be quite effective and, depending on you and your hunting circumstances, can be a pretty ideal option. I recently took my 6 Creed to Colorado and filled two list B bull tags, and my buddy killed an additional bull, using 93-grain McGuire Ballistics Copper Rose bullets leaving the muzzle of my 20-inch barrel at 3,050 fps. Shots ranged from 150 to 370 yards and killed the bulls decisively.

The general consensus is that this old, belted magnum is the best caliber for elk hunting. That is, the best of the best for terminating elk without beating up shooters. It has a near perfect balance of length, width, power, and recoil. With the right bullets, it can actually hit harder than the 375 H&H Magnum at longer ranges.

The .338 Win. Mag. arrived in 1958 as a short magnum derived from the .375 H&H belted magnum case. “Short” in the Elvis era meant standard or 30/06 length: 3.340-inches overall cartridge length, down from the 3.6-inches of the parent cartridge. This meant the .338 Win. Mag. could be chambered in the standard Model 70 rifles of the day and eventually most other standard length bolt action rifles. Think Remington M700s, Savage M110s, Ruger M77s, and even the Browning Autoloading Rifle. Any rifle strong enough to contain the 7mm Rem. Mag. or .264 Win. Mag. will handle the .338 Win. Mag. because maximum average chamber pressure is 64,000 psi. That’s 1,000 psi less than the little .22/250 Remington.

Winchester’s original loadings included a 200-grain bullet rated 3,000 fps, a 250-grain at 2,700 fps, and a heavy 300-grain at 2,450 fps from 24-inch barrels. Today most ammo makers also offer 225-grain bullets in a variety of controlled expansion configurations that retain mass well enough to penetrate better than the original 250-grain cup/core styles while delivering equal, sometimes more muzzle energy thanks to higher muzzle velocity. Most 225-grain factory loads are rated 2,750 to 2,800 fps, enough velocity to put about 3,900 ft-lb of energy into that little bullet. In comparison a 250-grain slug at 2,600 fps will churn up just 3,750 ft-lb.

With good form, a boat tail 225-grain .338 slug should rate a B.C. in the mid .4s to low .5s, good enough to provide maximum point blank range of 320 to 340 yards on a 10-inch target zone. At 300 yards that bullet should still carry nearly 2,700 ft-lb energy, which is close to what a 180-grain .308 Winchester coughs up at the muzzle. Elk don’t like any of this.

But elk stopping performance typically comes with a price: recoil. The 225-grain load in an eight-pound rifle should generate about 40.8 ft-lb of recoil energy. Heft up to a 10-pound rifle and kick will calm to just 32.7 ft-lb.

Handloaders report optimum performance with a sleek, high B.C., 225-grain bullet pushed to an impressive 2,882 fps from a 24-inch barrel. Zeroed 3.3 inches high at 100 yards, that projectile will drop just two inches at 300 yards, deflect just five inches in a 10 mph right angle wind, and cling to 2,866 ft-lb energy. You’ll appreciate that kind of performance if you can handle the recoil.

This little brother to the .338 Win. Mag. hatched five years later and gave big brother a real run for his money. Weatherby’s .300 magnum had been dazzling magnum fans since the mid 1940s, but it was a full-length magnum caliber available only in expensive custom or Weatherby Rifles. Winchester borrowed the same basic belted magnum case, blew out the walls, pushed the 25-degree shoulder forward, shortened overall length to .30/06 standard, and created a powder reservoir holding about 90 grains of water. It’s a 3.34-inch magnum cartridge that fits the same standard-length action rifles as the .338 Win. Mag. but doesn’t kick as hard. Sales bloomed immediately when it released.

In 1963, everyone knew the all-American .30/06, so they had a benchmark against which to judge this belted magnum throwing the same familiar bullets 200 fps faster. No rough-and-tough manly man in the 1960s was going to admit he couldn’t handle that. And he could get it in affordable rifles from several manufacturers.

This was and is one key to the .300 Win. Mag’s. worldwide popularity. Because Winchester didn’t insist on the caliber being proprietary, anyone could load and chamber for it. It was and still is Everyman’s (and woman’s) 30 magnum. It hits harder than the .30/06 and even the old .300 H&H Magnum without as much recoil as the .338. What’s not to like? Well, bullet weight, perhaps?

Many an elk hunter has suggested the .300 Win. Mag. cannot measure up to the .338 Win. Mag. because the latter rolls out those wide, .338-inch diameter 250-grain bullets. True. But .300 Win. Mag. bullets, weight to weight, have a higher SD for potentially better penetration. And while most heavy .300 Win. Mag. loads top out with 200-grain bullets, 210-, 220-, and 230-grain options are available. In the past, you had to handload to get these, but long-range shooters have created sufficient demand that many ammo makers now offer factory ammo featuring bullets heavier than 200-grains. Nevertheless, the 180-grain has long been the standard with elk hunters. Most factory loads from a 24-inch barrel are rated 2,960 fps. That’s good enough for 3,500 ft-lb muzzle punch. A high B.C. bullet like the Swift Scirocco will retain 2,350 ft-lb at 400 yards, 1,500 ft-lb clear out at 600 yards. If zeroed three inches high at 100 yards, that slug should drop just 1.7 inches at 300 yards and deflect just 5.7 inches in a 10 mph right angle wind.

Slide up to some of the new 190-gr. bullets with B.C. numbers hitting .590 or so, keep velocities at 3,000 fps with carefully built handloads, and 300-yard drop is only 2.3 inches, wind deflection 4.7 inches, retained energy 2,700 ft-lb. Those are better trajectory numbers than many .338 Win. Mag. loads. Add in the lighter recoil of the .300 Win. Mag., and you understand why it’s not just the most popular .300 magnum of all time, but one of the best all-round elk calibers of all time.

There are plenty of other .30-calibers for hire. Start with the .30/30 Winchester and climb until your nose bleeds. Thirty-calibers have been sending elk to the table for more than 125 years. As with any caliber, the lighter the bullet and lower the muzzle velocity, the easier the rifle is to handle and the closer you should get before taking your shot. With the right bullet and .308 hunting rifle, the ubiquitous .308 Winchester is more than adequate out to 300 yards where a high B.C. 180-gr. launched 2,600 fps will still be churning some 1,800 ft-lb energy. The right .30/06 load should add 100 fps velocity and 100 ft-lb energy.

After those stalwarts comes a blizzard of .30-calibers that claw their way up the power scale. Choosing one of them comes down to personal preference and the kinds of rifles one likes to carry. Some .30-calibers work best in lever actions, some in short actions, some in full magnum actions. Some come in ultralight rifles for easy mountain hunting, some in extra heavy platforms for stand hunting or longer range shooting. A few more calibers throwing those famous .308 bullets include the .300 Ruger Compact Magnum, .300 SAUM, .300 WSM, .30/308 Norma Mag., .300 H&H, 30 Nosler, .300 Wby. Mag., .300 RUM, and .30-378 Wby. Mag. Your taste in rifle weight, mass, and recoil will color your choices. Elk won’t care one way or the other so long as you use a suitable bullet and park it properly.

If the .338s are the best caliber on the upper end for elk, the 7mms are the best at the lower end. Certainly the .270s and 6.5mms can and do take elk annually, but one would be hard pressed to recommend them as optimum. The smaller the bullet, the higher its quality and the more precise its deposit must be. Many argue this about the .28-calibers, too, but the old 7mm Remington Magnum remains, since its inception in 1962, the most popular and effective 7mm elk caliber in the mountains. Believe it or not, with the right bullet, it can hang right with the .300 Win Mag without the recoil. For example:

Fire a Nosler 168-gr. AccuBond Long Range or a comparable bullet with its high B.C. of .616 from a 24-inch barrel 3,000 fps and a 190-gr. high B.C. slug from the .300 Win. Mag. at 3,000 fps. Zero both three inches high at 100 yards and at 400 yards they both drop 11.3 inches and deflect about 8.5 inches in that 10 mph breeze. The 190-gr. bullet will carry about 250 ft-lb more energy. By 600 yards that energy advantage will have dropped to just 180 ft-lb. Given that 1,500 ft-lb is considered “elk power,” both projectiles are golden. This takes us back to the launch. In eight-pound rifles, the 7mm Rem. Mag. will kick with 29.3 ft-lb, and the 300 will slap you with 37 ft-lb.

Lighter recoil matched to .300 Win. Mag. trajectory may explain how the 7mm Rem. Mag. buried one Winchester magnum and created another. When it first came out  in 1962, it sent the then four-year-old .264 Winchester Magnum into a tailspin. Here was the same basic belted magnum case, but the 7mm threw bullets as heavy as 175 grains. The .264 topped out at 140-grains. It worked fine for long range deer hunting, but elk hunters wanted more bullet. The immediate success of the 7mm Rem. Mag. may have inspired Winchester to bring out its marvelous .300 magnum the very next year.

There is a persistent myth flying about that the 7mm Rem Mag shoots “too fast.” Many claim its bullets don’t have time to open. Nonsense. I’ve just shown that it’s no faster than the .300 Win. Mag., and no one howls about that being too fast. Any real or perceived failure of the 7mm Rem. Mag. can be laid at the feet of the shooter or the bullet used. Bullets engineered to expand at 7mm terminal velocities are the ticket, and there are plenty of them. Hit an elk with a 175-gr. .284-inch bullet or a 180-gr. .308-inch diameter bullet carrying 1,500 ft-lb energy and I doubt they’ll notice any difference.

The 7mm family hatched around 1892 with the 7x57mm Mauser and has since grown to fifteen or more. The old 7×57 is itself a proven elk getter. After all, this was the round Bell used to collect 800 elephants and untold numbers of Cape buffalo. The right bullet in the right place. So match your 7mm elk dreams to your recoil tolerance and select among this big family. The .280 Ackley Improved is hot these days, flying about 100 fps behind the 7mm Rem. Mag. The 28 Nosler and Remington Ultra Magnum add about 250 fps to the Rem. Mag. The 7 WSM and 7 SAUM just about match it in handier short action rifles. We have an embarrassment of choices in 7mms, and elk don’t like any.

When we get down to brass tacks, the cartridge you pick to hunt elk is one of the least important considerations. Nearly any decent centerfire minted after 1900 will work, and there are older ones that work well too. The keys to finding an elk cartridge that will be best for you are to pick one that you can already shoot well — or that you’re willing to invest the time to master. If you can’t shoot worth a shit, you’re cooked any way you cut it. Pick from any number of proven hunting bullets, learn what your impact velocities will be in the conditions you will be hunting, and practice. Participating in competition like NRL Hunter can give you valuable reps in simulating the exciting situations when a shot is imminent. Learn to use the gear you’ll have with you, and practice deploying it under pressure. We all love to hash out the strengths and shortcomings of cartridges, but being able to keep your head, build a good position, and execute a good shot is what’s important. Pick a cartridge that allows you to do that, and you’ll have no complaints.

#1493. Pest Experts Reveal How to Get Rid of Stink Bugs and Prevent an Infestation

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

One of the easiest ways to deal with a bug in your house is by, well, squashing it. It’s not a pleasant experience for anyone, but it gets the job done. With a stink bug, however, doing this will only cause a bigger problem as the putrid odor it’s named for releases when it’s crushed. And if you have an infestation on your hands, you’ll need to resort to alternative methods. These insects thankfully aren’t harmful to people, pets, or your property, but they’re definitely a nuisance, especially if you’ve been seeing them around your house or yard.

To find out how to get rid of stink bugs and prevent future infestations, we reached out to Dr. Chad Gore, an entomologist and the market technical director at Terminix, for his expert insight. He tells House Beautiful that it’s sometimes difficult to stop a stink bug infestation, so it’s “important to hire a professional to help you determine your risk, identify opportunities for sealing the building and excluding stink bugs, and treating the building with insecticides at the right time of year to reduce the likelihood they'll bother you in the winter and early spring.”

Keep reading to learn more about these bugs and how to get rid of them.

There are many types of stink bugs in the United States, but the kind that usually comes inside your house is the brown marmorated stink bug. It's an invasive species that originated in East Asia and was accidentally introduced in Pennsylvania in the late 1990s, according to Penn State Extension. Since then, the shield-shaped, winged bugs have spread across the country. They can't do structural damage to your home, but they can damage your plants and potentially trigger your allergies.

Stink bugs are attracted by warmth, food, light, and moisture. Like pretty much any living organism, stink bugs are simply looking for what they need to survive. You'll likely notice stink bugs in your house when the weather starts to cool in the fall and early winter. They'll look for any opening to get in.

You may feel compelled to, but don't step on or crush stink bugs the way you might other creepy-crawlies. Remember, getting smashed is what releases their revolting stench. You can trap a stink bug in a cup (just put it on top and slide a piece of paper underneath) and release it outside, or sweep or vacuum them up. If you use a vacuum cleaner, just make sure to replace the vacuum bag and throw the used one out or empty the dust bin right away.

“One of the biggest mistakes made when dealing with stink bugs is waiting until they're already inside to try and do anything about them. By the time they're inside, it's too late,” Gore explains.

Though it’s hard to pinpoint exactly how stink bugs are entering your abode, there are some common things you can do to prevent them from coming in.

It's probably difficult to figure out where exactly the bugs are coming in, but that's exactly why you should seal off any and all potential entry points. It could be a screen window or door, a crack in the countertop, or really any hole or niche. Stink bugs can squeeze into very small spaces. According to Orkin, they most often sneak in through “cracks, crevices, gaps, and holes in foundations, window and door frames, soffits, attics, and underneath siding.”

Reinforcing these areas with clear silicone is a must. Pay extra attention to entry areas near fruit trees, gardens, or other agricultural hot spots since they tend to attract stink bugs.

Sometimes, the only way to truly prevent insects is with a chemical deterrent. Gore recommends consulting a professional to determine the most at-risk areas of your home—usually windows, exterior doors, crawl spaces, and attics—and line those spaces with an insecticide at the end of the summer to try to avoid the bugs coming inside to escape the dropping temperatures.

“Spraying insecticides might kill some as they emerge from attics, around fixtures, or from wall voids (assuming you've been lucky enough to guess accurately where they crawl out of and they walk across the material),” Gore says. If you really want to get rid of stink bugs, though, you need to treat the exterior of your home in the fall.

“The best approach is to target them in the fall on the outside of a building. This means applying residual insecticides to the exterior of a building, to the cracks and crevices they may try to enter (gaps around windows and doors, under fascia, gaps in soffit, attic vents, chimneys, around utility penetrations, etc.).”

If you've just been using chip clips, you might want to reassess and switch to airtight food storage containers. Food attracts stink bugs just like other pests. The goal is to make it harder for stink bugs to survive and thrive once they're in your home, so eliminating their access to food will make it less appealing to them. Trust us, you don't want to find a stink bug in your brown rice or tortilla chips.

Super-humid places with water buildup or poor ventilation are breeding grounds for stink bugs. Moisture attracts stink bugs, too. So dry it up, whether that means investing in a great dehumidifier or fixing a leaky faucet. You can also temporarily tape over any emergency drainage points if you think the bugs are coming in through the drain.

Like most insects, stink bugs are drawn to lights. If you keep your porch light (or any light for that matter, once they make their way inside) on after dark, they'll flock to it. The simple answer? Turn off the lights or put them on a motion-detection timer so they turn on only when someone walks by—that's more energy efficient and cost-effective anyway.

Like any creature, stink bugs can't survive the winter cold, so they search for a warmer place to wait the season out.

“The arrival of cool weather triggers them to search for someplace to spend the winter,” Gore explains. “In the spring, stink bugs that passed through the winter successfully and are able to leave their overwintering habitat will be attracted to plants of many varieties (host plants) in order to lay their eggs. This gives the newly emerged (hatched) insects a head start by starting life out on a plant they can feed from.”

Infestation might be the wrong word. Stink bugs aren't like ants or bedbugs that can cause damage; they're mostly just a nuisance.

As you know, prime lenses come in a wide range of sizes and focal lengths, and every once in a while, one comes along and seems to grab you by the scruff of your neck. One such lens is the Viltrox AF 16mm f/1.8 FE, which is an impressive and useful lens for astrophotography thanks to its wide-angle field of view and fast maximum aperture.

This lens is one of the wider prime lenses that are available and could be one of the best third-party lenses for astrophotography. With a price of just $464 (£533), this is one of the most cost-effective wide-angle primes, but rather than being a second-rate lens, its build quality and image quality are fantastic. It’s not the best, but it’s undoubtedly a lens you would enjoy.

Image quality is excellent overall, with no need for lens corrections, except for vignette removal when shooting wide open. With near-perfect sharpness and minimal distortion and aberrations straight out of the camera, perhaps the weight of the lens is a small price to pay.

The lens is made up of 15 elements in 12 groups and is built like a tank, with an all-metal body. It is pretty heavy, though, weighing in at 19.4 oz (550 g) despite its relatively compact size. Attach this lens to one of the best Sony cameras and you will have a formidable combination. The 16mm f/1.8 is also available in the Nikon Z-mount.

With a focal length of 16mm and a maximum aperture of f/1.8, this is clearly a great lens for astrophotography from a technical point of view. The light-gathering capabilities are excellent, to say the least, and during testing it was possible to shoot at a range of ISO levels with a moonless sky. Even ISO 800 was often high enough for a correct exposure.

The image quality produced by the 16mm f/1.8 is great, with excellent levels of sharpness across the frame. What’s most important here is that even corner sharpness is fantastic when wide open at f/1.8. This is the aperture setting where vignetting is at its strongest, but it reduces drastically when you stop down to f/2.8 and is completely gone by the time you stop down to f/4.

Luckily, though, there is a lens profile in Adobe Camera Raw and Adobe Lightroom that completely removes vignetting. There’s also only minimal chromatic aberration and a tiny amount of barrel distortion, which is impressive given the wide field of view produced by the lens.

As mentioned before, sharpness is great wide open and improves incrementally as you stop down up to the sweet spot of the lens, which is f/8. Sharpness drops slightly at f/11, and it’s at f/16 where diffraction begins to soften the image overall. This increases further when you stop down to f/22.

These latter apertures aren’t relevant to astrophotography; the medium ones aren’t either but the sharpness produced at f/1.8 and f/2.8 is exactly what you want.

The 16mm f/1.8 lens is something of an oddity in one respect: it’s both compact and heavy considering its size, although this comes down to its optical construction. The lens weighs 19.4 oz (550 g) with a size of just 3.35 x 4.06 in (85.2 x 103 mm). It does, however, balance well with Sony A-series cameras.

The 16mm f/1.8 is made up of 15 elements in 12 groups, including four ED glass and three aspherical lenses. The optical construction is designed to deliver sharpness, detail and high contrast with minimal distortion. It’s fair to say it achieves these. Of course, you will experience some perspective distortion when tilting the lens up to capture more of the night sky when there are ground elements in the foreground.

Unlike some wide-angle lenses, the front element isn’t bulbous, and it features an HD nano multilayer coating with a water-resistant and antifouling coating. The filter thread is also just 77mm, so you can easily attach screw-in and square filters if you use them for your astrophotography.

The minimum focusing distance of the lens is 10.6 inches (27 cm), which is great for landscape photographers but less important for astrophotographers.

Build quality is impressive thanks to an all-metal body and weather-sealing. This gives the lens a premium look, and it feels robust in the hand. There’s also a useful digital color screen that displays lens information.

When it comes to controls, there are possibly a few more on this lens than you might find on the average prime. There’s a manual focusing ring that’s wide enough for use when wearing gloves, a manual aperture ring that can be set to stepped and stepless operation using a switch on one side of the lens, two customizable Fn buttons and an AF/MF switch.

The Viltrox AF 16mm f/1.8 FE is a full-frame lens offering a generous 105.6-degree field of view, allowing you to capture more of the night sky. This lens naturally works with all Sony FE bodies, and if you shoot APS-C, it will give you an equivalent focal length of 24mm. This means if you currently own an APS-C camera but plan to upgrade to full-frame, this lens could comfortably join you on that journey.

As prime lenses go, this one has a few more bells and whistles than most, so there are a few more things to talk about than usual. Focusing is internal, so the front element doesn’t rotate or extend outwards, which is great when using filters.

For astrophotography, manual focus is the only way to go, but this lens also has autofocus for when you’re shooting other subjects. The autofocus isn't the fastest we’ve seen, taking about 0.25 seconds to lock onto subjects when the lens is wide open, or 0.5 seconds when stopped down. This is driven by an SMT stepping motor, which is quiet but audible.

One feature that you don’t see on many lenses is a small color digital screen on the top of the lens. This shows the focus distance, aperture setting and the active Fn control. Is it a gimmick? A bit. Is it useful? Yes. For astrophotography, the screen is definitely useful because it’s illuminated.

As with most Viltrox lenses, there’s also a USB-C port on the lens mount so it can be powered. This allows you to connect the lens to the Viltrox Lens app via Bluetooth to update firmware and customize the Fn buttons and the digital screen’s welcome screen. This makes firmware updates incredibly easy, which is a great feature to have.

In online reviews, users consistently highlight the lens's value and image quality. Build quality comes up time and time again, thanks to the all-metal build.

Many users report that overall, the lens offers much more than you’d expect for the price and even more than some more expensive lenses.
