#4452. Designing an AI serving harness built to last

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The engineering system surrounding an AI model increasingly determines whether its intelligence can be delivered reliably, economically, and at scale.

The next competitive advantage in enterprise AI has less to do with which model you choose than with what you build around it.

That’s an important shift. For the past several years, much of the AI conversation has centered on model capability: bigger models, better reasoning, larger context windows, higher benchmark scores. Those advances have been extraordinary. But as AI moves from experimentation to production, the question facing enterprise leaders has changed—it’s no longer, “what can the model do?”—it’s now, “Can we deliver that intelligence reliably, economically, and at scale?”

Increasingly, the answer depends on the serving harness—the engineering system surrounding the model, including the architecture, memory management, scheduling, and optimization decisions that determine how efficiently model intelligence becomes a production capability.

For technical executives, that changes where AI investment should go—and what they should demand from the architecture supporting it.

There is no universally “best” model or AI architecture. There is only the right solution for the business problem.

A customer-facing assistant optimized for fast responses has fundamentally different requirements from a document-processing pipeline that can run overnight. An internal knowledge application and a high-volume customer service platform may use the same foundation model but require very different serving architectures.

The workload defines the constraints. The constraints should determine the architecture.

That sounds simple, but it runs counter to the way many organizations have approached early AI deployments. During a proof of concept, model accuracy naturally dominates the discussion. Production introduces constraints that are much easier to ignore when only a handful of users are involved: latency, concurrency, memory pressure, GPU utilization, infrastructure cost, and unpredictable traffic.

A model that produces marginally better answers is not necessarily the better production choice if it doubles inference costs or cannot meet the application's latency requirements. Not every workload needs a Ferrari. Sometimes the smarter engineering decision is the model that delivers exactly the performance the business needs without paying for capability it doesn't.

The objective should be to deploy the right intelligence at the right cost for the workload. That makes serving architecture more than an engineering concern. It becomes an investment decision: where to spend on compute, where to optimize, and where additional model capability simply isn't worth the cost.

Once AI reaches production, seemingly isolated engineering decisions begin affecting one another. Serving architecture influences memory utilization. Memory management affects concurrency. Concurrency shapes batching and scheduling, and scheduling affects GPU utilization and latency.

Only then can techniques such as quantization, speculative decoding, or optimized attention mechanisms deliver their full value. This interdependence matters because organizations can spend considerably more on infrastructure without solving the constraint limiting performance.

GPU memory is a good example. During inference, the model's weights remain fixed, but the key-value (KV) cache grows with active requests and context length. As concurrency increases, a system can run short of usable GPU memory even when compute capacity is still available. Adding more compute without understanding that bottleneck may simply make an inefficient system more expensive.

The lesson for technology leaders is broader than memory management: Measure before you optimize. Identify the dominant constraint. Address it. Measure the result. Then determine if another bottleneck emerged. Repeat.

Don't think of optimization techniques as Pokémon cards: The goal isn't to collect them all. The goal is to apply the technique that removes the highest-impact constraint while preserving the application's business requirements.

For technology leaders, that demands a different governance question. Instead of asking which AI capabilities the organization can deploy, ask which constraints stand between a proven capability and sustainable business value.

Perhaps the most important reason to invest in the serving harness is that today's leading model will probably not be tomorrow’s. Models will improve. Hardware will evolve. New optimization techniques will emerge. The architecture surrounding them should make those changes easier to absorb, not force the enterprise to rebuild its AI platform every time technology advances.

That makes adaptability an increasingly important outcome of AI strategy.

Organizations that build serving systems around workload requirements, clear service-level objectives, and measurable production economics gain the ability to evaluate new models and infrastructure against what the business actually needs. A model change can become an evaluation and configuration decision rather than an architectural reset.

Increasingly, enterprises have access to many of the same models. Sustainable differentiation comes from how effectively an organization turns that intelligence into business outcomes.

The model provides the intelligence. The harness determines whether the enterprise can deliver outcomes reliably, economically, and at scale.

#1311. What to Know About Cotchford Farm, the English Country House Behind Winnie-the-Pooh

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The beloved children’s classic turns 100 in 2026.

This year officially marks a century since Winnie the Pooh, Piglet, Eeyore, and the rest of the beloved crew from the Hundred Acre Wood captured the public imagination. Dreamed up by English author A.A. Milne to amuse his young son Christopher Robin, the original Winnie-the-Pooh book was published in 1926 and spawned countless books, TV shows, movies, stories, and paraphernalia that continue to delight children—and adults—around the world. By some reports, more than 50 million copies of the books have been sold over the past 100 years.

Lesser known is that the fictional enchanted forest that the characters inhabit is actually based on a real-life place: Cotchford Farm, the landmark estate where Milne and his family would relax and unwind away from their London home base. The bucolic compound even had a famous (or infamous) life after the Milnes moved out. Brian Jones, founder of legendary rock band The Rolling Stones, also once called it home in the 1960s.

Read on for more details about the property—including how you can even spend the night.

Cotchford Farm is a Grade II-listed estate dating to the 17th century, per Historic England. The timber-framed residence follows an L-shaped plan and has three stories, with original Tudor beams still preserved throughout the interiors. The house is located in the southern English village of Hartfield, surrounded by Ashdown Forest in East Sussex, England. It’s a roughly 1.5-hour drive from London.

The six-bedroom home spans roughly 3,400 square feet and features many of the details from Milne’s time as owner, such as inglenook fireplaces, leaded-glass windows, and wide-plank oak flooring. It sits on roughly two acres that include a heated pool, manicured gardens, tennis court, and wildflower meadows.

Milne bought Cotchford Farm as a country home in 1925, when he was already an established poet, screenwriter, and novelist. It’s set on one of England’s most awe-inspiring landscapes, which captivated Milne’s young son, Christopher Robin. Inspired by the enchanting environment, Milne wrote the first Winnie-the-Pooh book at Cotchford Farm, plus all of the subsequent stories about the characters.

As Christopher Robin once lovingly recalled: “Cotchford was different. Cotchford was ours, and on an autumn morning we drove down to take possession. No, I have got it wrong. It was Cotchford that took possession of us.”

Among the elements still intact are Christopher Robin’s former bedroom as well as Milne’s original study, where he was known to write. Milne retired in 1952, following a stroke, and spent the remainder of his life at Cotchford Farm, where he passed away in 1956 at age 74.

After Milne’s time as owner, an American couple purchased Cotchford Farm and installed a pool. They then sold the estate to Brian Jones, one of the founders of rock ’n’ roll group The Rolling Stones. Jones drowned in the pool in the summer of 1969, when he was just 27.

In July, the historic estate hit the market as a rental property for roughly $13,470 per month, with short-term stays also permitted. It will cease those offerings and return to being a private residence in February 2027. A limited number of bookings are available on the official website.

On Oct. 22, 1992, the space shuttle Columbia launched into space with the ashes of Gene Roddenberry, the creator of "Star Trek," aboard.

During mission STS-52, NASA astronaut and shuttle commander James Wetherbee brought a small container with a portion of Roddenberry's ashes into space as part of his personal effects at the request of Roddenberry's widow, Majel Barrett. It was Roddenberry's lifelong dream to go to space, but he never got to go while he was alive.

But thanks to his wife, a little bit of Roddenberry finally made it to space almost exactly one year after his death. NASA and his family didn't tell anyone about it until almost two years later in 1994.

Wetherbee said he was "honored to be asked" by Barrett to carry Roddenberry's ashses on the spaceflight. "I'm just glad I was able to bring a little happiness to the family," he said, according to Newsweek.

The launch of Roddenberry's ashes into space on NASA's STS-52 mission aboard the space shuttle Columbia marked the world's first "space burial," but it wouldn't be the last.

About five years after Roddenberry's memorial flight, in 1997, the space memorial company Celestis began launching a series of "burial" flights by flying capsules with human cremains on rockets . In fact, more of Roddenberry's ashes flew on Celestis' "founders flight" in April 1997 aboard an air-launched Pegasus rocket that took off from the Canary Islands in Spain. The ashes of Roddenberry's wife and of Star Trek actor James Doohan have also flown in space, among others.

Celestis has gone on to launch human cremains on many suborbital and orbital flights, including some on SpaceX Falcon 9 rockets. The company even launched the ashes of the late astronomer Eugene Shoemaker to the moon in 1999 on the Luna 1 flight, with another set of cremains launched into deep space on the Enterprise mission in 2023.

A related feature of Old Order Amish communities is their devotion to a life in harmony with nature and the soil, as exemplified by the simple life of the early Christian era that continued in America during much of our early national life. Broadly speaking, the Old Order Amish religion pervades and determines the entire mode of life of its adherents. Buoyed by exceptional character work and movie-quality dialogue, “talking” winds up as one of the most important and legitimately entertaining things you’ll ever do in the games. #2471. What Happens to Representative Ilhan Omar's Congressional Seat if the United States Files Suit to Denaturalize Her?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

[This post was co-authored with Professor Seth Barrett Tillman.]

There have been many news reports suggesting that the United States Department of Justice may pursue denaturalization proceedings against Representative Ilhan Omar. These proceedings can take the form of a civil suit under 8 U.S.C. § 1451. The United States may also bring a criminal indictment under 18 U.S.C. § 1425. While the criminal statute has a ten-year statute of limitations, the civil statute does not have any statute of limitation. Moreover, the civil statute requires a lower burden of proof: "clear, convincing, and unequivocal evidence which does not leave the issue in doubt." The civil proceeding also does not afford the defendant a jury trial. USCIS and Congress.gov have published detailed analyses of how denaturalization operates.

Here, we do not opine on the merits of a potential civil or criminal denaturalization proceeding against Omar, or what sorts of procedures and defenses she would have access to. Instead, we intend to explain what would happen to her congressional seat in light of denaturalization proceedings.

We start with the relevant constitutional text. Article I, Section 5, Clause 1 provides that "Each House shall be the Judge of the Elections, Returns and Qualifications of its own Members . . . ." (emphasis added) And Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that "No Person shall be a Representative who shall not have attained to the Age of twenty five Years, and been seven Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State in which he shall be chosen."

Since the beginning of the Republic, there have been challenges as to whether a member-elect has the requisite years of citizenship. Professor Derek Muller's entry in The Heritage Guide to the Constitution (3d) on the Judge of Elections Clause explains two historically noteworthy challenges to qualifications:

In the decades after the Founding, both houses of Congress routinely judged the elections, returns, and qualifications of their members. One early and contested instance arose in 1793. The Pennsylvania legislature elected Albert Gallatin to the [United States] Senate. Gallatin, a member of the [Jeffersonian] Democratic-Republican party, was a rival of Alexander Hamilton, a prominent Federalist. Doubts arose about Gallatin's eligibility for the Senate. He was born in Switzerland and had resided in the United States for thirteen years. But it was unclear whether Gallatin had been a U.S. citizen for the requisite nine years. Two Senate committees dominated by Federalists found against Gallatin, and he was barred from the seat. . . .

A[] notorious contest during Reconstruction came from the election of Hiram Revels [in 1870], the first black Senator. Opponents first charged that his election was improper because the governor of Mississippi who signed his certificate of election was only the provisional governor and therefore not authorized to certify the legislature's selection of Revels. Challengers also claimed that Revels was not "nine years a citizen" because he was not a citizen under the Supreme Court's decision in Dred Scott until the ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment in 1868 [and so Revels was only a citizen between 1868 and 1870]. Defenders noted that Revels had previously resided in Ohio, where he had voted in that state and must have been a citizen. In the end, the Senate seated him over the objection of a few Democrats.

For the sake of argument, let us assume that the United States initiates civil denaturalization proceedings before the new Congress meets in January 2027. This procedure would certainly take some time, and it may not even reach a final judgment (up to the resolution of certiorari) during the duration of Omar's (likely) elected two-year term. Still, absent a final judgment, we sketch out several possible issues.

First, could the House of Representatives determine that Omar lacks the qualifications to be a member of Congress (i.e., a citizen for seven years) in the absence of some judicial finding by a federal district court? The answer is "yes." The House of Representatives is a coordinate branch of government that can make its own determinations of fact when seating a member. The Constitution empowers the House to determine if a member-elect is a citizen and for how long he has been a citizen. There is no need to wait for a federal court to rule on the matter. No such prior judicial proceedings occurred in the case of Albert Gallatin. That question and its answer, we think, should not be controversial.

Second, could the House of Representatives judge Omar's qualifications in the middle of her elected term? Or, can those qualifications only be judged at the time when she is seated? We think it is reasonable to suggest, based on the text, that qualifications should only be determined at the outset of the new Congress, or when a member is first admitted and allowed to take her oath, but we admit there is no definitive answer here. The alternative view is that a member must have the requisite qualifications over the entire course of her elected term, and her house always retains the ability to adjudicate those qualifications if an objection is raised by another member. In these circumstances, a member could simply raise an objection to Omar's qualifications and seek a roll call vote. If determinations about qualifications continuously require a mere majority, as they do at the start of a two-year congressional term, then a mere majority might effectively "expel" Omar. On the other hand, it might be argued that where a mid-session disqualification vote has the effect of expulsion, then a two-thirds vote is required. We are not aware of any congressional precedents that provide clear analogues or guidance on this point.

Third, what happens at noon on January 3 when the new Congress meets? At that juncture, a member could raise an objection to Omar's qualifications. And under established House precedents, Omar would be asked to "step aside" until her qualifications are settled. The potential chaos that could follow is ably described in an excellent article by Professor Brian Kalt:

Under U.S. House precedent, any member-elect can challenge the right of any other member-elect to take the oath of office at the beginning of a new term. The uncontested members-elect then swear in and decide the fate of those who were forced to stand aside. If the House is closely divided and there are disputed elections at the margins, a minority party could exploit this procedure to try to seize control of the House. This would be outrageous and damaging, even if the effort failed. Contending for ultimate control, both sides could level motions, appeals, and tit-for-tat pre-oath challenges. The proto-House would degenerate into a chaotic mass of votes, meta-votes (about who gets to vote), and meta-meta-votes before anyone has even been sworn in. Instead of the House being controlled by the party that won the most seats in the election, it might go instead to the party that is most disciplined and unified—or, failing that, to the party that is more adept at parliamentary machinations. This nightmare is not completely hypothetical; the House once witnessed a power grab much like this—and it succeeded. But even an unsuccessful attempt could worsen the national partisan divide, weaken the House's legitimacy, and threaten the House's already dangerously low levels of comity. So could an attempt by a majority to bolster its advantage by a seat or two.

If the House splits 217-to-217, and Omar would be the 218th vote giving the Democrats an actual majority, the situation could quickly reach an impasse. Indeed, there may not even be enough votes to select a Speaker until Omar's contest is settled. Likewise, in the absence of any working majority, there may not even be enough support to appoint a committee to look into the matter.

Fourth, let us consider whether Omar could be arrested or detained at the start of immigration proceedings. She could be arrested and then incarcerated if indicted under the criminal denaturalization statute. The Constitution's Privilege from Arrest Clause (Article I, Section 6, Clause 1) provides "Senators and Representatives . . .  shall in all Cases, except Treason, Felony, and Breach of the Peace, be privileged from Arrest during their Attendance at the Session of their respective Houses, and in going to and returning from the same." It is clear enough the criminal denaturalization offense is a felony. The clause would not immunize her against arrest for allegedly violating this statute.

There is a separate issue whether this clause would prohibit the government from seizing and then detaining Omar in civil immigration facilities under the civil denaturalization statute. We do not take a position whether the allegations made in this civil proceeding would amount to a "breach of the peace" under the clause. One might believe that this constitutional provision would preclude arrest of a Representative based on ongoing civil denaturalization proceedings, but because the Constitution's "breach of the peace" language lacks substantial development from the courts, we, at this juncture, take no position on this issue.

We raise two other issues that do not have settled answers. First, there is some authority for believing that the scope of the constitutional privilege against arrest extends to members-elect, and not merely just to seated members. So even if the House "sets aside" Omar during an election contest based on qualifications, she would still enjoy the same privilege against arrest that seated members enjoy. Second, there is an open question about when, during the House's two-year constitutional term, the privilege applies. The clause applies "during [a member's] Attendance at the Session of their respective Houses, and in going to and returning from the same." In light of NLRB v. Noel Canning, we have doubts this clause applies during intersession recesses longer than three days. During a period of time when the clause does not apply, neither Omar nor any other member (or member-elect) would enjoy any privilege against arrest.

Fifth, let us assume that a federal court issues a final judgment denaturalizing Omar, and all of her appeals and opportunities to seek reconsideration are exhausted. At that moment, she would no longer be a citizen of the United States. But the Constitution does not expressly require that a person presently be a citizen. The text provides:

No Person shall be a Representative who shall not have attained to the Age of twenty five Years, and been seven Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State in which he shall be chosen.

The citizenship requirement is phrased in the past tense: "been seven Years a Citizen." Once a person has held U.S. citizenship for seven years, that person will have satisfied the citizenship qualification for the rest of their life—even if, as an adult, they subsequently voluntarily, intentionally, and knowingly renounce their U.S. citizenship, and even if they do so while actually serving as a member. The text does not clearly require that a person have seven years of citizenship in the seven immediate years before he was elected. Under a literal reading of the text, a person who has held U.S. citizenship for seven years, followed by a renunciation of his citizenship while an adult (arguably) should be allowed to serve in Congress, even if he would not be able under federal law to vote for himself in any federal election. Admittedly, this literal reading of the Constitution's House Qualifications Clause is counter-intuitive. In our prior scholarly writings, we have repeatedly emphasized Dixonian strict legalism as the appropriate method of interpretation of constitutional text involving qualifications in order to maximize voter choice. That is the Democracy Canon.

Sixth, if Omar were denaturalized in 2027, one must still ask was she a citizen of the United States for seven years? Was she ever a citizen? In other words, is the effect of denaturalization to simply withhold citizenship and its benefits from that person going forward post-adjudication, or is the effect to create a legal fiction such that the person was never a citizen in the first place? Is denaturalization retroactive? By way of analogy, some have argued that Donald Trump ceased being President at some point on or after January 6, 2021, and all actions taken in the final two weeks of his administration were ultra vires. They argue that Mike Pence was, in fact, the real President! This position resembles the arguments that Hiram Revels was not a citizen before the ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment, in light of Dred Scott. We addressed this point in our article on Amendment XIV, Section 3:

Is it really that implausible to think that Section 3 could apply retroactively to a person who was already out of office? Retrospective law-making is an American tradition. Consider the case of Senator Hiram Revels, who was the first black person elected to the United States Senate. His Senate term began in 1870. At the time, did he meet the requirements of being a citizen for seven years? After all, stretching from 1857 to 1863, Dred Scott was still the law of the land, and the Fourteenth Amendment was only ratified in 1868. Some senators argued that Revels was not qualified [arguing that Revel's citizenship only existed after the Fourteenth Amendment went into effect]. But the Senate administered the oath to Revels, in effect retroactively applying Section 1 of the Fourteenth Amendment's citizenship provision. Prior to 1868, Revels might not have experienced life as an American citizen for seven years. Under Dred Scott (1857), he was not a citizen. But the Fourteenth Amendment changed all that. Section 1 apparently retroactively made Revels a citizen for the time period he had experienced life as a non-citizen. And the Senate seated Revels.

When seating Revels, Reconstruction-era Republicans argued that Revels was a citizen for nine years (as is required for senators) even if he did not enjoy or experience any of the incidents of citizenship prior to 1868. By contrast, today, Republicans may argue that Omar was never a citizen, much less a citizen for seven years, even though she had all of the incidents of citizenship prior to January 2027.

The civil statute provides that denaturalization "shall be effective as of the original date of the order and certificate." In other words, the process relates back. If this understanding of our law is correct, even though Omar clearly spent more than seven years living in the United States as a citizen with a passport and even served in Congress, she would have never actually become a citizen. She may be viewed as something akin to a "de facto officer." (We use "akin" as we do not think rank-and-file members of Congress are "officers".) It appears that the law here may be unsettled. We expect that if a final judgment were reached by the courts denaturalizing Omar, and if Omar had exhausted all opportunities for reconsideration and appeal, then the House would rely on that finding. We do not say that the House would be strictly bound to rely on that final judgment. And again, if the federal courts only so concluded after Omar was sworn in and seated as a member, it is unclear what vote—a mere majority or two-thirds—would be needed to unseat and/or expel her.

Seventh, what happens to any actions, including votes, taken by Omar if a court rules that she was not a citizen at the time she served in the House? To make the hypothetical more useful, imagine that she was the deciding vote that gave the House a majority to pass a bill. Had her vote not been counted, the House would have deadlocked 217-to-217, and the President would not have been able to sign it into law. If the vote took place under a prior Congress, such legislation would be insulated against judicial review inquiring into the qualification of members by Chief Justice Chase's decision in Griffin's Case. If the vote took place in the current Congress Congress, such legislation would be insulated against judicial review inquiring into the qualification of members by the enrolled bill rule, which establishes that the authentication of a bill by the House's presiding officer should be viewed as conclusive evidence that all constitutional procedures for bill approval were complied with. So any legislation Omar had voted on was and would remain as valid as it ever was.

Eighth, and finally, what happens if the federal district court rules that Omar is denaturalized, and in reliance on that final judgment, she is expelled from the current House or not seated in a new House, but that judicial ruling is subsequently reversed by a higher federal court, e.g., the United States Supreme Court? Here we quote from our article on Section 3 of the Fourteenth Amendment:

A prominent example may illustrate this principle: the case of Representative Victor L. Berger. In November 1918, Berger, a socialist, was elected to represent a House district for Milwaukee, Wisconsin in the 66th Congress. In February 1919, Berger was convicted in federal district court proceedings for violating the Espionage Act of 1917. When the 66th Congress met on May 19, 1919, Berger was free on bail. Faced with this conviction, the House proceeded to exclude Berger by resolution on November 10, 1919. The standard narrative is that his exclusion was based upon his antiwar agitation during World War I—in other words, his exclusion was purportedly founded upon Section 3 of the Fourteenth Amendment. However, the legal standard or rationale on which the House refused to seat Berger is far from clear.

Berger's seat was declared vacant. A new election was held, in which Berger was a candidate, and notwithstanding his re-election to the seat, the 66th House refused to seat him a second time. In November 1920, Berger ran yet again in the general election for a seat in the 67th House. He was not excluded from the ballot. Berger did not prevail in that election. In January 1921, before the 66th House expired, the Supreme Court overturned his conviction. After the Supreme Court's ruling, Berger ran, and prevailed in, three subsequent elections. He was seated in the 68th, 69th, and 70th Houses.

[Professors] Baude and Paulsen write that "curiously, after the convictions were reversed the House allowed Berger to sit in the sixty-eighth through seventieth Congresses." They do not explain why this seating was "curious." The Supreme Court's decision vacating a conviction under the Espionage Act could have no legal effect on a Section 3 disqualification. Only Congress can relieve that disability by a 2/3 vote of each house. And no such vote took place.

In short, if Omar was undenaturalized—or maybe better to say she was never denaturalized in the first place—a future Congress could seat her. Alternatively, even if a federal court denaturalizes Omar, our view is that members of Congress could independently determine that Omar was and is a citizen for purposes of the House Qualifications Clause. At that point, the tension would not be between the House and the President, but between the House and the federal judiciary.

The relationship between citizenship and qualifications is a tale as old as the Republic. Denaturalization would add a new twist to this old drama.

#2791. Are Climbers Allowed to Scale the Spires in the New Chiricahua National Park?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Massai Point in Chiricahua National Park. (Photo: R. Stewart / NPS)

All week, I’ve been studying photos of our country’s newest national park, wondering: Can you climb that?

Rock, after all, defines much of the 12,000-acre Chiricahua National Park in southeastern Arizona. The park’s nickname of “Wonderland of Rocks” says it all. And the local Apache people with roots in the area, refer to it as “The Land of Standing-Up Rocks” for good reason.

All the rock owes its existence to a massive volcanic eruption that occurred 27 million years ago. Today, hundreds of pinnacles, spires, and balanced rocks intermingle with oak and manzanita trees at around 6,000 feet above sea level.

These rocks are all rhyolite, which forms from lava as it cools, hardens in place, and erodes into the funky formations we see today. Ranger Nowell, a representative of Chiricahua National Park who declined to share his first name, described the rock to Climbing as “ancient ash.”

Yet plenty of routes and boulder problems exist on this igneous rock, from New Mexico to the Lakes District in the United Kingdom.

So can you climb it? Sadly for climbers, the answer is “no.” A statement emailed to Climbing from the National Park Service’s Intermountain Public Affairs office states: “Climbing, ascending, or otherwise traversing Chiricahua’s rock pinnacles is prohibited under the Superintendent’s Compendium, pursuant to 36 CFR § 2.1.”

Current park policies state, “Climbing, ascending, or otherwise traversing the rock pinnacles is prohibited” because climbing “causes damage to the natural and cultural resources, including wildlife habitat.”

Chiricahua Ranger Nowell cited rockfall danger and instability as another reason for the ban. “A lot of the rock is volcanic, igneous rock,” Nowell told Climbing. “It’s not sturdy at all.”

But that hasn’t stopped climbers before. Erik Murdock, the deputy director of programs, policy, and government affairs at Access Fund and a former Arizona resident, said that the rock at Chiricahua is “definitely climbable.” He shared that several climbers put up “pretty cool” ascents on some of the volcanic towers and formations in the past. “Bob Kamps did some wild ascents of bulging blobs in the ‘60s and ‘70s,” Murdock said. “The rock is as good as the stuff at Oak Flat and it would be good to gain climbing access to some of the formations that are not on the main trails.”

Ranger Nowell said that climbing ban likely dates back to when the area became protected wilderness. The government designated 9,440 acres of Chiricahua National Monument as wilderness on October 20, 1976. An addition 900 acres became wilderness through the Arizona Wilderness Bill in 1984. Currently, the wilderness designation applies to 86% of the park.

Yet climbing has been allowed in wilderness areas across the country, such as on Yosemite’s El Capitan, for many years. Earlier this year, the PARC Act formally protected climbing in wilderness, permitting the occasional placement of bolted anchors. However, allowing climbing does not necessarily mean allowing bolts. For example, Canyonlands National Park in Utah bans the addition of new bolts without a permit, but it’s still a world-class destination for tower climbing.

“The restriction is rooted in resource protection,” the NPS Public Affairs office clarified of the ban. The purpose is to protect the park’s natural resources, cultural resources, and wildlife habitat.

Ranger Nowell noted that the ban on climbing in Chiricahua is also about protecting park visitors. “Rocks are coming down pretty much daily,” Nowell explained. “We don’t advise [climbing] and it is prohibited.”

Do determined climbers have any argument for requesting that the national park service revisit the ban? While the PARC Act does protect climbing as an activity in the wilderness, it also says that individual parks can institute their own management plans, require their own permits. Basically, they can set their own rules when it comes to climbing. Climbers continue to await the release of final wilderness climbing management plans following a comment period this summer.

As for Chiricahua, the Public Affairs statement noted that while the park’s Compendium is reviewed annually, they have “no change to announce regarding the climbing restriction at this time.” The. NPS suggested climbers check out routes in the Cochise Stronghold area of the Dragoon Mountains, located about 40 minutes from the park.

While you can’t legally climb the rhyolite inside the park boundaries, there’s plenty of volcanic rock to get after nearby, and many opportunities for first ascents.

A 2014 trip report published by the American Alpine Club describes the first ascent of Chiricahua’s Cathedral Tower in the Chiricahua Mountains, outside the boundaries of what was then the national monument. Joanna Coleman, John McComb, and Don Morris climbed the tower’s east ridge in December 1961. About a decade later, Joan Bousman soloed a feature just east of Cathedral Tower and named it Parsonage Peak.

But Cathedral Tower remained unnamed until 2013, when Mark Richey and Mark Wilford climbed a new 800-foot 5.10 route up the formation. “Although the welded rhyolite tuff can be friable and loose in places, the rock on our route was quite solid and featured,” Richey wrote, “with short cracks and deep holes that would accept natural gear and was fun to climb …” He said that the area had “significant potential.”

That’s exactly how active local climber Christian Lopez describes the area. A climber of around four years based in Bisbee, Arizona, Lopez has been busy developing routes and boulder problems across the rhyolite of the Chiricahua Mountains outside the park’s boundaries.

Most of this climbing exists about 20 miles or a 90-minute drive southeast of the new national park in the Chiricahua Mountains. Lopez refers to it as the Portal Area, or the east side of the Chiricahuas. “The climbing is all really good there, but nothing has been developed,” Lopez told Climbing of the area’s potential. “There’s still so much to do here. There’s so much out there I want to put up.” His main barrier? Cash for more fixed anchors in bolts.

In the past year or so, Lopez and his friends have established two 5.10 multi-pitch trad climbs and around 10 bouldering problems ranging from V0 to V4 in the Idlewilde area. He describes the climbing as runout and “questy.”

One of his new routes, Yaqui Dance (5.10b/c R), ascends the Yaqui Dome; the name comes from the local Pascua Yaqui Tribe. The climb consists of six pitches, starting with a 5.8+ R following huecos. He says most of the belaying is off chickenheads and trees. “It has a really nice 10c pitch that’s a full crack most of the way,” Lopez says. He describes his favorite bouldering problem in the Chiricahua area as an overhanging arête.

Until Lopez can find the resources and time for more route development, these nascent routes of the Chiricahua Mountains are great options for adventure climbers. But others looking to visit the new Chiricahua National Park without the runouts and choss can look to the nearby areas of Mt. Lemmon in Tucson, or further south in Cochise Stronghold, where Lopez also develops new routes. But while you’re in the Chiricahua park boundaries, you’d better stick to hiking.

As for whether the new national park designation could alter the character of the Portal area and the Chiricahua Mountains beyond, Lopez observes: “It’s definitely getting crowded. It was really low-key before. You would rarely see anyone out there, which I personally like, but it’s good for the park.”

The "eclipse of the century" will plunge some of ancient Egypt's most spectacular sites into darkness on Aug. 2, 2027. These temples, tombs and monuments are inside the path of totality.

The total solar eclipse on Aug. 2, 2027, has an ancient feel. It will certainly be a celestial event for the ages — at 6 minutes 23 seconds, it will feature the longest totality on land of the 21st century and the longest anywhere until 2114. However, its point of greatest eclipse occurs just a few miles from Thebes, the magnificent religious and political capital of ancient Egypt's Middle Kingdom and New Kingdom.

On the east bank of the mighty Nile is Luxor, the great city of the living, with its massive temple complexes of Karnak and Luxor. On its west bank is the city of the dead, which includes the Valley of the Kings and the Temple of Hatshepsut. Modern Luxor, population 285,000, is set to become a focal point for eclipse chasers on Aug. 2, 2027, and will attract many thousands of international visitors. They'll come for the forecasted 0% chance of clouds and the era-defining duration of totality, but also to tour ancient Egypt's temples and monuments.

The chances of actually witnessing totality at any of the major temples are probably slim. Still, almost every eclipse chaser headed to Luxor will want to tour them before or after the eclipse. Visitors should be prepared for extreme August heat and check official opening and access arrangements before planning to watch the eclipse at any archaeological site. Certified eclipse glasses are essential during the partial phases; only during totality, when the sun is completely covered, is it safe to look without them.

Not all of ancient Egypt will experience totality. Egypt's top-ranking monument, the Pyramids at Giza, near Cairo, lies north of the path of totality and will get a 95% partial solar eclipse. Abu Simbel, on the shores of Lake Nasser south of Aswan, will also see a 92% partial solar eclipse. No matter, Luxor is home to some of the world's most impressive and well-preserved ancient monuments. Here are its temples, necropolises and palaces — and those nearby, also in the 160-mile (257-kilometer)-wide path of totality — that will have the moon's umbral shadow fall upon them on Aug. 2, 2027. Dedicated to the god Amun-Ra — a fusion of the hidden god of the sun Amun and the ancient sun god Ra — this vast temple complex will be the hottest ticket in town for the eclipse. One of Egypt's most impressive and well-preserved ancient monuments, it's a place designed by ancient astronomers — the winter solstice sunrise still illuminates the innermost sanctuary. Access arrangements for eclipse day have yet to be confirmed, so keep an eye on official announcements before making plans to watch totality from the complex.

This is one of Egypt's best-preserved ancient temples and a must-see during the 2027 eclipse. It's on the banks of the Nile near the city center, so it's relatively easy to visit. Dedicated to the god Amun, it is known for its beautiful pillars and intricate carvings. A 75-foot (23 meters) tall obelisk on the east side of its entrance will point at the eclipsed sun almost directly overhead on Aug. 2, 2027. Its missing right-hand obelisk is in the Place de la Concorde, Paris.

This incredible necropolis is home to the tombs of the pharaohs and is a testament to the advanced engineering and architectural skills of the ancient Egyptians. Of course, you don't want to be underground for totality, but some of the tombs are a must-see. Your tickets include entry to any three tombs of your choice from those open that day, with extra tickets available for the really impressive ones—the spectacular KV17 (Seti I), small but iconic KV62 (Tutankhamun), and KV9 (Ramesses V & VI). The latter's Corridor G has a ceiling decoration that may depict a deep partial solar eclipse from 1143 BC.

If one temple is best suited to crowds for an eclipse, it's this vast, open-air gem dedicated to the legendary queen who became pharaoh. However, with almost no shade, it could be the least practical option. Three huge terraces rise above the desert floor and into the cliffs of Deir el-Bahari to the west. It's an absolute masterpiece.

This is where we get to temples that are fairly well known, but likely won't have as much pressure on them from eclipse chasers as the big three — Karnak, Luxor and Hatshepsut. However, anyone looking for ways to get temple architecture into images of an eclipse at zenith should look no further than these two large stone statues of Pharaoh Amenhotep III. Behind them are the ruins of the largest temple in the Theban Necropolis.

Getting off the beaten track in August 2027 will be near-impossible, but here's an option — the Mortuary Temple of Ramesses III. Featuring towering stone columns, colorful reliefs (despite being 3,200 years old) and famous carvings, it's bypassed by tour buses heading straight for Hatshepsut.

Another temple that, without Karnak and Luxor nearby, would be rammed with visitors, is the Ramesseum — the mortuary temple of Ramesses II. Featuring the remains of a huge fallen statue that inspired Shelley's poem Ozymandias, it's in an area once used by the artisans who built the royal tombs, and slightly off the tourist route.

Start of totality and duration: 1:02 p.m. EEST; 5 minutes, 47 seconds

If you want to avoid the scrum of eclipse chasers in Luxor on and around Aug. 2, the Temple of Khnum could be an option. Located in Esna, about 34 miles (55 kilometers) south of Luxor, the so-called 'Rainbow Temple' (for its colorful ceilings) is dedicated to the ram-headed creator god Khnum. Dating from the Greco-Roman era, there's a vast Hypostyle Hall that sits below modern street level, which could be good for some shade.

Start of totality and duration: 1:01 p.m. EEST; 6 minutes, 15 seconds

If you want to escape Luxor but (almost) keep its long totality, head to Qena, 40 miles (65 km) north, where one of Egypt's best-preserved and most beautiful temples stands. Dendera was the cult center for Hathor, one of the most important and popular deities in ancient Egypt and known as the 'lady of the sky.' The temple's entrance has six massive columns, each decorated with Hathor's head, and its ceiling features astronomical engravings.

Start of totality and duration: 1:04 p.m. EEST; 5 minutes, 20 seconds

Another great option for those who prioritize ancient Egyptian surroundings over the longest possible totality is the Temple of Horus, one of the most striking and best-preserved temples in ancient Egypt. About 68 miles (110 km) south of Luxor, it's dedicated to the sun and sky god Horus, one of the main gods in ancient Egypt, whose right eye represents the sun and left eye the moon. The temple's stone roofs are intact, so its Hypostyle Halls, inner chambers and central sanctuary provide some shade.
