#3454. 4 Yin Yoga Poses for an Intense Stretch in Your Quads

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

If you struggle with tight quads and hip flexors, there are several intense yin yoga stretches that can bring you relief. In yin yoga, you’re not trying to use any kind of muscular effort. Instead, you’re trying to release into the shape so that the target muscles are inactive. This transfers the intensity of the stretch to the connective tissues, which require different circumstances than muscles to release. Basically, you want to find the edge of your comfort level, be still, and linger here for about three minutes.

When I say find your edge, that probably means something less intense than you think. It shouldn’t be painful. Just sensational. On a scale of one to 10 intensity-wise, if you feel nothing at one and you feel pain in 10, you want to stay at a four or a five. You want to find a stretch that feels sustainable for a few minutes. During the pose, your body might release and you might find yourself sliding a little more deeply into the pose without having to force or push your way there. Then resolve to be still.

Your breath is super important in these intense stretches. Let it be slow and easy. At the same time, try to relax anything in your body that isn’t currently being stretched.

Anyone is welcome to explore this practice, although these more intense yin yoga stretches are definitely not something you want to practice if you are new to this style of yoga. There are other poses that you can use as an introduction to yin yoga.

One of the hip flexors, found along the front of the hips, is the psoas, which is said to be the muscle of the soul. So as you practice, you might want to consider, what is it that you want to release from this space?

Remember, for each yin yoga stretch, you want to remain in it for three to five minutes. I like to keep props within reach to help support the body for these poses. Try to have a blanket and two blocks or a bolster. If you experience a lot of tightness and limited range of motion in the quads and hip flexors, you might want to have a strap or belt as well to help you find the shape.

I like to start by taking a moment to sit comfortably and get grounded. If you want to try this, let your hands rest on your knees and close your eyes. Taking some slow breaths in and out through the nose, letting go of the day you’ve had or anything else that might be on your mind and distracting you from your practice. See if you can bring your awareness to your quads, hip flexors, and low back. Notice if there’s any tension, discomfort, or tightness.

Start lying down on your belly with your feet about hip-distance apart. This is a pose where you might want or need to use a strap. Cross your left forearm on the mat in front of you and prop yourself up. Bend your right knee, catch hold of your right foot, and draw your heel toward the glutes. You want to feel a stretch along the front of your thigh but not pain.

The first thing that tends to happen is the right knee will lean toward the side. Try to keep it in line with your hip and parallel to your other leg. You want the front of your thigh facing the mat.

When you reach back for your foot, if you experience difficulty reaching it, this is when you’ll loop a strap or belt around the front of your ankle and hold the ends in your hand in Half Bow Pose.

Let your forehead rest on your forearm as you draw your foot toward you but without forcing it. Try to lengthen your tailbone toward your knees or press your pubic bone into the mat to shift more of the stretch to the quads. Stay here for at least 3 minutes.

Carefully release your right foot back to the mat, nice and slow. Then make your way to the other side, crossing your right forearm on the mat, bending into your left knee without letting it stray to the side, and reach back with your left hand. Reach your tailbone toward the back of your mat and stay here for a few minutes.

When you’re ready to come out of the pose, slide your hands underneath your chest and press yourself back to hands and knees.

Place your hands on either the mat or blocks or lower onto your forearms. For the quad stretch part of the pose, reach your right hand toward the back of the mat, bend your left knee (which is why the blanket is so useful here!), and catch hold of your left foot or wrap the strap or belt around the right foot. I like to let the neck relax and the head be heavy. Try to stay here for at least 3 minutes but see how it goes.

Very slowly release your back foot and take your time to come out of the pose. Be careful not to let your foot slingshot down to the mat. Before doing this on the other side, it might feel good to take whatever movement feels good or come into Child’s Pose just for a few breaths.

Then make your way into the same pose, but on the other side. So step your left foot forward to the outer edge of your left hand and keep everything facing forward or rotate onto the left side of your left foot and then maybe come down onto some blocks or onto the forearms, finding your quad stretch. Your left arm reaches toward the back of your mat, bend into your right knee, and catch hold of your right foot.

When you’re ready to come out of the stretch, let yourself move or rest a little here.

If it’s difficult, you can place a block or a folded blanket underneath your hips so you’re a little higher. If this feels uncomfortable for your knees, another thing that you can do is place a rolled or folded blanket directly behind the knees.

As you sit tall, scoop the tailbone under you a little before setting your hips back down, and then slowly lower yourself back to the mat, either lowering onto your forearms or all the way down onto the mat, in Reclined Hero. You can also support yourself by bringing blocks or bolsters or pillows as high as you want beneath your upper back and head.

Take as long as you need to get settled and comfortable into the pose. You simply want a stretch in the quads. There should be no pinching or pain in the knees or the low back. Feel the steady flow of your breath in and out through your nose.

In order to get out of this pose, take your time and do it slowly and mindfully. You can tuck your chin to your chest if you are reclined, and use your forearms or palms, to push into the soles of the feet. Slowly lift your way back up, and move your blocks off to the side. Make your way forward to hands and knees, and maybe stretch your legs.

Bend your left knee and bring your heel behind you. See if you can reach your right hand behind you and hold onto the top of your left foot with your hand or a strap and bring your heel toward the glutes. You will probably feel this stretch along your left quads. Release your right shoulder toward the mat but it’s okay if it doesn’t touch. Relax your chest and shoulders, try not to fidget, and simply breathe in Cat Pulling Its Tail.

When you’re ready, come out of this step by step. Release the hold of your left foot, bend your right knee, and just roll onto your back. Before doing the other side, just lie flat with the legs straight for 5 breaths. Notice and feel the difference from one side to the next.

Then do the same on the other side, keeping your right leg straight and drawing your left knee toward your chest and then crossing it over your body. Reach your left arm straight out to the side. Keep your left leg bent or straighten it. Then bend your right knee, try to catch hold of your foot behind you with your left hand while releasing your left shoulder toward the mat. I’m trying to keep my collarbones facing straight up to the sky.

Stay where you are on the mat and take any final movements you might need before releasing your arms at your sides, palms facing up, in Savasana. Notice any difference in your body, specifically your quads, hip flexors, and low back. Let your body be heavy and your breath be natural.

Start to lengthen your breath. Maybe you wiggle your fingers and toes and then stretch your arms alongside your head. Eventually, roll onto one side and make your way to a comfortable seat. Maybe you close your eyes and finish your practice with a few moments of quiet.

#1263. What Happens To Your Body When You Cut Back—But Don’t Quit—Alcohol?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

If you’ve spent any time on social media recently, your feed is probably packed with people talking up how amazing they feel without alcohol in their lives. But while a growing body of research has found that no amount of alcohol is technically good for you, it’s understandable to still want to enjoy the odd glass of wine or cocktail with friends.

There are a few studies out there on the potential health impacts of avoiding alcohol, but what happens if you just cut back? An all-or-nothing approach doesn’t work for some people, so it’s fair to wonder if you’d still be getting health perks by going this route.

We tapped two doctors who study the impact of alcohol on the body to learn more.

Meet the experts: Megan Strowger, PhD, a post-doctoral research associate at University at Buffalo; Emily A. Gordon, MD, MPH, assistant professor of medicine and program director of Addiction Medicine Fellowship at Rutgers New Jersey Medical School

You’ll still get plenty of health perks if you just have the odd drink, says Megan Strowger, PhD, a post-doctoral research associate at University at Buffalo who recently studied the impact on the body of cutting out alcohol for a month. In fact, she says cutting back can have a “profound impact” on your health, especially if you’ve been a heavy drinker in the past.

Strowger points to an older clinical trial that found people had lower blood pressure, better liver function, and reported a better quality of life within four months of cutting back on their drinking. That shows that “even a moderate reduction in consumption can lead to meaningful improvements for both the body and mind,” Strowger says.

It’s not a bad idea to leave room for a little alcohol in your life if you enjoy it and don’t struggle with alcohol use disorder, says Emily A. Gordon, MD, MPH, assistant professor of medicine and program director of Addiction Medicine Fellowship at Rutgers New Jersey Medical School. “There is a benefit to having a glass of wine to relax and enjoy time with friends, as long as it’s not chronic use,” she says. “The mental health benefits need to be weighed along with the physical.”

There is no hard and fast number, but there is some general guidance to consider. “The World Health Organization categorizes drinking into different ‘risk levels,’ and research shows that moving down just one or two of these levels can lead to significant physical health improvements and fewer alcohol-related complications a year later,” Strowger says.

Gordon points out that there are healthy drinking limits in place (no more than two drinks a day for men and one drink a day for women). “But they are probably higher than they should be,” she says. “As long as you’re moderating your intake, it’s likely healthy.”

If you really want to focus on health, Gordon suggests just trying to minimize how much you drink. “Ultimately, the healthiest approach is to keep consumption as low as possible, as there is no level of use that is considered entirely risk-free,” she says.

You may be just fine deciding you’re only going to drink here and there, but some people prefer a little more guidance. If you fall into the second camp, Gordon recommends thinking about when you tend to drink. “Is it to ease a little anxiety, socially, or out of boredom?” she says. “Then, try to think of healthier things that can be substituted.” That might mean incorporating daily stress-reducing walks into your day, taking up a new hobby, or diving into all the NA options out there to find something you enjoy that you can sip while you socialize with friends.

Tracking how often you drink (including the type and number of drinks you have) can be helpful, too, Strowger says. “This habit is associated with sustained reductions in drinking even a month later,” she says.

If you respond well to clear boundaries, Strowger recommends giving yourself a specific limit on drinks you’ll have in each sitting or per week. “However, it is important to practice self-compassion,” she says. “If you don't always hit your targets, simply carry on and try again during the next week or occasion.” It can even be helpful to practice ways to politely decline when someone offers you a drink after you’ve hit your personal limit, she says.

Ultimately, just do your best. “In general, the less you drink, the better,” Gordon says. “But we do need to recognize the importance of socializing and spending time with friends.” Basically, don’t feel bad about catching up with your crew over a glass of wine. It’s unlikely to hurt your health—and could even give your mental health a boost.

#1230. Will There Be a <i><em>Nobody Wants This</em></i> Season 4? Here’s What We Know

Creator Erin Foster has already weighed in on the show’s future.

Spoilers for Nobody Wants This season 3 below.

Three seasons into Nobody Wants This, Joanne (Kristen Bell) and Noah (Adam Brody) have finally reached the altar—or, more accurately, a Malibu beach. But does their wedding also mark the end of the series?

The third season, which premiered on Netflix on October 22, sees the couple scrap their carefully planned nuptials and exchange vows shortly after Joanne completes her conversion to Judaism.

It’s a significant development for a couple whose differences have driven much of the series since its 2024 debut. But while Joanne and Noah have settled one major question about their future, plenty of others remain unanswered. Morgan (Justine Lupe), Sasha (Timothy Simons), and Esther (Jackie Tohn), in particular, have some unfinished business.

So, does the season 3 finale mark the end of Nobody Wants This, or is there another chapter ahead? Here’s everything we know about a potential fourth season.

Not yet. As of October 26, Netflix has not announced whether Nobody Wants This will return for a fourth season.

That doesn’t necessarily mean the series is ending. Netflix has previously waited until after a season’s premiere to announce its renewal. Season 2 debuted on Oct. 23, 2025, and the streamer confirmed season 3 less than two weeks later, on November 4.

Series creator Erin Foster, meanwhile, has expressed interest in continuing the story.

In an October 23 interview with Parade, Foster discussed the possibilities for Joanne and Noah after their wedding, drawing on her own experience of getting married at 37.

“There’s those next steps as people who are not in their twenties, getting married and you’re starting off already kind of behind everyone else,” she said. “I experienced that when I got married; I was 37, and it’s a different experience. So, yeah, I do think that there’s more story to tell.”

Still, Foster isn’t assuming another season is guaranteed. She explained that she prefers to consider how audiences respond rather than commit to a particular outcome.

“I never want to have a show that goes on longer than people want to see it. I’d rather people want more than want less,” she told the outlet.

For now, the decision rests with Netflix.

The third season gives Joanne and Noah a relatively conclusive ending, but their wedding also introduces a different stage of their relationship.

After getting engaged in episode 4, the couple spends much of the season trying to plan a ceremony that accommodates their families, particularly Noah’s opinionated mother, Bina (Tovah Feldshuh). Eventually, they decide to marry on their own terms, exchanging vows on the beach after Joanne’s mikvah, the ritual immersion that completes her conversion to Judaism.

Their marriage could provide the starting point for season 4, especially given Foster’s comments about the challenges of starting a family later in life. The show has already introduced the possibility of children for Joanne and Noah, although no future storyline has been confirmed.

There is also the matter of their families finding out about the wedding. In her Parade interview, Foster suggested that Bina would be particularly upset to discover that the ceremony she had spent so much time organizing happened without her.

Then there’s the considerably more complicated situation between Morgan, Sasha, and Esther.

After separating from Esther at the end of season 2, Sasha grows closer to Morgan, and the two eventually sleep together during a trip to New York. But their potential romance stalls when Sasha admits he isn’t ready for another serious relationship. He subsequently reconnects with Esther, and the former spouses sleep together as well.

By the finale, both women know about Sasha’s involvement with the other, leaving the three characters without a clear resolution.

Ahead of the season 3 premiere, Tohn spoke to ELLE about Esther’s relationship with Sasha and whether the two might eventually reconcile.

“I think Sasha and Esther get back together, but I have no idea. I’m not in the writers’ room,” she said.

Tohn also acknowledged that audiences are divided over whom Sasha should end up with, having seen fans advocate for both Esther and Morgan.

For her part, Lupe has welcomed the growing interest in Morgan and Sasha’s relationship. In an October 22 interview with WWD, the actress discussed why she thinks viewers have connected with the unlikely pairing.

“I think people gravitate toward things that are a little bit more strange sometimes, and it’s nice to be representing the black sheep or the underdogs or the loser siblings,” she said.

Whether Sasha ultimately pursues Morgan, reunites with Esther, or chooses neither remains to be seen. But unlike Joanne and Noah’s marriage, that storyline has yet to reach a definitive conclusion.

Without an official renewal, Netflix has not announced a release date or production timeline for season 4.

All three seasons of Nobody Wants This are currently streaming on Netflix.

Walt Whitman (May 31, 1819–March 26, 1892) was thirty-six when he self-published Leaves of Grass (public library | public domain). Amid its dispiriting initial reception, he received a soul-saving letter of encouragement from Emerson, who by that point had become America’s most influential literary tastemaker. Whitman carried it in his pocket for a long time, proudly showing to friends and lovers, and eventually reprinted it in full in the second edition, on the spine of which a particularly vitalizing sentence from the letter — “I Greet You at the Beginning of a Great Career” — was stamped in gold.

Without Emerson’s emboldening missive, the young poet may have perished in obscurity. Praising the book as brimming with “incomparable things said incomparably well,” Emerson buoyed Whitman’s spirit and soon sculpted public opinion into appreciation. Leaves of Grass went on to become one of most beautiful and beloved poetic works ever written.

Whitman’s words in the preface to the original edition are at least as radiant and rousing as the verses themselves — words that continue to enliven heart, mind, and spirit a century and a half later. He writes:

The land and sea, the animals, fishes, and birds, the sky of heaven and the orbs, the forests, mountains, and rivers, are not small themes … but folks expect of the poet to indicate more than the beauty and dignity which always attach to dumb real objects … they expect him to indicate the path between reality and their souls.

And yet he does indicate the path. In a passage partway between sermon and commencement address, he writes:

This is what you shall do: Love the earth and sun and the animals, despise riches, give alms to every one that asks, stand up for the stupid and crazy, devote your income and labor to others, hate tyrants, argue not concerning God, have patience and indulgence toward the people, take off your hat to nothing known or unknown or to any man or number of men, go freely with powerful uneducated persons and with the young and with the mothers of families, read these leaves in the open air every season of every year of your life, re-examine all you have been told at school or church or in any book, dismiss whatever insults your own soul, and your very flesh shall be a great poem and have the richest fluency not only in its words but in the silent lines of its lips and face and between the lashes of your eyes and in every motion and joint of your body.

In a sentiment which Pulitzer-winning poet Mark Strand would come to echo nearly 150 years later in contemplating the artist’s task to bear witness to the universe, Whitman extols the poet’s singular role in granting us access to this richness of being:

The known universe has one complete lover and that is the greatest poet. He consumes an eternal passion and is indifferent which chance happens and which possible contingency of fortune or misfortune and persuades daily and hourly his delicious pay. What baulks or breaks others is fuel for his burning progress to contact and amorous joy.

Without effort and without exposing in the least how it is done the greatest poet brings the spirit of any or all events and passions and scenes and persons some more and some less to bear on your individual character as you hear or read. To do this well is to compete with the laws that pursue and follow time.

He ends the lengthy preface with a piercing reflection on the measure of how an artist dances this dance with the laws of time:

The proof of a poet is that his country absorbs him as affectionately as he has absorbed it.

Absorb the timelessly rewarding Leaves of Grass and complement it with Whitman on the power of music, healthcare and the human spirit, and why a robust society is a feminist society.

Van Morrison once characterized Bob Dylan (b. May 24, 1941) as the greatest living poet. And since poetry, per Muriel Rukeyser’s beautiful definition, is an art that relies on the “moving relation between individual consciousness and the world,” to glimpse Dylan’s poetic prowess is to grasp at once his singular consciousness and our broader experience of the world. That’s precisely what shines through in Paul Zollo’s 1991 interview with Dylan, found in Songwriters On Songwriting (public library) — that excellent and extensive treasure trove that gave us Pete Seeger on originality and also features conversations with such celebrated musicians as Suzanne Vega, Leonard Cohen, k.d. lang, David Byrne, Carole King, and Neil Young, whose insights on songwriting extend to the broader realm of creative work in a multitude of disciplines.

Zollo captures Dylan’s singular creative footprint:

Pete Seeger said, “All songwriters are links in a chain,” yet there are few artists in this evolutionary arc whose influence is as profound as that of Bob Dylan. It’s hard to imagine the art of songwriting as we know it without him.

There’s an unmistakable elegance in Dylan’s words, an almost biblical beauty that has sustained his songs throughout the years.

One essential aspect of Dylan’s creative process that comes up again and again in the interview is the notion of the unconscious and the optimal environment for its free reign. Dylan tells Zollo:

It’s nice to be able to put yourself in an environment where you can completely accept all the unconscious stuff that comes to you from your inner workings of your mind. And block yourself off to where you can control it all, take it down…

Like many creators, Dylan values that unconscious aspect of creativity far more than rational deliberation, speaking to the idea that the muse cannot be willed, only welcomed — a testament to the role of unconscious processing in the psychological stages of creative work. He tells Zollo:

The best songs to me — my best songs — are songs which were written very quickly. Yeah, very, very quickly. Just about as much time as it takes to write it down is about as long as it takes to write it.

In order to do that, he adds, one must “stay in the unconscious frame of mind to pull it off, which is the state of mind you have to be in anyway.” Contrary to Bukowski’s punchy assertion that the ideal environment for creativity is an irrelevant delusion and E.B. White’s admonition that “a writer who waits for ideal conditions under which to work will die without putting a word on paper,” Dylan believes this optimal frame of mind can be induced — or, at least, greatly aided — by the right conditions:

For me, the environment to write the song is extremely important. The environment has to bring something out in me that wants to be brought out. It’s a contemplative, reflective thing…

Environment is very important. People need peaceful, invigorating environments. Stimulating environments.

To foster such unconscious receptivity, Dylan argues that “you have to be able to get the thoughts out of your mind” and explains:

First of all, there’s two kinds of thoughts in your mind: there’s good thoughts and evil thoughts. Both come through your mind. Some people are more loaded down with one than another. Nevertheless, they come through. And you have to be able to sort them out, if you want to be a songwriter, if you want to be a song singer. You must get rid of all that baggage. You ought to be able to sort out those thoughts, because they don’t mean anything, they’re just pulling you around, too. It’s important to get rid of them thoughts.

Then you can do something from some kind of surveillance of the situation. You have some kind of place where you can see it but it can’t affect you. Where you can bring something to the matter, besides just take, take, take, take, take. As so many situations in life are today. Take, take, take, that’s all that it is. What’s in it for me? That syndrome which started in the Me Decade, whenever that was. We’re still in that. It’s still happening.

Dylan makes a seemingly controversial statement that resonates with new layers of poignancy in our present age of seemingly infinite cloud libraries of streamable music and a constant, industrialized churning out of disposable pop hits:

The world don’t need any more songs… As a matter of fact, if nobody wrote any songs from this day on, the world ain’t gonna suffer for it. Nobody cares. There’s enough songs for people to listen to, if they want to listen to songs. For every man, woman and child on earth, they could be sent, probably, each of them, a hundred songs, and never be repeated. There’s enough songs.

Unless someone’s gonna come along with a pure heart and has something to say. That’s a different story.

But as far as songwriting, any idiot could do it… Everybody writes a song just like everybody’s got that one great novel in them.

In fact, Dylan seems to regard “popular entertainers” — despite counting himself among them — with a certain degree of contempt and mistrust:

It’s not a good idea and it’s bad luck to look for life’s guidance to popular entertainers.

Dylan considers what it takes to be among the few rare exceptions worthy of true creative respect:

Madonna’s good, she’s talented, she puts all kinds of stuff together, she’s learned her thing… But it’s the kind of thing which takes years and years out of your life to be able to do. You’ve got to sacrifice a whole lot to do that. Sacrifice. If you want to make it big, you’ve got to sacrifice a whole lot.

Poets do a lot of listening and … and usually they know why they’re poets! [Laughs]

#3472. This Standing Yoga Pose Teaches You the Secret to Steady Balance

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

(Photo: Andrew Clark | Design in Canva by Laura Harold)

In Yoga Journal’s Archives series, we share a curated collection of articles originally published in past issues beginning in 1975. This article about how to do Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana first appeared in the March-April 1996 issue of Yoga Journal. Find more of our Archives here.

We usually think of yoga postures as static physical positions, a perception perhaps encouraged by connotations of the word “posture” itself. But postures are better conceived of as “streams” of coordinated sequential movements with a beginning, middle, and end, more like a movie than a photo. It’s the middle phase—which is just one aspect or “frame” of the entire process—that we tend to isolate and emphasize as “the posture,” virtually ignoring what comes before and after it.

To more accurately evoke the vitality and fluency of posture, movement pioneer Moshe Feldenkrais has coined the word “acture.” An acture, says Feldenkrais, has four qualities: an absence of effort, “no matter what the actual expenditure of energy is”; an absence of resistance, meaning not acting “against ourselves”; the presence of reversibility, which means that at any time, the acture can be “stopped, withheld from continuing, or reversed without any preliminary change of attitude and without effort”; and ease of the breath.

The Mountain Posture (Tadasana), a basic upright stance, is the jumping off point, as well as the terminus, of all the standing postures. It is a condition of poise in which our major skeletal segments—the skull, rib cage, pelvis, and spine—are strung like beads on the thread of our body’s central line of gravity. Here we ponder the chain of movements to come, and establish the fundamental awareness of the steady and comfortable uprightness that provides a constant background to our entire practice.

Next we smoothly fit ourselves into the energetic demands of the “stream” and shift into what we typically call “the posture.” Much of the success of this middle phase—whether we actually “fit in” or become fitful or discomfited—depends on our willingness to consciously immerse ourselves in the transition, to stay alert, receptive, and enthusiastic during the passage and not hurry ahead to what we imagine to be the destination.

Finally, after exploring the fresh slants of the modified standing position, we recoil to our home base, Tadasana.

The standing posture I’ve chosen to explore here is Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana, a mouthful that literally means the “elevated (or standing) hand-and-big-toe posture.” Like its relatives, Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana begins and ends in Tadasana. It asks us to stand in a most unusual way—with one leg raised parallel (or slightly above parallel) to the floor, held in place by the grip of the index and middle fingers and the thumb of the same-side hand on the big toe.

Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana will strengthen your legs, ankles, feet, and lower back; stretch the back of your legs—the hamstrings, calves, and Achilles tendons; and improve your balance and teach you to stand on your own one foot.

People who tend to lock or hyperextend their knees should avoid allowing the knee caps—especially on the standing leg—to roll in “cross-eyed.” Align the center of each knee cap with the center of its companion ankle, and contract the front thigh muscles (quadriceps) to lift each knee cap toward the center of its companion hip joint.

We’ll begin by focusing on the “bottom” of Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana (and all the standing postures, for that matter), the functional unit of the ankle and foot, which shoulders our weight when we stand, and hikes it when we move. The sole connects us to the earth and, with its intelligently designed system of arches, acts as a shock absorber when we walk, run, hop, skip, or jump along.

This system of arches forms a kind of vault that, when we stand, contacts the floor at three points: the bases of the big and little toes and the center of the heel. Look at your right sole. Press your right thumb into the ball of the big toe and left thumb into the ball of the little toe. Wiggle these two points back and forth awhile, to loosen things up a bit, then stretch them away from each other to widen the ball of the foot. Since all the standing postures are, to some extent, balancing positions, awareness of this widening while executing these asanas will expand our area of contact with the floor and make us more secure.

Turn your sole up and run a thumb counterclockwise around the circumference of the heel a few times, as if limning a wheel. The image of this wheel spinning counterclockwise reinforces the arch and prevents pronation (usually called a flat foot)—the inward collapse of the foot vault. The “hub” of this wheel—press it with your thumb—is the center of the heel, the third point of the vault. Now repeat with the left foot (its heel rotates clockwise).

The ankle is a hinge joint. What we commonly call the ankle bone—that pair of knobs on the inner and outer leg above the heel—is not the ankle bone at all. These “knobs” are actually the bulging lower ends of the two leg bones: the tibia or shin (the bone we’re forever banging on the edge of the bed) and its mate, the fibula.

The real ankle bone, and the apex of the foot vault, is the talus, which is wedged between the projections of the tibia and fibula like a movable tenon in a mortise. Since this bone transmits our weight to the foot, a misaligned ankle affects the foot’s capacity to adequately support and balance the body.

Each ankle knob or malleolus—”little hammer”—serves as a pulley for muscles that originate in the lower leg and reach down, through long tendons, into the foot. These muscles contribute to the work of moving the foot and stabilizing its vault. Try the following exercise, first with the right foot lifted off the floor, then the left. Imagine there is a string tied to the base of the big toe that loops over the inner malleolus and attaches deep inside the lower leg, at the back of the tibia below the knee. Pull on the tibia end of the string and watch the foot flex (toes point away from the shin); then extend the foot (lift it toward the shin) and feel the string stretch around the pulley. Seesaw each foot a few times with the image of the string and pulley in mind.

For these preliminaries, get a sand bag (or any firm support two or three inches thick) and a block.

Stand with the feet hip-width apart, second toes parallel, and spread the balls of the feet. Sway the body over the feet, first clockwise, then counterclockwise, and feel each of the six points on the soles as they press in turn into the floor. Start with large circles, then gradually narrow down until you stop with all six points evenly touching. Stay with this even contact for a few minutes. This is the position of contact you will want to recreate in all your standing postures.

Be sure to enliven the skin of the soles—don’t just stand there! Attend, for example, to the pulsing of the feet on the floor, feeling the way, as Stanley Keleman puts it, “the feet in conjunction with the ground form a reverberating drum.” According to B.K.S. lyengar, this is the seed of self-awareness, which “starts from the skin on the soles of the feet when standing.”

Now set the balls of your feet onto the sand bag, heels on the floor, and exhale into a forward bend (bend your knees slightly or support your hands on a block if the floor is too far away). In your imagination, tug on the tibia ends of your leg strings and shorten the bases of the big toes toward the inner heels (without lifting the toes off the bag), then pull up strongly along the back of the tibia toward the knee. If you need tactile aid, brush a finger along the route, from the base of the big toe to the inner heel, around the malleolus, and up the inner calf.

Once this is established, stroke along the outer edges of the feet from the heels to the little toes and grow these toes until they’re the longest toes on the feet. Then pinch the fronts of the ankles together with your hands, squeezing the inner malleolus out and the outer in. Finally, bring your hands to the calves just below the knees and stroke down to the floor, planting the heels deep into the earth.

All these exercises (for which I’m indebted to Mabel Todd and one of her students, Barbara Clark) train us to “stand our ground.” They make our feet and ankles stronger and more flexible—and in turn improve our balance—by integrating the toes with the ankle and leg, centering the foot under the ankle, and reinforcing the vault. Now inhale, come up, step off the bag and, with these lessons in mind, stand in a “steady and comfortable” Tadasana.

This practice is one phase of Supta Padangusthasana, the “reclining big toe posture,” the supine equivalent of Utthita Haste Padangusthasana. I use Supta Padangusthasana as a preparation for Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana, because I can rehearse many of the details of the standing posture without any concern about keeling over. Get a long strap, a sticky mat, and a blanket or two. Remember to apply, in this and the following practices, all of the preliminary exercises we just went over.

Lie supine on your mat with your knees slightly bent and heels against a wall. Let the front groins (where the front thighs joins the pelvis) sink onto the floor, as if they’re being weighed down by an enormous sand bag. As the groins sink, push through the legs, dig the heels into the wall, and straighten the knees. Simultaneously scrub the back of the pelvis along the mat and elongate—but don’t flatten—the lower back.

Exhale, draw the right knee up toward the chest, wrap the hands around the shin, and squeeze the thigh against the abdomen. Notice if the left knee crooks and if the pressure of the heel against the wall lessens. If so, make the top of the left thigh even heavier and, as you continue to hug the right thigh to the belly, redig the left heel into the wall.

This solid contact of heel to wall will help elongate the spine. But if, at any time during this practice, the back of your neck crimps and your chin juts up to the ceiling, lay a folded blanket under your head to protect the cervical spine.

The full posture is customarily performed by gripping the big toe in the manner described for Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana above, but even if you can easily do this, use a strap. First double the strap, then split it so that one half spans the back of the ball of the foot—where the ball meets the vault—and one half spans the front of the heel—where the heel meets the vault.

Now imagine that you’re pushing the right groin toward the wall, extend through the heel, and straighten the leg with an exhale. If you can, take the leg perpendicular to the floor; if you can’t, hold it below 90 degrees, or bend the knee enough to get just the thigh perpendicular. You might eventually be able, as the hamstrings surrender, to straighten the knee and take the leg higher. Don’t use the strap, though, to pull the leg beyond where the muscles of the hip and thigh can reasonably take it.

Hold the strap in your left hand, burrow the right thumb into the front groin, and accentuate the outward rotation of the thigh (Figure 1). Rotate the floor leg inward as the outer hip lifts toward the ceiling. Gently furrow the doubled strap into the sole and lift the three points of the vault toward the ceiling.

This is a supine “mirror” of the middle phase of Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana. Pay particular attention to the contact of the heel on the wall, and the effect that contact has on the extension of the spine; to the rotation of the two thighs, especially to how the floor thigh rolls in as the hip lifts up; and to the level contact of the back of the pelvis on the floor, and the way it lengthens down through the tail.

Stay for two to five minutes, then exhale and slowly lower the raised leg to the floor, controlling the movement from the hip. Do the left leg for an equal amount of time.

Next we’ll approximate Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana by supporting the raised leg on a chair. Get a chair (or any steady support), a strap, a blanket or two, and a block. Set the chair a few inches away from a wall (with its back toward the wall), and pad the chair back with the blanket.

Stand and face the chair, feet together. Spread the balls of the feet and shift your weight to the left, moving the mid-line of the pelvis over the mid-line of the left foot (a line from the center of the ankle, across the top of the foot, and out the second toe). Exhale, bend and lift the right knee as high as you can, and balance on the left foot. Here you’re reproducing the starting position of the previous practice, except now you’re vertical and without the reassuring embrace of the floor.

Bring your hands onto your hips, touch the index fingers to the hip points, and adjust an imaginary line between these two points parallel both to the wall and to the floor. The right hip might be a bit higher than the left: If so, soften the outer left hip, firm the inner left thigh, and release the right front groin to the floor.

Exhale, and as the groin continues dropping, gracefully extend through the right leg, rest the back of the ankle on the padded chair back, and tunnel your heel into the wall. As you do this, keep the left foot steadfastly glued to the floor, pointing straight ahead. In this and the following practices, be sure not to clutch at the floor with your toes, but keep them wide and soft. It’s common for the right hip now to be somewhat ahead of the left. With your left hand, roll the left hip toward the wall as you press the top left thigh away from the wall—and down into the heel—and rerelease the right groin.

It’s not unheard of for the right hip to be a whole lot ahead of the left—accompanying symptoms include wobbly knees, hunched back, and a foreboding of imminent doom (Figure 2). More than likely the problem is that your support is too high for the present capacity of your hamstrings. You need to either lower your foot to the chair seat (or to a block on the chair seat), or saw a few inches off each of your chair legs; whichever you decide to do, this supported version is probably as far as you can go with Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana for the time being.

Once the pelvis is neutral, at whatever height you can manage, increase the stretch on the back of the raised leg. Exhale and, with the left heel pressed actively into the floor, lean forward from the hip joints. Rest your hands on your thigh, shin, ankle, or on the wall, whatever your hamstrings will allow (Figure 3).

Stay for one to three minutes on each side. When you’re ready to bring the leg down, inhale to vertical, exhale, then inhale and draw the raised knee back toward the torso (use your hands if needed), and finally exhale and lightly return the foot to the floor. Stand in Tadasana for a few breaths after each side.

You might try one more intermediate stage before the full unsupported posture. Lean back against the wall, with your heels a couple of inches out from the wall, in a kind of supported Tadasana (have your strap over one shoulder). Exhale, bend and raise the right knee, clasp the back thigh with your hands, and draw the front thigh to your abdomen (Figure 4).

Hold the strap in the right hand, reach inside the thigh, and loop it around the ball of the right foot. Inhale, lift the chest, then exhale and extend out through the heel, just as you did in the supported practice. See if you can bring the leg parallel to the floor: If that seems too high, swing your leg down a few degrees; if it’s comfortable, try to elevate the leg a little higher.

Wait a bit, then carefully push your torso off the wall with your left hand, reaching out actively through the right heel and hinging over the left ankle. Brace yourself with the hand against the wall for a few breaths, and finally hook it over the same-side hip. Stay for a minute or two in the middle phase of Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana, then exhale and mindfully lower the foot to the floor. Repeat on the left side.

Step away from the wall for the entire sequence of Utthita Hasta Padangusthasana. You might continue using the strap for a while longer. When you’re primed to try the full posture without any props—and you should occasionally, even if you think it still needs work—grip the big toe as described above.

Remember, though, that physical performance is not the only measure of your success. Rather, the value of such outer performance must be judged in light of the quality of awareness that you bring to the “acture,” the inner sense of effortlessness, self-acceptance, and composure—a word related to posture—with the accompanying spontaneity of breath, from the inception of the asana, through its various phases, to its end.

#431. NASA’s Perseverance Offers Clues to Protect Crews From Mars Dust

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Over 1,645 Martian days, NASA’s Perseverance Mars rover measured the fine and ultrafine Martian dust particles found clinging to samples of spacesuit materials that are carried aboard the rover, including some that are small enough to penetrate the lungs and enter the bloodstream. The findings will be published Tuesday, Oct. 27, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences and could inform spacesuit and habitat design, dust-removal procedures, and crew health standards for future NASA missions to Mars.

“NASA has a long history of turning challenges into workable solutions and of finding creative ways to use the data that we have to answer questions beyond what it was originally collected for,” said Lindsay Hays, acting director, Planetary Science Division, NASA Headquarters in Washington. “Dust has been a surface-exploration challenge since the Apollo program. Perseverance is giving our engineers and flight surgeons measurements they can use to improve our existing designs well before the first American astronauts arrive on Mars.”

Lunar dust scratched visors, degraded outer spacesuit layers, and damaged their seals during NASA’s Apollo missions, and some crew members reported symptoms similar to hay fever.

Rounded by wind-driven collisions with other particles, dust grains on Mars are less abrasive than those on the Moon. They also are enriched in sulfur, chlorine, and iron and contain more silica than lunar dust. Regional and global dust storms on Mars can keep the grains recirculating throughout the atmosphere as well.

The rover carries a set of five samples of spacesuit material. The swatches, each three-quarters of an inch (20 millimeters) square, are part of a calibration target used to test the settings of SHERLOC (Scanning Habitable Environments with Raman and Luminescence for Organics and Chemicals), an instrument on Perseverance’s robotic arm. The calibration target for another instrument that provided the dust measurements, called PIXL (Planetary Instrument for X-ray Lithochemistry), carries a sample of Teflon and of basaltic glass.

Because Teflon contains no elements that PIXL can directly detect, whatever elemental signals the X-ray spectrometer picks up from that sample comes from dust resting on it.

The team also analyzed swatches of Teflon and Vectran, two materials used in spacesuits, on SHERLOC’s calibration target. NASA sent the swatches to Mars to study how spacesuit materials hold up on the planet’s surface.

“Perseverance’s main job is to search for signs of ancient microbial life, but the mission was also designed to help prepare for human explorers. That effort includes technology demonstrations like MOXIE (Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment) and Ingenuity, as well as the spacesuit samples,” said Katie Stack Morgan, Perseverance’s project scientist at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California.

While earlier measurements on Mars resolved dust particles down to about 3 micrometers across, PIXL detected two categories of even smaller particles: fine particles measuring less than 2.5 micrometers (a human hair is about 70 micrometers wide) and ultrafine particles around 100 nanometers (about 700 times thinner than a human hair). Particles on PIXL’s Teflon target and SHERLOC’s Vectran swatch averaged about 2 micrometers. On PIXL’s basaltic glass and SHERLOC’s Teflon swatch, dust particle sizes ranged down to about 100 to 300 nanometers.

Static electricity appears to determine which size dust particles stick to which surface. Dust grains become charged as they collide in the air, and different materials on the calibration target attract grains of different sizes. The finest particles stayed attached through years of driving and drilling. Organic polymers like SHERLOC’s Teflon spacesuit sample are common in the outer layers of spacesuits, and the results show they readily collect fine dust.

The study also found that some materials hold less dust than others, which could guide future choices of materials and protective coatings for spacesuits and habitats. Designs such as suitports, which let astronauts enter a suit attached to the outside of a habitat, and dual-chamber airlocks could reduce how much dust gets inside astronauts’ living quarters.

“The existence of Mars dust at these ultrafine sizes has been predicted for a long time, and PIXL gave us the chance to measure it directly on Mars,” said Scott VanBommel, a Perseverance scientist at Washington University in St. Louis and the paper’s lead author. “The larger clumps, the Martian dust bunnies, get shaken off over time. But the smallest particles stay behind, and those are the ones that pose the greatest challenge for human support systems.”

Chemically, the dust matches the iron- and sulfur-rich composition that earlier missions measured. The PIXL instrument also found the dust to be enriched in phosphorus and detected signs of calcium sulfate.

On Earth, airborne particles, or particulate matter, below 2.5 micrometers (that is, fine particles) are tied to a range of negative health effects because they can penetrate deep into the lungs and enter the bloodstream. Studies have shown that ultrafine particles (those no larger than 100 nanometers) can travel from the nasal cavity to the brain along the olfactory nerve.

Martian dust is composed predominantly of silicate minerals and carries chemically reactive compounds, including perchlorates that can disrupt thyroid function and reactive iron that induces cellular damage. Scientists believe that the health risks of Mars dust depend on the quantity of dust and length of exposure.

The PIXL results are the first on-site measurements of fine and ultrafine particles on Mars, and this research can inform broader efforts to refine indoor dust-exposure limits for astronauts, develop safe habits, and establish ways to remove dust while exploring Mars.

NASA is already working on solutions to extraterrestrial dust hazards, using ground test facilities and lunar missions as proving grounds for future Mars explorers. In March 2025, the agency tested an Electrodynamic Dust Shield, which used electric fields to clear lunar regolith from glass and thermal radiator surfaces on the Moon. Developed at NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida, it landed on the Moon aboard Firefly Aerospace’s Blue Ghost Mission 1. At NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston, engineers at the Lunar Development and Test Facility use vacuum chambers and lunar regolith simulant to test spacesuit components, mechanisms, and a Handheld Lunar Electrostatic Dust Mitigation tool ahead of Artemis missions to the lunar surface.

Managed for NASA by Caltech, JPL built and manages operations of the Perseverance rover on behalf of the agency’s Science Mission Directorate as part of NASA’s Mars Exploration Program portfolio. NASA JPL also built PIXL and SHERLOC.

#3975. Speaking Faster Can Make You More Persuasive — Until This Happens

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Your best argument may lose its edge if listeners struggle to keep up with how quickly you deliver it.

Your best argument may lose its edge if listeners struggle to keep up with how quickly you deliver it.

Speaking at a lively pace can make a strong argument more convincing, but rushing can weaken its impact.

Very fast speech makes it harder for listeners to weigh up what they hear, reducing the advantage that good reasons have over weak ones.

The findings suggest that persuading someone depends partly on giving them enough opportunity to think.

The conclusions come from six experiments involving almost 4,000 people who listened to persuasive messages delivered at different speeds.

Across the experiments, increasing the pace from very slow speech initially improved impressions of the speaker’s confidence and encouraged closer consideration of the message.

However, these benefits eventually weakened or reversed as the delivery became faster.

This helps explain why simply telling someone to speak more quickly is incomplete advice.

A faster delivery can make an argument easier to engage with until the listener begins struggling to keep up.

Crucially, the consequences depend on whether the reasons being offered stand up to scrutiny.

In the two main experiments, listeners heard a proposal that would allow university students to pay lower tuition fees in exchange for working part-time.

Some heard strong arguments emphasising benefits such as smaller classes and better services.

Others heard weak arguments that included reduced leisure time and superficial improvements to the campus.

The recordings were digitally altered to change speaking speed while keeping other vocal features, such as pitch and intonation, unchanged.

Participants then rated their support for the proposal, with some also recording the thoughts that had occurred to them while listening.

The combined results showed that strong arguments gained an increasing advantage over weak ones as speech became faster, but lost some of that advantage at higher speeds.

In other words, delivery affected how effectively listeners distinguished a convincing case from an unconvincing one.

A central part of persuasion is how carefully people consider the reasons they are given.

When listeners examine a message closely, they have more opportunity to recognise its strengths, spot its weaknesses and form their own reactions.

This benefits a strong argument, while exposing the shortcomings of a weak one.

Very fast delivery can interfere with that process, making the merits of the message less influential.

The analyses suggested that changes in how easily listeners felt they could follow the reasoning helped explain this pattern.

Although speaking speed also affected how confident the speaker sounded, confidence alone did not adequately explain the overall pattern.

One possible reason very slow speech is less effective is that it makes sustaining attention more demanding, while excessively fast speech leaves too little time to absorb the content.

The experiments tested seven speaking speeds, ranging from 113 to 218 words per minute, but did not establish one ideal pace.

The most effective delivery is likely to depend on factors including the complexity of the message, the audience and the presence of distractions.

The findings also concern recorded persuasive messages, so they do not establish a universal rule for every conversation or public speech.

For someone presenting a strong case, the findings suggest keeping the delivery lively while giving listeners time to follow the reasoning.

Rushing may reduce the very scrutiny that would otherwise make a good argument convincing.

The study was published in the Journal of Nonverbal Behavior (Guyer et al., 2025).

Astronomers have spotted a remarkable gamma-ray burst (GRB) that's a real Incredible Hulk compared to other cosmic explosions of the same type.

What makes this GRB so special is the fact that the central engine powering it remained active for around 27 days, flaring in X-rays following the initial blast. That's 20 days longer than the longest previous GRB on record.

This record-breaking burst — which is designated GRB 220706A and was spotted on July 6, 2022 — is even more exciting because scientists don't know what the central engine powering it actually is.

"In a nutshell, GRB 220706A is an extremely late X-ray flare. X-ray flaring is common following GRBs, but previously it has always come within the first few days after we detect the burst," study team member Benjamin Gompertz, of the University of Birmingham in England, told Space.com.

"The fact that we see flaring at around 27 days in the GRB’s rest frame tells us that there’s an ongoing active process, and that’s unprecedented in the known population," Gompertz added. "The previous record for delayed flaring was about six days after the GRB."

GRBs are some of the most powerful explosions in the cosmos. They have been linked to the collapse of a massive star, which also triggers a supernova and creates a black hole. This results in a long-duration GRB, while short-duration GRBs are thought to be triggered when two stellar remnants called neutron stars collide.

GRB 220706A is an example of an ultra-long GRB, a rare cosmic blast of gamma rays in which the engine powering the GRB stays active for longer. They are incredibly rare, with their cause still unknown.

"We don’t know what the central engine driving this outburst is, and that’s what makes this such a compelling event," Gompertz said. "The fact that we see a supernova tells us that a star has collapsed, which is the standard power source for GRBs, but it doesn’t immediately account for the extremely late flaring or unusually bright supernova. We can interpret that through previous events to some degree."

Gompertz added that another ultra-long GRB detected in 2011, GRB 111209A, was associated with an unusually bright supernova (SN2011k) that had clear evidence for a central engine. The team behind that research determined that GRB 111209A is powered by a magnetar, a neutron star with a particular powerful magnetic field. In this case, the magnetic field is rotating at around 1,000 times per second.

"Our supernova looks a lot like that one, so a magnetar might be present here. That ties in nicely with super-luminous supernovas, which are also thought to be magnetar-driven," Gompertz said. "Another possibility could be that the GRB jet and the supernova are colliding with shells of dense material that were ejected towards the end of the parent star’s life, as it became unstable and began ejecting its outer layers. This sort of event is also seen in the super-luminous supernova population."

The team isn't quite sure how the central engine of this ultra-long GRB differs from that of others and why that resulted in 27 days of flaring.

"One possibility is that it’s a much bigger star than we usually see producing GRBs," Gompertz said. "A bigger star would take longer to fall in on itself and so provide a longer-lived source of energy to power the jet."

GRB sources may be members of a stellar population of massive, compact stars called Wolf-Rayet stars. These are 10 times wider that the sun, with around 10 times the mass of our star. However, even the collapse of such a star can't explain GRB 220706A.

"To power GRB 220706A, we estimated we need a star more like 1,000 times the radius of the sun, which would require a population of stars known as blue supergiants," Gompertz said. "On the other hand, it may be the remnant that’s different. If this star produced a magnetar, it may be that 'normal' GRBs just collapse to black holes."

The team thinks that the big question with GRBs like this one is whether they are members of the normal GRB population or a different kind of event. Having this second event, in addition to GRB 111209A, with an unusually bright supernova potentially adds weight to the idea that these are different stars. But the problem is not yet solved.

"The incredibly late flares also strain our models for how jets are powered," Gompertz said. "It will be fascinating to see how future theoretical work can incorporate a power source from a collapsing star that can last for a month!"

There could be even longer-lasting GRBs out in the cosmos that we have thus far failed to detect. Gompertz explained why such detections are tricky.

"GRBs this long are hard to detect because most of the satellites we use to find them are in low Earth orbit, which means the target is periodically blocked every 90 minutes or so," he explained. "That's fine for your typical 10- to 100-second-long GRB but becomes a nuisance when tracking much longer events. Long-lasting GRBs also seem to be more active in the X-rays rather than the gamma rays."

The University of Birmingham researcher added that newly launched missions like the Einstein Probe, which is more sensitive in X-rays than gamma rays, may be the perfect instruments to find more ultra-long GRBs.

"With any luck, we’ll get a few more of these to chase so that we can start trying to understand the incredible power sources that drive them," Gompertz concluded.

#2236. Airbnb Just Did Something Guests Have Been Asking for Years — Here’s What Changed

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Airbnb is putting limits on checkout chores, including washing dishes and stripping beds. Here's what hosts can still ask guests to do.

Everyone loves a vacation. But the cleanup and tidying as the trip is finishing up? Not so much.

It’s not always clear what guests are responsible for cleaning. Dishes, sheets, towels, and taking out the trash are sometimes part of checkout instructions at Airbnb rentals. With checkout typically at 11 a.m., the last morning of a trip can turn into a mad dash through an unreasonably long chore list, even when a cleaning fee has been tacked onto the bill.

That frustration, along with a growing number of travelers returning to hotels for more consistent quality and fees, is behind Airbnb’s new checkout rules. Learn which tasks guests may still face, and how hosts are responding.

Starting with reservations booked on or after November 20, 2026, Airbnb will require hosts to clearly explain their house rules and checkout requests before guests book. Hosts won’t be allowed to add new requirements through messages or signs inside the rental after a reservation is confirmed.

“House Rules are now displayed more prominently on your listing, where guests can easily see them before they book,” according to Airbnb’s announcement. Guests can also find these instructions in their Trips tab and reservation reminders.

Under the new policy, hosts generally can’t require guests to strip beds, start laundry, wash and put away dishes, or take trash to an off-site disposal facility. However, hosts can still request simpler tasks, such as placing dirty dishes in the sink, gathering used towels, or putting trash and recycling in designated bins. Guests may also be asked to turn off lights and return keys before leaving.

Airbnb’s policy distinguishes between house rules and requests. “Guests must follow hosts’ house rules, and are encouraged to comply with hosts’ requests,” according to the company. Some exceptions may apply. For example, rentals in areas with bears may require guests to use animal-proof trash containers, while drought restrictions could affect water use.

Guests are still responsible for leaving the property reasonably tidy. Airbnb’s ground rules for guests prohibit excessive messes and require guests to take responsibility for damage beyond normal wear and tear. Hosts can still charge standard cleaning fees and request reimbursement for unexpected cleaning costs or damage.

Hosts who don’t follow Airbnb’s new checkout rules could receive warnings or requests to update their listings. Repeated violations may lead to notices on their listings or lower placement in search results. Hosts can also appeal flagged requests.

The policy also prevents hosts from charging their own fines for missed chores or restricting access to amenities already included in a reservation.

However, not every host supports the changes. Some believe washing dishes after cooking is a reasonable responsibility for guests, while others worry the new rules could increase cleaning costs. A Change.org petition has called on Airbnb to reverse its restrictions on requiring guests to wash dishes.

Guests who encounter unexpected chores or requests that violate the new policy can contact Airbnb for help.

ChatGPT is cracking down on photorealistic, political deepfakes ahead of next week’s US midterm elections.

One of the biggest problems of the AI age is that AI deepfakes are now so realistic that they’re practically indistinguishable from real photos. That matters a lot during election season, when voters are looking for reliable, truthful information on their candidates and polling sites. OpenAI is implementing a new safeguard to prevent people from making political deepfakes.

The US midterm election is only a week away on Nov. 3. Tech issues are taking center stage in many races, especially as AI and data center concerns rise for folks across the country. (Check out CNET’s guide to spotting political scams.)

If you were to go into ChatGPT today and ask it to generate an image “depicting election interference or that may discourage voting,” as OpenAI put it in an Oct. 20 update, the chatbot would create the image, given it doesn’t violate any other content guidelines. But it will be in an illustrated or cartoon style — all requests to have the image appear photorealistic should be ignored.

For example, CNET asked ChatGPT to create an image of Immigration and Customs Enforcement officers standing outside a polling location. Every such prompt generated an image in a cartoon style. A photorealistic version of this kind of image would likely spark anxiety, if not outright panic, for voters.

The idea is to make it harder for bad actors to create realistic political deepfakes that could interfere with or question the integrity of an election without stymying people’s ability to create political content in general. In another example, CNET Managing Editor Jon Reed couldn’t trick ChatGPT into creating a photojournalist-style image that showed a voting machine recording the wrong choice.

“The problem with deepfakes isn’t social commentary, it’s deception. So our aim is to degrade the evidentiary value of an image, while preserving room for illustrations and social commentary,” Josh Lawson, OpenAI’s head of elections, told CNET. The goal is to “make our platform less useful to bad actors by adding another layer of friction,” he said.

This cartoonification of AI images is a practical way to make political deepfakes a little harder to create. Improving AI image models, like ChatGPT Images 2.5, is a double-edged sword. Newer models make fewer errors or hallucinations, making better content for creators. But removing those flubs makes it even harder for everyone else to identify AI-created content with the naked eye. Verification or AI labeling methods have tried to help, but those methods aren’t industry-wide nor foolproof.

When I asked Gemini maker Google about any precautions it is taking specifically for AI images, a spokesperson pointed me to its midterm election policy, which says the company is “rolling out additional protections to limit the creation of misleading election-related images” but doesn’t include any details on how.

Anthropic doesn’t have a traditional image or video generator, and a spokesperson told CNET that any attempts to use Claude to deceive voters or disrupt elections violate its policies. The company also posted this general election guidance earlier this month.

There’s no guarantee that OpenAI will keep this system in place once the election cycle is over. But it’s a solid sign from one of the biggest AI labs that it is taking its role in safeguarding American elections’ integrity at least somewhat seriously.
