#971. Paramount and Warner Bros merge into one Hollywood giant

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Paramount has completed an $81 billion purchase of Warner Bros. Discovery, as its owner Skydance creates a new entertainment behemoth. Billionaire David Ellison's hostile takeover bid defeated rival efforts from Netflix.

Paramount on Tuesday finalized its takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery, completing a hostile acquisition that was months in the making and almost faltered at several hurdles.

The takeover cost a total of around $81 billion (roughly €72 billion), or almost $111 billion including the Warner Bros. debts passed on as part of the deal.

The merger brings together two of the oldest US filmmaking studios and will form a new giant called Skydance Corporation.

The new name Skydance hails from billionaire investor David Ellison, who first bought out Paramount and its subsidiaries and then moved on to target the much larger fish that is Warner Bros.

The bid faced stiff competition in a bidding war with Netflix, and later scrutiny from US regulators and politicians, but ultimately went through.

"Today is a historic day, not just for Skydance but for our entire industry," said Ellison, whose family has ties to US President Donald Trump.

Skydance shares began trading on the New York Stock Exchange on Tuesday under the ticker SKYD, while Warner Bros. Discovery shareholders will receive about $31 per share.

The deal closed less than a week after a federal judge in California approved a settlement between Paramount and 12 US states that had sued to block the transaction on antitrust grounds.

As well as the two giant film studios, Paramount Pictures and Warner Bros. Pictures, the deal also brings together the CBS News and CNN news networks, and the HBO and CBS television brands. Ellison says he plans to merge the subscription streaming platforms Paramount+ and HBO Max.

Ellison will serve as chairman and CEO of the new company. Former Mattel boss Ynon Kreiz is operating as co-CEO in charge of day-to-day operations.

Skydance Corp. looks set to have a total revenue in the region of $70 billion, based on the performance to date of its component parts. It says it's targeting cost savings of around $6 billion within three years as it takes advantage of overlaps created by the merger.

#949. Nobel Prize in literature goes to Canada's Anne Carson

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Carson won for her "bold and inventive oeuvre." The decorated poet and essayist is known for her reverence for Latin and Greek classics.

The Swedish Academy awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature to Anne Carson on Thursday, praising her "bold and inventive oeuvre."

Her work has "in playful dialogue with the classical tradition...created new forms for contemporary literature," said Ingrid Carlberg, permanent secretary of the academy.

The 76-year-old writer is a longtime professor of classics, creative writing, and comparative literature at prestigious institutions like McGill and Princeton universities. She is only the 19th woman of 123 laureates to win the award.

Born in Toronto in 1950, Carson moved with her family among several small towns in Canada during her youth. She has credited a beloved Latin teacher for sparking her interest in language and classics.

Her first book of criticism, 1986’s Eros the Bittersweet, was published to great acclaim and has had significant influence on other classicists, poets, and even film and television. Carson has also published her own poetry such as the 2001 collection The Beauty of the Husband as well as highly regarded translation of ancient Greek poets such as Sappho and Sophocles.

Carson is also heavily influenced by the King James Bible and more modern writers including Emily Dickinson and Virginia Woolf. She has received numerous awards such as the T.S. Eliot Prize for Poetry, a MacArthur fellowship, and the PEN/Nabokov Award.

Deeply private, Carson often declines to speak about her personal life in interviews and discourages autobiographical interpretations of her work. However, she did reveal in 2024 that she has Parkinson's disease.

The winner of the Nobel Prize in literature receives prize money of 12 million Swedish kronor, equivalent to about $1.2 million. The winner also receives a medal and a diploma at an official ceremony in December, and is expected to deliver a speech known as a Nobel lecture.

On Friday, the winner of the Nobel Peace Prize will be announced, followed by the Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel on Monday.

Patrick Stewart, who rose from poverty in England to become an accomplished Shakespearean stage actor and then achieved global stardom as the erudite Captain Jean-Luc Picard in TV’s “Star Trek: The Next Generation” and the telepathic Professor Charles Xavier in the “X-Men” movies, has died at ​the age of 86.

Stewart, knighted in 2010 by Queen Elizabeth II for his contributions to drama, died at his home in Los Angeles, according to a statement on his ‌X account. “We announce with great sorrow the passing of Sir Patrick Stewart yesterday at his home in Los Angeles, ​in the loving care of his beloved wife Sunny following a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer’s Disease and Lewy Body Dementia,” ⁠it said.

Stewart became an actor at age 18 and gained acclaim on the stage as a member of the Royal Shakespeare Company beginning in the 1960s. But it was not until after he was hired at age 47 for “Star Trek: The Next Generation” - a TV series that ran from 1987 to 1994 as a sequel to ‌the original 1960s “Star Trek” - that he gained mainstream fame, acting into his 80s.

“Make it so,” his character Picard often said, instructing a subordinate to carry out a command, or sometimes deploying the similar catch phrase, “Engage.”

It was a role he had debated even taking ‌after his longtime friend and fellow Shakespearean Ian McKellen advised him to spurn “Star Trek” and remain in theater. Stewart did take the role ‌and became ⁠both a fan favorite and an indispensable part of a lucrative sci-fi franchise, playing Picard not only in 176 episodes over seven ⁠seasons of that series but also in several “Star Trek” films and in the later streaming series “Star Trek:

Picard” that began airing in 2020 when he was 79. “As the seven seasons of TNG (”The Next Generation”) went by, the distinction between Jean-Luc Picard and Patrick Stewart became thinner and thinner, until it was impossible for me to know where he left off and I ​began. So much of what I believed and felt went into ‌that show,” Stewart told the Guardian newspaper in 2022.

Stewart also initially balked at being cast as the wheelchair-using mutant Professor X in the “X-Men” movies, based on the popular Marvel comic book character. “I said, ‘Look, I know this isn’t science fiction, but it’s fantasy, and I’ve done that.’ But they persuaded me that it wouldn’t be like ‘Star Trek,’ so I did it. And yet again I was proved wrong,” Stewart told the Guardian.

He became a ‌critical part of another big-money franchise starting with the first entry “X-Men” in 2000 and followed by several more.

Stewart was ​born on July 13, 1940, in the English town of Mirfield in Yorkshire. He was raised in poverty, with a father who he said returned from World War Two an alcoholic who suffered from post-traumatic stress syndrome and physically abused Stewart’s mother. Stewart ⁠recalled putting his body between his father and mother to try to protect her.

As a child, Stewart said, acting became a “safe space” after a schoolteacher introduced him to Shakespeare - specifically “The Merchant of Venice” - during a class when he was 12. He started losing his hair at age 17 and was bald by ‌19. He was embarrassed at the time, but eventually embraced it.

“Star Trek,” created by Gene Roddenberry, presented a hopeful vision of the future, with humankind venturing into the cosmos in spaceships exceeding the speed of light and encountering alien races. The enduring popularity of the original series and the success of spinoff films prompted Roddenberry to create “The Next Generation” sequel series.

The Picard character was the opposite of the swashbuckling and sometimes unintentionally buffoonish Captain James T. Kirk, played by William Shatner, of the original series - envisioned as more cerebral and disciplined, though sometimes coming across as stuffy, humourless, or self-important.

Stewart recalled being a little bit too serious toward his cast mates early on.

“I tell the story of calling a meeting of my principal fellow cast members and telling them that I thought ‌we were not disciplined enough, that we should not be fooling about on the set as much as we were but taking everything seriously,” Stewart told the New Yorker in 2023.

“And ​one of the cast members said, ‘Oh, come on, Patrick, we’ve got to have some fun.’ And I thumped the chair and said, ‘We are not here to have fun ’- which they have never let me forget. They taught me so much about ⁠loosening up and being freer,” Stewart added.

Stewart also felt the series improved as it went along, faulting his own portrayal of Picard in the first two seasons. “I ⁠found it lacking intimacy. It was very authoritative, but there were few other qualities in him,” Stewart said, adding that his work got better in subsequent seasons.

In fact, the finest work for the entire cast may have been the two-part, time-bending series finale.

He became Sir ‌Patrick Stewart when he was knighted by the queen at Buckingham Palace in 2010. He told reporters that his heroes were British actors Laurence Olivier, John Gielgud and Alec Guinness, who also had been knighted, adding that being “in that company is the grandest thing that has professionally happened to me.”

Stewart ​was married three times and had two children. His third marriage, in 2013, was to American musician Sunny Ozell.

#213. Patrick Stewart, ‘Star Trek’ Captain Picard and ‘X-Men’ actor, dies at 86

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Stewart died in Los Angeles after battling Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia.

Patrick Stewart, the acclaimed British actor who played Captain Jean-Luc Picard in “Star Trek” and Professor Charles Xavier in the “X-Men” films, has died. He was 86.

Stewart died on Wednesday in Los Angeles following “a brief and poignant journey” with Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia, spokesperson Kelly Bush Novak said in a statement on Thursday.

Although best known for his film and television roles, Stewart spent much of his career onstage, performing Shakespeare and other classics.

His career included performances as Claudius and Macbeth, a one-man adaptation of “A Christmas Carol”, and West End and Broadway productions of Harold Pinter’s “No Man’s Land” and Samuel Beckett’s “Waiting for Godot” alongside longtime friend Ian McKellen.

Stewart earned some of the best reviews of his career for his 2007 portrayal of Macbeth, including his only Tony Award nomination. The Times of London described his Macbeth performance as “an enthralling creation of frailty, appetite, egotism and grim humour”.

“Nearly 50 years I’ve waited,” Stewart told the Associated Press news agency in 2008. “I learned it when I was 14. I memorized all the soliloquies just because I thought they were great. And they stuck, so I didn’t have to learn those bits of the play, which is a blessing, because learning lines has now become the one curse of this job.”

Raised in Mirfield, West Yorkshire, a working-class corner of northern England, the future 24th-century commander of a starship grew up without a bathroom in his house.

He joined an amateur drama group at 12 and, as a teenager, memorised Shakespeare for fun. A scholarship to the Bristol Old Vic Theatre School helped launch his acting career.

Stewart, who developed alopecia at a young age, had been bald since he was 19.

His childhood was also marked by domestic violence. His father, a former regimental sergeant major in the British Army, was prone to outbursts of “repeated violence against my mother,” as Stewart wrote in a newspaper piece. “Violence is always a choice a man makes and he alone is responsible for it.”

Stewart later became a patron of Refuge, a charity supporting survivors of domestic abuse.

Stewart spent 14 years with the Royal Shakespeare Company, playing everyone from Mark Antony and Henry IV to Shylock and Oberon.

“People who were not in the company would say to me, ‘Give it a break. Why don’t you go somewhere else?’ And I would say, ‘To do what?'” he told the AP. “Telly?”

He eventually left and appeared in the 1981 film “Excalibur”. In 1987, shortly after performing in “Who’s Afraid of Virginia Woolf?”, he was offered a role in a science fiction series.

“I found myself in Hollywood shooting a syndicated science fiction series, which I was assured would fail,” he said.

But “Star Trek: The Next Generation” ran for seven seasons and 178 episodes. Stewart became known worldwide as Captain Jean-Luc Picard, whose command, “Make it so”, became one of the show’s best-known lines.

Stewart also played Professor Charles Xavier, the leader of the X-Men, beginning with the 2000 film “X-Men”. He returned to the role in several sequels, including 2017’s “Logan”, alongside Hugh Jackman as Wolverine.

Stewart died just days after Walter Koenig, who played Ensign Pavel Chekov in the original “Star Trek” series, which aired from 1966 to 1969.

Queen Elizabeth II knighted Stewart in June 2010. He also served as an ambassador for the International Rescue Committee.

Lewy body dementias is an umbrella term for two types of dementia that involve damage to the brain caused by abnormal deposits of Lewy bodies.

A Lewy body is a tiny tangle of protein that can appear in a brain cell.

It can cause damage that affects a patient's movement, thinking and behaviour, Dementia Australia says.

There are a range of symptoms associated with the disease including inattentiveness, difficulties planning and reacting, and memory loss.

While it is known that Lewy bodies cause the disease, Dementia Australia says researchers still do not understand why Lewy bodies form.

The charity says anyone can develop the disease, but it is more common in older people.

It estimates more than 100,000 Australians have Lewy body dementia.

Elke Sommer, the striking blonde bombshell of German descent who was a mainstay of 1960s Hollywood comedies and espionage movies including “The Prize” and “A Shot in the Dark,” has died. She was 85.

Sommer died at home in Bel Air, in Los Angeles, on Wednesday, her stepson Sebastian Walther told the German outlet Bild. The Los Angeles Police Department told The Associated Press that it responded to the death of an 85-year-old woman on Sommer’s block Wednesday night.

With big almond eyes and golden locks, often styled high in a beehive, Sommer worked with some of the biggest stars of the time, including Paul Newman in “The Prize,” Peter Sellers in the Pink Panther film “A Shot in the Dark,” James Garner and Dick Van Dyke in “The Art of Love,” Bob Hope in “Boy, Did I Get a Wrong Number!” and Dean Martin and Sharon Tate in “The Wrecking Crew.”

Sommer was born Elke Schletz in Berlin in the midst of the World War II, on Nov. 5, 1940, though the family had to evacuate to the south in 1942. Her father, who died when she was 14, was a Protestant pastor.

She’d intended to study languages, but during a trip to Italy with her mother, Renata, in 1958, she caught the eye of someone in the Italian film industry who invited her to appear in her first film: “The Friend of the Jaguar.” Actor and filmmaker Vittorio De Sica noticed her too, and soon she was working nonstop throughout Europe, in Italy, Spain and England, with the newly adopted surname Sommer.

Her big Hollywood break came from producer Pandro S. Berman, who had seen her in a German drama that screened for the Golden Globes, and offered her a role in “The Prize” where she played chaperone and love interest to Newman’s Nobel Prize-winning character. In the trade publication Variety’s review, they wrote that Sommer, “hasn’t a very scintillating role, but has the looks to make that a secondary issue.” And it would win her the Golden Globe for most promising newcomer.

In Blake Edwards’ Pink Panther sequel “A Shot in the Dark,” she played Maria Gambrelli, a maid framed for murder, who also sparks the romantic interest of Sellers’ Inspector Clouseau. She also posed for Playboy Magazine around that time, in photographs shot by her first husband Joe Hyams. They married in November 1964 and divorced in 1981.

In 1993 she married German hotelier Wolf Walther, who died in July at age 77.

Sommer didn’t take the sex symbol designation too seriously, saying that it was essentially meaningless. She told Film Talk in a 2014 interview that some of her favorite actors, like Rita Hayworth and Ava Gardner, were also considered sex symbols. Besides, she said, she was always just acting the part.

#211. Elke Sommer, prolific German star of A Shot in the Dark and And Then There Were None, dies aged 85

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The Berlin-born performer had a gift for languages that took her to Hollywood, landing roles alongside Peter Sellers, Dick Van Dyke and Paul Newman

Elke Sommer, the prolific German star of the Pink Panther film A Shot in the Dark, early 70s Agatha Christie adaptation And Then there Were None, and British sex comedy Carry on Behind, has died aged 85. Bild magazine reported that Sommer died in Los Angeles of heart failure.

German-born Sommer, whose best-known film roles were in the 1960s and 70s, was one of the wave of European performers who crossed over to Hollywood and English-language cinema with considerable success, following the postwar emergence of European new wave cinema. Sommer was one of the few German actors to make waves at the time, as Hollywood made much of her bombshell image.

Born Elke Schletz in 1940 in Berlin, the daughter of a Protestant pastor, she was evacuated with her family to Bavaria during the war. Intending to work as an interpreter, she was spotted by celebrated Italian director Vittorio De Sica after winning a beauty contest on a trip to Italy in 1958, and subsequently offered roles in a string of Italian comedy films. She crossed back into more serious work with the West German drama The Death Ship, opposite Horst Buchholz (who would go on to appear in cowboy classic The Magnificent Seven).

With her gift for languages, Sommer made her first film in English, the 1961 British comedy Don’t Bother to Knock, opposite Richard Todd, and was billed as one of the “six most exciting women in the world” alongside Melina Mercouri, Jeanne Moreau and Romy Schneider in Carl Foreman’s 1963 war fable, The Victors. Sommer’s Hollywood breakthrough arrived in the same year, with The Prize; she was cast as Paul Newman’s lover in an adaptation of Irving Wallace’s hit novel, for which she was a joint winner of a most promising newcomer Golden Globe.

Sommer’s Hollywood career then took off with A Shot in the Dark, in which she leaned heavily into her “sexpot” image as a maid suspected of a series of murders in the sequel to The Pink Panther, starring Peter Sellers. Following its success, Sommer appeared in a string of high profile Hollywood films, including The Art of Love with James Garner and Dick Van Dyke, The Oscar with Stephen Boyd, and the Bob Hope comedy Boy, Did I Get a Wrong Number! In 1968, she achieved leading actor status with The Wicked Dreams of Paula Schultz, a comedy about an East German athlete who pole-vaults over the Berlin Wall.

In the 1970s, Sommer appeared in two cult horror films by Mario Bava, Baron Blood and Lisa and the Devil, and sex comedies Percy and Percy’s Progress for British director Ralph Thomas. She followed these with two high-profile British films: as secretary Vera Clyde in And Then There Were None in 1974, adapted from Christie’s hit novel, and a year later, Carry on Behind, the 27th in the Carry On series.

After another Sellers film, The Prisoner of Zenda, and Ralph Thomas’s heist film A Nightingale Sang in Berkeley Square, Sommer scaled back her screen appearances and concentrated on painting. In the mid-1980s, she also became embroiled in a long-running feud with Zsa Zsa Gabor after they appeared together on TV show Circus of the Stars; Sommer was ultimately awarded $3.3m in damages for defamation.

Sommer was married twice, to writer Joe Hyams from 1964 until 1981, and hotelier Wolf Walther from 1993 until his death in 2026.

Right-wing Brazilian Senator Flavio Bolsonaro has fled to the US following his election defeat to incumbent Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva, Reuters and Brazilian news outlet Jovem Pan reported on Tuesday, citing sources.

Bolsonaro, the son of jailed former Brazilian President Jair Bolsonaro and an ally of US President Donald Trump, narrowly lost Sunday’s runoff to Lula despite receiving more votes in the first round earlier this month.

He is under investigation for money laundering and currency evasion involving financial ties to lawyer Willer Tomaz and businesses linked to online gambling. Bolsonaro also faces a separate investigation into suspected irregularities in the financing of ‘Dark Horse’, a biographical film about his father. He denies wrongdoing.

His brother Eduardo, also a politician, moved to the US in February 2025, citing fears of political persecution and arrest. He was subsequently convicted of attempting to interfere with judicial proceedings against his father by lobbying Washington to impose sanctions on Brazilian officials. Eduardo, who remains in the US, was sentenced to four years and two months in prison in June 2026.

“They will not silence me. They will not intimidate me. Let’s hold our heads high and keep fighting for our Brazil, whatever happens,” Bolsonaro said in a video posted on Monday, although he later dropped his demand for annulment.
