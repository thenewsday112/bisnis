#931. The Best of Inez and Vinoodh—This Year’s CFDA Lifetime Achievement Honorees—in Vogue

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

There are few fashion photographers who can look back over a four-decade career like Inez van Lamsweerde and Vinoodh Matadin. On November 2 at the CFDA Awards, the organization will honor the pair with the Geoffrey Beene Lifetime Achievement Award. The prize commemorates their deep impact and contribution to the visual language of the industry.

Both Dutch natives, the two first met while studying at the Amsterdam Fashion Academy before connecting as fashion collaborators in 1986. Over the course of a generation, the wife-and-husband power duo have crafted an experimental, high-energy, and star-powered signature look that spans a long lasting relationship with Björk, advertising work for labels such as Chanel, Miu Miu, and Yohji Yamamoto, and many major magazine editorials, including in the international pages of Vogue.

The duo started contributing to American Vogue in 1995. In a 1996 body-hugging spread titled “Futuristic Fit,” Van Lamsweerde and Matadin were described as up-and-comers utilizing brand-new technology at the forefront of their hyperrealistic creations—they were early adopters of Photoshop’s precursor, Quantel Paintbox. “We wanted to capture a kind of frozen high energy to create a surrealistic effect,” Lamsweerde told Vogue at the time.

As you’ll see in this selection of portraiture and fashion work, their pictures have a joyfully explosive quality. Their palette feels Technicolor, and the sitters seem to take on superpowers, filled with a sense of spirit and motion. Also often a sense of humor; see for example Karlie Kloss with a single caterpillar-like eyebrow. And talk about million dollar smiles—no one gets subjects to say cheese (or kaas, as the case may be) like Inez and Vinoodh.

#941. We Can’t Get Enough of All of the Art on the Spring 2027 Runways

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

“Is fashion art?” is the perennial question in this industry. Designers are inherently creative people, who, both out of natural habit and in perpetual search for inspiration, are constantly engaging with different mediums and the cool communities that surround them. What is changing is the public prominence of these conversations, exemplified by the creators with which they partner, such as Amelia, a 32-year-old TikTok art historian and Columbia student better known as Meelz on Art, whose analysis has gotten her seated at shows by Loewe and Dior.

“I think art is probably the largest, most influential cultural movement on fashion designers, on designers of all kinds,” Dr. Elizabeth Way, curator of Art X Fashion at the Museum at FIT, tells Vogue. For proof, look no further than brands like Louis Vuitton and Dior who flexed their cultural prowess with cruise shows at The Frick and LACMA, respectively, earlier this year. But you don’t need a museum to make a connection. Vogue’s Laird Borrelli-Persson combed through over 400 of our spring 2027 reviews for explicit art callouts. Naturally wide-ranging—some Dadaism here, some 18th-century-inspired contemporary art there—the list provides a fun window into designers’ brains.

Some polar opposite sources can produce strikingly complementary results. Lii’s love for Alex Katz’s graphic flair is miles away from Loewe’s references to 17th century Spanish painters Francisco de Zurbarán and Diego Velázquez (the former of which is now on view at the Louvre), and yet both spring 2027 collections were filled with unique visions of futuristic sportswear. “Loewe was especially intriguing because it was so paradoxical,” says Amelia. “It was at once extremely contemporary and simultaneously steeped in tradition. Many of the pieces were bright and saturated and exhibited severe, geometric purity. This showed up in dramatic drapery a la Zurbarán and courtly embellishment, like the clothes in a Velázquez portrait.”

At Dior things followed in a more traditional vein. “The most explicit art historical connection in his recent show came in the form of Jonathan Anderson’s replication of an 18th-century wooden chair from his personal collection which served as seats for some of the guests,” Amelia says. That said, the airy refinement of the clothing took on the precise motion blur of Gerhard Richter’s paintings. For further classical tradition, Bora Aksu drew inspiration from 17th-century portrait artist Mary Beale, while Zimmermann’s Nicky Zimmermann looked to the 19th-century natural history illustrators Harriet and Helena Scott.

Alexander Calder at the Tate Gallery, London.

In contrast, the whimsical Alexander Calder popped up as inspiration on multiple occasions, both at Givenchy, where Sarah Burton drew reference from Cirque Calder, and at Michael Kors, who zeroed in on the more mod aspects of the sculptor’s shapes. Rachel Comey’s 25th anniversary show was an ode to the visual artist and novelist Rosalyn Drexler, and Kiko Kostadinov took on an experimental edge with the works of Italian artists Agostino Bonalumi and Nanda Vigo.

Meanwhile, Prada’s 63 skirts made a subtle nod to one of its own traveling exhibitions from the early 2000s, titled “Waist Down.” “We used the skirt as a framework for different ideas, different approaches, like a raw material to express concepts,” said Miuccia Prada in the show notes. “The limitation of its simple shape, almost like a canvas, can force ideas, make you reinvent the form within its strict confines.” The skirts’ gradient dyed leather was rather reminiscent of the emotionally charged works of Mark Rothko.

“Art teaches clothing how to transcend its necessity,” says Anne Higonnet, art historian and professor at Barnard. “Every day, people out there are using color, shape, texture, composition, and rhythm to go beyond covering our vulnerable, knobby, oozing, mobile anatomies, beyond even expressing our position in society.” If you’re going to wear it, you might as well know the reference, too.

#969. Vivienne Westwood Spring 2027 Ready-to-Wear

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

For spring 2027, the Vivienne Westwood design team continued its ongoing dialogue with the house archives, finding playful and expansive new ways to interpret its standards. The starting point was Vive La Bagatelle, Westwood’s flirty spring 1997 collection and the prettily tied ribbon being flirtatiously undone that was central to it. Indulging in that push and pull between propriety and provocation, the team drew on central Westwoodisms: the fetishization of Englishness, the intimacy of the boudoir, and the titillating appeal of clothes that refuse to behave themselves.

There was plenty to recognize here in the familiar sweep of a Sunday dress and the off-kilter construction of ‘drunken’ tailoring, interwoven with some thrilling and ambitious developments. The new Mona corset, inspired by the house’s original 17th-century-style corset, introduced reworked side panels that pulled in the waist even further, with softer boning making for a surprisingly comfortable fit. The label has always understood the art of body-contouring, but this season found more subversive ways to cinch, twist, and contain the figure. Ruched and knotted skirts in denim and knit clung and released in unexpected places, leaving the wearer to decide how to indulge.

The newly developed Chi Chi dress was a standout, a dramatic black taffeta gown with a sculptural bodice and arpeggiating skirt that brought together echoes of the archival Bird of Paradise silhouette and the irreverence of the Wake Up, Cave Girl! collection. Black provided a strong undercurrent amid the plaids in off-kilter colors, appearing across cady, crepe jersey, and eveningwear inspired by John Singer Sargent’s Madame X.

Even more fun was to be had with the prints. Ripe grapes, revisited from spring 1997, sprawled across a drunken shirt, with its deliberately distorted construction giving the impression of movement even when standing still. Magnolia blossoms appeared on voluminous skirts and outerwear, and François Boucher’s Mars and Venus Surprised by Vulcan brought the familiar strain of rococo to a casual jersey tee. There were micro-ginghams and broken pinstripes, too—a particularly Westwoodian exercise in articulating respectable conventions of English dressing with a bit of rebel spirit.

Tailoring was deviously compelling. Waistcoat jackets, a popular silhouette in the 2000s, came paired with the louche and architecturally draped dovetail trousers. Elsewhere, double-breasted jackets were paired with Pirate buckle back trousers, and relaxed Wreck jackets featured low-slung pockets. One killer jacket was cinched with belts low on the waist and high on the shoulders, a sartorial bondage-y badinage. Romance abounded with the traditional Nottinghamshire-made Cluny lace—a now rarified artistic practice—as well as with fine, fluid, sparkle-dashed knitwear and shorter iterations of the Sunday dress.

Accessories continued the archival conversation: the stacked Clomper sole made a return, as did the anatomical-toe as slingbacks. This year marks 15 years of the house’s Made in Kenya partnership with the Ethical Fashion Initiative and Nairobi-based social enterprise Artisan Fashion, which supports local craftsmanship and artisan communities. Among the accessories were exquisite hand-beaded and woven bags from Kenyan artisans.

The breadth of references is always a delight, but this they were especially dexterously rearranged and reimagined. The late, great Dame’s design vocabulary has all the more to give when stretched, sliced, and plied with some spirits.

Assemble them ahead of time and Turkey Day will be a breeze.

Maybe you’re trying to avoid a spicy mother-in-law and want to be sequestered in the kitchen all day on Thanksgiving (and more power to you). But for most people, the idea of having your house full of family and friends and being trapped standing at the stove the whole time isn’t ideal. That’s where these make-ahead sides that actually reheat well come into play. You see, no one wants clumped-up, days-old, dried-out mashed potatoes on Turkey Day, nor do they want to be hand-mashing while everyone laughs and drinks in the living room. But with recipes like these, a happy medium is possible.

Sweet potato casserole is a must at every Thanksgiving feast. When else is it socially acceptable to serve a vegetable topped with marshmallows? All The Healthy Things’ recipe includes special notes of orange and brown butter, as well as a few notes about different ways to prep the recipe ahead of time and save yourself some work the day-of.

Just go ahead and whip up All The Healthy Things’ slow cooker mashed potatoes in advance; they’ll microwave beautifully, or fit right back into the slow cooker on “keep warm.” This way you can focus all your efforts on the turkey gravy.

Make Simply LaKita’s corn pudding ahead of time so you can use that oven space for other things on Thanksgiving. Her reheating instructions say you can put the whole dish back into the oven at 300 degrees, or just put individual portions in the microwave.

Who knew you could make stuffing ahead of time? Just follow Damn Delicious’ recipe up to step five (which is when you combine all the ingredients in the baking dish). Instead of putting it straight into the oven, let it cool and pop it in the fridge overnight, then bake when you’re ready to serve the following day.

Just like the stuffing, you can make this green bean casserole from Damn Delicious nearly to completion — just hold off on popping the breadcrumbs and fried shallots on top until you’re ready to bake it. Then, they’ll come out crispy and perfect and right on time.

Princess Pinky Girl’s scalloped potato recipe is perfect for planning ahead. You can bake it completely and reheat individual portions, assemble but wait to bake, or even bake it halfway and finish it off on the day of Thanksgiving dinner.

Damn Delicious’ make-ahead yeast rolls are perfectly plump and fluffy, ideal for spreading with butter or mopping up gravy (mmm). Her post explains that you can refrigerate the rolls overnight so they double in size before baking, or simply bake, freeze, and reheat, so you can make them much farther in advance.

Fried apples may not be a staple on every Thanksgiving table, but in the South, they often make an appearance at holiday meals. Simply LaKita’s recipe is simple and easy, and includes instructions for reheating the apples in the microwave or a skillet.

This mac and cheese recipe from Averie Cooks is incredibly easy and will definitely reheat beautifully. It’s so creamy and saucy, it won’t dry out like a premade baked mac tends to.

Whether you choose to reheat them in the microwave or the oven, Simply LaKita promises her candied yams come out tender and flavorful every time. You can make them up to two days in advance, so this could be one of your Tuesday evening preps.

Which of these recipes will you try this year? Personally, I’m eyeing that green bean casserole pretty hard...

#1068. 20 Years Later, Amy Winehouse’s Personal Style Is More Important Than Ever

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Remembering the late singer’s style, as her Back to Black album celebrates its 20th anniversary.

With her swoop of jet-black beehive hair, sharp-as-a-knife cat-eye eyeliner, and skinny jeans, Amy Winehouse defied the neat, overly curated pop star style of the early aughts. Rather than looking like everyone else, she was a purely individualistic amalgamation of vintage and her own curated, undone personal style pulled from the aesthetic of her local Camden in London as well as her favorite style stars like her grandmother and Brigitte Bardot.

Twenty years after the release of her second and final album, Back to Black, it’s impossible to ignore the late singer’s influence on music and fashion. With a new exhibition in London showcasing her iconic looks, a massive tribute concert, Back to Black’s special 20th anniversary re-release, and 2008 fashion on the rise, Winehouse’s style is as important as ever.

She wore white tank tops, little denim shorts with black leather studded belts and tiny gingham dresses. Winehouse’s tattoos, visible black and red bra straps, and thick gold chains were as key to her look as her hair and makeup (the cat-eye eyeliner, which she proudly did herself). Beyond winning five Grammy Awards in one night, she defined a short but impactful era. “I don’t want to be the prettiest or the sexiest or whatever. I just want to look different and to look like me,” she told The Guardian in 2004. Early on, Amy Winehouse worked with the stylist Lou Wood for photo shoots. “One day she turned up in a massive pair of high heels and hot pants, carrying a guitar,” she recently told The Independent. “We were walking for five hours,” she says, laughing. “She wasn’t a manufactured artist. She had her own mind definitely, and her own style.” Winehouse also collaborated with her longtime friend Naomi Parry as a stylist.

Winehouse pulled from the retro style of 1950s and 1960s icons who wore pedal pushers, tight sweaters, and denim, and made it her own, piling on the accessories like belts from Topshop or jumbles of gold, real and fake, curated from a mix of places ranging from her grandmother to street markets. At the Brit Awards in 2007, she wore a bright lemon yellow minidress by Preen with a scarlet heart Moschino bag and cherry red hoop earrings with white polka dots. She often mixed prints like plaid with leopard print. She named Elizabeth Taylor, Miles Davis, and Busta Rhymes as her style icons. She even inspired Chanel’s pre-fall 2008 collection, with Karl Lagerfeld famously citing, “She’s beautiful and gifted. And I very much like her hairdo. Amy is the new Brigitte Bardot.”

But perhaps her most iconic style was her ballet flats, which she famously wore ’til they were blackened, torn, and shredded to the ground. #584. The surprisingly simple exercise that could lower your blood pressure

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

New research suggests that exercising just once or twice a week may be enough to maintain some of the blood pressure benefits gained from regular physical activity, at least over a short period. However, people who continue exercising three times weekly may experience larger and more sustained improvements.

The findings, published in BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine, suggest that even a single weekly session of isometric exercise could help people preserve improvements in blood pressure when they cannot commit to a more frequent workout schedule.

Isometric exercise involves tightening muscles while holding the body in a fixed position for several minutes. Common examples include wall squats, planks, and handgrip exercises.

Earlier research has found that isometric training can lower blood pressure more effectively than other types of exercise, sometimes producing reductions comparable to or greater than those achieved with certain medications. However, researchers have not fully understood how often these exercises need to be performed to maintain their cardiovascular benefits.

To investigate, researchers enrolled 100 adults whose average age was 37.4 years. Participants had systolic blood pressure ranging from normal to high-normal levels (120-140 mmHg) and did not regularly participate in structured exercise programs.

Under expert guidelines, systolic blood pressure between 130 and 139 mmHg falls into the "high-normal" category, which warrants specific lifestyle recommendations from healthcare professionals. None of the participants had been diagnosed with hypertension.

To qualify, volunteers had to be free of injuries and illnesses, have no current or previous use of blood pressure medication, and be nonsmokers. They also had to consume no more than 14 units of alcohol per week.

Participants were randomly assigned to five groups. One served as a control group, while the other four began the same four-week exercise program at home, focusing on isometric movements involving the lower body.

The training consisted of four wall squat holds per session, with two minutes of seated recovery between each hold. Participants completed this routine three times weekly during the first four weeks.

After this initial training period, the four exercise groups followed different schedules for another four weeks. One continued with three weekly sessions, another reduced its frequency to twice weekly, and a third performed the exercises only once a week. The fourth group stopped training altogether.

Researchers evaluated blood pressure, heart rate, and additional measures of cardiovascular function at the beginning of the experiment and again after four and eight weeks.

Even One Weekly Workout Helped Preserve Blood Pressure Benefits

After the first four weeks, all four exercise groups showed significant improvements compared with the control group. These included reductions in systolic blood pressure, which measures pressure when the heart contracts, and diastolic blood pressure, which measures pressure when the heart relaxes. Mean arterial pressure, a measure of average blood pressure throughout the cardiac cycle, also decreased significantly.

These initial results were consistent with earlier studies demonstrating the blood pressure benefits of isometric exercise. However, after participants switched to their assigned maintenance schedules, the groups no longer showed statistically significant differences in diastolic blood pressure.

By the end of the eight-week study, participants exercising just once or twice weekly had maintained significant reductions in systolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure compared with the control group. Neither schedule maintained a statistically significant improvement in diastolic blood pressure.

The greatest benefits appeared among participants who continued performing wall squats three times per week. Their systolic blood pressure fell by an average of 13.73 mmHg (-13.73 mmHg) from the beginning of the study.

By comparison, the twice-weekly group experienced an average reduction of 6.95 mmHg, while participants exercising once a week recorded a decrease of 6.56 mmHg.

Those who maintained the three-session schedule also preserved improvements in other cardiovascular measurements, including total peripheral resistance (an indication that blood vessels have narrowed) and heart rate variability.

Stopping Exercise Caused Blood Pressure Benefits to Fade

The results also revealed what happened when participants stopped exercising completely.

Those who discontinued wall squats after the initial four weeks experienced a reversal of their earlier blood pressure improvements. By the end of the study, their readings were no longer statistically different from those of the control group.

This finding suggests that maintaining some level of regular isometric exercise may be important for preserving the cardiovascular improvements gained during training.

However, the researchers emphasized that the maintenance phase lasted only four weeks. Additional studies following participants for longer periods will be needed to determine whether less frequent training can preserve these benefits over months or years.

"The study demonstrates that while lower frequency isometric exercise training may be sufficient to maintain blood pressure reductions over the short term, higher frequencies are required to preserve these benefits and the associated vascular and cardiac autonomic adaptations," the researchers write.

"The study offers evidence that lower frequency isometric exercise training can help maintain short-term blood pressure reductions, providing a pragmatic option for individuals with limited time or adherence challenges."

If I had a nickel for every time I wished I knew what my dog was thinking… well… let’s just say I’d be in a different tax bracket by now. It can be frustrating not to know what is going on in those furry heads of theirs, not just in regard to how they’re feeling physically, but on a behavioral level as well. One example that immediately comes to mind is my own dog’s peculiar habit of stealing my spot on the couch as soon as I leave it.

For a little bit of background, my dog’s name is Luna. She is an 8-year-old yellow lab who loves to snore, wag her tail in a helicopter motion, hoard tennis balls and never give them back, and, yes, steal other people’s seats on the furniture. It doesn’t matter if she’s sitting in a similar spot to us, or even right next to us; she will proceed to take your seat the moment you get up to get a snack or use the bathroom or simply grab a nearby blanket. It’s equal parts frustrating and amusing. What exactly is making her do this? As much as I wish my dog could answer that question for me directly (and believe me, I have wondered if she is secretly a human trapped in a dog’s body due to some sort of Beauty and the Beast-style enchantment, but that probably isn’t the case), the habit has inspired me to seek answers elsewhere.

Since I’m sure my dog isn’t the only canine committing furniture theft, Scary Mommy reached out to a few experts to get their insight on what your pup’s real motive may be, and the reason is actually pretty heartwarming.

Right up front: Take comfort in knowing your dog isn’t doing this to upset you. In fact, they love you so much that this is simply an attempt to feel closer to you. “First, your body has warmed the spot, so your dog gets a cozy temperature-controlled bed instantly,” celebrity dog trainer Chrissy Joy shares with Scary Mommy. “Second, your scent is concentrated there, and familiar human scent can be comforting and reassuring to dogs.”

Tracy Murray, dog expert and founder of Saving Fido Dog Walking, echoes this sentiment, explaining that it’s all coming from a place of love. "Warmth is a big part of it,” she admits. “In general, dogs are drawn to heat, and the spot you just left is prime. But it's not just about the warmth, because otherwise they'd be equally drawn to a patch of sun on the floor." Murray adds, "A dog uses their nose like we use our eyes, so the fact that the spot smells like someone they love is awesome. Taking over your place is like sleeping on a pile of your clothes."

And for those of you who worry your furry friend is doing this to establish themselves as the alpha over you, fear not. "The dominance idea has been pretty much debunked,” Murray continues. “With my background in psychology and years working with dogs, what I see is a dog who wants to be close to her person, not one trying to overtake or outrank them."

Just when you thought that little furball couldn’t get any sweeter!

On the other hand, it’s also possible that you yourself have accidentally encouraged this behavior without realizing it. “Another possibility is that stealing your spot has become a learned behavior,” licensed veterinarian Dr. Sarah-Jane Molier explains. “If you react gleefully or offer your dog attention when they jump in your spot, this is a form of ‘positive reinforcement.’ In other words, your dog has learned that stealing your spot gains them positive attention.”

Basically, the main takeaway here is that your dog is just as obsessed with you and you are with them! They want to be wherever you are (or were), and you can’t help but snuggle them for their thievery. So the next time you get up from your comfortable spot on the couch and come back to find it’s been taken over by a hairy, slobbery, wet-nosed seat-stealer, just remember that it’s their way of showing how much they love you.

And, you know what? I guess I can live with that. Luna has already stolen a big piece of my heart. I suppose she might as well have my spot on the couch too.

#1240. The Best Products and Techniques for a Flawless, Long-Lasting Silk Press

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Every Black woman can smell it before ever making a move or an appointment: a silk press. As children, it was the style du jour for special occasions—birthdays, Easter Sunday, picture day—marked by visible (minor forehead burns) and audible (sizzling from flat irons or an “ouch” or two) memories forever ingrained in our brains. But it’s 2026, and the greasy bump and curl presses of yesteryear have evolved with us, and with that, a plethora of products and new techniques have emerged to help you achieve the look without much hassle.

“A silk press is a salon straightening service most commonly associated with Black salons and professionals who work with afro-textured hair in its many forms. It is the modern evolution of the traditional press-and-curl service popular in the 1960s and 1970s,” Ladosha Wright, trichologist, salon owner, author, and RevAir’s resident expert, explains. Believe it or not, the silk press is not as simple as it seems. “Today, it is typically performed with an electric flat iron, often featuring ceramic plates enhanced with other minerals, along with a product such as oil, grease, serum, or a smoothing solution. The goal is to temporarily straighten the hair using controlled high heat, often between 430 and 470 degrees, depending on the hair’s texture, density, condition, and strength.”

Given the high heat and layering of products, a silk press truly begins with step one: the shampoo station—before heat styling—and requires maintenance long after you’ve left the chair. So what does a silk press look like these days? We consulted Wright and celebrity hairstylist Anthony Dickey of NYC’s Hair Rules salon to break down how to get the most flawless silk press.

A wash day session is imperative to any style’s final look. However, if the end goal requires loads of heat, what your hair actually needs is the exact opposite at first. “What people fail to realize is the objective for textured, kinky curly hair—particularly for women who have natural hair and don’t want to ruin their natural growth pattern and want flexibility and versatility to be able to wear their hair curly or straight—is moisture. Moisture, moisture, moisture—not protein, not all that bond building,” Dickey says. If your hair is already dry, a hydrating shampoo is the direction you need to go. If your hair is oilier, you could benefit from a clarifying shampoo that also hydrates. Moisturized hair will result in shinier, smoother strands, eliminating the need for layers upon layers of products.

Heat and technique are key here. “Starting on freshly washed, wet hair, I use my RevAir Wright Comb to thoroughly detangle and section the hair into four to eight parts based on its density, texture, and length. I then go in directly with the RevAir Reverse-Air Hair Dryer, using it from wet to dry to stretch and smooth each subsection,” Wright explains. From there, she uses low heat on setting two or three to create tension before “finishing with high heat in the final moments to maximize stretch and smoothness. Each completed subsection is combed through and clipped down to reduce tangling and minimize reversion.”

When it comes to layering products, heat protectant is a must, preferably one that’s lightweight so it doesn’t weigh down your hair (like heavy oils or creams). “I have to add enough moisture to their hair—leave-in conditioners and buffers, like heat protectant—and the hair will tell me how many passes to do, which is no more than one,” Dickey says.

Then, it’s time for straightening. Wright’s rule of thumb? Less heat doesn’t automatically equate to a healthier, smoother finish. When styling clients, she uses the RevAir Micro Edge Styler, which is “designed for detailed work and controlled straightening at the root.” She adds that “ultra-thin ceramic plates allow ultimate control without excessive heat exposure, making it especially effective for coily, curly, short, and textured hair while supporting a more precise, controlled silk press overall.”

Using smaller 1/8 partings and the Edge Styler supports smoothness and humidity resistance, making textured hair less likely to revert. “Using a heat-resistant rat-tail comb, I guide the hair downward with precision to smooth the cuticle and create a sleek, silken finish. The end result is shiny, polished hair with lasting smoothness and strong humidity resistance,” Wright says.

How your stylist styles your hair is just the beginning—the real work continues at home. Putting heat on already-dried-out hair is a surefire way to increase dryness and breakage. Your at-home routine—both washing and styling—should focus on retaining moisture, whether with lightweight oils and serums or wash-and-go styles between silk presses. Why? “Adding leave-in conditioners or oils to the hair before you stretch it out or before you do any kind of protective style allows the hair to be in better condition to keep it as far away from dry as possible,” Dickey continues. “Then the hair retains moisture, and then your silk press is more effective. You get more shine because shine comes from hydration.” Once your silk press has reached its end and it’s time for thorough cleansing, your curls should bounce back because of all the moisture it retained. “Whereas putting heat on a head that’s been dried out or stripped or over-clarified, it’s just not going to revert as easily.”

The push to make hair more heat-resistant has also sparked a new conversation in the hair community: Can hair really be “heat-trained”? Yes. Is it damaged? Sort of. Heat-training often means frequently heat-styling hair to help it withstand heat better. Dickey says that heat-trained hair is typically the result of strands “on the cusp of damage.” Over time, what you’ll notice with “heat-trained” hair is a looser curl pattern, whereas heat-damaged hair reveals structural changes to the strands, such as excessive breakage and frayed ends, leaving parts of the hair completely straight. However, with proper prep, technique, and maintenance, you can avoid completely damaging your strands.

And then I hope they ask what else might be going on.

#1397. The Tree of Heaven Is One of the Most Notorious Invasive Plants—Experts Say You Should Do THIS If You Have One on Your Property

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

This fast-growing tree is tougher to remove than it looks.

The tree of heaven (Ailanthus altissima) may be one of the most ironically named plants in the entire plant kingdom. Far from being a heavenly entity, its presence on your property could signal pests, ecological distress, and even damage to pipes, if it’s close enough to a building.

As with many other invasive plants, you’ll want to be careful with how you get rid of it—cutting this tree down will only make things worse. For that reason, we tapped two experts to explain exactly how to deal with a tree of heaven in your backyard and (hopefully) prevent it from returning.

The tree of heaven is native to China and is considered to be the most invasive tree in America. In fact, as Logan Foltz, certified arborist at Urban Arborists, says, the tree “outcompetes” many of our North American trees in their natural habitats and is “notoriously challenging” to control. It’s also extremely fast-growing (up to 15 feet in the first year) and very resilient.

“The tree of heaven’s ability to thrive in many different climates and locations is what led it to rapidly spread from the East Coast gardens of Philadelphia to the California coast,” explains horticulturist and organic farmer Nathan Heinrich.

The plant can be identified by its slim but sturdy trunk and branches, and its uniform, fern-like fronds that are distinguished by having “glandular teeth” at the base of each leaf. Female varieties of the plant will have red, yellow, or green seed clusters. Note that native, non-threatening black walnut trees and some varieties of sumac also possess similar physical characteristics to trees of heaven, so always be sure you know which plant you’re dealing with.

The tree of heaven has been labeled as invasive because of its ability to produce up to thousands of seeds every year. These seeds can germinate in almost any condition, from sidewalk cracks to gutters to your very own garden. The tree’s root system, which can spread out as much as 50 feet from the trunk, can even infiltrate and crack hardscaping and pipes as it seeks out sources of water. And it’ll choke out neighboring plants.

“If the root system is damaged or the trunk is cut, the tree will respond by sending up suckers from the roots that will grow into new full-size trees,” Foltz says.

They’re considered very nearly “unkillable” because, as Heinrich explains, the tree of heaven has “virtually no natural enemies, drought tolerance, rapid reproduction, and the ability to resurrect by resprouting after being cut down.”

If that weren’t bad enough, trees of heaven are also a host plant for the equally invasive and destructive spotted lanternfly, according to the U.S. Department of Agriculture. Lanternflies, another species without a natural predator in the U.S., suck the sap out of trees and vulnerable plants and can spread plantborne diseases.

Although they’re virtually indestructible, there are some ways experts approach these tough-to-kill trees. Calling this an “old-school approach,” Foltz believes that the best way to get rid of a tree of heaven is to cut the tree down to its base, drill the remaining stump full of holes, then fill those holes with rock salt.

“This method slowly desiccates the stump and connected root system, stopping the tree of heaven from resprouting,” Foltz says. “The drawback here is that the rock salt will make the soil inhospitable for new plantings.” So, this method may be better used for areas of your property where you’re not trying to grow anything.

Heinrich agrees with this method, saying that it’s best to do this in July and August. As an alternative to using rock salt, he recommends injecting multiple rounds of herbicide into the drilled holes and slits in the stump.

He adds that another way to potentially eradicate a tree of heaven is to exhaust its energy reserves by repeatedly cutting it back until its roots can no longer sustain growth.

Trees of heaven are a menacing presence, but is it necessary to get rid of them? Foltz says that it depends.

“These trees are not inherently evil—they are simply incredibly successful at what they do, which is to make more trees,” he notes. “While I do typically recommend removing the new trees as you see them sprout up, some mature specimens may be worth retaining. Consider the overall ecological impact of your tree, and assess whether a native planting would better serve the community.”

#1564. This Is Exactly What to Do With Your Peonies in October for Better Blooms Next Year

Blowsy pink peonies in full bloom are one of the joys of late spring. But if you want to encourage a greater flush of flowers next year, October is the time to give them a little extra attention.

This doesn't mean getting overexcited and pruning before your peonies are ready. According to Julian Palphramand, head of plants at British Garden Centres, timing is everything.

“The main job for peonies in October is to let the foliage finish its work before cutting it back. The leaves are still feeding the roots and helping to form next year’s buds, so cutting them too early can reduce the following season’s flowers,” he tells us.

“Once the foliage has turned yellow and started to die back, cut it down to about five centimeters (about two inches) above ground level. Clear away the cut leaves and any other debris from the bed and dispose of it, as this helps reduce the risk of fungal diseases overwintering on the plant.”

If you're keen to grow even more plants next year, or perhaps want to move your peonies to a new spot, late October is the time to act.

“If your peony is established and you want to move or divide it, late October into early November is the ideal window,” Palphramand says. “Lift the clump carefully, keeping as much root as possible and making sure each division has at least three to five ‘eyes’ or buds, then replant with the crown no more than five centimeters below the surface. Don't plant too deep, as this can reduce flowering.”

In terms of caring for these plants, Palphramand also has a few key tips. “Add a thin layer of compost or well‑rotted manure around the base, but avoid covering the crown or letting mulch sit against the stems. If the autumn is dry, water at the base rather than overhead, as wet leaves can encourage disease.”

Palphramand also recommends not feeding your peonies now. Instead, he suggests saving your fertilizer for early spring when the flowers will most benefit.

Keep reading to find seven things you can do with raked leaves besides throwing them away.

If your leaf layer is less than one inch thick, you can mow over leaves, breaking them up to add nutrients back to your lawn. Let leaves dry as much as possible, then go over them with a mower. A mulching mower, which keeps grass particles suspended to be recut into tiny pieces, is best. For a non-mulching mower, you can do a second pass at a right angle to chew the leaves up into small particles.

If you aren’t sure what kind of mower you have, search online for your model number. You can also change the regular blades to mulching blades on some mower models.

According to the National Wildlife Federation, you can rake leaves into garden beds to provide winter cover for beneficial insects, spiders, turtles, toads, and small mammals that spend the cold months under leaf litter. Moths and butterflies may also remain in their chrysalis stage in underbrush until they emerge next year. Keep these leaves whole for better coverage.

Use your mower’s bag attachment, or rake the leaves into a pile, then run them over with the mower a few times, as described above. You can then use the shredded leaves as mulch around shrubs and perennials, according to the University of Delaware. A two- to three-inch layer is sufficient to keep down weeds and reduce soil erosion. This mulch can also insulate roots and prevent plants, especially newly-planted shrubs and perennials, from heaving out of the ground with the freeze-thaw cycles that can occur during the winter.

Shredded leaves work best when tilled into garden beds, but you can use small amounts of whole leaves, if necessary. Till into beds in the fall, or spread over surfaces and work into the soil in early spring. You don’t need a tiller; an old-fashioned garden fork or garden spade works fine to turn over the soil a few times and bury the leaves.

You can invest in a composter or start a simple pile in a corner or behind a shed. You can even make an inexpensive composter by using chicken wire or hardware cloth. Then save a bag of leaves, called “browns,” to add to the composter throughout the next growing season to ensure the proper ratio of green and brown materials for efficient decomposition. If there’s an area in your garden that’s always muddy, or you want to convert a path from expensive mulch to free leaves, now’s the time to take advantage of the fall bounty. Again, shredded leaves are best. Whole leaves can be slippery, and they tend to mat down too easily.

A thick layer of leaves (either whole or shredded) can extend the growing season of many greens such as Swiss chard and perennial herbs like sage, parsley, and oregano. Simply mound leaves up three to five inches all around and on top of the plants. Keep the leaves in place by making small cages of chicken wire to contain them. You should be able to harvest for much of the winter, even in cold climates.

#4230. Coffee doesn’t have to keep you awake to disrupt your sleep

📅 Sep 22, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

For years, people have debated whether drinking coffee in the evening interferes with sleep. Some people can have caffeine late in the day and fall asleep easily, while others may remain awake for hours. But researchers are increasingly finding that simply asking whether coffee delays sleep may miss a more important issue: what caffeine does to the brain after sleep begins.

To investigate those effects, scientists are using EEG, or electroencephalography -- a method used to record the brain's electrical activity. EEG measurements can reveal much more than how long someone sleeps or how often they wake up. They can also provide clues about the biological quality and depth of that sleep.

"EEG allows us to see not only whether a person is sleeping, but also how the brain is sleeping. Classical sleep assessment assesses sleep duration and its stages, whereas quantitative EEG analysis reveals more subtle changes, such as reduced slow-wave activity, which is an important marker of sleep depth and its restorative character," explains Prof. Donata Kurpas from the Department of Nursing, Wroclaw Medical University.

Slow waves are a major feature of deep sleep -- the stage associated with physical recovery, replenishing energy reserves, and maintaining healthy brain function.

Caffeine does not always affect sleep in obvious ways. A person may still fall asleep at a normal time and remain asleep for what appears to be a full night, yet the quality of that rest may still be altered.

"Caffeine may shorten sleep or make it more difficult to fall asleep; however, even when sleep duration appears normal, it may reduce slow-wave activity and shift the EEG pattern toward a more 'wakeful' brain," says Prof. Kurpas.

In practical terms, someone might spend eight hours in bed without getting the same level of neurological recovery they would normally expect. Because the disruption may not cause obvious awakenings, it can easily go unnoticed.

"The subjective feeling of having slept well does not always correspond to what we observe in neurophysiological recordings. A person may fall asleep without major difficulty and not remember awakenings, while the brain may display fewer features of deep sleep," the expert adds.

Another major finding from caffeine research is that responses vary considerably from person to person. Genetics, metabolism, age, stress, and chronic fatigue can all influence how strongly caffeine affects sleep.

For some people, even coffee consumed early in the day may still have an impact by nighttime.

"It is not only about coffee consumed just before bedtime. For some people, the total amount of caffeine consumed during the day and whether the body has enough time to metabolize it before nightfall may also be important," Prof. Kurpas emphasizes.

That possibility may be especially relevant for people who rely on caffeine to improve focus, stamina, or performance, including athletes, people doing mentally demanding work, and anyone who regularly uses coffee or other caffeinated products to stay alert.

Caffeine can temporarily increase alertness and make fatigue feel less noticeable. However, experts caution that the boost may sometimes come at the expense of the body's ability to recover fully during sleep.

In that sense, caffeine can resemble "borrowing energy" from the body. If nighttime recovery becomes less effective, the person may feel more tired the following day and turn to even more caffeine for help.

"If caffeine helps a person function during the day while simultaneously worsening the quality of nighttime recovery, a vicious circle may develop: greater fatigue, greater need for stimulation, and poorer sleep," says Prof. Kurpas.

These findings are one reason sleep researchers are paying less attention to sleep duration alone. Increasingly, the focus is shifting toward what the brain is actually doing during the night and whether that sleep is biologically restorative.

"Caffeine is neither 'good' nor 'bad'. It is a biologically active substance whose effects depend on dose, time of day, age, lifestyle, sleep quality, stress burden, and individual sensitivity," the expert concludes.
