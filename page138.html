#3368. Inside the 24-Hour World Record Attempt That Raised Over $500K+ for a Local Food Bank

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ethan Hunger attempts to break the Guinness World Record for the most super-sprint triathlons in 24 hours. (Photo: Grayson Baum/Minus 7, Inc)

Beating the Guinness World Record for the most super-sprint triathlons completed in 24 hours was the goal of my friend Ethan Hunger’s annual community fundraising event. But the record itself was really only a small part of it. The attempt was just one piece of his much bigger, yearlong goal to raise $500,000 for our local food bank – a fundraiser that has been growing through various endurance challenges since 2021.

The idea for his annual “Hunger vs. Hunger” fundraiser was born out of the COVID-related growth of food insecurity in his local area of Bellingham, Washington. What started as a one-year challenge that raised $30,000 has now amassed a total of $1.2 million in donations, and this year’s $500,000 challenge would equate to roughly 7% of the food bank’s annual budget.

The concept and message Ethan shares through these extreme challenges is simple: “No matter how hard a day I create for myself, it will never parallel the struggles of food insecurity.”

For year six, Hunger wanted to go bigger than ever, and a triathlon world record would certainly tick that box while also adding more eyes to the cause. Initially, the record sounded relatively simple when the idea came to light: swim, bike, run, and transition continuously for 24 straight hours – non-stop through the entire night – and beat the previous record of 18 complete triathlons. (Technically, Guinness records the attempt in legs, so he needed 55 completed to surpass the previous record).

However, it quickly became more challenging throughout the buildup. The logistics of putting on a “race” like this at a public park while also adhering to more than 30 pages of Guinness rules began to set in – and that was before considering the largest fundraising goal Hunger had ever attempted.

Here’s how it all unfolded: the behind-the-scenes difficulties of a Guinness World Record, what 24 hours of triathlon actually felt like, how the fundraiser brought it all together, and why he might have stumbled on a revolutionary triathlon idea.

There is a massive difference between saying you broke a world record and having Guinness officially verify it. During the buildup, we found the endless list of verification rules a bit redundant and overly complicated. However, they create a rigorous, no-corner-cutting environment that helps maintain the integrity of the records.

Still, we wished it didn’t cost an estimated $14,000 to send an adjudicator to verify our attempt on-site – which would’ve made everything a lot easier.

Hunger applied for the records six months in advance and was approved. Not everyone gets this approval, as part of the application involves demonstrating that you not only have the experience and skills to attempt something like this, but also the connections and know-how to pull off the necessary verification.

Guinness has a lot of requirements before ratifying an attempt: video, timing, GPS files, logs, photos, and documentation for seemingly everything. So in a feat nothing short of a miracle, we scrambled together seven main cameras to record for 24 hours straight, three additional dedicated cameramen for an upcoming documentary, multiple photographers, timing data, and GPS tracking. But despite all that, one requirement quickly became much more problematic than the others: witnesses.

We discovered late that an individual witness could only cover a four-hour period before needing a four-hour break. We were also further limited in who could qualify, so close friends and family couldn’t simply fill every opening (as we had initially planned).

So, less than a week before a 24-hour event, on top of figuring out nutrition, bikes, course logistics, lights, cameras, batteries, timing, and how the hell any of us planned to stay awake for 24 hours, Ethan now needed to find people willing to stand beside a lake, jump in a safety car for the bike, and on mountain bikes for the run at ridiculous hours of the night and officially confirm that he was, in fact, doing the correct distances.

“If I had to pick one thing that made the event coordination the hardest, it was the witnesses and their four-hour time constraint,” Hunger says.

Somehow, through social media and word of mouth, people volunteered. They came out at midnight, early in the morning, and at all sorts of inconvenient hours. Some of them didn’t know Ethan at all – they simply heard what he was trying to do and wanted to help.

That should have been our first indication of what this event was eventually going to become. The world record might have been Ethan’s, but a lot of people were beginning to feel like they had a small piece of it.

By the end of his record, Hunger had amassed 5.47 miles of swimming, 136.7 miles of biking, and 34.2 miles of running. Yet those numbers are not what he will remember.

I asked him what stands out the most, and his answer goes back to minute one. It paints a picture of the day perfectly:

“The one that lingered for me the longest was likely the starting line. I had been frantically running around for 20 hours trying to get everything set up. I ran over to the start line with my goggles and swim cap, and I looked around with 60 seconds left and finally had the moment sink in of all the incredible people who were there to support me. I saw an older gentleman who I used to volunteer with at the food bank, who I knew had driven 30 minutes to town just to see me start. I saw friends who had flown from around the country. I saw people who currently work at the food bank who came to cheer, and I saw friends who currently rely on the food bank who came to cheer. It was incredibly moving.”

He might have been the one whose name would be attached to the attempt and record, but very quickly it stopped feeling like something he was doing alone. That came partly from the decision to use a real course. From a logistical standpoint, doing the challenge at a pool and track would have been infinitely easier to film, manage, illuminate, and monitor. Every time we left transition on the bike or run course, we disappeared from the main event area, which made everything more complicated.

“There were a ton of moving parts to this, and so many of them were out of my control once the clock started,” Hunger said. “I was able to do my best to get it set up ahead of time and put people in charge of tasks, but then I had to be willing to let it go and hope that it worked out.”

And it did work out: People were joining in on the swim, bike, and run legs throughout almost every reasonable hour (staying behind Ethan to avoid drafting). There was a DJ playing music, food, a campfire, friends sitting around talking, people donating, and strangers coming down simply because they’d heard something ridiculous was happening. Because the distances were short, people could also participate without committing to an enormous endurance challenge of their own. Someone could join one swim, somebody else could do four runs, and others could ride a bunch of the bike legs. Because every leg eventually returned to the same transition area, the group kept coming back together again.

That made the atmosphere completely different from a traditional long-distance triathlon where an athlete might disappear for several hours. If Ethan left on the bike, everyone knew he’d be back in around 25 minutes. If somebody wanted to join the next run, they had a good idea of when it would start.

It helped break the endless cycle of triathlon feeling like such an individual sport and started to feel more like a 24-hour triathlon festival.

We had no idea going in what to expect physically. We’d only practiced this format for about six hours, none of us were truly trained as ultra-triathletes, and sure, we’d completed an Ironman before, but we all safely assumed that somewhere around hour 12 or 15 our bodies would start falling apart.

Weirdly, however, that wasn’t really what happened. Because every individual leg was short and we weren’t moving particularly fast, you’d finish a swim, bike, and run and think, That actually wasn’t too bad. The problem was overcoming the sleep fatigue and the dread of doing specific legs again (and again) with so many hours still ahead.

“My lowest moment was around 3 or 3:30 in the morning,” Ethan says. “I had just finished a triathlon where I was cold the entire time, and I was incredibly sleepy, too. The wind was still causing chop on the lake, and we had headwinds on our bike ride out. I just remember dreading going back in the water because I was already cold, and I knew when I finished the swim, and the wind hit me on the beach, I’d instantly be cold again.”

There were many other smaller obstacles along the way, too. One of which stands out for its irony. Hunger had spent a lot of effort organizing with Outbound Lighting to help illuminate the docks and buoys, to make the night swim one of the coolest parts of the whole experience, yet, in a synchronous moment of shared disbelief, we realized we all had forgotten clear-lens goggles.

On the bike, going from wet to cold to warm meant clothes never quite dried, while chafing, tired hands, wind, darkness, and some sketchy late-night traffic (picture drunk drivers yelling out the window) slowly added up.

Despite that, the hardest part for me might have been the small act of leaving the transition itself: even in the middle of the night, there was a campfire, music, food, and people hanging out, and sitting down beside that fire and prolonging the transition was all too enticing.

Maybe the best way to picture the mental struggle of this challenge is to imagine doing 15 hours of triathlon, getting to 9 a.m. when the sun was finally up, feeling like you’ve survived the hard part – then realizing you still have nine hours left.

For something that had taken 24 hours, the finish felt surprisingly quick. There were lots of people, lots of emotion, and we were all incredibly proud of Ethan. After 22 complete super-sprint triathlons, four more than the previous record, it was finally over.

Then we quickly cleaned everything up and headed to a big party, where Ethan somehow managed to give a speech in front of a few hundred people gathered to celebrate what it was all actually for. The Foodbank.

The number is already over the $500,000 goal, and with still much of the year left, and a documentary release still to come, that should have him far surpassing his expectations.

If you want to donate, you can do so here.

He describes just how much it means to the organization that now helps feed almost 5,000 families weekly in our county:

“My fundraiser is big enough and has been around long enough that the food bank now builds this fundraiser into its annual projection for income. If you combine that with the 2025 federal budget cuts that cost the Bellingham Food Bank around $900,000 and food banks across the country well over $1 billion, the need is there now more than ever.”

There are times when attempting a world record can appear outwardly selfish, done for vanity, to gain attention, or to level up your status. Ethan even received some comments on social media saying so, despite the clear purpose behind the fundraiser. But when you were on-site, you realized there wasn’t much glory in slogging through an attempt like this, and if you dealt with the months of buildup and preparation, you would quickly realize it probably wasn’t worth doing purely for the token of a Guinness stamp.

But doing it for a purpose, and with friends and a community around, made it all worth it. The record gave everyone something ridiculous to rally around; it gave hope, gave purpose, and made every small act feel like it was contributing to something much bigger.

Speaking of bigger, Ethan has already planned next year’s challenge: a 100-mile run around our local track and stadium.

#3547. An Elite Marathoner Blamed a Prescription for Her Positive Doping Test. The Problem? The ‘Doctor’ Who Wrote It Wasn’t a Doctor.

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Ruti Aga has been on the podium at six World Marathon Majors and won the 2019 Tokyo Marathon.

Ruti Aga said that a positive drug test after winning a half marathon in China was due to a prescription for treatment of a knee injury. Upon investigation, anti-doping regulators determined the clinic listed wasn’t real, and the person who signed it was a family member who wasn’t a registered doctor.

The 32-year-old Ethiopian marathoner has now been banned for six years, the Athletics Integrity Unit announced Thursday, after testing positive for methylprednisolone following her win at the Meishan Renshou Half Marathon on March 15. The drug is prohibited in competition under World Anti-Doping Agency rules.

Aga, the winner of the 2019 Tokyo Marathon and one of the world’s elite women’s distance runners, said in an April interview with investigators that she’d been given the medication in Ethiopia to treat knee pain, and turned over a prescription in May.

However, AIU officials found the person who signed the prescription was a relative and not recognized as a physician by the country’s medical authority. The family member told investigators they had worked at the clinic and had written the prescription on old clinic stationery.

The AIU, with support from the Ethiopian Anti-Doping Authority, determined that the document was forged, resulting in a second anti-doping rule violation for tampering.

Aga had been provisionally suspended since September, when the allegations first became public. Her final sanction includes two years for the prohibited-substance violation and another four for tampering.

The ban is especially significant given Aga’s standing in the sport. She was ranked 11th in the world when she tested positive, and she had won the previous two editions of the Xiamen Marathon. Her resume also includes the 2019 Tokyo title, six top-three finishes at World Marathon Majors, and a 2:18:09 personal best.

“We have made significant progress against doping in marathon running over the past six to seven years, but much remains to be done,” AIU chief executive Brett Clothier said. “Investigations are vital to uncovering doping and exposing false explanations. Protecting clean competition takes resources, but this case sends a clear message: attempts to cheat will be exposed.”

#2205. Today’s Inflation Is Really an Energy Shock

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Americans are frustrated with today’s high cost of living, especially fuel prices. Although incomes rose faster than prices last year, it’s been the opposite since February. The only good news is that this inflation will subside when overseas conflicts are resolved, but there is likely more pain to come for consumers until then.

The significant reacceleration of inflation that began in March is due to disruptions in global energy markets. The wars with Iran and in the Ukraine have not only shut off a considerable amount of the world’s crude oil supply, but refined products too, like gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, and home heating oil.

Nations worldwide have coped with this reduction in supply by draining energy reserves, like the Strategic Petroleum Reserve in the U.S., and running refineries at maximum capacity—even delaying critical maintenance. These extraordinary measures blunted but didn’t eliminate the energy shock.

In recent months, Ukrainian strikes on Russian energy infrastructure have reduced Russia’s diesel export capacity by about 90%, effectively taking one of the world’s largest diesel producers out of the market. That additional supply shock has caused diesel prices in particular to skyrocket.

>>> If It’s Good Enough for Wartime, It’s Good Enough for Peacetime 

It’s clear that such energy shocks will affect energy prices, but people often underestimate how much energy affects the price of everything we do and everything we buy. Thus, the energy shock is slowly transforming into an “everything shock,” and it’s showing up in the data.

When someone grabs an item off a store shelf, whether it’s a gallon of milk or a box of screws, that item arrived there on a truck fueled by diesel—and often a train fueled by diesel too. Higher transportation costs put upward pressure on prices everywhere.

Many products are also energy-intensive to produce, like aluminum, where about one-third of its cost of production is electricity. Increasing that input cost makes thousands of items, from soda cans to airplanes, more expensive.

Moreover, energy is a direct input for countless products, from fertilizer to cosmetics to plastics—all synthesized from oil and natural gas. More expensive fertilizer drives up food prices, and plastics are in just about everything today. As elevated energy prices migrate through supply chains, prices everywhere are starting to rise, even if only mildly.

This is a stark contrast to supply-chain data back in January and February, before the Iran war, which indicated prices for many consumer staples would be falling by this time, not rising. That’s still true for some items like certain pharmaceuticals and dairy products, but higher energy prices have simply overpowered any deflationary pressure on most consumer prices this year.

>>> The Red-Blue Energy Divide: How Overregulation Inflates Prices 

Import price data also confirm that today’s inflation is really an energy shock. America has weathered the oil shortage better than most countries because it’s the world’s largest energy producer, pumping a record amount of oil today. Oil prices elsewhere have been higher, which in turn has increased non-energy prices on products and services faster.

Fuel import prices in September rose 4.8% from August and a whopping 34.5% from a year prior. But non-fuel import prices are also rising fast, up 6% over the last 12 months, the fastest annual increase in almost four and a half years. Conversely, non-fuel prices in the U.S. rose at just one-tenth that pace over the same period.

Despite America’s relatively insulated position, the high energy prices since February—especially the high diesel prices more recently—are already spilling into every corner of the economy. Months of elevated inflation are already baked into the cake, so even if the wars in Iran and the Ukraine end immediately, the effects will continue for a while.

Despite that negative short-term outlook, there is some reason for optimism. Since the inflation this year stems from temporary conflicts overseas and not something wrong with the American economy’s fundamentals, energy prices should start coming down once those conflicts end and oil markets normalize.

Until then, however, people will continue to complain about the economy, with negative responses in survey data like consumer sentiment, consumer expectations, and consumer confidence. That’ll remain true even as—or perhaps especially as—the energy shock looks less and less like an energy shock.

KFC is officially breaking up with Pepsi, and after nearly 40 years together, I think we can call this a pretty significant split.

The fried chicken chain announced that it’s switching from PepsiCo to Coca-Cola products at its U.S. restaurants, ending a beverage partnership that dates all the way back to 1986. Starting in January 2027, KFC will begin replacing its current drink lineup with Coca-Cola beverages, with the transition expected to be complete nationwide by spring 2027.

That means customers can expect to see familiar PepsiCo drinks like Pepsi, Mountain Dew, and Starry replaced with Coca-Cola favorites like Coke, Sprite, and Fanta. So if you’ve spent years washing down your KFC chicken with a Pepsi, you might want to enjoy a few more while you still can.

You may be surprised to learn that PepsiCo purchased KFC in 1986, making Pepsi the chain’s exclusive beverage partner. Even after PepsiCo sold off its restaurant business in 1997, eventually becoming part of Yum! Brands, the soda relationship continued.

Now, KFC is ready for something different.

“I’m incredibly excited to bring KFC and Coca-Cola together. These are two originals, with deep consumer love,” Catherine Tan-Gillespie, president of KFC U.S., said in a statement.

The change is part of KFC’s larger comeback strategy, which has included bringing back fan favorites like potato wedges and popcorn chicken, introducing new value offerings, and refreshing its menu and marketing.

There is some good news for Pepsi loyalists, though. The switch only applies to KFC’s U.S. restaurants, and PepsiCo is expected to remain a beverage partner in international markets.

Personally, I’m more interested in seeing what KFC does with its expanded drink possibilities. Fried chicken and an ice-cold Coke sound pretty good already, but if this partnership leads to some interesting new beverages, I’m listening.

#1561. Inside the Long Lines and Lotteries Keeping Runners From Their Dream Trail Races

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As trail running booms, bucket list races are tougher for athletes to enter. Here’s why.

Welcome to the Wild World of Trail Running! We're diving deep on the races, training tips, and gear you need to know to get outside off paved roads to run a whole new way. Click here for more stories about the trail explosion.

“I’M THE LAUGHINGSTOCK of the Western States.”

That’s Jeff Allen. For ten years, the South Carolina running coach has been applying for a spot in the Western States Endurance Run, one of the US’s flagship ultramarathon trail races. And for ten years, Allen has come up short in the race’s lottery system, meaning he’ll have to wait another year to run the 100-mile course through the Granite Chief Wilderness, west of Lake Tahoe. To be eligible for this year’s lottery, he’s had to run another qualifying race of 100 kilometers or more to have his name pulled in the 2027 edition.

“Every year, I’m like, ‘this is my year’ …well, as the lottery gods may have it, I did not get selected again,” he says. The yearly experience has soured him on what was once his dream race. “I just go through the motions now because I have so many years vested into it. Now, hopefully the day I get in, maybe my sentiments change… but I am a shell of the runner I used to be so many years ago.”

Allen may be one of the runners who’s waited the longest, but he’s not alone in yearning to get into one of America’s premier ultras. It’s a problem facing tens of thousands of American endurance enthusiasts—literally. In the 2026 lottery for the Western States, more than 11,000 runners were vying for 257 spots in the race. And it’s not just this race where runners are being shut out.

“Ultra running is the new marathon,” says Fred Abramowitz, the founder and co-director of Run, Rabbit, Run, an ultra race in Steamboat Springs, Colorado. Anybody who’s “got a couple of screws loose,” he says, can train and learn the tactics to enter and finish one of these extreme events.

As running has generally boomed, even more people are showing up with screws loose. In 2016, the first year Allen vied for a spot in the Western States, 58,425 runners entered ultras in the U.S., according to statistics from Ultra Running magazine. In 2025, that number had ballooned to more than 100,000. And as the popularity of the sport grows, the chances of getting into its most sought-after races via the lottery are becoming slimmer and slimmer.

Some runners who have missed out on races spin theories about lottery conspiracies, spots being given to sponsor influencers, and other skullduggery through which spots are being stolen. But the truth is, runners hoping to check big races off their bucket lists face a less nefarious but even more formidable foe: Math.

IN 2006, WHEN the Western States featured prominently in Dean Karnazes’ book, Ultramarathon Man, the 100-mile mountain race already had a lottery. But the odds were much closer to a coin flip. Only 841 runners applied to vie for the legendary prize of a silver belt buckle emblazoned with a cougar that year, so about one-third got a spot, even if they were first-time entrants into the lottery.

Twenty years later, a first-time qualifier’s chances of getting picked have dropped to 0.4 percent. The race still starts with the same number of runners—369. Other top ultras are the same way. The Hard Rock 100, held in Silverton, Colorado, has 147 spots for runners at its start line. The Leadville 100 Trail Run starts around 700 runners per year (710 in 2025, 657 in 2026).

With so much more interest in ultras recently, why don’t these races just let more people enter?

The answer is simple, and difficult to hear for desperate endurance junkies: They legally can’t.

“The Western States is unique among races in the world in that we go through a federally designated wilderness area, the Granite Chief Wilderness,” says Craig Thornley, the event’s race director. When the California Wilderness Act of 1984 was passed, the Western States, which was already 10 years old, had to lobby for the race to be able to continue along this protected trail. “We got permission to continue to use the trail so long as we didn’t have more starters than we did prior to the creation of the Wilderness. And it just so happened in 1984, we had 369 [starters].”

At the Hard Rock 100, run director Dale Garland and his team need nine different permits from different authorities to have their annual race.

“That means the town of Telluride has a way they want us to conduct business, and the U.S. Forest Service says we can only have so many people,” he says. Factoring in event staff, medical support, and aid station volunteers and pacers for runners, there’s only room for 147 entrants each year.

These races are thus legally barred from making their races bigger, even if disappointed runners don’t always realize it. And these jilted athletes compare the limited start spots to what they see in videos from less restrictive events like UTMB, the Ultra-Trail du Mont-Blanc, where more than 10,000 runners toe the line of the HOKA-sponsored race through the Alps that starts and finishes in France.

“[Some runners] can bring an unrealistic set of expectations to Hard Rock… the management of the land isn’t as big of a deal as it is here,” Garland says. “To be honest, I’m not sure I’d want 2,000 people running through the San Juan Mountains… I have to share the land with four-wheel drive people and side-by-side people and mountain bikers, hikers, that kind of thing. We all have to work together. And if we don’t all do our part to manage it, it’s going to get trashed.”

These space-limited American races, then, have to use more exclusive lotteries and qualifying races and standards to fill their limited spots than UTMB’s events (which are still limited by qualifying rules, but have more spaces). The Leadville 100 has a plain-old lottery. Sign up, and your name goes into a hat. You either get a spot, or you don’t. Apply for a few years, and unless you’re unlucky, you’ll get in.

The Western States provides a list of hundreds of qualifying races that are 100 kilometers or more. Finish one of these races with a qualifying time, and you’re awarded with a ticket in the Western States lottery. If you’re not selected in the first year (and with a 0.4 percent chance, it’s unlikely you will be), and you’ll get two more tickets for running a qualifying time in your second year. The number of tickets goes up by 2^(n-1) for each year a runner qualifies and applies. In a runner’s third year, they’ve got 4 tickets. After waiting 9 years, like Jeff Allen had been at last year’s lottery, they’ve got 256.

For the Hard Rock 100, the bottleneck for runners starts at qualifying. There are fewer races runners can choose from to qualify for what Garland and the other race officials call the “post-graduate” difficulty of Hard Rock, meaning fewer entrants are in the lottery pool.

Those who do enter that pool are put into a Beautiful Mind-level lottery system that’s actually four lotteries. There’s one lottery for men who have never finished the race, one for men who have finished it before, and one of each of the same for women. The split between men and women is determined by the ratio of applicants, so if there are 80 percent male entrants, 80 percent of the spots will be for men. The split between “never finished” and those who have finished before is 83 total starters who have never finished (split between men and women), and 64 who have finished before. Both groups get more entries for each year they don’t get in.

This finished/never finished split, though, means people who have run Hard Rock before are more likely to get in again. In the 2025 Hard Rock lottery, 265 previous finishers applied, and 64 runners were selected, a 24 percent chance of getting in. People who hadn’t finished before had just a 2.9 percent chance of being selected.

“It’s one of those really weird things about the sport that it’s supposed to be so egalitarian, and everybody’s the same … except when it comes to this, then it’s really different,” says Scott Kummer, an avid ultra runner, race organizer, and host of the 10 Junk Miles podcast. “All these people, it’s their dream to do this race someday. And then every year I’ll see a post by Western States or Hard Rock that says, ‘we’re welcoming Jim Smith to his 29th Hard Rock.’ And I’m just like, ‘that’s great—but what about the other people?’”

Allen, who has been applying for the Hard Rock 100 since 2018 in addition to his 10 years of trying to win a spot in the Western States, says he always knew the Silverton race would be tough to get into. He’s frustrated by the “finished/never finished” disadvantage, but is more frustrated by his white whale, the Western States. In most years, runners who have waited as long as Allen would be added to the top of the race’s wait list, but in this year’s edition, Thornley says, there were too many runners who’d been waiting for years for this to be feasible.

The number of long waiters for the race may continue to grow. While the rules used to stipulate that runners needed to qualify each year to stay in the lottery, prospective runners can now skip a year without losing their tickets. That means they can’t be selected in a year when they didn’t qualify, but if they have 32 tickets this year, they’ll go to 64 when they qualify again.

Allen, like other Western States waiters, feels rankled by this, and by the race’s “automatic” slots. This group of roughly 100 runners is pulled from a bunch of different groups—those who finished in the top 10 of the previous year’s race for men and women, people who win a series of “Golden Ticket” races, but also board members and representatives of a few corporate sponsors. (Contrary to the beliefs of the conspiracy-minded waiters, the corporate sponsor spots have actually gone down slightly over the past 10 years, from a peak of 22 in 2016 to 17 last year.)

“I don’t know how many special consideration spots were given out before I got here,” Thornley says, “but I think it was more common.” For what it’s worth, the Western States is transparent about the number of slots taken up by these automatics, and publishes a breakdown from each year on their website.

GARRETT BURTON HAS dreamed of running Leadville for years. The former Navy SEAL drives from his Indiana home to Colorado each year, where he’s slowly been ticking off climbs of all of the state’s fifty-three 14,000-foot peaks, also called “fourteeners.” His first fourteener was Mt. Elbert, found in Leadville, Colorado. So the 100-mile trail race there has an almost magical draw for the 33-year old. But he hasn’t had a magic touch in the lottery. For five years running, he’s been shut out.

“I’ve been unlucky,” he says. “I haven’t really felt frustrated until this year … I’ve tried to get into it for half a decade now, and I have no more information than when I first started. It’s to the point where it’s like, I don’t necessarily know if I want to run the race anymore.”

Burton’s experience underlines one issue with “just run another race.” The premier ultras are bucket list events for many runners, given their long tradition and reputation—not unlike the Boston Marathon for road running, but even more exclusive. Each year, they not only have to sign up for some of these races, but run a qualifier. During his 10-year wait for the Western States, Jeff Allen says he’s run 100-kilometer races with a torn calf, among other injuries, to continue to pile up chances at his dream race. For this year’s qualifying, the now 40-year old Allen ran an ultra in Arkansas just months after being immobilized with a stress fracture in his pelvis. “I’m doing harm to my body,” he says, “because I’ve put this race on a pedestal.”

When Burton missed out on Leadville last year, he did choose another race for the year: Run, Rabbit, Run. As of 2027, the Steamboat Springs race will boast the largest prize of any American ultra—$100,000 each to the male and female winners. Burton says the views and experience were stellar.

But while they’ve benefited from people like Burton using it as an “alternate” run, the race directors of Run, Rabbit, Run think “just run another race” isn’t always great advice. Unlike road marathons, where you’re never far from other people or a hospital, ultras are held in real wilderness, where bad situations can quickly become dangerous. At the Moab 240 held in October of this year, 70 runners needed to be evacuated when they were stranded by extreme rain. At a race with less experienced organizers and resources, those situations can become dire, Abramowitz says.

“I would be reluctant to enter a race that does not have an established history of putting on a good event, of being safe,” he says. “I would never enter a race that’s new. I’ve done it before. I’ve regretted it.”

THE SOLUTION TO runners being shut out of these races could start with veteran ultra runners, says Kummer.

“I always use the analogy of there’s a restaurant in town and nobody can ever get in, and there’s a big line of people waiting outside every day getting turned down,” he says. “If you walk out of there for the fourth time this week, don’t you feel like an asshole?”

Having veterans in the mix is part of what keeps the community, we’re-all-in-this camaraderie of ultras, says Garland—but he understands this frustration. At the Western States, there’s an incentive that keeps previous finishers entering the lottery. After finishing 10 (or 20) editions of the race, runners get a special belt buckle that’s different from the single finish award.

“I wish my predecessors hadn’t put that 10-year buckle out there as a goal,” Thornley says. He doesn’t fault people who are near that goal from achieving it, as they spent many years chasing the special prize. “I do wish, at times, that once you hit 10, you stop.”

Runners may also choose to run different races. More events are becoming established with different skill sets and experiences in mind: Longer races, like the two-day Cocodona 250, the Big Foot 200, and the Arizona Monster 300, have begun to capture the imaginations of the endurance-inclined. But even these are filling up quickly and becoming lottery-limited, with Cocodona instituting its first lottery for next year’s edition.

The solution may come down to race directors helping runners feel safer running races that aren’t as well-established. Abramowitz and his fellow Run, Rabbit, Run race director, Paul Sachs, think ultra running could benefit from an organizing body that sets standards.

“Some kind of organization that tells runners, ‘hey, this one follows safety protocols. This one is well-organized,’” Abramowitz says. The Run, Rabbit, Run directors say they’re in the midst of conversations with other race directors about just such a body. Garland mentioned similar discussions.

Until such systems, or new bucket list races, are established, runners will have to continue to wait. With 512 tickets for this year’s Western States’ lottery, Allen’s wait, for one, is likely to end at December’s draw. In last year’s lottery, that number would have given him an 88.8 percent chance of being selected. But he’s not so sure of the outcome.

“I don’t know until I know,” he says. “My love for Western States has dissipated over the years … but I hope that when I do eventually get in and get to run it, maybe some of my jadedness turns around. I’ll be like, ‘hey, this is my story. This is what it is. This is how it happened, and I’m OK with it.’”

#1512. Not All Runners Feel Welcome on the Trails. These Groups Are Getting Everyone Outside.

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Runners are moving to the trails—and making space for people from all backgrounds to be included.

Welcome to the Wild World of Trail Running! We're diving deep on the races, training tips, and gear you need to know to get outside off paved roads to run a whole new way. Click here for more stories about the trail explosion.

ANTOINE HAUGABOOK, 46, has been immersed in the road running community for more than a decade. A Nashville-based account manager in information technology, Haugabook has served as the co-captain of the city's local chapter of Black Men Run since its inception in 2013 and has also formed relationships with other clubs throughout the region. But when Haugabook and his wife, Yolanda "Yo" Lopez Haugabook, ventured off the roads they were familiar with and into the local trail running scene, they felt like they didn't belong.

"We immediately noticed we were the only people of color and didn't feel like we really related to anyone else out on the trails," Lopez Haugabook, 41, says. "On our third Saturday trail run, one of the other runners finally acknowledged us, saying 'You're back,’ and followed it up with '...Y'all ain't had enough yet?'"

The couple had hit the trails to try something different. After feeling burned out with road running, Antoine applied to and was accepted to the Running Industry Diversity Coalition's 2025 Freedom to Run: Back Outside program, which was designed to get runners of diverse backgrounds to explore the trails. The RIDC program was launched alongside a downloadable resource titled "Opening the Path to Trail Running: A Toolkit for Runners, Retailers, and Communities," which provides BIPOC runners with useful information and resources that act as a welcoming and approachable introduction to trail running. The Haugabooks, kitted out with the gear and coaching provided by RIDC, ventured out on the trails, only to be met with that disconcerting reception.

These types of programs are unfortunately necessary because it’s not an uncommon experience for many people of color when they try to get outside. Nearly 69 percent of outdoor participants are white, according to The Outdoor Industry Association’s 2024 report. Not feeling a sense of belonging is one of the biggest factors that can deter minority runners from exploring trails—but it’s not the only one. Runners who spoke to MH also shared concerns about safety, discouraging finish time cutoffs, and financial constraints, such as high entry fees.

That first experience felt very personal to the Haugabooks. Having long been acquainted with the message that running was for everyone, that microaggression messaged that not everyone was willing to include people who looked like them in the community and reinforced stereotypes that people of color fear and don't enjoy the outdoors.

Those early moments were uncomfortable—but the pair weren’t detered from staying on trail. That just served as fuel to their fire and led them to start Sin Miedo Trail Runners, a group for people of color that currently has around 60 members—most of whom are at the beginner level and experiencing their first introduction to trail running and racing.

"'Sin miedo' literally translates to 'without fear,' but our message is intended more as 'live fearlessly,' because fear tends to take over a lot of BIPOC experiences, whether it's the fear of being persecuted or excluded, and not just in running," Lopez Haugabook says. "'Sin miedo' was a catalyst saying we're going to show up and we will not be afraid to take up space, we will not be afraid to experience the outdoors, even though, culturally, we've been told to be afraid of being in the woods by ourselves, and rightfully so."

Witnessing this lack of representation firsthand prompted Detroit-area content creator Tommie Bailey, a.k.a. "Tommie Runz," 42, to create his "Black Skin Looks Good on Trail" campaign. Bailey has a long list of trail bonafides: he took on the 340 mile Speed Project course from Los Angeles to Las Vegas solo in 2025, he ran this year's Western States Endurance Run 100-miler, and has been a presence at the prestigious Ultra Trail du Mont Blanc (UTMB) series in France over the last two years with major brands.

"I've heard from so many Black and Brown runners over the years saying that they've been inspired to get out on the trails after seeing the content I've put out over the years, and I feel blessed that I've always felt like I've had the freedom to talk about these topics even when collaborating with brands at these events," Bailey says. "We aren't being highlighted to the same degree because there just aren't a lot of Black Americans at the top of roads or trails because it's still new to a lot of us, but I think we need to have the conversations about why Black and Brown people don't feel like they're being represented at these races."

THE CONCEPT OF creating their own spaces isn't new for many minority community leaders, especially as the running industry and corporate America as a whole have shifted away from commitments made during the 2020 social justice reckoning that came after the tragic murders of Ahmaud Arbery and George Floyd. Large brands, including Google, Meta, and Target, have cut DEI programs and positions meant to support employees from marginalized groups, and running community leaders have had to work even harder to highlight instances of racial injustice and accessibility and remind the industry and community that problems weren't solved in 2020.

In 2025, Alison Mariella Désir, co-founder of the RIDC and author of Running While Black: Finding Freedom in a Sport That Wasn't Built for Us, created the We Out Here Trail Festival, which offers race distances from 1.9 miles to two hours, in her home base of Seattle, along with her friend and local running coach and personal trainer Aaliyah Earvin.

"We chose these distance options because most trail races tend to be focused on longer/ultra distances and have discouraging finish time cutoffs that may make newer trail runners hesitant to sign up," Désir, 41, says. "We also wanted distances that people could not compare to road distances so that there were no expectations around what it should feel like, and so they could fully embrace trail running apart from their experiences in road running."

Having grown tired of calling out prominent trail racing leadership for their lack of concrete efforts to diversify their fields and create welcoming communities, she was inspired to create her own event where minority runners could show up as their true selves.

"We Out Here Trail Fest centers the experience of people of color, but white people are there too; we just created it with us in mind," Désir says. "If you can welcome people who are new to the trail and give them this incredible experience, and then they go off to other spaces, but they have this fundamental understanding that they belong, which is a huge service that we can do for the community. I want people to leave with a feeling that trail running is joyful and that it's for them."

Sabrina Pace-Humphreys, 48, a Cotswolds, U.K.-based ultrarunner, similarly used her experiences to create her own community. In 2019, while training for a race in the Italian Alps, Pace-Humphreys was traversing a narrow ridge on a mountain range and had a near-fatal experience when she slipped off the path and was hanging on to the side of the mountain. She recalls being ignored by five men who ran past her as she screamed for help.

"It was kind of a sliding doors moment because I had asked for help and hadn't been given it, and I very much felt as though had I been blonde-haired, blue-eyed, and white, I would have got help a lot quicker," she says. "This incident made me realize that I had to make a decision, which was to either leave trail running because I cannot continue to be in spaces where I feel physically unsafe and uncared for and unseen, or I start something of my own."

This led Pace-Humphreys to create Black Trail Runners, a global community and nonprofit that uses trail running to connect BIPOC runners and their allies with the outdoors. The organization hosts events like Black to the Trails, which organizers call Europe's most ethnically diverse trail running event, with 1K, 5K, and 10K races.

"At the time, there were a lot of running group crews in the U.K., but none that specialized in trail, and none that centered the Black experience in trail running," she says. "With Black Trail Runners, we qualify trail leaders and trail coaches while also addressing barriers to access, skills and representation, both out on the trails and through the media."

DÉSIR ALSO HOSTS the Earth Movers podcast with professional ultramarathoner and 2021 USATF 100-mile national champion Stefanie Flippin and Verna NezBegay Volker, a fellow activist and founder of Native Women Run, a nonprofit dedicated to representing and empowering Native women across the U.S. and Canada. Now in its third season, the three women created the podcast with the goal to amplify the stories and people of color on the trails and beyond, many of whom have not had the opportunity to do so in a corner of the sport that frequently uplifts the same familiar, homogenous voices.

"Trail running is very white-forward right now and it continues to center the experiences of those who are benefiting the most," Flippin, 37, says. "For example, there's the current narrative that women are showing up so much more in trail running, which is incredible, but it's still equally important to highlight the experiences of those who are less represented and things like their safety and other barriers to access that they've had to consider to also exist in this space."

For Flippin, who is based outside of Denver, such experiences are personal. She was on the receiving end of anti-Asian verbal harassment at the height of the Covid-19 pandemic.

"Pre-pandemic, safety was definitely a consideration for me out on the trails, but it wasn't at the forefront of my mind like oh this could be a potentially dangerous place for us to be living in or for me," Flippin says. "During that time, I was harassed, stared at and called racial slurs out on the trails, and now I just don't have the same sense of empowerment and autonomy to run by myself on the trails like I used to, especially now that I live in a much more rural area."

FOR MANY TRAIL and ultramarathon aficionados, races like Western States and UTMB are viewed as the holy grail in this corner of the space. But they're also some of the least accessible, as they are notoriously difficult to gain entry. Entry fees are over $400 and $500, respectively, and participants also have to account for significant travel costs, as well as shoes, gear, and other training necessities.

"UTMB week is pitched as like the 'Super Bowl of trail running, or the who's who of trail running,' where amateur athletes can run the same trails as elite athletes. But in order to do that, you have to have a good disposable income, so we need to look at the fallacy around 'if you want to be here, you can,' when it's really more like 'if you look a certain way and if you have a certain means of financial support, you could be here,'" Pace-Humphreys says. "Brands and race directors need to put their money where their mouths are, because what we're seeing geopolitically across the board at the moment is a massive pullback in terms of budgets for any kind of DEI initiatives."

Désir emphasizes the need for tracking racial demographic data at large races, even though being confronted with and potentially publicizing low numbers is likely to make leadership uncomfortable. Bailey echoes that sentiment, noting that trail running brands and community leaders need to be cognizant about who they choose to partner with if they care about making meaningful changes.

Although the trail running community still has huge strides to make to become less homogeneous, there are small signs of progress that will hopefully encourage more runners of color to enter the space. This year, Ben Dhiman, who is Indian-American, won the prestigious 108-mile UTMB race, becoming the first runner to break 19 hours and just the second American man to win. Earlier this year, Jennifer Lichter—who was born in Bogotá, Colombia, and lived there until she was nine years old—set a new women’s course record at the Black Canyon 100K, becoming the first woman to finish in under eight hours. Lichter also broke the women’s course record at Western States, which was also her first 100-mile race.

Outside the pro ranks, the programs and creator-led efforts leading the way are also making their mark to bring more everyday people outside.

"Our experience with the RIDC program showed that the doors of the trails are open for you to come as you are—that you already belong," Haugabook says. "It's allowed us to take up space unapologetically—space that's rightfully already ours, and not the other way around."

After all, expanding the field is the only way true growth happens. Bailey has seen that in the mainstream running world—and he knows that’s the way forward on the trails, too.

"Road running is not booming because it stayed white and pushed white and just kept going the same—more voices from different communities and different countries now affect what we all look like and feel like, and how we dress, and the shoes we're wearing," Bailey says. "All of that has come from more voices pouring into it, whether that's in run clubs or who brands decide will wear their clothes and send to events. Trail leadership needs to also remember that is how things are grown and move forward, and if you want to evolve, and be more welcoming in general, then you have to start investing in inviting people that maybe wouldn't otherwise be there."

#1551. I Was Stuck in a Running Rut. Taking on a Trail Race Helped Rewire My Brain.

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I got over my burnout when I stepped away from the road.

Welcome to the Wild World of Trail Running! We're diving deep on the races, training tips, and gear you need to know to get outside off paved roads to run a whole new way. Click here for more stories about the trail explosion.

I HAD BEEN following the path for just over two hours when I finally thought to look at my watch to check my pace. I was making my way through the Alps during the MCC, a 40-kilometer race that’s part of the legendary Ultra Trail du Mont Blanc (UTMB) series, a weeklong, multi-race event many consider to be the Super Bowl of the trail running world.

Before this, I had never run a trail race. My competitive experience was on the roads or the track, and that mentality bleeds into my workouts in the way I typically prioritize speed over everything. When I’m running on the street, I check my watch every few minutes (if not on the minute) to monitor my pace, obsessively keeping up with the plan I had for my workout or race goal. I do my best not to stop (or even slow down for traffic), even though there’s no one forcing me to keep going. When I’m running more than 10 miles, I carry water with me and take fuel on the run so I don’t have to pause my watch. I constantly compare myself to my best race performance, and I don’t want to fall behind.

But on the paths from Martigny-Combe to Chamonix, during the toughest running contest of my life, I wasn’t stressed by the numbers. I was too busy keeping my eyes on the rocks and roots on the ground ahead of me and the sweeping mountainscape on the horizon. I leaned into my trekking poles to make my way up the steep paths, my heart pounding and breath ragged as if I were moving three times as fast. This felt completely different than my years of experience on the road. Trail running helped to calm my anxious runner brain—and it could do the same for yours, too.

MY JOURNEY TO the trail was a frustrating one. Like lots of runners, I had reached a sticking point. I had taken on at least one major race a year since 2023, and I didn’t feel like I was getting any faster. Part of that stasis is due to injuries, shifting personal and work priorities interrupting training cycles, and the simple fact that I’m not bouncing back as quickly as I age.

But my road malaise was also due to my weariness with chasing benchmarks. Most runners know them: The sub-4, then sub-3 hour marathon. The sub-2 hour half marathon. The minutes-per-mile pace that will put them at the front of their run club pack.

I bonked (runner-speak for reaching a point of exhaustion after depleting the body’s energy stores) pursuing a sub-three hour time at the New York Marathon in 2023, and since then, I’ve been haunted by the standards I haven’t been able to hit. And thanks to the relative uniformity of the road, I could quantify everything down to the second. Even workouts were affected, since tracking and sharing every session on Strava showed how I struggled to keep up with faster friends.

I wasn’t alone. “People post their splits, but then it's like you can't even just be happy that you went for a run anymore,” says Jes Woods, a Nike Run Coach and founder of the Brooklyn Trail Club. “It's like, ‘Oh, now I also have to be proud of what pace or whatever.”

Tracking progress is important to achieve goals. But getting caught up in the stats can become self-defeating, according to mental performance consultant Luis Torres, M.Ed, CMPC. “A lot of times we can become data-driven by this and have an idea of ‘this is what peak performance looks like, that I have to hit these numbers.’”

When that data-imposed pressure becomes overwhelming, Torres suggests taking a moment to consider what else constitutes success. “Let's identify everything within my control that I can directly impact to get me closer to [the goal],” he says, citing non-running factors like sleep and recovery. “It's kind of like working backwards from where you want to be, and thinking about how we get there.”

I mentioned this struggle to Woods. She says that when the athletes she coaches have these issues, she recommends they explore new terrain. “If people are in a running slump, or chronically injured, or even if they just finished a marathon build and they don't know what to do with themselves, I'll always recommend them to try trail running instead,” she says. “I'm not saying that trail running fixes everything, but honestly, it can fix a whole lot of things.”

But I—and other people who do most of their running in urban environments—couldn’t transport myself to the trails immediately. What I could do was take a cue from trail running principles and find some other challenges to help me turn my brain off. Part of the appeal of trail, according to Woods, is that the challenges of the environment can help to ease the focus on data. “When it comes to trail running, the dumber the better,” she says. “As in, the more technical the terrain, the better. The steeper the climb, the better.”

Thankfully, I had some steep climbs on the horizon. I was offered the opportunity to the UTMB race via its sponsor, Hoka—and to be able to even finish the 40 kilometer (24.9 mile) race, with its 2,350 meters (about 7,709 feet) of elevation gain, I knew I would need to prepare like I never had before. But there was an exciting factor here for me: For the first time I could remember, I wasn’t chasing a time standard. I just needed to train to finish.

THE WAY TO introduce new challenges to my stale routine was all about planning. I tended to step out the door with a rough idea about how far and fast I wanted to go on my runs, but no specifics otherwise if I needed to stop and make an adjustment. You can do that in a busy city, but you can’t get away with that on a trail. Changing that approach would take effort in a different sense. And as Woods told me, it's all about effort on the trails over pace.

Instead of putting all my focus into making my mile splits faster, I took more time to plot my routes and strategize how and where I would stop. And even though it was the peak of summer, I purposely carried less fuel than I knew I would need for longer runs. Instead, I made a point to pause my watch and grab something to eat (Nerds gummy clusters being my number one pick) and a sports drink if I felt drained. I had to either plan these stops ahead of time, or really keep my eye on my surroundings to know when I would have the chance to get what I needed. I wasn’t exactly dodging rocks and roots on a trail, but this took concerted effort. This was meant to take something else Woods had told me into consideration. “You're going to be paying more attention where you're going, and you know you have to stay mentally alert at all times,” she told me.

I also considered Torres’s advice to think more holistically about achieving my goals. I realized that not all my training needed to be on the roads—especially since I wouldn’t be able to replicate the steep climbs I’d face on the trails to Chamonix in New York City. I could break the run down beyond just speed, since even if I wanted to, I wouldn’t be able to go fast for long portions of the race. I would need to spend time power-hiking and climbing on the course. So instead of just cranking a treadmill up to the highest incline, I strapped on a 20-pound weight vest and started walking up and down the five flights of stairs in my apartment building. Eventually, I added another 20 pounds. I would set a timer for 30 minutes, turn on an audiobook, and just march up and down the steps. It was much tougher than I expected. By the end of these sessions (and especially, on the following days), my calves were toast. But I felt much more capable.

Still, there were some moments during the training cycle that I felt overwhelmed. How would I be able to actually run through the mountains? Hoka athlete Hans Troyer, who placed third in the 107 kilometer (around 66 mile) CCC race at UTMB this year, shared a strategy he credits to ultra legend Courtney DeWalter that helped. “She talks about how she has a three-minute rule: In a race, if something's starting to go bad, she likes to take three minutes to have a problem-solving session in her head,” he told me (while also admitting he needs to get better at applying this practice himself). “Your entire race isn’t going to be ruined by three minutes.”

That advice applied doubly to workouts, which weren’t being tracked by anyone but me. Once I was able to give myself that permission to step back and be more thoughtful in my prep, most of the self-imposed pressure went away.

BY THE TIME my race rolled around, I had shifted my training practices and adjusted my mindset on the roads. But I wasn’t sure how the run would go. I practiced on an actual trail only once through the whole cycle, and those Ohio hills were a far cry from the Alps. I waited at the starting line in the valley town of Martigny-Combe, Switzerland, alternating between pacing, stretching, and sitting to conserve energy, always with an eye on the mountains in the distance I would soon have to climb.

The first part of the race was an ascent out of the valley on asphalt roadways and sidewalks. This didn’t feel so different than my road running—aside from the absurd incline of the course. Immediately my heart rate spiked, and I worried I might blow my energy early in the race. Still, the old temptation to fixate on my pace roiled in the back of my head on the familiar terrain. But I also remembered something Troyer had told me about a week before the race: “In marathoning it's all about PRs, and then in trail running, it's completion. You finish the race, that's awesome,” he said. “I think it's kind of liberating—now it's kind of the purest form of racing for me. It’s people against people, and time doesn't matter because the course changes.”

I truly let go of my temptation to chase numbers on that ascent. And as I began to eye my competitors, I couldn’t help but think about the old adage that you can’t run someone else’s race. I realized as I was fighting to make my way up those first hills that on the trail, it goes a step further: You can only run the race the trail gives you. If you try to push beyond, there are too many factors that will humble you, quick. You can come prepared and equipped as best you can, but once the gun goes off, you’re going against the terrain, the weather, your stomach—the list goes on.

“There's so much more problem solving,” Woods said. “There are so many other factors that come into play.”

I walked, climbed, and ran up that hill. I buried my pride and stepped off the course to take my trekking poles out of my pack way earlier than I planned, using them as an extra push upward. Once the asphalt roads turned to wooded trails, I found my rhythm. I was climbing slowly, putting the deliberate training on my stairwells into practice. I kept putting one foot in front of the other, using my climbing poles for extra purchase, and eventually, I settled. I didn’t feel like I had dismissed my mind’s compulsion to check my speed by flipping a switch; instead, I had quieted the impulse and embraced the process of moving through my environment, taking careful steps to avoid slipping and managing my energy for all the steps to come.

Woods had talked about this. “You're not turning your brain off—we're not like, meditating out in nature,” she said. “Your brain is still on, but in a different capacity. You're focusing on different things.”

Aside from staying upright and managing my energy, what I was most focused on was how lucky I was to be there at that moment. I ran through heavily wooded paths, jumped from rock to rock on scrambles, and felt like one of the Fellowship in The Lord of the Rings out in the wide open, surrounded my mountains on every side. Time in nature has been shown to improve mental health—and the sense of wonder from being in the Alps felt like another source of fuel to keep me moving.

That’s around the time that I finally checked my watch to do more than just keep up with my fueling. I wasn’t as far along the course as I expected I might be, but I wasn’t concerned. It was hot and I was barely moving at a clip that would be considered running. I didn’t care. I was working with the trail, not against my watch. That was the key.

After almost eight hours on my feet, I finally entered Chamonix, where the town’s streets were staged for an epic last stretch to the finish line. Crowds of people ringed the road and cheered for runners, a stark shift from the quiet wooded paths I had just left. After the hills, woods, and wide open valleys, I was back on pavement. But even as I steeled myself to go faster, running free of the obstructions I had mastered behind me, checking my pace wasn’t a concern. My sense of satisfaction and accomplishment beat any feeling I’ve had besting a benchmark. The trail gave me far more. When I stopped my watch after finishing, the numbers didn’t mean as much as the experience as a whole.

#1532. Is the Secret to Ultrarunning Success Downing Crazy High Carbs?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Carbs are rising. Records are falling. How David Roche found a new way to fuel to go the distance.

Welcome to the Wild World of Trail Running! We're diving deep on the races, training tips, and gear you need to know to get outside off paved roads to run a whole new way. Click here for more stories about the trail explosion.

This story was originally published on July 17, 2025.

IN THE SUMMER of 2024, runner and coach David Roche made a stunning announcement. He’d won a slot in the Leadville Trail 100 Mile, one of the oldest and most iconic races in the sport of Ultramarathon, which includes any race over 26.2 miles.

Not only is Leadville one of the most storied races, but it also had one of the oldest course records, which many people believe was unbeatable. But Roche was not one of them. He said he could beat the nearly two decade-old time of 15 hours, 42 minutes and 59 seconds.

The announcement was shocking for many reasons, but one stood out: Roche had never raced a hundred miles.

There were many doubters, including Roche himself. “I was so full of doubt and questioning,” he says now. “I had no idea if it was actually possible. But I knew based on the numbers that I could put out that if I was able to execute the way I thought could, then not only was the record possible, it could go down by a substantial margin.”

There were several reasons for Roche’s confidence. Ultrarunning is witnessing a number of innovations that are pushing times down: New carbon-plated trail shoes, advances in understanding the benefits of heat training, and the adoption of consuming sodium bicarbonate by elite runners to name a few.

But one innovation has garnered the most attention: The growing use of a “high carb” approach to training and racing, a tactic that started in cycling and is now moving into the rest of the endurance world, including ultrarunning.

Roche, as a coach and a runner, is one of high-carb’s leading proponents, and at Leadville he planned to consume between 120 to 140 grams of carbohydrates—around 500 calories—per hour. When he looked at his spreadsheet, it predicted he would beat the record by more than 15 minutes.

THE START LINE of one of ultrarunning’s most legendary races was, by some measures, an unlikely place for Roche to end up. He grew up in farming country on the eastern shore of Maryland. In high school he played football, weighed in at 200 pounds, and even played for Columbia University, where he studied environmental science. But after his freshman year, he quit football and started biking and running. He never imagined himself as a ultrarunner

“I used to think that my best event would be 800 meters and anything above that, I would get progressively worse, because I come from a football background. I always assumed it wasn't going to be my skill set.”

After college, when Roche moved to North Carolina to study environmental law at Duke University, he started running on the trails around Durham. He also met his wife Megan, who was studying neuroscience and playing field hockey. The two started frequenting the trails around the city together and both got more into running. Roche started doing trail races, and started coaching. In 2018, after Megan finished her medical program at Stanford, the two moved to Boulder, Colorado, where they both coach full time.

Roche had always advised his athletes to “shoot their shot” and go for big goals. But life was catching up. The couple had their first son in 2022, and time was slipping by. So in early 2023, Megan gave him a wakeup call. “She was like ‘David look: You're 35 and who knows how many years you have at the top level. Start to do what you say and shoot your shot.”

That April Roche signed up for the Canyons 100k in Auburn, California, which is a “Golden Ticket” race for the Western States 100 Mile, the most prestigious race in ultrarunning. The race went well, until he took a gel from an aid station that didn’t agree with him. He immediately vomited, then couldn’t keep anything down and ended up walking for six miles, finishing in fifth place. He knew he needed to figure out exactly what nutrition would work.

At the time, Roche was reading about, and experimenting with, higher levels of carbohydrate intake which had become standard in elite cycling, even though it was assumed that the body could only process 60 to 90 grams per hour. More fuel allows you to sustain a higher effort for longer.

Trying different gels and drink mixes, he pushed his carbs up to 120, 130 and 140 grams per hour. And in December of 2023, he entered the McDowell Mountain Frenzy 50 Miler and won. By the summer of 2024, he was ready for the Silver Rush 50 mile in Leadville.

He won by 25 minutes, and earned a slot in the Leadville 100 mile.

ROCHE KEPT TRAINING, both his legs and his stomach, taking in carbs at levels many regard as controversial. But even as he did, getting to the start line of Leadville was itself a kind of victory for other reasons.

In training, Roche only runs five days a week (and at lower mileage than most elite ultrarunners, with a focus on speed). Some days he rides his bike, which is what he was doing in April of 2024 when a car turned into him and sent him flying into a fence. He woke up with a concussion and a broken wrist. But as soon as he was recovered enough, he kept training (running with his arm in a sling) and keeping up the carbs.

“I trained like a competitive eater,” Roche says, learning to take in the amount of fluid and gels he’d need. “People in the competitive eating world actually practice with fluids, because they don't want to eat hot dogs every day. So I would use fluids to practice the bloat and teach my body not to reject that amount, because I knew I'd have to take in up to 56 ounces per hour and not reject it.”

By August, Roche was ready in his body. In his head, slightly less so.

“I still had so much doubt about whether it was possible or even a worthwhile goal to chase, with kids and work all the stuff going on,” he says now.

But Megan did want him to race, and so at 4 a.m. in Leadville, when the starting gun went off, he pushed those doubts aside and focused on the numbers. Not long after the start, he checked his heart rate.

“I knew how many calories-ish I burned at certain heart rates,” Roche says. “And when I looked at my heart rate, it was saying: ‘Look, you can go faster and still replace your calories.’ So a few miles into the race I was like, ‘All right, I guess it's time to race now.”

Roche made a rule for himself that he had to run every step of the way, with no walking. He describes the race as “incredibly fun,” which is not something people usually say about 100-milers.

“We saw him at Twin Lakes (mile 40) the first time and he looked great,” says his pacer Teddy Bross. “When came back into Twin Lakes (mile 60), in all honesty, he looked about the same.”

Bross ran the last 40 miles with Roche, and says there was one touch-and-go moment on the “power lines” climb where Roche started getting nauseous. But he was able to slow down, breathe and get it under control. Then the two runners sped on.

“On flats and downhills, I think we were somewhere around seven minutes a mile,” says Bross. “We were cooking,”

As they approached the finish line, Roche’s gap over second place widened. By the time he crossed it, he’d beaten his own prediction by one minute.

His time of 15:26:34 beat the Leadville record by more than 16 minutes.

THERE ARE MANY opinions on the so-called “high carb revolution,” or “carb mania,” as Alex Hutchinson, author of Endure: Mind, Body, and the Curiously Elastic Limits of Human Performance, calls it.

“The high-carb stuff is very much an open question scientifically,” Hutchinson says, “There's some logic, some anecdotes, and a tiny bit of data, but there are also lots of reasons to be skeptical. The thing with ultrarunning is that it's pretty much impossible to run randomized trials where people do all-out hundred-milers over and over. Everyone is making some guesses and doing some self-experimentation, but some people are more willing to try longshots.”

Andy Jones-Wilkins, a coach who has been running ultras since the 1990s, has seen a lot of trends come and go. He calls the push for high-carb fueling an "incredible marketing opportunity” which companies are taking advantage of, but cautions that it might not be the best strategy for mid- and back-of-the-pack runners.

“For athletes who are maybe a little older, in their 40s or above, somewhere in the range of 60 to 70 grams of carbs is per hour is plenty, especially if you're going to be going for more than six, seven eight hours.” The main issue, he says, is gastrointestinal distress, since slower runners aren’t working at the same intensity as elites, and just don’t need as many carbs.

“We know that ultras give people enough GI tract problems,” says Jones-Wilkins, “And to add to that by encouraging people to pump in over 100 grams of carbs, I would just urge people to tread carefully and experiment, because there's all kinds of stuff that can happen when you're eating a gel version of Thanksgiving dinner, then trying to run.”

Jason Koop, author of Training Essentials for Ultrarunning, says that while high-carb fueling seems new, it’s not. “The pro peloton [in cycling] has been doing this for years,” Koops says. “So anybody who thinks they're doing something new is either fooling themselves or hasn’t been paying attention. The origin of it is in very high-performance cycling, of which extremely few humans on the planet actually have a need to—or even can—ingest that amount of carbohydrate.”

Tyler Fox is a coach and a runner (coached by Roche) who’s had some success at the “lower end of high carb,” And while he does recommend higher carb intake for his athletes, it’s with a lot of caveats, including an athlete’s fitness and ability to maintain intensity.

“I think the narrative should be ‘fueling the work,’” Fox says. “Because if you're not able to sustain things from a mechanical perspective you're limited later by your ability to put out power.” According to Fox, in a 10-hour race, if you can run at a high intensity, you're going to be able to use more carbs, “but if you run at a conservative, less taxing output, your burn rate is going to be lower. And that's where you could run into high carb backfiring.”

In other words, if the carbs aren't being burned, they’ll find a way out though other channels, and you're risking a blowout on the side of the trail. Still, Fox sees the upside to higher carb intake with nuance. “I'm not suggesting that it's exclusively for higher level athletes” Fox says. “It does allow you to do more work, if you're prepared for it. But your training has to support it.”

Anna Gibson, a track and sub-ultra trail runner also coached by Roche, is sold on the benefits of high-carb fueling, and definitely has the training to support it. Last year the former NCAA athlete ran a 1500 meter PR, and competed in the Olympic trials. “In my last race I was putting down 110 grams of carbs per hour,” Gibson says. “It definitely took a lot of gut training to get to that point. But I credit that with my increase in performance.”

That race was the Gorge Waterfalls 30k, in which she won, broke the course record and finished second outright.

Despite all the unknowns, there’s no doubt that Roche’s output is enough to burn whatever he can cram in. “I think David has a clear understanding of what the science shows,” says Hutchinson, “and, just as importantly, what it doesn't show. Some people might look at that evidence and say, 'Nah, not quite enough to convince me to try it.' But David's an optimist: he weighs the odds and says, 'So you're telling me there's a chance!'

“I used to always think that I just sucked at the end of long races,” he says, “I can remember so many 50ks where I was like, 'Well, if I'm dying at mile 26, why would I go farther than this? Clearly I'm fighting my physiology.’

“Then I essentially went from normal fueling to high fueling and all of a sudden I become one of the best. So at the end of the day, it's really just: You give me 75 gels and I can give you the world.”

BACK HOME AFTER the Leadville 50, Roche’s recovery was fast. He says that after a day of feeling terrible, he improved quickly. In a couple weeks he was back to running form, which he attributes to his high-carb approach.

“I think the magic of carbs isn't race day performance necessarily. It's how much it helps you adapt and recover from training styles that will level up your fitness over time. So I'm going to be able to respond better to this workout, then come back stronger from it and stack up training in a way that makes me 36-years-old going on 22. “

Yet even as he recovered, Roche started to wonder if he got lucky in Leadville. “I thought, ‘What if it was all a fluke? What if I didn't actually learn anything about high-carb fueling or training or anything? What if I just had a good day at the right time and basically it was the stars aligning?”

To banish his doubts, Roche signed up for the Javelina Jundred in Arizona, just two months after Leadville. The race would be hot—nearly 100 degrees—so he started heat training, and practicing getting more fluids in.

At Javelina, Roche flew through the course, winning in 12:45:04 and missing the course record by less than two minutes. “The difference between Leadville and Javelina,” Roche says, “is that at Leadville, even in the moment, it felt like something magical was happening. It felt like something I don't know if I'm ever going to be able to repeat. But at Javelina I felt like I could do this any Saturday.”

The victory also earned him a “golden ticket” to the prestigious Western States 100 Mile.

Roche continued training and racing through the spring of 2025, with some of the usual ultrarunning ups and dowsn. In March he sprained an ankle in the Big Alta 50k, and had to drop out. But in May, he raced the Quad Rock, 50 Miler, winning by over 40 minutes and setting a new course record. The win gave him a “huge boost in confidence” for Western States in June.

WHETHER HIGH-CARB fueling will work for most runners remains to be seen. While there is some indication it might be different from previous fads like barefoot running, fat-adapted training and so on, runners should still proceed with care.

“Your average ultrarunner,” says Koop, “and even your good ultrarunner, needs to be cautious about titrating this up just because they saw somebody on Instagram do it. In many cases it's not necessary based on the energetic demands, and in most cases it’s not even pragmatic, because they can't take in that much carbohydrate.”

Jones-Wilkins agrees. “My prediction is that we’ll see kind of a settling into the fact that high carb works for the elites, but it doesn't work for the vast majority of people. They'll settle somewhere in the middle. But I urge athletes to tread carefully and experiment with what works for them. Because what works for me might not work for you and might not work for the next guy.”

ON JUNE 28, Roche lined up with some of the best in the world including legendary mountain runner Kilian Jornet, who won the race in 2011. This time, he wasn’t making any bold predictions.

“I’m a big-time underdog,” Roche said before the race, “even an underdog for top five. And that's the way I like it. I know I can run fast there relative to historic times. It's just a question of how fast everybody else goes.”

They went out very fast, with Roche in the lead up initial four-mile climb. Then as they moved through the mountains, a pack of at least twelve elite men pushed a sub-record pace. They hung together for the first 30 miles, all running within a minute or two of each other.

Roche was in the mix, hovering around fifth place, running shoulder-to-shoulder with Jornet and the others. The race was going according to plan. By mile 40, in the heat of the day, things started to spread out. At mile 40, Roche came in right behind race leaders Chris Myers and (eventual winner) Caleb Olson. At mile 56, he was solidly in fifth place.

“I felt great almost the whole time, even when I came into Michigan Bluff right behind Jeff. I was in a great spot. In retrospect, I was in the ideal spot.”

But soon after leaving the Michigan Bluff aid station, on the downhill, Roche’s race also started going downhill. He suspects he took in too many electrolytes given his heat training. But for whatever reason, his vision got blurry and he had some minor GI distress. So he dialed down the carbs, which he now thinks was a mistake.

“I went lower when I should have gone higher,” he says. “The gels were going down fine. But even after that I was able to recover once my sodium levels got better. I moved up and felt great. Then the lack of gels hit me, and that lead to some problems on the mental end.

Roche started fixating on his health, catastrophizing, and was suddenly filled with anxiety about seeing his kids again. He hesitates to call it a panic attack, but when his pacer, Teddy Bross (who is a physician) gave him some breathing exercises, they helped. Looking back he feels like he prepared his body, but not his mind.

“I think because of life being so white knuckled with two young kids and everything,” he says, “I didn't do the basic stuff I always ask athletes to do as a coach—like make it okay to get to that place where it feels kind of scary. Basically, I didn't nail down the mental side as much as I needed to going into a 100 mile.” He ended up dropping at 62 miles.

But as every ultrarunner knows, DNFs are just as important as getting on the podium, if not moreso. “The magic of ultrarunning,” Roche says, “is that you get stripped down to your core and from that you can grow back stronger. So I'm excited to get back out there and do another 100. And if I do qualify [for Western States] again, you’d better believe I'm coming back.”

#1524. How to Train for a Trail Race in a City

I SIGNED UP for a desert trail race in Arizona last year, my first ever. Right as my training cycle started, I moved from Salt Lake City to Brooklyn, trading easy access to Utah's parks and mountains for the paved city streets of New York. My new home was about as far from the cactus-lined single-track of the Javelina 31K I was preparing to run at as I could imagine.

I'm not the kind of person with a wall full of medals from road races (I've never run one), but I do love the outdoors. Spending time outside was a big part of my life throughout a decade of living in the American West, and I’ve been a casual trail runner for years, mostly because it lets me move through nature faster than hiking. Once I was on the East Coast, trading dirt for pavement was my only option. I trained however I could: over city bridges, through Brooklyn's Prospect Park, and even on a treadmill. And somehow? It worked. A few months after my first trail race, I leveled up to run another, a 55K in Moab—and I trained in the city for that, too.

More people than ever are dipping their toes into trail races and ultramarathons. In 2024 alone, over 40,000 people completed an ultra for the first time, according to UltraRunning Magazine. But while road marathons are (relatively) predictable—the consistent asphalt surface, unobstructed routes, and regular aid stations—trail races demand a different kind of training. You’ll need to prepare for long climbs, technical terrain, and hours on your feet. Some athletes get caught up in making sure their training environment is as close to race day as possible, so urban-dwelling trail runners might be worried that they could never properly prepare for their long, off-road runs close to home. But it’s totally doable. Here’s how to get ready for a trail race when getting out into nature isn't so easy.

ULTRAMARATHONS COME IN a range of distances—from 50K and 50 miles to 100K, 100 miles, and even races over 200 miles (maybe don’t start there.) The first step is to pick your distance and your race.

“With a road marathon, 95 percent of them are basically the same elevation profile,” says Ron Hammett, running coach for Sundog Running who I worked with for both races. But trail races can vary dramatically, and your race’s location will dictate your approach. For example, if you pick a race with 6,000 feet of elevation gain and loss, you’ll need to prepare your body for that vertical challenge. Similarly, races on particularly rugged terrain require extra joint and stability work.

For city-based beginners, you'll be best-suited to choose a race that’s less technical. The Javelina 31K had minimal elevation and smooth trails—and even though the Moab Red Hot 55K race featured technical terrain, much of it was on firm slickrock, which is almost as hard as pavement, so my body was ready for the impact after road training.

RUNNING ON ROADS often means repeating the same motion over and over: The same pace, with the same foot strike, using the same muscle groups. But with trail running, you’ll be leaping over rocks, side-shuffling around roots, and landing at odd angles. Add in steep climbs and pounding descents, and suddenly your usual run training doesn’t cut it.

Hammett recommends incorporating lower-body dominant compound movements—squats, Romanian deadlifts, and step-ups—into your routine. Core strength is also crucial for balance and stability, and calf raises can help to keep your legs from feeling like jello on mile 15, while also making your ankles more resilient.

“Another method of simulating those technical trails is plyometrics,” says Hammett. Warm-up by jumping rope or hopping back and forth on one foot to get the tissue ready to handle the load of running.

Then, get more specific with your exercises based on your personal needs. For me, this meant stretching and strengthening my calves to prevent shin splints, and strengthening my hip flexors and outer glutes for hip stability.

CITY TRAINING CAN rarely match the natural elevation of trails, but it’s important to work with what you have nearby. Dave Hashim, an NYC-based ultrarunner who takes on huge chunks of city miles and has completed races like Black Canyon 100K and the Escarpment Trail Run (along with city running feats like traversing the perimeter of Manhattan), integrates the Williamsburg Bridge and Queensboro bridge into nearly every run to get some elevation gain into his training plan.

If you can’t access any hills, you might have to embrace the treadmill for hill sprints—or even for hiking. By putting on a weighted vest or your race-day pack and setting a steady incline, you can simulate a significant portion of your course's vert. For my training, Hammett programmed weighted treadmill hikes with 1,200 feet of climbing at a five to 15 percent incline. As race day neared, I was logging 2,000 to 3,000 feet of elevation gain per session on the treadmill.

But the treadmill can get boring very quickly, so it helps to find ways to keep it interesting, like intervals. For one long run, I downloaded the Moab Red Hot course map onto my Suunto app and adjusted the incline on the treadmill to follow the elevation profile for the first 17 miles of the run. Brutal, but worth it.

TRAIL RACES OFTEN require a level of self-sufficiency: you might go miles without an aid station, and you’ll need to carry food, water, and extra layers of clothing. “In the city, there are actually a lot of different ways to train for that—you just have to be open to being uncomfortable,” says Hashim.

Instead of repeating the same loop every weekend, he maps out new routes across different neighborhoods. This novelty helps simulate the unpredictability of race day—new terrain, surprising turns, or mental fatigue from being somewhere unfamiliar. “It gets you more comfortable when you are doing an ultra, where you’re running in places you’ve never been,” he says.

You’ll also have to mix up your workouts. If you’ve trained for road races, you know the program can be very formulaic: Easy runs, a track day, a tempo run, a weekend long run. The goal is to hit your stride and maintain the same pace throughout the entire race. But this approach doesn’t work as well for trail ultras, where you’ll need to adjust your pace based on your terrain.

Hashim’s workouts might include two-mile hill repeats over six hours or downhill sprints followed by a three-mile tempo run—workouts that don’t make sense for road racing but perfectly mimic the unpredictable nature of trail events.

JUST REMEMBER THAT the trail can—and will—be an unpredictable environment. “If you're running on carbon plated shoes on the road, and you're hitting a seven flat pace expecting to do this time, you’re in for a world of pain,” says Hashim.

Trail pace is slower. A lot slower. Mileage that takes you two hours on pavement could easily take four hours on dirt. And when the terrain changes every mile, pace just doesn’t matter the same way as it does for road races. That’s why Hashim never sets a strict time goal. Instead, he plans a general timeline to help plan nutrition and hydration. “I think you have to be okay with throwing what you learned about road racing out the window,” he says. “A lot of people focus on stats and heart rate. But for me, how I feel during a race is paramount.”

That mindset starts in training. Try using rate of perceived exertion (RPE)—a scale of one to ten that reflects how hard a workout feels—instead of chasing numbers from your watch. It’ll help you get used to running by feel, which matters a lot more when the trail throws a climb at you.

And even though it’s called a trail run—you will be hiking parts of it. “What's the most efficient way of getting from start to finish? For these longer races, it's definitely not running the whole way,” says Hammett. During your training runs, incorporate run-walk intervals (like 8:2 or 4:1) to help your body adjust to the constant stop/start rhythm. The hiking sections are a great time to hydrate and fuel—two things you definitely don’t want to slack on during an ultra.

Trail running isn’t just physically demanding—it requires a mindset shift from the conditions lots of runners are accustomed to for training and racing. You have to know that things will go sideways—and you have to keep moving anyway. The end results will prove to be worth the extra effort.

Hashim recalls a race in Marin County, California that tested his limits: “It just felt like waves crashing, and I just wasn't able to swim out of it,” he said.

But then he looked up, and saw exactly where his fight against the trail had taken him. “I was living in a screensaver. It was just rolling green hills for miles, blue sky. I thought, how can I be angry and frustrated when this is what I get to look at?”

TENERIFE, Spain — The commander of NASA's Artemis II mission briefly feared that he and his crew would never return home from their trip around the moon after overshooting their turn-back point.

Commander Reid Wiseman and his three crewmates were supposed to reach 219,667 nautical miles (252,788 standard miles, or 406,823 kilometers) — farther than any humans have ever traveled from their home planet — before turning back toward Earth on the sixth day of their 10-day mission to the moon.

But the odometer on the Integrity spacecraft continued to tick upward as they shot past their intended point of return, leading the astronaut to fear that something had gone terribly wrong, he told an audience here at the Starmus VIII festival earlier this week. (This semi-annual event is held to celebrate achievements in science, exploration, and creativity.)

"Mission control in Houston had told us our maximum distance would be 219,667 nautical miles and that was the furthest we were supposed to be from Earth," Wiseman said. "I was watching this number, and I saw it hit 219,667, and I thought, 'Great, this is the farthest we will be from Earth, and the next number I will see will be 219,666 ‪—‬ we'll be going home.' But about three minutes later, I didn't see 219,666; I saw 219,668. It went up. I was like. 'Huh? That's weird.'
