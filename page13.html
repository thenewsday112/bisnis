#734. Live: Federal government launches probe into future of Australia Post

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The inquiry will examine if the postal service is meeting the needs of Australians.  (ABC News: Christopher Gillette)

Communications Minister Anika Wells says a parliamentary inquiry will examine whether Australia Post is keeping pace with Australians' needs for the future.

A very quick parliamentary inquiry has been launched into new legislation handing Defence new powers to manage drones flying over its bases.

Drones are a rapidly growing problem for Defence, which yesterday told Estimates it is seeing hundreds of incidents each year where drones are flown into, or over, sensitive bases.

A series of incidents at one of the Air Force's most sensitive bases caused alarm earlier this year, when drones were seen flying over Williamtown, near Newcastle, which houses much of Australia's fleet of f-35 fighter jets.

Reports that staff on the base were instructed to close the blinds prompted questions over whether the ADF was capable of, or even allowed to, take down drones flying over its bases.

Legislation introduced to parliament this week aims to remedy that, giving the ADF the legal authority to "conduct counter-measures"—that is, take down—drones if it needs to.

Hearings are already underway this morning, with Defence officials up first, and companies like DroneShield up later on.

Australia's peak trucking and road freight bodies are urging the government to reinstate fuel tax relief.

This week's September inflation figures came in hotter than expected, with soaring fuel prices contributing to the rise.

The treasurer has so far ruled out repeating the temporary cuts to the fuel excise and heavy vehicle road user charge, which were implemented earlier this year.

But NatRoad CEO Warren Clark says the government should bring the road user charge down to zero until the end of January.

"What it does is give small businesses the cash flow injection, the cash flow help that it needs to actually remain in business through that really difficult and challenging time," Clark tells AM.

The Australian Trucking Association's Mathew Munro agrees, saying that with diesel prices once again on the rise, targeted relief must be back on the table for the industry.

"While the government can't do anything about the war in Iran, they certainly can do something about the taxation measures that they apply," the CEO says.

"We think that given the circumstances now are comparable to what they were early on in this crisis, we think that relief is now warranted again."

A Cairns man has been charged with threatening a federal parliamentarian on social media, marking the latest crackdown by federal authorities on digital abuse aimed at public figures.

The arrest comes just over a week after an alleged attack on Independent MP Helen Haines shocked the nation and highlighted the volatile environment facing Australia's elected representatives.

An Australian Federal Police statement said one of their National Security Investigations (NSI) teams executed a search warrant at the 53-year-old's Earlville home.

He was served a Notice to Appear in court for using a carriage service to menace, harass, or cause offence under section 474.17(1) of the Commonwealth Criminal Code.

The charge carries a maximum penalty of five years’ imprisonment.

The man is scheduled to appear in the Cairns Magistrates Court on December 3.

Labor MP Susan Templeman, chair of the communications, arts and sport committee, says the probe into Australia Post announced today will have a broad remit to look at the role the service plays in communities across the country.

Templeman said the committee would examine its ongoing sustainability and accessibility in both metropolitan and regional Australia.

"It's an absolutely vital part of all our communities … vital for business, and vital for our economy," she said.

The committee will consider growing competition from other parcel carriers and what that means for Australia Post's long-term future, while also considering how the service can diversify and what additional offerings it could provide.

It will also look at the reliability of postal services, particularly for small businesses that rely on the network to reach customers.

One Nation Senator Sean Bell has told News 24 the prime minister shouldn't be in charge of staffing levels for other parties.

One Nation isn't happy with Anthony Albanese after he apparently offered MP David Farley a $200,000 staffing allocation.

One Nation Leader Pauline Hanson, as well as a number of other parliamentarians, including former Labor senator Fatima Payman, have been pleading with the PM to bump up their staffing levels, which he has so far refused to do.

Bell says Albanese is playing "dirty games".

"This is all occurring because the prime minister is desperate," he says.

"They've got a budget that's a disaster, a treasurer under pressure, and the prime minister himself is under an incredible amount of pressure because he's been caught abusing the grants program".

The CSIRO has told a Senate estimates hearing that job losses are a part of the organisation's efforts to stay "contemporary and modern".

The science agency has slashed hundreds of jobs in recent years and says it is consulting on changes that could affect another 86 full-time jobs in its biosecurity and technology research units.

CEO Doug Hilton said the units could potentially be merged or transferred to make room for reinvestment in other research areas.

"There are opportunities to invest in areas that we think could generate more impact for the community," he said.

The reinvestment process could create 150 new roles, but Mr Hilton said it was not always possible to transfer staff between units.

He said that, for the past 20 years, the CSIRO had usually conducted an annual review cycle across its research portfolio, a process that could result in redundancies.

Deputy Opposition Leader Jane Hume says there is broad Coalition support for free trade, despite internal debate over the EU Free Trade Agreement.

The Nationals are arguing Australia should hold out for a better deal, but some Liberals say support for free trade is a core economic principle of their party.

Hume says the EU deal is simply "not fair" in its current form.

"It exposes some of our most important in industries to some unfair competition... and weakens our sovereignty by making international agreements legally binding," she told Radio National Breakfast.

Trade Minister Don Farrell told the National Farmers Federation last night that it was essentially this deal or no deal.

Hume says while the Coalition wants a "foot in the door" in Europe, it cannot come "at any price".

Labor has denied Prime Minister Anthony Albanese is trying to woo One Nation MP David Farley by offering him an extra staff member.

One Nation leader Pauline Hanson yesterday criticised the offer as an attempt by the government to "exert undue political influence" and split her party.

Farley received a letter from Albanese this week authorising him to hire an additional staffer on a $200,000 salary package, an "unsolicited" offer the Farrer MP has rejected.

Appearing on Channel 7, Employment Minister Amanda Rishworth denied the offer was an attempt to lure Farley over to Labor.

"Quite frankly, I think Pauline is jumping at shadows. If she's already having trouble keeping her party together, well that says something about her," she says.

"Just to be clear, lower house members wouldn't be doing their job if they were not meeting with ministers and meeting with the prime minister."

Trade Minister Don Farrell delivered some blunt hard truths to the National Farmers Federation last night, arguing an Australia-EU free trade agreement is vital to the nation's economic prosperity and could generate $10 billion in additional trade in its first year.

The National Farmers Federation have been some of the most vocal opponents to the FTA.

"In a world of increasing geopolitical uncertainty, when protectionism and tariffs are barriers to growth, we cannot afford to close ourselves to the world," Farrell said.

He told farmers that despite calls from the Coalition, the deal was as good as it was going to get and was not open to renegotiation.

"Take a look at the TV right now. Protests in France, far right governments in Germany, protestors occupying the Colosseum in Italy," he said.

"There is no way [Nationals leader] Matt Canavan can get a better deal".

Farrell acknowledged that beef and lamb industries wanted more out of the deal but said he had to represent all Australian industries, 95% of which say they support the agreement.

"I cannot in good conscience say that just because one sector didn't get everything they wanted, the entire economy gets nothing," he said.

The Albanese government is launching a parliamentary inquiry in a bid to shore up the long-term viability of Australia Post.

The 217-year-old institution has faced challenges in recent times, with falling foot traffic at some post offices and a drop in letters.

Last financial year, there were about 1.42 billion letters sent through Australia post — but that was down 14.7 per cent from the year before.

Communications Minister Anika Wells says changing consumer behaviour and commercial conditions posed significant challenges to the sustainability of Australia Post.

"That's why I've asked the House committee to explore how we can better support a sustainable and accessible postal service that delivers for all Australians," she said.

An Australia Post spokesperson said it looked forward to engaging constructively in the inquiry.

"For several years, we have highlighted the growing challenges facing Australia Post, which are shared by postal organisations around the world," they said.

"We welcome the government's action to address these challenges and support our long-term sustainability."

The inquiry is due to report back by the middle of next year.

#233. Palestinians face growing bans from Al-Aqsa as Israel tightens control

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

As Israel renews a ban on Al-Aqsa imam, Islamic Waqf officials warn of shrinking authority at Muslim holy site.

For more than two years, Sheikh Ekrima Sabri has been barred from occupied East Jerusalem’s Al-Aqsa Mosque, unable to pray or deliver sermons at the holy site.

And with his latest exclusion order extending the ban until April 2027, the Palestinian preacher – the head of the Higher Islamic Council in Jerusalem – is among those facing Israeli restrictions at the site – the third holiest in Islam and a Palestinian national symbol.

Sabri has faced repeated exclusion orders since August 2024. The latest order, issued on October 20, prevents Sabri from praying or delivering sermons at Al-Aqsa until April 2027.

At the same time, far-right Jewish religious activists have increasingly been allowed to pray at the site under Israeli police protection, going against a longstanding status quo and opposition from ultra-Orthodox Jewish religious authorities.

The restrictions began after Israeli police detained Sabri in August 2024 over a sermon in which he mourned the Hamas political leader Ismail Haniyeh, who was assassinated by Israel.

Sabri, one of the most prominent Palestinians in East Jerusalem, has faced repeated Israeli interrogations.

Palestinians say this is part of a campaign against them at Al-Aqsa. The Wadi Hilweh Information Center, a Palestinian human rights monitoring group, says Israeli authorities have issued more than 1,000 exclusion orders barring Palestinians from Al-Aqsa Mosque and the Old City since the beginning of 2026, including against religious figures and Islamic Waqf employees.

The centre reported an increase in exclusion orders ahead of Jewish holidays, documenting more than 40 issued over five days in early September.

Sabri told Al Jazeera that the latest extension was the fifth time Israeli authorities have issued an exclusion order against him.

He described the decision as unjust, illegal, inhumane and uncivilised, saying it was intended to silence those speaking out about Al-Aqsa.

Sabri said that he would continue defending Al-Aqsa despite the restrictions, describing the mosque as an integral part of his Islamic faith.

“These measures are aimed at diminishing the Palestinian presence at the mosque, while simultaneously allowing settlers to carry out incursions in large numbers,” Sabri said. “Such actions represent an aggressive policy targeting the Al-Aqsa Mosque and the Islamic presence within it.”

Ibrahim al-Natsheh was released by Israeli police earlier this month after spending a week in detention after he was arrested while attending funeral prayers for his cousin at Al-Aqsa Mosque.

He told Al Jazeera that officers took him to a police room and handed him a six-month exclusion order barring him from East Jerusalem’s Old City. He said no specific charge was brought against him.

According to al-Natsheh, the order warns that he could face arrest and further penalties, including a fine or imprisonment, if he enters the prohibited area.

Al-Natsheh said he has received so many exclusion orders that he has lost count. He said the restrictions began about nine years ago, following an Israeli crackdown on Islamic study circles at the mosque.

Palestinian students, teachers and activists associated with the activities have faced arrests and exclusion orders.

“The orders banning us from the mosque provide no specific reason; they simply cite ‘disturbing the peace’, even though we are there solely to pray,” al-Natsheh told Al Jazeera. “Ahead of the Jewish holidays, they sent a message to me and a group of other worshippers, warning us not to approach the mosque or else we will be arrested.”

Even when he is not subject to a formal exclusion order, al-Natsheh said, Israeli police have prevented him from entering Al-Aqsa. He also alleged that officers have repeatedly arrested him at Al-Aqsa’s gates and severely beaten him while in detention.

“The path to the mosque has become incredibly difficult; it is swarming with soldiers and settlers,” he said. “We are denied the ability to reach it with any ease, and if we try, we face arrest and beatings with batons. It is deeply painful to witness what is happening to Al-Aqsa now.”

Al Jazeera has reached out to Israeli authorities for comment, but has yet to receive a response.

Al-Aqsa Mosque has seen an escalation in Israeli restrictions and violations throughout 2026.

After the United States and Israel launched their war on Iran on February 28, Israeli authorities closed Al-Aqsa to most worshippers for 40 days, reopening the compound on April 9.

Meanwhile, according to the Palestinian Authority’s Jerusalem Governorate, almost 28,000 visits by Israeli settlers were recorded at Al-Aqsa between July and September.

Ma’rouf al-Rifai, an adviser to the Jerusalem Governorate, told Al Jazeera that during recent Jewish holidays, Israeli restrictions on Muslim worshippers had intensified.

These measures included closing the mosque for periods of time, preventing noon prayers on at least one occasion, and restricting access for residents of the Old City during visits to the site by Israeli settlers and Jewish religious activists.

Al-Rifai said the Islamic Waqf Department faces mounting constraints on its role in managing the mosque’s affairs.

He said Israeli police have prevented Waqf officials from accompanying Jewish groups when they entered the site, curtailed the authority of mosque guards, and arrested or barred several employees from the compound.

As a result, he said, fewer than 15 guards are now on duty during the morning shift, compared with approximately 50 previously.

He pointed out that the closure of the mosque for 40 days during the war with Iran took place without consulting the Waqf or providing prior notification, which he said demonstrated the Waqf’s diminishing role in the mosque’s administration.

“Settler incursions have undergone a shift over the past three years; they now include Jewish prayers and religious rituals, the raising of Israeli flags, and attempts to bring in sacrificial offerings,” al-Rifai said.

These changes have undermined longstanding arrangements at the compound, under which the Waqf manages Muslim religious affairs while Israeli police oversee security and access.

According to al-Rifai, Israeli authorities have prevented the Waqf from carrying out restoration work at the Bab al-Rahma prayer hall and restricted maintenance of the compound’s trees and gardens. He said the committee responsible for restoration work at Al-Aqsa has been unable to carry out its duties for years.

He warned of efforts to alter the status quo at Al-Aqsa Mosque, pointing to several proposals and developments, including demands to allow Jewish visitors into the compound on Saturdays, plans advanced by Israeli politicians to divide access to parts of the site between Muslims and Jews, and an Israeli police recruitment drive for volunteers tasked with escorting Israeli incursions at the site.

Al-Rifai said these developments represented a further erosion of the Waqf’s authority and warned that they could eventually transform the arrangements governing worship and access at the holy site.

#755. Controversial goldmine subject of new Aboriginal protection application

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Belubula River originates at the mine site. (ABC News: Xanthe Gregory)

A group of Orange Aboriginal Land Council members is seeking emergency protection of a river it says is sacred and under immediate threat from the McPhillamys Gold Project.

The Federal Court recently overturned another Aboriginal protection order declared by former environment minister Tanya Plibersek on the same site.

The federal environment department will now assess the application.

A faction of the Orange Local Aboriginal Land Council (OLALC) has made a fresh bid to save a "sacred" river system, a move that could halt a billion-dollar New South Wales gold project.

It comes just weeks after the Federal Court threw out a protection order over the same area.

"Concerned members" of the council have submitted a section 9 application, seeking emergency protection for a section of the McPhillamys Gold Project near Blayney, in the state's Central West.

The application, submitted independently of the OLALC, describes the area as being under "imminent threat of desecration".

The concerns of a dozen members were raised in an email seen by the ABC that was sent to the federal Environment Minister Murray Watt this week.

"We respectfully urge you to consider this application as a matter of urgency and take all available lawful steps to protect these Sacred Waters from irreversible harm," the application said.

"The Bila Bula [Belubula] is far more than a River."

Regis Resources argued the Commonwealth's decision rendered its goldmine unviable in its current form. (ABC News: Lani Oataway)

On October 14, Regis Resources successfully challenged the former federal environment minister Tanya Plibersek's decision in August 2024 to grant an Aboriginal protection order over the site in question.

The project had been put on hold and the company said the minister's declaration meant the open-cut operation was "not viable".

The original application was made by Wiradyuri elder Aunty Nyree Reynolds and supported by the Wiradyuri Traditional Owners Central West Aboriginal Corporation (WTOCWAC).

The declaration was made under Section 10 of the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Heritage Protection Act to protect the headwaters and the springs of the Belubula River, where the tailings dam that holds mining waste was to be built.

But a fortnight ago the court ruled Ms Plibersek's decision was "invalid" due to procedural errors with how additional information about the area's cultural significance was assessed.

The Belubula River starts between Bathurst and Orange, stretching through farmland into the mouth of the Lachlan River.  (ABC News: Xanthe Gregory)

In considering the fresh application, the environment minister must now decide if he is satisfied that the site is a significant Aboriginal area that is under serious and immediate threat of injury or desecration.

The Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water (DCCEEW) has confirmed the application is being assessed, and no decision has been made.

OLALC CEO Annette Steele said she expected the department would ask OLAC to respond to the application submitted privately by a group of its members.

"Our response will be guided by the senior Wiradjuri elders who hold cultural authority for the area in question," Ms Steele said.

She said that all Aboriginal people within the OLALC's boundaries were eligible to be members, whether or not they were traditional owners.

"For this reason, cultural heritage matters are typically decided by those people with cultural authority, not the membership as a whole," she said.

"However, LACLs are democratic organisations and members are free to raise any concerns that they may have at meetings or to the board."

The new application highlights disputes playing out between the region's Aboriginal groups, including disagreements over who holds positions of cultural authority and who has the right to speak for country.

In 2019, before the mine was initially approved, OLALC made a submission in response to the McPhillamys Gold Project environmental impact statement.

It objected to the State Significant Development on the basis that "the proposed project will detrimentally and irreversibly impact the Aboriginal cultural heritage significance of the study area".

The Orange Local Aboriginal Land Council holds a "neutral" position on the mine.  (ABC Central West: Xanthe Gregory)

In 2023 OLALC changed its position on the mine from "object" to "neutral".

It has publicly questioned the accuracy and veracity of claims made about the area, including a Dreaming story.

The new section 9 application raises concerns that "Wiradjuri and other First Nations people with cultural connections and responsibilities to this country, were not given a meaningful opportunity to have our say".

"We were not consulted or asked for our views when the change in stance by the executive of the Orange Aboriginal Land Council was announced previously," it said.

In her statement of reasons for granting the protection order in 2024, Ms Plibersek found that development of the mine's tailings dam would cause "irreversible" and "permanent" loss to Aboriginal heritage.

She found the Belubula headwaters were sacred to the Wiradjuri people and their Blue Banded Bee Dreaming Tradition, which narrates how the springs were formed.

The site of the proposed McPhillamys goldmine near Blayney. (ABC Central West: Tim Fookes)

In his federal court judgement delivered in October, Justice James Stellios referenced a brief from DCCEEW that said the declaration was contrary to submissions and advice from OLALC.

The department concluded that "a consensus view amongst all Wiradjuri people is not necessary".

But it was the timing of the revelation about the Dreaming story that would ultimately unravel the legality of Ms Plibersek's declaration.

Justice Stellios's judgement said his decision to overturn the Commonwealth's section 10 was not "about the merits of the claim … whether it's a significant area or under threat of injury or desecration".

He found that "the decision-making process was fundamentally flawed because of the failure to treat the revelation of the Blue Banded Bee Dreaming story as a new application".

During the court case, Regis Resources argued that the Dreaming story did not exist and challenged why it was not raised in the initial protection order application.

In a statement provided to the ABC, Regis Resources said the project had been approved through both the NSW State Significant Development pathway and the federal Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act, "each of which considered Aboriginal cultural heritage matters".

The company said it "supports the protection of Aboriginal cultural heritage, through a planning process that provides certainty for all parties".

The ABC understands that a second section 9 application has also been lodged by a different group.

#368. Patrick Stewart, Star Trek actor and acclaimed thespian, dies aged 86

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Star Trek: the Next Generation and X-Men actor died at his home after ‘a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia’

Patrick Stewart dies aged 86 – latest updates

Patrick Stewart, the acclaimed British stage and screen actor renowned for both Shakespeare and sci-fi, has died. He was 86.

The Star Trek: the Next Generation and X-Men star died on Wednesday at his home in Los Angeles after “a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia”, said Kelly Bush Novak, his longtime spokesperson, to the Hollywood Reporter.

“He has been held in the warm embrace of family and friends, surrounded by live music, prayer, tears, laughter and the kind of profound emotion that only he could inspire after a lifetime of devotion to us all,” she wrote. “We will forever cherish the peaceful ease and reflection on his face, an enduring reminder of his invincible grace.”

With his authoritative baritone voice and commanding visage, Stewart was already a staple of the British stage by the late 1980s when, at 47, he was cast as a graying Capt Jean-Luc Picard in Star Trek: the Next Generation, the first live-action spin-off of the groundbreaking 1960s TV series. Over seven seasons onboard the Starship Enterprise, Stewart’s even-keeled performance and steely directives – “engage!” and “make it so” – turned him into a global star.

Stewart reprised his role in four feature film spin-offs – Star Trek Generations (1994), Star Trek: First Contact (1996), Star Trek: Insurrection (1998) and Star Trek: Nemesis (2002) – as well as the TV series Star Trek: Picard, which ran from 2020 until 2023.

Fellow Star Trek actor William Shatner paid tribute on social media. “From the stage to the bridge of the Enterprise, he brought intelligence, grace, and a deep sense of humanity to everything he did,” he wrote. “Captain Picard stood for the best of us, and so did the man who played him. I was filled with affection and admiration for him and his work.”

George Takei, who played Hikaru Sulu in the original series, also wrote about Stewart on Bluesky. “He inspired a whole next generation of star-gazers who looked longingly ahead to a brighter future for all humanity,” he posted. “Our hearts are heavy, indeed. Rest in the heavens, Captain.”

His other most well-known role was playing Prof Charles Xavier in the X-Men series. The actor had found work hard to obtain after feeling typecast as a result of his Star Trek role but had been intrigued by working with director Bryan Singer for 2000’s X-Men.

He went on to play the role in seven films and will be seen reprising the role in this December’s Avengers: Doomsday.

His X-Men co-star Hugh Jackman shared the following on Instagram today: “We lost one of the greats of stage and screen today. And, in my book, a tremendous friend and man. I learned so much from him. I will miss you, Patrick Stewart, all of my days.”

Marvel chief Kevin Feige has paid tribute in a statement calling him “brilliant, charming, warm, generous, with a quiet strength and gravitas”. He added: “Away from the cameras, he was even more remarkable, with a wonderfully sharp sense of humor. It was a true privilege to know him and to work with him. His legacy lives on not only in decades of extraordinary work, but in the many, many people he mentored. He will be deeply missed.”

His other film roles included the gay comedy Jeffrey, romantic thriller Conspiracy Theory, violent horror Green Room and voice roles in Ted, Gnomeo & Juliet and, most recently, The Sheep Detectives.

Born on 13 July 1940 in the small industrial village of Mirfield, England, Stewart was the youngest of three sons born to Alfred Stewart, a British military veteran turned semiskilled laborer, and Gladys Stewart, a weaver at a textile factory. His childhood, in a small house with no running hot water, was marred by alcoholism and domestic violence, a situation he later described, in an article for the Guardian from 2009, as “barely tolerable”.

He found respite in acting, first in school – he was 12 when an English teacher assigned him the part of Shylock in Shakespeare’s The Merchant of Venice – and then local drama groups. “I managed to find my own refuge in acting,” he wrote. “The stage was a far safer place for me than anything I had to live through at home – it offered escape. I could be someone else, in another place, in another time.”

After graduating from secondary school at 15, he worked as a local reporter and furniture salesman before a local arts council provided the funding to attend the prestigious Bristol Old Vic Theater School, where he enrolled in 1957, at 17. Having shed his Yorkshire accent, he took his first professional theater role two years later, and in 1966 joined the Royal Shakespeare Company. During his 16 years with the company, Stewart developed a reputation as one of the finest thespians of his era and a representative of Shakespeare to new audiences; it was while conducting master classes on Shakespeare in LA, at the invitation of a friend, that he first caught the eye of a TV casting director for Star Trek.

Stewart made his Broadway debut in 1971 in Peter Brook’s A Midsummer Night’s Dream, and appeared again 20 years later in a solo production of A Christmas Carol. The holiday season show, which he revived in 1992, 1994 and 2001, received a Drama Desk award.

Over the course of his career, Stewart embodied many of Shakespeare’s signature roles, including Macbeth, Othello, Prospero, Shylock and Mark Antony. He was the recipient of two Laurence Olivier awards – in 1979, for Antony and Cleopatra, and in 2009 for Hamlet – as well as a Grammy in 1996 for a spoken word children’s album Prokofiev: Peter and the Wolf. He was also nominated for a Tony and four Emmys.

His final major stage role was in 2016, when he appeared as Hirst in Harold Pinter’s No Man’s Land at London’s Wyndham Theatre. In 2023, he released his memoir called Making It So.

Stewart was a vocal fan of Huddersfield Town FC since he was a child and was seen cheering them on most recently in 2024. In a Facebook post from the official team, he was called “a titan of his craft and a legend and hero to millions worldwide”.

Stewart was married three times: to theater choreographer Sheila Falconer, from 1966 until 1990 and with whom he had two children, fellow actors Daniel and Sophie; to Star Trek producer Wendy Neuss from 2000 until 2003; and to jazz musician Sunny Ozell from 2013 until his death. His friend and occasional collaborator Ian McKellen, with whom Stewart starred in two Broadway productions in 2013, officiated his final wedding after a rehearsal.

“I think I’ve been in denial about my life for a very long time,” he said to the Guardian in a 2023 interview. “I never really took on board how successful I had become. Even today I sometimes think, ‘You’re a lucky son of a bitch, Patrick,’ when something unexpected or good happens to me.”
