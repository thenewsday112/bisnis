#675. Nord Stream trial opens in Germany, defense seeks dismissal

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Serhii Kuznietsov is accused of attacking civilian infrastructure in the 2022 sabotage of the Russian gas pipeline to Germany. His lawyers called Nord Stream a military target, and said their client is sworn to secrecy.

The trial of a former Ukrainian special forces officer, Serhii Kuznietsov, opened in the northern German city of Hamburg on Wednesday amid considerable public and press interest.

Kuznietsov is accused of leading the September 2022 sabotage operation that put the undersea Nord Stream 1 and 2 gas pipelines linking Russian to Germany out of action. The pipelines remain inactive four years later, with no sign of plans to ever reactivate them as Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine heads into its fifth winter.

Prosecutors accuse the Ukrainian of participation in a war crime by targeting civilian infrastructure, but his defense lawyers on Wednesday argued that the case should be dismissed entirely on multiple grounds.

A team of three lawyers representing Kuznietsov questioned the premise of the trial in court.

One of Kuznietsov's lawyers, Nicola Canestrini, argued that it was not valid to try the officer on war crimes charges for an attack on civilian infrastructure.

He argued that the pipeline, which provided half of Germany's natural gas imports prior to Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, was ultimately a "Russian weapon," given the importance of energy revenues for funding Moscow's military.

"Is it unlawful to destroy an aggressor's infrastructure without harming any human beings?" Canestrini asked the court.

After the charges were read out, the judge asked Kuznietsov if he wanted to comment, and he said that he did not.

His lawyers argue that this silence extends beyond not speaking in court and say this should be the main grounds for dismissal.

They say Kuznietsov is sworn to secrecy by his home government in Ukraine and so is unable to discuss his case or potential exonerating evidence with anyone, including his legal representatives.

"He is not permitted to speak. His two immediate superiors told him that he does not have authorization to make any statements — not even to his lawyers," defense attorney Jes Meyer-Lohkamp told the court. In German law, Meyer-Lohkamp argued: "Anyone who cannot speak out cannot be convicted."

DW's Matthew Moore was on hand in the court in northern Germany on Wednesday.

There is huge interest in this court case, Moore reported. No sooner had the court doors opened at 07:30 local time in Hamburg than the first journalists, observers and protesters began arriving.

A small pro-Ukraine protest held up a banner saying, "Russia is a terrorist." One participant from Germany told Moore they wanted to remind the world of "the war crimes taking place against the Ukrainian people."

Inside, a Ukrainian student told DW that he and others had made the journey from Kyiv to show support for the defendant, Serhii Kuznietsov.

"Serhii Kuznietsov is a hero," the student said. In the eyes of the German prosecutors, it is another matter. They accuse him of carrying out a war crime. If found guilty of his part in the gas pipeline bombing, Kuznietsov faces a lengthy prison sentence.

The case has been highly politically awkward for Germany — a staunch supporter of Ukraine despite investigators pursuing a trail that led to Kuznietsov and other suspects.

Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has repeatedly denied any Ukrainian involvement in the operation, and had also resisted Kuznietsov's extradition to Germany after his arrest in Italy last year, arguing that there was no case to answer.

German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul declined to comment on the trial on Wednesday, saying that the most important thing for now was for the case to proceed with no political interference or restrictions.

"We will deal with any outcome, and we will, of course, take it into account in our political calculations," he told a press conference in Berlin.

#666. Manchester City, the UAE and the UK's OM in a political tangle

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Nearly 20 years after arriving in England, Manchester City's UAE owners could exit amid cheating charges. That would pose a challenge for a city — and prime minister — closely tied to Abu Dhabi.

Defiance was the word that stood out as Manchester City returned home for the first time since they were found guilty of 114 financial charges by the Premier League in a case that's made headlines around the world.

City showed a measure of it on the pitch, even as they lost 4-2 to holders, Qatari-owned Paris Saint-Germain, in the Champions League group stages on Wednesday.

Among the home fanbase at the Etihad Stadium, DW found universal support for City's Abu Dhabi owners — headed by Sheikh Mansour, who is also UAE's vice president and deputy prime minister.

"I'm all in with the ownership now," said local fan Matt. "If we end up back in League Two [fourth tier], so be it. We've got 15 years of memories we wouldn't have otherwise and to me it's just an accounting line on a spreadsheet really."

The stadium name itself is a reminder of the takeover that changed this club, and its part of Manchester, in 2008. The national airline of the UAE, Etihad's inflated sponsorship of City is among the key planks of the case against them, while many buildings and businesses around the stadium bear the brand. It's a stadium that's also seen plenty of success of late.

"We've seen things other fans will never see," added fellow City fan Josh. "I think people have got to consider how many people this club has given great memories to, how many people's lives have been changed by it, myself included....you've got to be grateful for that."

Though many in sky blue shared those sentiments, most outside the club now view the trophies won in the relevant period as tarnished. On top of that, critics point to City's reticence in providing evidence in their case, the long-documented human rights abuses in UAE and the often dismissive tone with which the owners have dealt with being found guilty of charges they have now appealed.

But those City fans appear to have one powerful ally in British Prime Minister Andy Burnham. Though he has since sought to tone down his position on Mansour and co. somewhat, the former mayor of Greater Manchester said he "would be really concerned to lose them," after the verdict was announced in late September. "They've been such a huge partner in the building of modern Manchester," he added.

While Burnham has since denied being close to City's ownership, even as City were playing on Wednesday, The Guardian reported a club representative was invited on a government-backed trade delegation to India this week by Burnham's successor as Manchester mayor, Bev Craig.

Earlier the same day, Burnham was forced to confirm he had two additional dinners with the club chair, Khaldoon Al Mubarak, at the Etihad Stadium, having previously declared those only as matchday hospitality. It has become a serious domestic issue for the man who replaced Keir Starmer in July.

What is clear is that Burnham welcomed Abu Dhabi investment during his time in charge of the northwestern city. This came mainly in the form of residential and commercial buildings as well as libraries and sports centers, all popping up in previously run-down areas in and around the stadium.

Georgina Blakeley, a Professor at the University of Huddersfield who wrote a book called "The regeneration of east Manchester," told DW that UAE investment has transformed swathes of the city.

"One of the prime drivers for building this partnership with the Abu Dhabi United Group [a private equity arm of the state mostly concerned with the football club] was that at that point in time, 2014, there was no other private investor willing to invest at the scale that the council wanted. And to do so in a way that created a build-to-rent market," she said.

The UAE investment in Manchester, accelerated by the Manchester Life Partnership with the city's council signed in 2014, has been a key defense for the City owners and fans when faced with sportswashing allegations in the past. And while Blakeley believes councils in the UK are often forced to seek foreign investment, there has been some pushback on how east Manchester has changed.

"A lot of people would be critical of some of the developments in [the districts of] Ancoats and New Islington and say that it's gentrified the area and there are some tensions between newer populations coming in and living in the more expensive flats and those already there. I think there's some truth to it. But I also think there's some truth to the fact that people forget really, really quickly that if you go back to just the 1990s, Ancoats was a no-go area," she said.

While profit for both council and state have certainly been a driver of the partnership, Blakeley thinks that forging greater links to the community could be something City's owners see "as a beneficial side effect."

After 18 years in the club and city, the UAE are deeply embedded in this part of Manchester. With political ties between the UK and UAE now reportedly strained as a result of the charges, there are suggestions that City's owners will try to sell the club. They may also be forced to do so, with sanctions still to be decided pending City's appeal.

There is no such trouble at PSG, who look capable of winning a third straight Champions League powered by their own Gulf-State coffers.

What is clear is that state-owned football clubs can become successful fast in a free market, as the Premier League was when the UAE owners arrived. Deep pockets, strong contacts at the top tables of the sport and canny strategies to integrate with the local area can be a heady mix.

It's certainly one that's proved intoxicating to City's fans, who brush off secret second contracts, sham sponsorship and unreliable witnesses with a loyalty built on success, community and investment. Judging by the mood in Manchester on Wednesday, no sanctions are going to change that.

#654. Why Chinese tech brands are outpacing Western rivals from EVs to robot vacuums

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

From action cameras and smartphones to EVs and robot vacuums, Chinese brands are gaining ground worldwide, despite privacy and security concerns. What's driving their rise, and can European rivals keep up?

Do you remember the days when GoPro was synonymous with action cameras?

At its peak in 2015, the US company reigned supreme with around 6.6 million units sold that year and a market share of over 70%. Today, the pioneer is fighting for survival.

GoPro's biggest rivals, DJI and Insta360, entered the action camera market in 2019 and 2021, respectively. Today, they account for more than 90% of global smart handheld camera sales.

Alexander Hannemann, who regularly uses action cameras to film his motorcycle rides, says he switched from GoPro to Insta360 around two years ago.

"The quality is much better than a GoPro, even when I'm speeding up and going into corners at like 80 kilometers per hour," he told DW.

GoPro's case is not an exception. Since around 2010, many Chinese brands have not only caught up but surpassed once-dominant Western rivals across the consumer technology industry, from smart TVs to robot vacuums.

Gone are the days when "Made in China" stood for cheap, mass-produced products.

"China is still making affordable products today," says Chris Pereira, a Canadian entrepreneur and author with over 20 years of experience in China. "But the products are also high quality."

This combination has led to well over half of new consumer tech products globally coming from Chinese suppliers, Pereira adds.

BYD, Xpeng and other Chinese car manufactures sold almost two-thirds of all electric cars last year, surpassing incumbents like Tesla. In robot vacuums, Chinese brands dominate the global market, leaving former pioneer US company iRobot struggling to compete.

So why do Chinese companies increasingly outperform their Western rivals?

Pereira, whose consultancy iMpact helps Chinese companies gain traction overseas, attributes China's success to fierce domestic competition in cities like Shenzhen, Hangzhou and Shanghai; highly integrated supply chains and a can-do business culture that allows companies to innovate; and respond to problems quickly.

Other key reasons for China's competitive edge include unparalleled industrial automation, the strategic use of AI in manufacturing, responsiveness from regulators as well as relatively cheap labor, which together leads to significantly lower production costs and much faster innovation cycles than in the world's most advanced economies.

Joey Cheng, who moved to the US for his PhD studies over 20 years ago, is familiar with both the American consumer and the Chinese producer sides.

While his startup is based in the US, almost all the manufacturing is done in Shenzhen, where a large share of the world's hardware and consumer electronics are made.

"The supply chain in Shenzhen is basically inevitable for cost cutting and quick product development," Cheng says. "Especially as a startup you must get the product out quickly, or you'll get blown out of the water."

Although consumers are flocking to Chinese companies, some reservations persist.

A recent poll found that more than 90% of Europeans do not trust Chinese technology companies with their data, while about 80% distrust US firms. Germans expressed the highest level of mistrust.

Nadine, a German travel blogger who uses DJI and Insta360 products, says she wouldn't use any Asian products at home.

"My home robot vacuum is from a German company for precisely this reason: So the data remains compliant with German regulations. After all, manufacturers can connect directly to the camera," she said, referring to camera and audio hacking incidents involving robot vacuums.

Still, Pereira says that Chinese companies expanding abroad have to pay a "trust premium."

"This trust deficit means that they need to be extra competitive in terms of product and price in order for the market to choose them," he says, adding that the gap can be narrowed by a presence in overseas markets like BYD's first European passenger-car plant, expected to open in Hungary later this year.

Chinese companies say they comply with EU data protection rules. However, domestic security laws in the US and China can require firms to hand over data to authorities, and German officials note limited oversight of whether data is transferred from European servers abroad.

In addition to privacy concerns, Chinese consumer tech firms have come under scrutiny over espionage and other risks, most recently regarding connected devices like EVs.

In light of security concerns, trade deficits and China's overall dominance, the US and several other Western countries have hit Chinese imports and companies with tariffs, blacklists and strict requirements. In late July, the Trump administration banned the import of humanoid robots manufactured outside the US.

A representative of Chinese robot maker Primebot told DW that all Chinese consumer tech companies are feeling the effects.

"We might seek cooperation with a local US company. Another potential solution is building a factory there," he said.

European countries and the EU are also weighing more tariffs against Chinese companies.

Chris Pereira foresees that the tariffs will accelerate two trends among Chinese companies: Moving more manufacturing overseas and an increasing focus on challenging top-tier Western brands with premium goods.

"Chinese officials and companies recognize that manufacturing needs to be localized to a certain degree. One reason is to assuage the challenges of exporting, another is to create trust locally," Pereira sums up.

"The other trend is that once duties wipe out the price advantage at the bottom, the margin left is at the top, so Chinese brands are increasingly competing directly with established Western names, where trust matters most."

As China's dominance keeps growing, Western companies are scrambling to find ways to stay competitive.

Kaz Merchant, who works for a UK-based company that imports and exports IT hardware, says smaller companies, in particular, must carve out a geographic niche.

"They need to have their own unique selling point for a certain product, which China might not have for that region. Or they need to partner with a Chinese company," he says.

To Pereira, a key issue Europe needs to solve is regulators' responsiveness.

"Chinese regulators tell you very clearly and quickly what you can do. That speed helps companies iterate and succeed overseas," Pereira says.

"If you're waiting weeks for a response, you're going to lose business."

#642. Brazil's elections matter for the planet. This is why

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Brazil is heading back to the polls, but the impact could be felt worldwide. With the Amazon vital to the health of the planet, the election could help shape its future.

Few national elections carry as much environmental weight as Brazil's presidential race. And this one is being watched from far beyond the country's boundaries.

Home to the largest section of the Amazon rainforest, Brazil is guardian of a carbon sink of vital importance, making the country's conservation policies globally relevant.

Environmentalists have already voiced alarm at Flávio Bolsonaro — son of right-wing former president Jair Bolsonaro — coming out 2% ahead of incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in the first round of elections.

"It's going to be a second coming of Bolsonaro, therefore a more radical Bolsonaro," said Claudio Angelo, international policy coordinator at Climate Observatory, a network of Brazilian NGOs.

"At the risk of sounding alarmist, I would say Lula's re-election is the only thing standing between us and the end of the Amazon."

The Amazon rainforest stores huge amounts of the carbon dioxide (CO2) released into the atmosphere from the burning of fossil fuels. As a result, it helps regulate the Earth's climate.

For decades, the tropical forest has acted as one of nature's most powerful defenses against climate change. But it has also suffered from widespread deforestation.

During Jair Bolsonaro's presidency, from 2019 until 2023, environmental agencies were weakened and enforcement efforts curtailed, meaning tropical forest was cleared at unprecedented rates.

"The environment agenda was disregarded and the level of control the government had on pollution overall, but specifically on deforestation, was reversed," said Claudio Forner, head of climate policy at Berlin-based think tank Climate Analytics.

Over Bolsonaro's four-year term, an estimated 45,586km2 of the Amazon was destroyed, an area bigger than the size of Denmark. And the forest is not as resilient as it once was, with scientists warning that parts of it are now emitting more carbon than they absorb.

Lula came to office with a message of protection for the Amazon. Besides calling for an end to deforestation by 2030, he increased monitoring of the forest and strengthened Brazil's environmental enforcement agency IBAMA.

Deforestation has plummeted under his leadership, with 2026 set to be its lowest level in a decade. That represents a 41% drop overall compared to Jair Bolsonaro's tenure.

Experts have already criticized Flávio Bolsonaro's record on forest protection. As a senator in 2019, he authored draft legislation that could have stark impacts for the Amazon. While landowners are currently required to preserve 80% of their property for the growth of native plants, the bill would allow them to use their holdings as they please.

"The signal from the president will say 'now you can deforest' or 'it's harder to deforest,'" said Ana Yang, director of the Environment and Society Center at London-based think tank Chatham House.

"The moment that you decrease enforcement capability, deforestation grows," she told DW.

Bolsonaro has also pledged to end illegal deforestation by 2029, a year before Lula. But Claudio Angelo is critical of that message.

"He will end illegal deforestation not by enforcing the law, but by making it legal," he said.

More generally, environmental experts fear Bolsonaro might dismantle many of the protections strengthened under the current president.

"Unfortunately, climate change has become a partisan issue, so rather than offering a solution from their perspective, the right pictures climate change as an invented problem...that regulation goes against economic growth or it goes against competitiveness, that it's the aim of the left to control the means of production," Forner said.

In an interview with Brazilian media in August, Bolsonaro said he regarded extreme weather events to be cyclical and did not believe they were solely linked to human influence.

A Bolsonaro win is likely to see Brazil move away from its position as a climate leader, a role the country embraced last year as it hosted the COP30 climate conference in the Amazonian city of Belem.

But even a Lula victory would not represent a straightforward climate success story, say experts. While the current administration has overseen significant reductions in deforestation, it has also backed plans to expand oil production.

Angelo said the decision to allow exploratory drilling was a "betrayal" of Lula's mandate but added that the president's overall record on climate had been "quite impressive".

"Lula is the world's worst climate leader, except for all the others," he told DW.

The current president will face a runoff election with Bolsonaro on October 25. But while experts say the outcome could determine the fate of the Amazon and the wider efforts to curb global warming, for many Brazilians climate is not high on their list of priorities.

"Ultimately what people care about is access to education, sanitation and health," said Yang. A road built through the Amazon to access those services, while criticized from outside Brazil for destroying parts of the rainforest, would be desirable to voters, she added.

"They don't have time to care. Not because they don't want to. They care about their own survival," she said.

Lawmakers from Germany's Left Party turned their backs on a far-right AfD politician after he questioned the historical consensus that Germany had committed genocide in what is now Namibia. The protest came during a heated debate over reparations for descendants of the victims.

Germany and France reached a provisional agreement on Thursday that backs a scaling down of European emissions rules for new cars largely in line with the wishes of German carmakers, according to a draft document seen by news agencies.

The provisional agreement centers on a European Commission proposal last year backing off on banning new internal combustion engines by 2035, the new deal instead calls for a 90% drop in CO2 emissions from 2021 levels.

The remaining 10% was to be covered by compensatory measures like low-carbon ​steel and alternative fuels.

Now, the new agreement would scrap those measures, calling for the 10% to be compensated by manufacturers.

Europe's car industry, led by Germany, has been struggling in recent years amid the switch to electric vehicles and competition from China. This has led the EU to come up with ways to increase competitiveness while still maintaining pledges to reduce emissions. Germany and France have disagreed on how to do this.

The draft agreement still needs official confirmation. Then it faces approval by Germany's governing coalition led by the Christian Democrats (CDU), who have pushed for more watered-down emissions regulations than their Social Democratic Party (SPD) partners.

Then Germany and France will still need to convince other EU members and the European Parliament for the agreement to take effect.

Alcohol bans came into force at all German train stations when the clock struck midnight and Thursday began.

All the roughly 5,700 stations across Germany, including the train platforms themselves, are now booze-free zones.

Pubs, restaurants and other such hospitality businesses at train stations are exceptions to the rule, though, and can still serve alcohol. Consumption of alcohol will also remain legal on many trains, with long distance ones still serving beers and other tipples at their on-board bistros.

Rail operator Deutsche Bahn says the change aims to make train stations safer and cleaner, and more pleasant places to spend time waiting for a (who knows, perhaps delayed) train.

Various misdemeanors and incidents had prompted discussions on alcohol at stations over the years, but two this year in particular brought the issues into sharp focus.

A 26-year-old security staff member for Deutsche Bahn was beaten and kicked by a drunken passenger when trying to check their ticket. During the struggle the train door buckled and opened, and the Bahn employee fell from the train, suffering severe injuries. Earlier in the year, a train conductor was killed when attacked by a man without a valid ticket.

The new rules are part of DB's house rules, rather than being criminal law — meaning that at least in the first instance it is a matter for Bahn security staff to enforce rather than police.

"The alcohol consumption ban applies at stations every day, also on days with major events," Bahn spokeswoman Anja Bröder said when asked about often beer-fueled train usage days, like when football fans flock to Bundesliga games.

Around 40 stations piloted the project for some time prior to its implementation, including some of the largest like Cologne, Munich, Hamburg and Berlin. DB says it has had positive experiences with the early experiments, reporting more peaceful stations with less litter and travelers and employees saying they preferred the atmosphere.

A federal group dedicated to helping the homeless has criticized the measure, however, saying that for homeless people with addiction illnesses, train stations often served as "important, weatherproof and comparatively safe places" to shelter. A spokeswoman also told the Rheinische Post newspaper that if they were driven from sites like train stations, it would be harder for social workers to locate them.

The children's show "Die Sendung mit der Maus" has been on WDR on Sunday mornings since 1971.

The show has various educational and entertainment elements and has had an array of presenters over the years, but the animated shorts featuring the mouse that separate the segments are probably the best known parts.

These short segments of perhaps 30 seconds of 2D animation never involve the mouse, or its accomplices the duck and the elephant who joined later, speaking.

Typically they interact and try to solve some kind of problem, often seeking a reward, by experimentation and trial and error.

Potato prospects this year are poor after the prolonged hot and dry weather in the summer, according to the government's statistics office Destatis.

"The 2026 potato harvest will be considerably more meager than the previous year, according to forecasts, which will have consequences for the prices of potatoes at various economic levels," Destatis wrote.

In September, Destatis said, potatoes were 11.3% more expensive than in the same month in the previous years. For frozen produce like chips/fries, the difference was less pronounced at 4.7%.

But these figures vastly outstripped both overall year-on-year inflation for September, 3.35%, and particularly the rate of inflation for food and drink products, 0.4%.

Destatis estimated that the 2026 harvest would be almost exactly one-quarter smaller than the previous year — partly because of fewer hectares being used to grow spuds but in larger part because of reduced average yields on the farmed soil in use.

Forecasts predict a yield of around 38.9 metric tons per hectare this year, compared to 46 tons per hectare in 2025.

Mamlok was born on May 6, 1918, into a Jewish family in Berlin. They ran a wholesale wine business, and the family belonged to the vibrant Jewish middle class that formed an important part of Berlin's social and economic life during the Weimar Republic.

But Eva came of age just as Germany was descending into dictatorship. When Hitler became chancellor in 1933, she was only 14 years old. She was also a member of the Socialist Workers' Youth (SAJ). Following the suppression of the left-wing political youth organization later that year, she became active in the underground resistance.

According to testimony from Inge Berner (1922-2012), the sole survivor of Mamlok's later resistance group, Eva climbed onto the roof of a department store on Blücherplatz as a teenager and painted the slogan "Down with Hitler."

"She was caught doing that, though she wasn't arrested at that particular moment — that didn't happen until a year later," noted Weltz-Rombach.

Mamlok first appeared in official records in November 1934, after she was arrested for taking part in commemorations for socialist leaders Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht at Berlin's Friedrichsfelde Cemetery.

Authorities determined that the 16-year-old held "hostile views toward the state" and placed her in "protective custody until further notice" — a euphemism the regime used to justify imprisonment without trial. She was sent to the women's concentration camp at Moringen in Lower Saxony, where she remained until May 1935.

After her release, she continued her anti-Nazi activities despite the increasing danger, facing persecution both as a political dissident and as a Jew.

In these underground circles, she met Pieter Siemsen (1914–2004), and they remained a couple until he emigrated to Argentina in 1937. The only known surviving photograph of Eva comes from his personal archive.

She had a daughter, Tana, in 1939. The father's identity is unknown, however. The child was placed in a Jewish orphanage in Berlin and deported in 1942 to the Auschwitz-Birkenau extermination camp, where she was murdered.

Meanwhile, because she was Jewish, Mamlok was forced to perform compulsory labor at the Berlin firm F. Butzke Schrauben-Industrie in 1940. There she met other women who shared her opposition to Nazism.

Together they formed what later became known as the "Eva Mamlok Group," an informal resistance network of Jewish and non-Jewish women. They distributed anti-Nazi leaflets and wrote slogans critical of Hitler.

They also shared banned books. "They were books that are today part of Germany's educational canon," from Thomas Mann to Lion Feuchtwanger, said Weltz-Rombach. That the Berlin Central and Regional Library (ZLB), Germany's largest public library, stands on the new Eva-Mamlok-Platz is a fitting symbol of this act of resistance and the importance of books, she added.

Mamlok and others in her group were arrested for distributing this forbidden literature. On January 13, 1942, she was deported from Berlin to the Riga Ghetto in occupied Latvia.

According to Inge Berner's testimony, Mamlok remained active in the resistance there too. For example, she arranged for a contact still in Berlin to send her a cake with a miniature camera hidden inside it to document persecution in the ghetto.

In October 1944, she was sent to the Stutthof Nazi concentration camp, where she died in December of that year.

Blücherplatz was named after the Prussian field marshal Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher, whose most renowned achievement was leading his army against Napoleon at the Battle of Leipzig in 1813 and the Battle of Waterloo in 1815.

Several places, including streets and parks, have been named after him in Germany.

"There is no shortage of Blücher memorial sites," said Weltz-Rombach. "And perhaps it's also time to put an end to this old Franco-German enmity — which is, of course, inherent in the name Blücher, given its Prussian tradition — and to establish, in its place, a different form of remembrance."

The square also happens to be the site of the Carnival of Cultures, a multicultural street festival and parade held every year to celebrate Berlin's global diversity.

Having first heard about Mamlok when a Stolperstein commemorating her was laid in 2011, Weltz-Rombach has been researching her biography for the past few years. The Berlin-based filmmaker, producer and curator has also been sharing the resistance fighter's story through various projects, including an exhibition in 2024 and a podcast series with young Berliners.

Since Mamlok was only a teenager herself when she became politically active, the workshop participants can easily relate to her fate.

These teenagers learn about Mamlok's experiences at the actual locations where she was active, helping them imagine what it would be like to be arrested and imprisoned for six months without trial for doing things that normally wouldn't be considered crimes — such as simply expressing an opinion publicly or laying flowers at a commemorative event, explained Weltz-Rombach.

"Above all, she is, of course, a role model — an example of how one would like to act oneself, and how courageous one would like to be."

"[Women's] resistance was often of a more everyday nature, rather than planting bombs," said Weltz-Rombach. Their stories offer examples of "forms of resistance that have only recently received more attention." Other examples include a 2024 exhibition and the launch of a database dedicated to female resistance fighters.

Even though many of the eyewitnesses are now dead, Weltz-Rombach believes there are still many more of these stories that deserve to be brought into the spotlight: "There are definitely many names we do not know, that are waiting to be further researched."

#552. China's influence in the gaming industry is growing

Home to more than 700 million gamers, China is the world's largest gaming market — but it's also one of the most tightly regulated. The effects reach far beyond China, impacting players around the world.

But it is actually from "The Defiant." The game is currently being developed by the Chinese studio Hoothanes Technology in Hangzhou. It tells the story of Chinese forces fending off the Japanese army in the 1930s and 1940s.

Developer Connor Xu tells DW the weapons featured in the game are unique. They were used only in Asia and are therefore unfamiliar to many Western players, he says. The same goes for the story, which Xu says has never before been told in a video game.

"The Defiant" is also designed to appeal to an international audience. In just a few years, China has emerged as a major player in the global gaming industry despite strict regulations, censorship and a former ban on video game consoles.

"Games make life more interesting," developer Xu says. "I can try different roles, different stories, different characters, because we only have one life to live, but we can play a role in another story. That's really attractive, I think, to Chinese users."

People in China primarily play games on PCs and smartphones, while consoles are much less common. There are historical reasons for that: While gamers in Europe and North America debated whether PlayStation or Xbox was the better console in the early 2000s, the devices were not officially sold in China between 2000 and 2014. Chinese authorities said the restrictions were intended to protect children and teenagers from becoming addicted to gaming.

Minors in China are currently allowed to play online games for no more than three hours a week. There are no such limits for adults, but gaming is subject to strict controls. Early on, games released for Chinese players often appeared to be copies of Western titles. At the same time, Chinese tech companies such as Tencent and NetEase invested in numerous Western gaming companies.

One distinctive feature of the Chinese market is its licensing system. "Unlike a lot of the other big video games markets in the world, China has a tightly regulated and licensed official video games market," George E. Osborn tells DW. The journalist advises NGOs and governments on video games and spoke at the Gamescom congress. In his book "Power Play," he describes how authoritarian states use video games to pursue political objectives.

The rule for all games in China is simple: No license, no sales. "That system essentially forces developers who want to release their video games to go through multiple layers of approval from China's Ministry of Propaganda, Ministry of Culture," Osborn says.

Games are prohibited from containing material that threatens China's unity, sovereignty or territorial integrity, among other restrictions. Pornographic content and gambling are also banned. Games may not incite crimes that threaten national security or spread information authorities believe could disrupt public order.

"The whole point around this criteria is that it's deliberately designed to be pretty vague," Osborn says. Whether a game violates the rules is a matter of interpretation. Sometimes games are altered even after release. In the 2025 action role-playing game "Wuchang: Fallen Feathers," for example, enemies based on famous Chinese figures from the Ming Dynasty suddenly could no longer be killed. Instead, they would collapse from exhaustion.

The Chinese action role-playing game "Black Myth: Wukong" became an unexpected international hit in 2024. By then, it was clear Chinese developers could do more than cater to the domestic market — they could create successful games for a global audience as well. The game was inspired by the 16th-century Chinese literary classic "Journey to the West."

Osborn sees nothing wrong with bringing Chinese culture to audiences around the world. The question, he says, is how much the state actually intervenes in the games themselves. "I think in general, video game players are actually quite deeply skeptical of these kinds of things. They are generally very good at spotting when someone might be selling to them or someone might be propagandizing. And so I think if it's too obvious, it won't work," he says.

Because of the licensing system, Western developers also have to comply with government requirements if they want to sell their games in China. And they do want to, Osborn says, because the market is too big to ignore. "But I think when you start thinking about the potential impact this has on creative standards around the world, I think it's really important that developers ask themselves the question of what will we or won't we compromise on here?"

He fears the real danger lies in self-censorship. That could lead developers, for example, to leave queer characters out of their games. That would represent a major step backward, he says, because games help many people explore their identities.

China is not the only country where games are modified to comply with national regulations. In Germany, for example, symbols that violate the country's constitution, such as the swastika, may not be shown in games unless the game in question explicitly contributes to the country's culture of remembrance. Depictions of extreme violence also have to be removed from German versions.

Glorifying violence is prohibited in China, too, yet Xu's team is still allowed to develop a war game. How does this add up? Xu explains it this way: "I think in this game, the most important thing is not violence. The most important thing is to tell history."

Xu says the team is committed to delivering a historically accurate product. Whether it succeeds will be for historians to judge.

Workers in power plants, refineries and the chemical industry are also allowed to work up to 12 hours at a time.

Even Chancellor Friedrich Merz himself seemed unaware of this exception until quite recently. After visiting the UPM refinery in Leuna in September, he recounted to business leaders at the German Employers' Day in Berlin that he was "completely surprised" to learn that the facilities operate on a continuous 12-hour shift schedule, thanks to the approval of the works council.

Merz recounted the shift supervisor describing the work schedule as "really great" — with three-day work weeks. "He said we have plenty of time for our families — it's a great way to organize things." Merz went on to conclude that this demonstrates the benefits of relaxing the Working Hours Act.

"Working more than eight hours is not exploitation. Rather, it can be a good opportunity to organize working hours more flexibly — both for businesses and for the people working there," the chancellor said. "We should do it."

But there is resistance from within the SPD and from trade unions opposed to amending the Working Hours Act. The trade union Verdi argues that significantly longer workdays would have negative consequences for employees' health and would disadvantage people with family and caregiving responsibilities. In certain industries, employees could also be put under pressure to accept longer shifts.

An initial reform draft was drawn up in June 2026 by the Labor and Social Affairs Ministry, headed at the time by Bärbel Bas, an SPD politician who has since resigned for personal reasons.

The draft includes some conditions, including a collective bargaining agreement between employers and unions if any schedule changes were made, as well as health and safety regulations, and working hours to be tracked electronically.

Annika Klose, spokesperson for labor and social policy for the SPD parliamentary group, explained the thinking behind the idea. Flexibility, she said, is "not a one-way street." The coalition agreement explicitly aims to promote a better balance between family and work. "So that it's sometimes possible to take your child to football practice in the afternoon and then continue working afterward — but not that my boss tells me against my will: 'You're working the shift from 9 a.m. to 10 p.m. tomorrow.'"

The CDU/CSU immediately rejected the draft. Businesses were also up in arms. "This draft must be withdrawn and completely revised," demanded Dulger in a statement, saying it was "steeped in mistrust." The German Confederation of Skilled Crafts (ZDH) spoke of a "breach of the commitments agreed to in the coalition agreement." The reform has been on hold ever since.

At the Employers' Day event, Merz held out the prospect of progress. "This is indeed long overdue. I believe we can accomplish this relatively quickly and create flexibility in the labor market."

It remains to be seen, however, whether the new SPD Labor Minister, Alexander Schweitzer, shares this view. In early October, his party released a paper on reforming working hours that read like a declaration of war against the CDU. "Self-determination, Not Deregulation" is the title of the seven-page document that was signed by the SPD working group on labor, the youth organization Jusos, the "60plus" working group and the women's wing of the SPD.

In the paper, the Social Democrats not only reiterate the points that already appeared in Bas' draft back in June. They also demand that, in the future, employers and employees should decide on working hours "jointly and by mutual agreement." They argued that it is not "in the spirit of a modern reform of working time law" if only employers can determine when employees finish work.

From a political perspective, the dispute over reforming work hours has long become more than just a debate over legal provisions. It is a fundamental dispute over how much flexibility workers should be expected to tolerate in the future and to what extent the state should protect them. And it is yet another stumbling block for the already beleaguered government in Berlin.

#507. France: Young voters blase about Glucksmann's Socialists

Raphael Glucksmann wants to lead France's Socialists to power without working with the left-populist Jean-Luc Melenchon, who is particularly popular with young voters. How can the center-left candidate win them over?

The historically working-class Parisian neighborhood of Belleville has gentrified in recent years. It was here that Raphael Glucksmann, a member of the European Parliament for France's center-left Socialists, celebrated his victory in the party's presidential primary on October 11.

Two rounds of voting had been anticipated, but the 46-year-old son of philosopher and essayist Andre Glucksmann triumphed in the first round with a convincing 66% of the vote and will be the Socialist candidate in France's 2027 presidential election.

As he left the Cantine de Belleville restaurant, Glucksmann was flour-bombed by an unidentified assailant. The following morning, he called on his rival, the left-wing French populist Jean-Luc Melenchon, to condemn attacks carried out by his supporters, though it is not clear that the attacker was indeed a supporter of Melenchon's La France Insoumise (LFI).

The incident has highlighted the issue set to dog Glucksmann's presidential candidacy over the coming weeks: How can he win over Melenchon's supporters if he refuses to work with their candidate?

"Where do you stand on Melenchon?" That was the question that dominated much of the Socialists' primary campaign. Party leader Olivier Faure spoke in favor of leaving the door open for cooperation with the LFI, but Glucksmann called for a clear break with the far left. His decisive victory is therefore also seen as a vote in favor of a clear demarcation.

Despite Glucksmann's victory in the primary, his starting position is a difficult one. But the survey was conducted before Glucksmann won the primary, and so does not take into account any potential boost the nomination may give him. Still, 10% would be an improvement on the Socialists' performance in the last presidential election in 2022, when their candidate Anne Hidalgo won just 1.75% of the national vote.

There is also the complication that the Socialists are banking on a man who is not even a party member to lead them from irrelevance. Raphael Glucksmann actually belongs to a small center-left party called Place Publique.

Glucksmann must win over a lot of voters from Melenchon's camp if he is to have a chance of winning the presidency. This is likely to prove a much more difficult task than simply distancing himself from Melenchon.

#498. What Earth's sixth mass extinction event means for humans

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Human activity is driving plants and animals to extinction at an unprecedented rate. As countries meet in Armenia to discuss ways to reverse nature loss, DW reveals how declining biodiversity is already harming people.

From the tiniest microbes in the human gut to giant whales in the ocean, biodiversity is the sum of all life on the planet. It's another word for nature. And because nature is like a team, every player has a job.

When various species from trees to animals work together, they regulate weather, maintain soil health, purify water and support food production. These benefits are known as ecosystem services and they underpin human well-being.

"It is really an abstract concept, but we experience it absolutely every day," said Rebecca Shaw, chief scientist at conservation group WWF. "And we almost never think about it when nature is functioning."

But as biodiversity declines, Shaw and other scientists are calling on the world to really think about it. Because biodiversity is struggling as humans push ecosystems to the brink through intensive farming, burning fossil fuels and pollution from sources like plastic and chemicals.

Over the past 52 years, monitored vertebrate populations have collapsed by an average of 73%, while freshwater ecosystems are faring worse with a drop of 85%, according to WWF's recent Living Planet report.

It tallies with other studies that show a million animal and plant species face extinction unless humanity reduces its pressure on nature.

"It is a really steep decline," said Shaw. "What it's telling us is that nature is in trouble, and it's an early warning indicator that the fabric of life that we depend on for freshwater and food and for clean air is unraveling."

Some 200 countries are gathering in Yerevan, Armenia over the next two weeks for COP17, the latest UN biodiversity summit, to advance commitments to reverse nature's unraveling by 2030. But progress toward those targets remains insufficient, with funding and implementation among the biggest challenges.

Take wetlands as an example. Some 85% of these vital ecosystems disappeared between 1700 and 2000. One study from Canada's University of Waterloo found that habitats like marshes and swamps in southern Ontario provide C$4.2 billion (€2.63 billion) worth of water filtration services each year, removing nutrients like phosphorus and keeping drinking water sources clean.

The study found that as they disappear, water quality worsens, which massively increases the cost of treating it.

Another example is the decline in pollinators like bees and bats. More than 75% of global food crop types rely in part on animal pollination.

At the same time, soil and ecosystem degradation coupled with storms and heat waves linked to climate change are already damaging harvests. A recent UK government report stated that every critical ecosystem is on a "pathway to collapse," warning that nature's decline posed a grave threat to global food supply and geopolitical stability.

"The UK, like many other countries, relies on imports for much of the food and so is reliant on ecosystems around the world," said Louise McRae, a research fellow at the Zoological Society of London, who also contributed to WWF's Living Planet report. "This is why we need to care about global biodiversity."

Nature also provides the basis for many modern human medicines, with some 50% coming from natural compounds.

Penicillin, the first antibiotic, is produced by a mold and is estimated to have saved over 500 million lives. Before its discovery, a person could die from a simple cut. Around 70% of cancer drugs come from natural products or synthetic compounds "inspired by nature," according to the World Health Organization.

As biodiversity disappears, humans risk losing access to undiscovered treatments.

To return to the "nature as a team" metaphor, if a player disappears, sometimes a species with similar functions in the ecosystem can jump in as a substitute.

But not always, particularly if it's a "keystone species." They play a disproportionately large role in their environments, and their disappearance has significant knock-on effects.

The oyster is one such species. Wild oyster populations are facing massive declines, while 85% of their reefs have been lost worldwide. An adult oyster filters up to 50 gallons of water a day.

"No one would ever think they did anything but sit in a shell and be slimy," said Shaw. "But you lose oysters, you can lose water filtration and clean water, you can lose the habitat for many other species that we interact with or sometimes consume."

And, said Shaw, the humble oyster protects coastal communities from storm surges. If you lose it "you lose the coastal protection."

The loss of a single species can also lead to more disease. One study suggests a decline in red foxes in North America is linked to the spread of Lyme disease, a tick-borne illness that also affects humans.

Extinction is a natural process. But the planet has experienced five mass extinctions, when a large proportion of species globally disappeared over a short geological period. The rate at which species are disappearing today suggests Earth is experiencing a sixth event driven largely by human activity, according to scientists.

Both McRae and Shaw called the declines extremely worrying, but said they shouldn't provoke despair.

Though she acknowledges that the underlying global trend is moving in the wrong direction and cannot be ignored, McRae said "the statistics are there to just motivate us to action rather than to cause alarm."

And the Living Planet report also highlights conservation successes, showing that nature can recover.

The number of green sea turtles, for example, had fallen significantly, but has now bounced back. This is thanks to a massive worldwide effort to protect nesting beaches and changes to fishing practices that reduce accidental capture.

Sea otters are another example. Prized for their fur, in the 1800s they were hunted to near extinction on the US Pacific Coast. When they disappeared, it meant sea urchins had no predators, leaving them free to decimate kelp forests.

That matters because as well as providing habitat for other species, these giant underwater groves protect coastlines from storm surges and absorb carbon, making them important for climate protection.

Following a ban on commercial hunting, sea otters have also rebounded, helping kelp forests recover.

"You can see that ripple effect that really had an obvious and a measurable impact. But critically, it can be reversed," said McRae, adding that biodiversity loss and climate change are intertwined and exacerbate each other but have joint solutions.

"We're learning how to turn things around at very small scales," said Shaw. "Now the real challenge is to turn things around at very big scale so we can see it in the global indicators."
