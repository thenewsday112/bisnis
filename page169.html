#1392. What to Bring to a Dinner Party When the Host Says “Don’t Bring Anything”

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Whether you follow instructions or bring a little something, here’s how to be a guest they’ll invite back.

Martha Stewart has a reliable dinner-party gift: “Fresh eggs from my chickens,” she told ELLE Decor. It helps to have a flock, of course, but the rest of us can borrow the principle. Bring something your host will enjoy having around tomorrow, when the guests have gone and there’s no dinner to get on the table.

That’s especially good advice when the invitation comes with a request to bring nothing. There’s no need to arrive with another course or flowers that require immediate attention. If you’d like to bring a small thank-you, the designers and entertaining experts we spoke to have suggestions—and a few reservations about the things guests tend to grab on the way over.

“When a host says ‘don’t bring anything,’ I think they are fairly certain they have enough food and beverage, and probably don’t want excess,” says Amber Mayfield Hewett, founder of event production company To Be Hosted and author of Your Turn to Host. “I wouldn’t bring anything that needs to be served or pressures the host to make accommodations.”

An unexpected dish can put the host in an awkward position, particularly if they’ve planned around guests’ dietary restrictions. Interior designer Christina Nielsen generally avoids bringing food she expects to be served, though she makes exceptions for close friends whose tastes she knows. For them, she might arrive with a freshly baked baguette or pastries. Unless you have that level of familiarity, ask before bringing something for the table.

Nielsen’s suggestions include chocolates, good olive oil, and a bottle of wine intended for another night. Event designer and etiquette expert Hovik Harutyunyan adds coffee, tea, specialty honey, and cocktail olives, as these can all go into the pantry without becoming part of the evening’s preparations.

Choose according to what your host enjoys. Wine is appropriate for someone whose preferences you know, but it needn’t be the automatic purchase for every invitation. Andria Mitsakos, founder and creative director of the brand Anthologist, suggests gifts with a personal connection. She often brings friends things she has found on her travels.

“A gift doesn’t need to be expensive, and it certainly doesn’t need to have anything to do with dinner,” she says. Her other suggestions also include a book the recipient would enjoy or fingerless gloves for a friend who is always cold. “I love gifts that demonstrate you’ve paid attention to someone.”

For a friend whose interiors you know well, Nielsen likes a small vintage object, cocktail napkins, or candleholders. But there’s no need to choose something for the house if you’re less familiar with the owner’s taste—chocolates may be the better bet, she emphasizes.

Flowers seem easy until you’re the one answering the door with a bouquet in your arms and a saucepan on the stove. Nielsen points out that loose stems require a vase, trimming, and arranging while guests are arriving.

Harutyunyan would save them for afterward. Even an arrangement that needs no attention may not suit the room or table the host has prepared. He recommends sending flowers with a handwritten thank-you card one to three days after the dinner.

Arriving empty-handed doesn’t mean you’ve missed your chance. Hewett suggests a handwritten note or a gift card for coffee the following morning. A few specific lines about the evening give your host something they can enjoy without having to unwrap, refrigerate, or arrange it.

While you’re there, be interested in the other guests, enjoy the meal, and offer a helping hand. Hewett says assisting in the kitchen or staying to load the dishwasher can mean a great deal. Take your cue from the host, though—someone who declines help may genuinely prefer you to stay at the table.

“Being a guest is more important than the gift itself,” Nielsen says. “Be engaged and fun!” You can send flowers tomorrow. Tonight, they’ve invited you for your company.

#3441. Jan Frodeno and Flora Duffy on Olympic Gold vs. Ironman Paydays

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This roundtable conversation, facilitated by Triathlete contributor Jordan Blanco, explores the push and pull professional triathletes feel when it comes to deciding whether to pursue the Olympics or other race series for financial gain.

We are two years out from the Los Angeles Olympics, and short-course triathletes are approaching a decision that is both deeply personal and increasingly difficult to justify on a spreadsheet.

That tension was clearly on display at the recent Ironman World Championship in Kona, Hawaii. Taylor Knibb added an Ironman world title to her palmares, even as the American is collecting Olympic qualifying points in the hope of racing a third Olympics on home soil in Los Angeles. Vincent Luis, a three-time Olympian and two-time short-course world champion, won Kona on his debut, having spent much of his career chasing Olympic success before making a late transition to long-course racing.

Others also offered different perspectives. Marten Van Riel described having been stuck in the Olympic pipeline for a long time before finally getting the opportunity to race in Kona. Ironman Hawaii third-place finisher and Olympic silver medalist from Paris 2024, Julie Derron, has not ruled out a return to short-course racing and a place on the Swiss Olympic team in 2028.

Their experiences reflect the choices of a growing number of professional triathletes. An Olympic campaign can consume an athlete’s schedule for years. Qualification requires global travel, points chasing, federation politics, and enough patience to build multiple seasons around a race that offers only three medals and no conventional prize purse. At the same time, T100 and the Ironman Pro Series circuit offer credible alternatives: more races, more ways to earn significant sums of money, and for some athletes, a clearer pathway to build a career in professional triathlon.

Yet reducing the choice to money misses much of what gives the Olympics their power. Jan Frodeno and Flora Duffy built two of the most decorated careers in triathlon, moving between the Olympic and long-course worlds in very different ways. Frodeno’s victory at the 2008 Beijing Games was the first race he had ever won. He later became a multiple Ironman and Ironman 70.3 world champion but remained known far beyond triathlon first as an Olympic champion. Duffy competed at five Games, won gold in Tokyo, and became a national figure in Bermuda. Even after exploring longer-distance racing, the Olympics remained her north star.

Their experiences also complicate the idea that athletes must choose between Olympic meaning and financial reward. Both describe the Olympic title as commercially valuable, although not in the form of race-day prize money. For Duffy, contracts tied to success in Tokyo and the new sponsors she signed afterward made Olympic gold financially lucrative. For Frodeno, being an Olympic Champion is a credential that continues to open doors almost two decades later.

But neither describes gold as an uncomplicated ending. Frodeno found himself struggling for direction after reaching the apparent summit of the sport in his first victory. Duffy returned for another quad because the possibility of repeating victory remained more compelling than a run at Kona.

Triathlete spoke with Frodeno and Duffy separately, then brought their answers together. The conversations have been condensed and edited for clarity.

Jan Frodeno: I come from not just a non-sporting but almost anti-sporting family. My parents had nothing to do with sports, and the only time sport was really valued was when the Olympics were on television. I remember being at my grandparents’ house during the 1988 Olympics. For two and a half weeks, the television was on nonstop, and the athletes who came home were cherished as heroes.

That planted a seed somewhere deep in my subconscious. I was always active and always needed an outlet, but sport wasn’t appreciated in my family at that time. The Olympics gave me a horizon – hope that I could change my stars. It was such an audacious goal that nobody really understood what it meant or could tell me it was completely crazy. It seemed like a parallel world where different people lived.

[The Olympics] gave me meaning and hope for close to two decades, until I actually got there. Triathlon wasn’t even in the Olympics in 1988. Later I met Conrad Stoltz, who went to Sydney for South Africa, and he was my first real Olympic contact. He was just a regular guy who happened to be better at triathlon than other people. Seeing that made the Olympics accessible. It made me realize I wanted to pursue that dream.

Flora Duffy: The Olympics were always the ultimate goal. They were what I dreamed of doing. I saw the Games as the pinnacle of sport when I was growing up. Every four years, I would sit down and watch, and the Olympics would take over.

Sydney was the first Games I clearly remember watching. There was something special about it. The event is iconic, everybody knows about it and it has such a rich history. Maybe part of it is that it happens only every four years. There is scarcity value. The buildup starts years in advance – just as we’re already talking about Los Angeles now.

Duffy: My position is probably different from that of many athletes because I come from such a small place. Bermuda sends an extremely small team to the Olympics: 10 athletes would be a lot. In Tokyo, there were only two of us.

Tyler Butterfield was a few years older than me and raced in Athens. He was the first Bermudian Olympian I could really relate to. He did triathlon, I did triathlon, and he was competing at the Olympic Games. Suddenly I could think, “Maybe I can do that too.” He had also competed at the Junior World Championships and won a medal before I did. Those moments made the pathway feel real.

I later saw what an Olympic medal could do for a small country, and I got to live the biggest version of that by winning gold in Tokyo. It meant so much. It united the island and became this deep, meaningful moment that transcended swim, bike, and run. There are now landmarks in Bermuda associated with me: the track at the national stadium, a pool at my high school, a road, murals. That shows how powerful the Olympics can be.

Frodeno: You go to the Olympics as a national athlete and contribute to your country’s medal table, but you also walk away with the honor yourself. [Being an Olympic champion] is forever, and it doesn’t really matter where you are from. It is the one title that sticks outside the triathlon bubble.

Hawaii is the Wimbledon of our sport. We care very deeply about it, and it is extremely meaningful to me. But when I make a public appearance, I am still introduced as the Olympic champion. That title shines beyond any individual sport.

There is also the experience of going as part of a national team. Triathlon is such an individual sport, but at the Olympics you live with athletes from completely different sports and share the experience with them. One of my roommates in Beijing was Moritz Fürste, the captain of the German hockey team. I won my medal a few days before the hockey team won theirs, and it was wild.

Frodeno: You get one shot every four years. A world championship happens every year – and that is not to take anything away from a world championship – but having one chance in four years exponentially increases the potential for error. Most athletes might get one or two Olympic opportunities in a career. A few get three. Flora went five times, which is crazy.

That scarcity gives it something special. It reaches beyond the sport itself. The title of Olympic champion stays with you in a way that almost nothing does.

Duffy: There are only three medals. If you finish fourth, fifth or sixth, there is no prize money. You leave empty-handed after dedicating the previous four years – or more – to that one event.

At the same time, that uncertainty is part of what makes sport beautiful. You never know what is going to happen on the day. That is why athletes train and pursue the goal. They build their whole careers around chasing that one opportunity.

Duffy: For most of my career, racing short course was quite lucrative. I loved the fast, dynamic style of racing, and I had enough success early on to stay committed to it. Each Olympic build was different, and I was a different athlete in every cycle, so I wasn’t looking around and asking what else I could do in triathlon.

Now there are many more opportunities. Athletes are trying to decide whether to race short-course, T100, or the Ironman Pro Series while also attempting to qualify for the Olympics. The danger is they spread themselves too thin.

Are you pursuing the Olympics because that is your dream and your ultimate goal? Or do you want to focus on T100 and the Ironman Pro Series because you want to make money? For some athletes, that is the goal. They are not interested in the Olympics, and there is nothing wrong with that.

The other side is that short-course athletes may receive federation funding, health insurance, and travel support. They might not be earning a huge amount, but they have enough security to pursue the goal without relying entirely on sponsors or race results. The calculation is more complicated than looking only at prize purses.

Frodeno: I don’t know that I would have chosen differently. Don’t get me wrong: I am definitely economically motivated. But there were lucrative races in my era too. Hy-Vee put enormous money on the line, and I never really chased it.

I think it is fantastic that athletes now have so many possibilities. But more choice can also make the decision harder. Going through a federation can be frustrating and grating because volunteers are sometimes making the decisions that affect your professional career. It can look easier to get a pro license and pursue immediate prize money.

Still, if the Olympics are what genuinely pull you, that experience is not something you can recreate later. It is not something you can read about in a blog after the fact.

Duffy: Standing on an Olympic podium with a medal around your neck is priceless, but it can also lead to more sponsorship and exposure. My situation was unique because my contracts were geared toward Tokyo and winning there. For me, it was a very lucrative day. I also signed new sponsors afterward because I was the Olympic champion.

That success carried into the next cycle. A lot of my sponsors were geared toward Paris and short-course racing, so they were happy that I remained on that path. There was also all this momentum around returning to the Olympics as the defending champion, particularly as someone from a small island.

Frodeno: It changed my circumstances, but the lasting commercial value was not simply cashing in on the medal or earning a certain amount in sponsorship. That fades relatively quickly. The unique thing about being an Olympic champion is that it is a door opener.

That is still valued commercially now, 18 years later. I don’t walk around with my medal. It is more something that people say about you than something you need to say about yourself. But the title carries.

Frodeno: What many people don’t realize is that the Olympics were the first race I ever won. I had never won a World Cup. I went from never having tasted victory to reaching the apparent end state of the game on my first win.

That was wonderful, but it was also the pinnacle. Any World Cup afterward could not compare to three helicopters overhead, the media coverage, and the scale of the Olympics. It also threw me into some of the darkest times of my life because I struggled to find meaning afterward. It was seven years before I won my first world title, at the 2015 70.3 World Championship, and during that period I wondered whether I was a one-hit wonder.

I had thought winning gold would mean arriving at all my dreams. It took me from a miserable little apartment to a wonderful loft, a fancy car, and flying business class. But I didn’t see the writing on the wall soon enough that what I actually loved about triathlon was triathlon itself and the training. It was – and still is – just a love for the sport.

A few weeks before Beijing, I remember completing a brutal session in the rain and telling myself, “If you get through this, you never have to go this hard again.” In hindsight, that was strange, because hard training was what I chose to keep doing for nearly two more decades.

Duffy: Tokyo was hugely meaningful for me, my team and my country. Going into Paris, the intrigue was whether I could do it again. Could I win another medal? Could I defend the title and become a two-time Olympic champion?

The Olympics had always been my ultimate goal. Even late in my career, I never developed the dream of going to Kona and winning. I thought that bug would catch me but it never did.

Now that I am retired, if I ask myself whether I wish I had a proper shot at Kona or a better build into Paris, my regret is that I did not have a better build into Paris. I don’t regret not racing Kona. That says a lot about where the Olympics sat for me.

Frodeno: I went to two Olympics, but I did not have the guts to recognize that my Olympic ambition and campaign were effectively done by 2012. I loved the experience, but I probably should have gone a different way after Beijing. As John Mayer sings in Gravity, “Twice as much is not twice as good.”

It is difficult to give generic advice because everybody comes from a different place. The thing I did well was to listen to my gut. My mother wanted me to study because she was looking after my best interests, but I knew I had to go all in. At the time, almost nobody was making money in Olympic-distance triathlon, but I followed what I enjoyed most and found a way to build a career around it.

Duffy: For many, the Olympic pathway comes first, and then, assuming they remain healthy, they move to middle- or long-distance racing later. They have their Olympic experience, whether it was good, disappointing or successful, and then they begin the second part of their career.

My path was different because the Olympic goal continued to excite me. The possibility of returning for Paris meant more to me than moving fully into long-course racing. That will not be true for every athlete, and it should not be.

This is a hard lifestyle. What is going to get you out of bed to train for several hours a day and deal with injuries? The answer is different for everybody. For me, it was the Olympics.

Duffy: Athletes often go into the Olympics with slightly unrealistic expectations. On one hand, that is good. You have to dream and believe. But if you are not realistic, you can come out the other side deeply disappointed.

Everything is heightened at the Olympics: the pressure, expectations and noise. The buildup is stressful. There is team selection, chasing points around the world, dealing with injuries and trying to arrive at the last minute in the right shape. It is extremely difficult, but it is also extremely rewarding.

Unrealistic expectations can take some of the joy out of racing at an iconic venue and representing your country. I was guilty of that in Paris. It was my fifth and final Olympics, and I already had a gold medal. The result was not going to make or break my career. But I finished fifth and was extremely disappointed, and that disappointment took away some of the joy of being at the Games and watching athletes from around the world.

It is difficult to balance expectations with appreciating the moment. Athletes should dream big, but they should also understand how hard it is to perform on that one day.

Frodeno: Nowadays, the decision is probably harder because there is so much choice. The one thing I did well was listen to my gut instinct. If the Olympics really get to you – particularly with a home Games in Los Angeles – riding through the streets of LA with tens of thousands, hopefully hundreds of thousands of people cheering is priceless. It is not an economic choice. It is about living life to the fullest.

Experiencing the Olympics through your national team and alongside athletes from other sports is a unique construct. It brings a core group of people together in a way that money cannot buy.

The new models released this year are more advanced and easier to use than the previous generation. So, if you’re in the market for a new trail cam you’ll be treated to more features, better photos, and more performance for your money than ever before.

For the most part, cellular trail cameras have taken over the market. These cameras pair with apps and send images and videos directly to your phone. There are now several excellent cell cam options in the $100 to $150 range. This is on par with what a decent standard camera costs (but of course, you also have to pay for the data plan with cell cams). That’s why we focused most of our review on cell cams.

But not all hunting areas get cell reception and not all states allow the use of cellular trail cameras for hunting. With this in mind, we’ve included two great standard trail camera options.

We’ve also noticed that there’s a growing category of security cam and trail cam hybrids. These cameras promise a live video feed from the woods. In our field testing, however, most of these cameras didn’t perform up to our standards, and only one of them made the cut for this review.

Our trail camera testing really never ends. We’ll continue running these cameras and new additions throughout the fall. In this review, we’ve included both brand-new cameras and the top-performing cameras from previous seasons.

We put each camera through a consistent test protocol to evaluate trigger speed, detection range, image quality, and reliability. Here’s how we did it.

Each cellular trail camera was set to its fastest shooting mode and highest sensitivity. Markers were placed at 10 feet, 60 feet, and 110 feet. The tester walked past the camera (from left to right, then right to left) at a medium pace at each distance. The walk-through test was then repeated at night. The test is meant to measure the camera’s detection range, trigger speed, shutter speed, and flash range. We looked for blank photos, failures to trigger, blurry images, and overall photo quality.

From there, we set each camera in the field to see how it performed in real-world conditions. In the field, we evaluated the camera’s photo quality, reliability, and ease of setup. This included the ability to use each camera’s app to review and sort photos. Some of these cameras were field tested for an entire hunting season. Others are so new that they’ve only been in the field for a few weeks. We’re constantly testing the latest cameras and will update this review later this fall.

The video quality wasn’t great overall, and it was especially poor at night. What makes it worse is that the IR light over exposes the image and then adjusts down to a good setting. That blooming effect cost me a clear look at what I think is a stunner buck. One more knock on the night performance is the IR doesn’t illuminate brightly beyond 10 feet. A unique feature is that you can run a white light in video mode for color video. You can also remotely turn on the white light while viewing the live stream.

I think this trail and security camera hybrid has limited use cases for hunting, but in the right scenario it could be an asset. Probably the best use for it is to position it over a feeder. Before you walk into the stand over the bait site, you can turn on the live feed and use the camera panning feature to scan the area. If you’re in a state with hogs, you could use the camera’s white light as a feeder light. —S.E.

Cellular trail cameras offer the most convenience because you can hang them and leave them in the woods until the batteries die — all while getting near real-time scouting information sent to your phone. That saves time and gas. More importantly you’re not bumping deer and leaving scent by going into the woods to check camera cards. The main downsides are that you need cell service in the location you’re placing the camera, and cell cams come with additional monthly costs. Cell camera plans typically range from $4 to $15 per month, with additional charges for downloading full-length HD videos or hi-res photos. Cellular trail cameras are more tightly regulated with some states allowing standard trail cams but banning the use of cell cams.

Standard cameras don’t have monthly fees and have longer battery life. They also rival cell cams in photo quality. Of course, the main downside is that you have to physically check the cameras, and the data you’re getting is old.

Just like cameras used for photography a more expensive camera usually produces a better image and has more features. We’ve provided sample photos for each camera to help you see the difference between a $40 Tasco, a $150 Tactacam, and a $600 Reconyx.

Most hunters will either buy the cheapest trail cam and replace it every few years, or buy one in the $100 to $150 range. We’ve found that above $200 you start to lose value for your dollar, and running multiple cameras becomes very expensive. There are now several great cameras in the $100 to $150 range.

There are pros and cons for each type of camera flash. Here’s a quick and dirty break down.

We’ve looked into if trail camera flash spooks deer and after talking to biologists and hunters, several of them prefer white flash and feel it spooks deer less often. They especially like the color images provided by a flash. However, most of the best trail cameras are not offered with white flash.

Low-glow IR cameras produce a visible red glow from the infrared emitters when they take a picture or video at night. The red glow is typically faint, but can be seen when looking directly at the camera. These cameras typically take superior night images compared to the no-glow cams. Many hunters and experts believe the flash can be detected by deer.

No-glow cameras like the Tactacam and Stealth Cam have black filters over the infrared emitters to hide the red flash. These cameras are sometimes described as black flash cams. No-glow cams are much less likely to be detected by deer or humans at night, but they typically produce lower-quality nighttime images. However, Stealth, Tactacam, and Reconyx have proven to be exceptions to this rule.

There is some controversy in the hunting community about using cellular trail cameras to hunt big game (make sure to check your local regulations before buying and using a cellular trail camera). In 2022 the Boone and Crockett club revised its policy in regard to cell cams, stating: “The use of any technology that delivers real-time location data (including photos) to target or guide a hunter to any animal in a manner that elicits an immediate (real-time) response by the hunter is not permitted. ‘Real time’ is the key concept. Seeing a photo and harvesting an animal a few hours later, or even the same day, uses this technology to assure a kill. It also takes advantage of the animal, which cannot detect impending danger from a camera. Waiting several days, or even until the following season, to pursue an animal captured on camera is different, and would not be deemed an unethical use of a trail camera.”

Right now, I don’t see many hunters using cell cams to capture images of a buck on a food plot or corn pile and then running out there to kill him that moment. As live-feed video in cellular trail cam technology becomes more advanced and wide spread, however, it could be come a problem for fair-chase standards. More states will likely look to restrict the use of this technology for hunting. It’s up to hunters to use the tech responsibly. At their best, trail cams can provide consistent data on deer movement and hunters can interpret that data when making decisions on where to hunt. Most of us have limited days to hunt, so picking a prime spot is critical. But we must still use our woodsmanship, hunting knowledge, and fair chase principles. I think the trail cameras can only help with that. —A.R.

With modern trail camera technology, we don’t have to guess if deer are in the area or what time they’re moving. When used consistently, trail cams can paint a vivid picture of deer activity patterns in your area. But trail cameras are also notorious for malfunctioning, bugging, and dying in the field. So if you want to get serious about patterning the deer you hunt, do yourself a favor and get one of the best trail cameras available. Actually, get a few of them.

The latest for gun hunters and competitive shooters.

Outdoor Life’s shooting editor John B. Snow cut his teeth hunting Michigan’s Northwoods in his youth. Now, all these years later, he lives in Bozeman, Montana—the beating heart of big-game hunting in the West. He shoots in precision rifle competitions and, quite frankly, is a total long-range shooting nerd.

So, I’m counting on Snow to unite the two divided camps of deer hunters: those who hunt in the big woods with grandaddy’s old rifle vs. those who hunt in open country with the latest and greatest rig. We recently sat down to discuss and dispel some of the biggest myths around deer rifles, cartridges, and shooting. As editors and deer hunters, we hear the following misconceptions all the time. Here’s why they are total B.S.

The 6.5 Creed isn’t really overrated or underrated, it’s just misunderstood. It is not an ultra-fast, flat-shooting, physics-defying, super cartridge that some of its proponents make it out to be. Likewise, it’s not some pipsqueak of a cartridge that’s incapable of cleanly killing big game and is only popular because hipsters and metrosexuals think it’s cool.

In reality, the 6.5 Creed delivers projectiles with high ballistic coefficients from a smartly designed case that embodies all the elements of modern cartridge design. While classic 6.5 cartridges can be modified to incorporate most of what the 6.5 Creedmoor brings to the table, they aren’t able to deliver the whole package. The fact is we know more about how to design a better cartridge than ballisticians did in decades past. And the 6.5 Creedmoor’s track record in competition and in the field bears that out. Over years of rigorous testing, it’s proven to be an incredibly accurate cartridge in general.

“The 6.5 Creedmoor is an extraordinarily capable big game round and is more than sufficient for any whitetail that trots across the face of North America,” says Snow, who has hunted with the 6.5 Creedmoor all over the world and killed giant eland (which can weight 2,000 pounds) with it. “It’s a wonderfully balanced and inherently accurate round. It’s easy to shoot, has relatively mild recoil, and it’s also the type of cartridge that promotes and encourages people to practice and become better marksmen.”

In other words, it’s an ideal cartridge for medium-sized game like whitetail deer.

“Most people think about performance purely in terms of muzzle velocity,” Snow says. “They’ll say ‘well, this cartridge spits out this bullet faster, therefore it is a better performer.’ But that’s not a very sophisticated way of looking at performance. There’s more that goes into it than just muzzle velocity.”

Read Next: Why Small Cartridges Are So Effective for Big Game

“I’ve been guilty of using the term ‘knockdown power’ in the past, but that’s really a misunderstanding of terminal ballistics,” says Snow. “There’s no such thing as knockdown power with a cartridge.”

That’s because bullets kill in only two ways: they take out the central nervous system (a shot to the spine or brain), or through blood loss through tissue damage (known as exsanguination).

“How the bullet behaves within the target and also where that bullet is placed in the target define how effectively a bullet kills. The bullet doesn’t knock the animal down,” Snow says. “So if you start to think of the terminal effectiveness of a cartridge in those terms, it kind of shifts it a little bit too. Bullets that are a little smaller, driven at moderate velocities can do as much damage, in some cases even more, than a big boomer.”

But what about that time you watched granddaddy drop that big 10 pointer in its tracks with one shot from his old .30-06? Isn’t that knockdown power?

Actually, no. Animals will drop for a variety of reasons, but they’re not being knocked off their feet. Plus, every shooting scenario is unique. Sometimes a well-hit animal will run 100 yards before dropping (even if it was hit by granddaddy’s old .30-06). These personal experiences in the field all serve as impactful anecdotes, which work to form strong opinions among hunters, but they are not useful data.

Read Next: Debunking the Infamous Rifle Knockdown Power Myth

OL staff writer Tyler Freel did an in-depth test on the “brush busting bullets,” which you can read about here: The Best Brush Busting Bullets Put to the Test. His takeaways were pretty straight forward: the .45-70 Govt. is by far the best busting round; and mono-metal, copper bullets (like Black Hills’ HoneyBadger) deflected the least when shot through brush.

With that said, all bullets did deflect to some degree. And since most deer hunters aren’t shooting .45-70 these days, none of us should shoot through brush if we can avoid it.

“Except for very rare circumstances [like for a follow-up shot] we should not be shooting through brush at all,” Snow says. “It will deflect and possibly damage the bullet and introduce all sorts of vectors of chaos into the shot. Even with the .45-70 I wouldn’t take that initial shot through the brush.”

They just don’t make rifles like they used to—they make them much better.

“Look at the introduction of precision CNC machining, which is now commonplace in the gun industry,” Snow says. “It’s allowed companies, large and small, to manufacture barrels and actions to such a degree of perfection that you can literally buy the components yourself, screw them together and have a really high performing rifle. Whereas if you look at a classic Remington 700 from back in the day, their ability to control even the head spacing on the barrel was so poor that they would have a series of bolts to try to mix and match to get it to close correctly on the gauges they used to deem the rifle in our out of spec.”

But there is one thing that those old wood-stocked deer rifles did have going for them: craftsmanship and hand checkering.

“One of the things that’s been sad to see is that we have lost some of the craftsmanship, specifically in terms of checkering and nice wood stocks,” Snow says. “There’s still high-end gunmakers doing really nice checkering, but it is a bit of a dying art. We don’t have the benefit of that with our modern guns as everything has gone toward synthetics or in some cases laminates. The checkering such as you do see if often executed by machine and it looks ok—but you can tell that it’s been done by machine.”

Choosing the best deer cartridge used to be an important task. But today, bullet selection is really more important than cartridge selection.

“People do talk about cartridge selection a lot and certainly back in the day—rewind the clock 30 or 40 years—and we had a good variety of cartridges and a really minimal selection of bullets,” Snow says. “And those bullets weren’t very good, for the most part. Now we have these amazing bullets to choose from, which have cropped up in the last 20 years. So when looking at a hunting scenario, the bullet selection question really trumps the cartridge question. There are certain types of scenarios when one cartridge will outperform another, but take out the extremes and it comes down to bullet selection. And, we’re lucky for that to be the case.”

Many rifle companies promise “sub-MOA accuracy” out of the box and some will even send you a shot target with group that measures under an inch. But as Snow wrote about last month, those sub-MOA guarantees don’t really mean much.

“I think the whole obsession with the targets and the sub-MOA stuff has really clouded the reality of what even competition shooters need, let alone deer hunters,” Snow says. “We shoot thousands of rounds through dozens of guns every year [for review of the best rifles]. We have our 5-shot-group protocol, so we have a huge data set to look at. And really, with most hunting rifles and common factory ammo, if it shoots 1.25-inch, 5-shot groups, it’s a damn good rifle.”Plus, a rifle is only as accurate as the hunter who’s shooting it. So spend less time worrying about your deer rifle’s theoretical accuracy, and more time practicing for better accuracy in the field.

Bruce is the longtime owner of Becharof Outfitters (becharof.com). We met 19 years prior, when he invited me to film a couple episodes for an outdoor TV show I hosted. Four years after that we took the biggest brown bear of my life, a 10’ 9” behemoth.

In 2017 a buddy and I joined Hallingstad for a moose hunt up the King Salmon River, northeast of the village of Egegik. We shot two exceptional bulls. At that time my back problems had intensified. I struggled to even walk, let alone pack meat. I figured it would be the last moose hunt of my life. But two back surgeries later, I felt like a new man.

“I’m thinking of selling the operation in a year or two, so why don’t you come up for one last moose hunt this fall, just you and me,” Bruce offered last spring. He had battled some health issues, himself and now he wasn’t faring as well as we’d hoped when we’d set our plans in the spring.

“Don’t worry, I’ll have you head up to Carl’s camp for your moose,” Bruce continued as we sipped coffee, gazing out the big window overlooking Alaska’s Egegik Bay. From the old house I could see where many fond memories were made.

“There are two clients in that camp, so you can help them out, pack meat, and when they’re done you’ll hunt,” Bruce said. “It’s our farthest camp upriver, lots of moose move through later in the season, as the rut approaches. Fifteen days will be plenty.”

The day before the moose opener, Carl Adams, Tom Roll—another stud of a guide—myself and two clients, Peter and Stan, arrived in moose camp. The 23 mile boat ride quickly passed. “Okay guys, we’re camping in thick alders and moose can be around us at any given time,” Adams said before we hopped out of the boat. “We need to whisper all the time and be sure to have your rifle because brown bears are thick in here.”

Both clients were well into their 70s. It was their first time to Alaska. Peter was nearly deaf. Stan, not much better. Every word they spoke was just below a shout. “Guys, inside voices, remember,” Carl encouraged more than a half dozen times in the first hour.

I glassed nonstop for 13 hours each of the first three days. In that time, Peter spent less than an hour looking for moose. He stayed in the cook tent, drinking coffee and talking to anyone who walked by.

On the evening of day two, Adams spotted a big bull across the river. “Peter, you’ll want to come take a look at this bull,” he said. It was 8:00 p.m. Peter had been in bed for an hour. An hour of daylight still remained. “Is it big?” Peter asked. “He’s pushing 60 inches,” Adams replied. “Is that good?” Peter wondered.

Adams went to Peter’s tent and whispered, “Get your waders on, we can cross the river and sneak to within 300 yards of the bull, maybe even shoot it on the river bank.”

“I’ll just shoot it tomorrow,” Peter replied.

Adams tried to urge Peter to change his mind as he knew the opportunity at stake, with three tags to fill. “Stan, do you want to shoot it?” Adams asked. “Oh no, I’ll keep going on boat rides up river with Tom; any bull around camp, Peter can shoot.”

My spotting scope was set up so I captured several photos of the big bull, along with some videos. “Here, go show this to Peter, that’ll make him get up,” I whispered to Carl as I handed him my phone.

But the hunter’s response was, “I’ll just look at it tomorrow,” not even unzipping the tent fly.

The bull got to 517 yards. I had my rifle set solid in the tripod shooting sticks and the scope on 18x. “Peter, all you have to do is come out of the tent, walk 15 steps, shoot Scott’s gun, and you’ll have your dream Alaska moose,” Adams tried convincing him, one last time. “That’s too far,” Peter answered. Adams sensed my frustration.

“That’s part of being a guide,” Carl shrugged. “It’s the client’s hunt, not mine. Sometimes these hunts aren’t about filling a tag.”

The next morning Peter drank coffee and talked loud as ever for three hours. I sat on a knob glassing for moose nearly 200 yards away and could hear every word he uttered. Tom took Stan to another spot by boat that morning. Not until almost 10:30 a.m. did Peter ask about the bull from the previous night. When I finally showed him the video, Peter said, “Wow that’s big but I don’t really care if I kill a moose, I’m just here to have fun and hang out with you guys.”

“I’m not shooting a moose until you two tag out, and that bull cost us valuable time,” I responded. “There’ll be more,” Stan hollered, stumbling back to the cook tent, tipping the bottom of his coffee cup skyward.

That afternoon, Stan told me he didn’t care if he killed a bull, either. Here were two clients paying $30,000 each to hunt moose and they didn’t care if they shot a bull. As a full time outdoor writer of nearly 30 years whose career has been built on consistently killing game, it put me in a bad place.

All of Hallingstad’s moose camps are equipped with Starlink. Having lived for several years in remote Alaska, and traveled much of the state for the past 36 years, I think it’s safe to say that this technological advancement is a game changer. It greatly alleviates the stress of being out of touch with family and it increases people’s safety in many ways.

On day five, I was glassing for moose from camp when I got a call from Bruce. “Elise just got her hunter a bull, so I’m going to have you move down with her,” he shared. I was elated.

Elise Adams is Carl’s daughter. She’s 23 years old and had just finished nursing school, where she graduated at the top of her class. I’ve known Elise since she was four years old and I watched her grow up on the Egegik River. She runs her own commercial fishing boat, has guided salmon anglers for nearly a decade, and successfully guided hunters to six brown bears in recent years, including two over 10 feet. This was her first solo guided moose hunt, and she was the first in five camps to tag out.

Before I could hang up the phone with Hallingstad, I got a call from Tiffany, my wife, who was back home in Oregon. I ignored it. She immediately called again. I was still getting details from Hallingstad. Then Tiffany texted me. “Call me NOW!”

She was bawling and could hardly speak. I feared something had happened to one of our two sons, or our aging parents. “It’s Kona, he has stomach twist and they have to put him down.”

Kona, our prized Pudelpointer, was in his prime, only 10 years old. I’d hunted with his dad, Lon, the most decorated Pudelpointer ever to set foot on American soil. Until Kona, Lon was the best dog I’d hunted with. Kona ran circles around anything Lon ever did. He was that once-in-a-lifetime dog for me, and I’ve owned a number of them. Most were good. Kona was exceptional.

Kona survived stomach twist when he was four. We were minutes away from losing him, then. This time it struck in the intestines. Though Tiff got Kona to the vet with plenty of time, no tests, or even x-rays, pointed toward torsion. By the time it was figured out, it was too late.

I spent the next two days in my tent, alone. I didn’t want to see or talk to anyone. I tried getting home but couldn’t because the river was too rough to navigate in the extreme winds. I cried more than I can ever recall. I couldn’t eat for two days. An 80-inch moose could have stood outside my tent and I couldn’t have cared less.

I don’t know what was worse, knowing Tiffany was dealing with Kona, alone, or being helplessly stranded in isolated Alaska. I’ve survived sinking boats, a downed bush plane, a submerged Argo, getting lost in storms and being stuck for days in 45º below zero temperatures in total darkness. But this was the most desolate Alaska had ever felt. I’d never felt so helpless and trapped by the wilderness.

Carl consoled me. He was right, being a guide is about more than just filling a tag.

Finally, two days later, conditions calmed and Elise made it upriver to get me. By now, the last place I wanted to be was home. I wanted to hold my wife, but I couldn’t yet face being home without Kona. He was always at my side, his spirits high. He knew what I was going to do before I did it. We spent every day together. He hunted for upland birds, waterfowl, sheds, squirrels and was great at it all. It is not an exaggeration to say that he was my best hunting partner.

“I’m so sorry about your dog,” Peter told me in a firm voice as I left camp, putting his hand on my shoulder. “People don’t realize how hard it is losing a truly special dog,” he continued. “My wife recently passed away, then, right after that, my dog of 14 years did too. It was the best hunting dog a man can ask for,” he told me. “It’s hard but you’ll make it through this.”

That’s when I realized that amid my selfishness of trying to get a moose, I’d failed to hear Peter’s full story.

“Now, go get a big bull,” Peter said. “I don’t care if I shoot one.”

That afternoon, Elise was preparing dinner when my wife texted me with more news. “She’s okay, but your mom is in the hospital with heart issues. She’s getting ready for a long surgery. Send prayers.”

By now our oldest son had flown home to be with Tiffany. Between them and my dad, Tiff’ assured me they had things under control and that there was nothing I could do, even if I was there. “Just do what you need to do to get through this,” she followed up. Tiffany is the strongest, most caring woman I’ve ever met. We’ve endured a lot, together.

After dinner, Elise and I hiked to a high point to glass until dark. Exactly 2.18 miles away, we saw a bull moose with the naked eye. It was standing across an expansive, marshy flat and its white antlers looked like two sheets of plywood. “That’s the biggest bull I’ve ever seen,” Elise said. I agreed.

“If you’re up for it, we’ll be here at daylight, try to find that bull, then make a move on it,” Elise suggested.

The next morning we found the bull, half the distance away as the evening prior. It was in a swamp surrounded by 30-foot-tall alders. “It’s impossible to get close to that thing in this wind,” Elise said. “Let’s watch and try to see where it goes.”

After 15 minutes, the bull lay down. We watched the spot for 12 hours but never saw it move.

The following day, the bull was closer to where we’d first seen it. “Let’s get Greg and go after that thing,” Elise decided. I again agreed with my young guide.

Greg Zackar is a native kid from the village of Igiugig, Alaska, near Lake Iliamna. Elise comes from a native lineage, too.

This was a dream I so wanted to relive. I needed it.

In 1989, I went to dinner with Tiffany. We grew up together, had known each other since first grade. Our parents and grandparents went to school together. We were fresh out of college, each with teaching degrees. “What are you going to do now,” Tiffany asked. “I’m moving to Arctic Alaska to teach and learn to hunt and fish with the Eskimos,” I told her. (That was a politically correct term back then, in fact, they were proud to be recognized as such). “Well, you better do it now while you’re single because no woman will ever follow you up there,” she replied.

Four months later, we were engaged. Four months after that, we were married and living in Point Lay, Alaska, a village of fewer than 100 Inupiat people at the time. That was 36 years ago.

For the next seven years, I hunted, fished, and trapped with the indigenous people of the high Arctic. Those were some of the most memorable times of my life. The outdoors connected our two worlds. We shared a common passion in a harsh, yet glorious part of the world that few people will ever truly understand. Out there, the genuine nature of the native peoples shone. They’re among the most caring, giving, hard-working cultures I’ve met. Elise and Greg carried the same values.

Moose or no moose, just being with those two kids helped heal me. Many hours were spent glassing, exchanging only smiles. That’s what I needed. No words. No condolences. Just an occasional, heartfelt smile I knew meant something.

Following a five-mile boat ride and a four-mile hike, Elise, Greg, and I were glassing for the bull from an elevated knoll. We eventually spotted it in the bottom of the swamp, 1.4 miles away. We hiked over 12 miles that day in some of the most demanding terrain imaginable. I’ve been fortunate to make a living hunting big game around the world. Of all the habitats I’ve been in, the tundra ranks as the most challenging to navigate. More times than not, it’s simply impassable. This was one of those times.

On the last of our seven days together, the wind was finally right to make a move on the big bull. We towed a raft downriver, paddled 1.25 miles up a slough, hiked another mile through a bog and dense alders, and got to where we’d last seen the bull. Fresh rakes on tattered alders were prevalent, and the stench of bull urine in multiple dry wallows permeated the air. Then the winds picked up.

In a matter of minutes, swirling gusts up to 40 mph left us helpless. We tried calling, but the wind was too powerful. We hiked in circles amid thick alders, trying to locate the bull. With visibility through the dense cover being less than 15 yards most of the time, our efforts were futile.

We’d put all our efforts into one bull and came up empty. I could have shot two lesser bulls, but didn’t. One hunter in camp shot the first bull, a 60-incher that appeared tiny compared to the monster we were after. Carl informed us Peter had shot a bull on day 10, and Stan filled his tag on day 12. I was happy for them.

Returning home to Oregon was hard, much harder than anticipated. Thankfully, my mom was recovering from heart surgery, slowly. But the house was so empty, so calm. With upland season in full swing and waterfowl season about to kick off, I felt lost. I still have Echo, Kona’s half-sister, to share hunts with. But the clock is chasing her, too. She’ll be 13 years old soon. Together, we’ll hunt when we can this season. It won’t be easy, but we’ll make the most of it. I owe that to her.

Kona took a piece of me with him. There’s a void in my heart I fear will forever remain. But he left me with so much more than what I had before he came into my life. Looking at pictures of Kona and all the adventures that we had together is painful. But those memories are what help heal me. For all who knew him, Kona made the world a better place. He made me a better man, and that’s the ultimate reward of getting to share time, be it brief, with a hunting dog you know can never be replaced.

Naturally, this collected look couldn’t happen overnight. Nearly two years before the couple moved in, they started acquiring furniture and decor from artisanal boutiques, antique shows, and international markets. Travel played a huge role in the overall design language; a range of small objects and larger heirlooms they’ve accumulated over the years fill every room. Before breaking ground on the project, the couple and their three children even spent several weeks in Mexico City, a trip which helped reinforce their love of layered and imperfect interiors.

Butcher led the interior design, selections, and documentation for the project, while Kale was heavily involved in sourcing materials through his relationships with makers and suppliers around the world—a benefit that comes with the duo owning Riad Tile. “We spend so much of our lives thinking about materials—where they come from, how they are made, and the people behind them,” Butcher explains. “Building our own home allowed us to use those materials very personally and, in some cases, a little unexpectedly.”

As anyone can imagine for a couple who own a tile company, intentionally sourced stone for countertops as well as layers of handmade tiles can be found in most of the rooms, the unique materiality introducing imperfect dimensionality into each space. They had to think of these architectural details early on in the building process—patina and texture couldn’t solely be added through the furnishings.

“We used reclaimed wood, aged and natural stone, handcrafted tile, wide-plank oak flooring, and materials that inherently have variation,” Butcher says, adding that the entry and living areas specifically incorporate reclaimed fir overhead. One look inside the home now, and no one would believe it was a new build. It’s a personal haven for the family of five, filled with items and elements that represent the couple’s lives, from their childhoods to their travels and their business. Craftsmanship is held in the highest regard and authenticity reigns, but nothing feels too polished.

“Our children live here. Friends are constantly here—our children’s friends are over a lot,” Butcher says. “Things get moved and worn and used. Inside this tiny room, Butcher chose a transportive theme. #2271. Lebanon: Amend Media Law to Protect Free Expression

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Lebanese lawmakers at the parliament building in downtown Beirut, January 9, 2025. © 2025 Hussein Malla/AP Photo

(Beirut) – Lebanon’s parliament should amend the recently-adopted media law to fully remove all provisions in Lebanese law that restrict the right to free expression, Human Rights Watch said today. Parliament passed the law on August 11, 2026, but a new draft law introduced by 10 members of parliament on October 5 offers amendments to the law that would resolve its key problematic provisions.

“Given the history of public officials and powerful individuals abusing Lebanon’s laws to clamp down on journalists, activists, and artists, parliament should act to prevent further abuse,” said Ramzi Kaiss, Lebanon researcher at Human Rights Watch. “No one should face abusive retaliation for peacefully expressing their views, and parliament has a chance to end these abuses right here and now.”

The media law, which parliament adopted after 16 years of deliberation, includes significant improvements. Some of the key advances include ending pretrial detention for free expression-related crimes committed online or through media-related means. The law establishes individuals’ right to create online media platforms or issue publications without prior state authorization and limits the military court’s jurisdiction over expression-related crimes committed online or through media-related means. It also decriminalizes defamation and insult in certain cases, transferring them to civil court jurisdiction.

The law also includes provisions that would promote media independence, including by prohibiting undue interference in journalists’ work, promoting media workers’ rights to unionize, protecting sources’ anonymity, and limiting the ability of security services to search and seize journalists’ electronic equipment and documents.

The adopted media law, however, includes a provision, article 104(b), which criminalizes the “intentional fabrication of falsehoods and dissemination of false and harmful news” providing for a sentence of up to three years in prison. For decades, Lebanese journalists, artists, media organizations, and civil society groups have repeatedly faced lawsuits based on criminal defamation charges and other vague legal provisions in retaliation for their work.

This provision, which includes vague and undefined terms such as “harmful news,” risks enabling continued threats of criminal proceedings against journalists as well as such lawsuits.

While the adopted media law decriminalizes defamation and insult in some cases, provisions found in Lebanon’s Penal Code, which the law does not mention, annul, or make inapplicable, would continue to criminalize libel, slander, and insult directed against a foreign state or its president, representatives, army, flag, or emblems. 

Such provisions also criminalize defamation against Lebanon’s president, state institutions, and army, among others, imposing prison sentences of up to two years. Provisions of the Lebanon Military Justice Code, which also remain in effect, include the criminalization of “publicly disparaging the flag or the army or infringing upon its dignity, reputation or morale,” imposing a prison sentence of up to three years.

The amendments sought in the October 5 draft law would annul article 104(b) of the media law and make these articles, in both Lebanon’s Penal Code and Military Justice Code, inapplicable to cases covered by the media law, including for both media-related or online forms of expression. 

The amendments would also limit security forces’ ability to interrogate and investigate media professionals for expression-related crimes, limiting these powers solely to the public prosecution’s jurisdiction. Human Rights Watch has repeatedly documented violations, including, physical and psychological interrogation tactics and bias by Lebanese security agencies in enforcing criminal defamation laws against journalists and activists. 

Since Lebanon’s media law only applies to acts of media-related and online forms of expression, Lebanon’s parliament should fully repeal all provisions, including in Lebanon’s Penal Code and Military Justice Code, related to criminal defamation or insult, Human Rights Watch said. It should make any such cases, including offline and non-media related expression, subject to civil court jurisdiction, in accordance with international human rights standards.

“For years Lebanese civil society has fought for increased protections to the right to freedom of expression and the press,” Kaiss said. “Passing the proposed amendments to the media law would be a breakthrough.”
