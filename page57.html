#4194. Why this new Fitbit feature takes us one step closer to non-invasive glucose tracking

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Our gifting experts are obsessively searching for holiday gifts that are unique, thoughtful and suitable for any budget.

Insulin resistance and blood pressure trends are now available on two new Fitbit devices.

Google just added insulin resistance and blood pressure trends to the Fitbit Air and Edge, taking these devices beyond basic fitness monitoring and sleep tracking and into deeper health insights. The new features are part of Google’s “Health Guardian” suite of proactive safety tools for the Pixel Watch and Fitbit.

Also: Are AI health coach subscriptions a scam? My verdict after testing Fitbit’s for a month

They’re available solely for Google Health Premium users, a $100 annual subscription that grants users access to Google’s AI health coach, advanced sleep tracking, and a more expansive selection of health-tracking features.

While it doesn’t measure your insulin or glucose outright, its algorithm analyzes heart rate, activity level, sleep, and other metrics to show changes in metabolic health. The Fitbit will also track your blood pressure patterns without a cuff, monitor your pulse while you’re asleep or awake, and, at the end of the month, provide you with insights. Eligible Fitbit Air and Fitbit Edge users who have worn their device for five days or more will see this insight in the Health tab of the app.

Similar to blood pressure trends, the insulin resistance feature uses Fitbit sensors to monitor shifts in metabolic health. Insulin resistance is a precursor to type 2 diabetes, potentially giving users insight into changes in their insulin levels in a less invasive way, but pointing more toward overall trends than a direct diagnosis.

Unlike a continuous glucose monitor, which penetrates the skin and uses blood to gather metabolic data, the insulin resistance feature was built to assess biometric and metabolic patterns through its multimodal model. Google trained its insulin resistance model on data gathered by other wearables as well as compiled demographic data, according to its paper in Nature.

Also: I tested Google’s Fitbit Edge – its standout feature also left me wanting more

Once the feature detects elevated insulin resistance patterns, it notifies the user to take additional action. Fitbit users can even connect their Abbott CGM to the Google Health app for additional glucose monitoring. By offering these Health Guardian features to more devices than just its flagship Pixel Watches, Google is giving more users — specifically budget-conscious users who own a $100 and $180 Fitbit Air or Edge — the ability to track and stay ahead of some common and serious health conditions.

While the insulin resistance feature is nowhere near the capabilities of a non-invasive CGM, Google’s development of a multimodal model for tracking biometric responses to insulin activity suggests the company is getting closer to that non-invasive reality.

Also: The 5 most popular Garmin watches of 2026: What readers are buying most, and last chance Prime Day prices

Non-invasive glucose monitoring is like the foldable phone of wearable technology. It’s a product idea that’s been around for years now, and every tech company is racing to deploy its own, with varying degrees of success and adoption. Even Steve Jobs had dreams of an Apple glucose monitor that people could use — no finger prick necessary. Rumors suggest that Apple has also been working on this for years now.

But Google might be a few steps ahead of Apple on this one. Its deployment of insulin resistance trends to new Fitbit devices is one way it is advancing toward a CGM that uses algorithmic AI instead of needles and blood.

#1105. 9 Death-Inspired Runway Collections to Get You in the Halloween Spirit

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Fashion can be a lot of things—glamorous, elegant, cool, fun, sexy—but can it also be…scary? An interesting thought to consider as Halloween approaches. And the answer is, well, of course. As one of life’s great mysteries, it’s no big surprise that fashion does grapple, from time to time, with ideas of death and the afterlife.

“I have always found it interesting that death often reveals something profoundly human. It can bring people closer to their most authentic selves,” says French creative director, costume designer, and hair and makeup artist Charlie Le Mindu. “The prospect of losing everything changes our understanding of what is important. I sometimes wonder why we need to approach the end of life to begin living with that level of honesty.”

But Le Mindu is hardly the only designer to have confronted their own fears of death through fashion—designers have long used this medium to wrestle with what is paradoxically both life’s only guarantee and one of its greatest mysteries. Across cultures and eras, fashion has served as a means of reflecting on this universal human experience, giving tangible form to the otherwise incomprehensible. From Ann Demeulemeester’s spring 2019 gothic veils inspired by story of L’Inconnue de la Seine to Alexander McQueen’s spring 2008 tribute to his late friend and champion, Isabella Blow, some have drawn on the visual language of funerary dress, while others have transformed the runway into meditations on grief. Here, we revisit nine collections that explore the many ways fashion has confronted death—and, in turn, what it means to be alive.

“It is important to look at death because it is a part of life,” Alexander McQueen told Nick Knight in a 2009 SHOWstudio interview. Few designers embraced that fascination more fervently than McQueen, whose work repeatedly grappled with mortality, violence, religion, and the afterlife, particularly in his eighth runway collection, Dante, which propelled him to international acclaim. Named after the Italian poet whose Divine Comedy journeys through hell, purgatory, and Paradise, the fall 1996 collection explored the intersection of religion, warfare, and historical mourning traditions. Dedicated to his close friend and muse, Isabella Blow, the show unfolded on a crucifix-shaped runway inside London’s candlelit Christ Church Spitalfields, where a skeleton watched from the front row as the spectacle unfolded to a haunting soundtrack punctuated by gunfire. McQueen juxtaposed religious imagery with the brutality of war, incorporating Don McCullin’s photographs of the Vietnam War into garments alongside ecclesiastical tailoring, bumster trousers, and thornlike headpieces. Among the most striking pieces was a lilac silk faille corset embellished with black lace and jet beading, drawing directly on the colors and materials of Victorian mourning dress.

A decade later, McQueen’s fall 2006 collection, The Widows of Culloden, painted historical loss through an elegiac runway that he described as “romantic but melancholic and austere at the same time.” A tribute to the women widowed by the 1746 Battle of Culloden, which devastated the Scottish Highlands, the deeply personal collection drew on McQueen’s Scottish ancestry through family tartans, black lace, feathers, and mourning-inspired silhouettes. Its haunting finale featured a spectral Kate Moss floating inside a glass pyramid, conjured through a 19th-century illusion known as Pepper’s Ghost. The collection exemplified what curator Andrew Bolton has described as McQueen’s gift for “finding poetry and beauty in death,” a sensibility that permeated his work throughout his career.

Charlie Le Mindu’s fall 2010 collection, Sex Religion Bullshit Lucifer, grappled with his own mortality during a period of deteriorating health. “Rather than approaching death as something tragic, I became interested in the strange freedom that comes with accepting its inevitability,” recalls the French hairstylist and designer, who has also collaborated with brands including Balmain, Mugler, and Vivienne Westwood, and styled artists such as Lady Gaga, Cindy Sherman, Julia Fox, and more. “I wanted to celebrate it, not because I wished to die, but because I had never felt such an urgency to live.” Questioning the religious and moral structures that teach us to fear death, Le Mindu embraced Lucifer as a symbol of society’s anxieties surrounding desire and disobedience through his signature haute coiffure, in a collection of hair constructions, towering jeweled crosses, and mourning veils. Among its most striking creations was an oversized black lacquered rose fascinator, which Gaga wore later in the year. “Something that began with the possibility of dying ultimately changed the way I approached being alive,” he reflects. “The collection was never really about death itself, but about the extraordinary value we give to life once we understand that it is finite. I wonder why we need to approach the end of life to begin living with that level of honesty.”

For her fall 2011 haute couture collection, Capriole, Dutch designer Iris van Herpen found inspiration in the sensation of free-falling during a parachute jump. Exploring the tension between freedom and fear in a moment suspended between life and death, the 19-look collection featured a striking white 3D-printed skeleton dress, developed in collaboration with architect Isaïe Bloch and Belgian 3D-printing company Materialise. Constructed from laser-sintered polyamide, its bonelike structure enveloped the body like an exoskeleton. “This dress visualizes the inside of the body outside,” van Herpen explained to Vogue. “To me it represents freedom and imperfection, and between those two is where beauty can be found.” Later acquired by the Metropolitan Museum of Art and featured in its “Manus x Machina” exhibition, and now on view at “Iris van Herpen: Sculpting the Senses” at the Brooklyn Museum, the piece transformed a traditional symbol of mortality into an expression of liberation.

For fall 2012, Thom Browne took the expression “dying for fashion” literally, staging an elaborate funeral tableau complete with flickering candles and 10 coffins containing, as an officiant announced, “10 beautiful girls who died for fashion.” The models subsequently emerged from their coffins, resurrected by their love of clothing, in extravagant ensembles featuring exaggerated hourglass silhouettes, embroidery, and gilded embellishments. Juxtaposing the solemnity of Victorian mourning with his characteristically surreal theatricality, Browne transformed the runway into a narrative about fashion’s power to bring its devotees back to life.

For Comme des Garçons’ fall 2015 collection, The Ceremony of Separation, Rei Kawakubo explored an abstract interpretation of loss through 18 sculptural looks in black, white, and gold, colors associated with burial across different traditions. Models approached, passed, and separated from one another as though enacting a final parting between the living and the dead in voluminous constructions of intricate lace, oversized bows, and densely layered fabrics that enveloped the body. Among the standout looks were a white dress resembling bundles of bedsheets tied with bows, a tiered gold lace structure recalling religious vestments, and a black ensemble adorned with miniature Victorian-style children’s dresses and bonnets. The show culminated in a voluminous black tulle cocoon, its shroudlike silhouette reinforcing the collection’s meditation on mortality.

For Ann Demeulemeester’s spring 2019 collection, creative director Sébastien Meunier found inspiration in the story of L’Inconnue de la Seine, a mysterious young woman allegedly pulled from the Seine in the late 19th century, whose death mask has fascinated artists for generations. Meunier, who keeps a replica of the mask in his apartment, translated his fascination with her into a hauntingly romantic collection of diaphanous, nightdress-like gowns, fluid tailoring, and trailing silhouettes. Models walked with their faces obscured by veils in billowing black-and-white dresses, gauzy blouses, and long coats with an unfinished, waterlogged finish. Some of the most recognized looks were a sheer white dress layered beneath leather straps and chains, pale pink gowns cinched with harnesses, and tailored jackets adorned with clusters of flowers, evoking the spectral beauty of the drowned woman. “We don’t know a lot about her, so you can dream up lots of things and transpose your own world on it,” Meunier told Vogue.

Alessandro Michele staged Gucci’s cruise 2019 show, which explored what he described as “the idea of death as fascination,” after dark in the Alyscamps, an ancient Roman necropolis in Arles, France. The burial ground was transformed into what the house called a “party in a cemetery” against a backdrop of flames, tolling bells, and stone sarcophagi, and models appeared as mourning widows in velvet gowns and capes alongside skeleton-embroidered garments and leggings bearing the word memento mori. Among the most memorable ensembles were a black gown with a skeletal rib cage embroidered across the torso and a floral cape paired with a cathedral-like headdress, injecting funerary imagery into Michele’s signature maximalism.

Named after a river of the Greek underworld depicted in Dante’s Inferno as a torrent of boiling blood, Rick Owens’s Phlegethon visualized the anxieties of the pandemic and environmental crisis. Presented amid fog and flashing lights outside Venice’s Lido Casino, the dystopian collection featured towering thigh-high platform boots resembling waders, an allusion to both the mythical river and the city’s rising waters. Armor-like shoulders, oversized sleeveless jackets, and leather micro-shorts contrasted with sheer silk chiffon, fishnet dresses, and sculptural tulle ruffles. Against a largely black palette, flashes of alarm red and bubblegum pink heightened the collection’s apocalyptic ethos. Owens described the imposing shoulders as an “exaggerated middle finger to doom,” framing the collection as an act of defiance. “In the face of adversity…we have to pull ourselves up,” he said.

Drawing on the Greek myth of Orpheus and Eurydice, Jackson Wiederhoeft’s fall 2023 collection imagined a subversive afterlife in which the heroine refuses to be rescued from the underworld. “In our version of the story, Eurydice chooses hell,” said the designer. “We explore a post-Orpheus, post-gender underworld where interpretations of ancient Greek and Catholic mythologies meet at the intersection of Virgil’s Aeneid. A Venn diagram of fanaticism, fate, fear. Afterparty versus afterlife, prey versus predator, friend versus foe, fate versus free will. Fate as a consequence of free will.” Staged as an operatic spectacle, the collection opened with a voluminous white gown fashioned from plasticized taffeta, its sheer bodice revealing a tightly cinched corset beneath. Shimmering gold-sequined dresses, corseted denim, and a coat embroidered with stained-glass-like sequins further blurred the boundaries between gothic romance and extravagance, while sheer slip dresses featured hand-embroidered, tattoo-like imagery referencing the myth of Orpheus and other Catholic iconography.

#1069. Revisiting Chanel’s Most Iconic Runway Shows

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Rizzoli’s Chanel Shows Fashion Mise-en-Scène catalogs the brand’s evolution from intimate salon presentations to today’s extravagant productions

For his Spring 2027 Chanel collection, Matthieu Blazy drew on Gabrielle Chanel’s founding premise, putting forth clothes meant to liberate the women they dress. But he drew on her successor Karl Lagerfeld’s legacy to produce a show that projected the collection into the stratosphere. Blazy used birds as symbols of freedom: 95 larger-than-life white seagulls hung suspended from the ceiling of the Grand Palais as if frozen midflight. The moon, also part of the diorama, appeared to cast an after-hours light upon them. The musical selections? Camille Saint-Saëns’s “The Swan” and the Cinematic Orchestra’s “Arrival of the Birds.”

Since his appointment three seasons ago, Blazy has staged the kind of high-drama Chanel shows that became associated with the French fashion house during Lagerfeld’s tenure and continued throughout Virginie Viard’s. The brand has upped the ante of fashion show decadence, establishing a standard of production across the industry. But it didn’t start out that way. When Gabrielle Chanel was working a century ago, she preferred a more modest salon-style presentation characteristic of the couture shows of the time.

A new book, Chanel Shows Fashion Mise-en-Scène, looks to catalog the evolution of the brand’s shows over the past hundred-plus years—a timeline that also encapsulates major changes in fashion and culture. The coffee-table book is both a visual retrospective of the grandeur of Chanel runways, and an in-depth look at the through-line between the first couture presentations and the larger-than-life spectacles of today.

Chanel founded her namesake label in 1909 in Paris. Around the same time, the modern concept of the fashion show with models walking a runway was taking shape. In 1918, Gabrielle moved into Number 31, Rue Cambon, establishing both a headquarters for the brand and a home for the house’s runway shows. Her atelier showed off her personal style, a characteristic mishmash of simplicity and excess. She juxtaposed elements from throughout history—set against a mirrored staircase were painted wood furnishings, nineteenth-century lacquered panels, and Eastern ceramics. A dazzling rotation of imagery reminds us that the backdrops were consistent even as the collections evolved.

The book is sectioned into seven themes inspired by Gabrielle Chanel’s guiding principles, including “the future favors the bold” and “inspiration is a force of nature.” Though Lagerfeld, who took over roughly a decade after her death in 1971, at first kept with the more intimate style of shows, his visions of grandeur quickly outgrew the space. Moving into the 1990s, brand image began to equate, if not outshine, the collections themselves. With six annual shows—two ready-to-wear, two couture, cruise, and métiers d’art—and a global audience, the theatrics began to grow. When Paris’s Grand Palais reopened in 2005, Lagerfeld established a new runway home base, hosting a number of ready-to-wear and couture shows there. (Cruise and métiers d’art collections are often shown on remote location.)

For Lagerfeld's first show at the Grand Palais Spring 2006, he staged a giant reproduction of the new iMac, which had just overtaken the tech market. This was the new precedent. “[He] had that magnificent 1948 book by André Granet, Temporary Installations: Décors éphémères – Paris 1909–1948, which he had republished as a facsimile reprint,” writes Bruno Pavlovsky, president of fashion at Chanel, in an interview in the book. “It contained photographs of Granet’s installations for large-scale exhibitions and fairs in Paris, some of which featured a locomotive, or even airplanes, under the Grand Palais dome. It all started with that book.”

This glimpse behind the curtain reminds us that it takes 12 days of prep (and something like six months of planning) for a show that lasts 15 minutes. These fantastical events transport guests to extravagant worlds they might otherwise never have access to. The Spring 2012 couture show transformed the Grand Palais into a full-size reproduction of an airplane, where guests sat on passenger seats—all first class, of course—and models used the center aisle as a runway. (Spring 2016 transformed it into an airport terminal complete with arrivals, departures, check-in counters, and baggage conveyor belts.) Fall 2014 made a supermarket, shelves stacked with the groceries you’d expect rebranded through the Chanel lens. Then, there was the famous rocket for Fall 2017 that appeared to take off courtesy of clever pyrotechnics at the close of the show, soundtracked, of course, to Elton John’s “Rocket Man.”

Per the text, Lagerfeld was always in on the joke. “‘Fashion is neither moral nor immoral but is meant to boost morale,’ Karl Lagerfeld would say.” There’s even a quick Q&A with Michel Gaubert, who famously soundtracks the Chanel shows.

You can sit for hours just leafing through the fabulous imagery of all of these productions, from Lagerfeld on through Viard and Blazy. These days, over-the-top shows are commonplace across the industry. For Louis Vuitton’s Spring 2027 men’s collection, the brand built a multistory set piece designed to look like a tidal wave and featuring real water. Haider Ackermann’s new Tom Ford comes with models in character, slinking around the room and making devilish eye contact with the audience. But a Chanel show is somehow still magical, whether you stream it on your laptop or sit beneath the glass ceiling of the Grand Palais.

“The perfect show doesn’t exist, because it’s not an exact exercise. There’s always this danger,” writes Blazy to close the book. “There is everything you can control, but there is also everything you can’t. So that's what makes a show a moment that needs to be lived.”

#4541. Roblox users have made a playable version of Counter-Strike and full, working PlayStation games that you can play right now

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Multiple remakes of beloved games including Counter Strike, The Simpsons: Hit and Run, and Super Hot, are available to play on Roblox right now. These have not been uploaded by the original game's creators.

One game - which is listed as an 'experience' in Roblox parlance, has been released by Roblox user Takeoff Interactive and listed as 'just a passion project', is a game "inspired by Counter-Strike" and has no monetisation according to its description. Eurogamer played this game today, and it looks and plays exactly like Counter-Strike.

The game includes the standard bomb diffusion mode, where two teams fight over two bomb sites. There are missing sound effects, the smoke doesn't look as good, and a friendly bot stabbed me in the back while diffusing a bomb, but otherwise it looks to be a largely identical recreation of Valve's game, albeit without Valve's involvement.

As for the PlayStation Experience, it's a full working PlayStation 4 dashboard with a media gallery, web browser, and trophies. There's even a server-based chat feature, in which Roblox players can talk to each other. The dashboard includes seven playable games - most of which are indie games. These include: The Simpsons Hit and Run, Cluster Truck, Superhot, Gang Beasts, Five Nights at Freddy's, Undertale, and PaRappaTheRapper.

Eurogamer played The Simpsons Hit and Run, and likewise found it to be a largely identical recreation of Activision's game. Players can drive around Springfield and cause mayhem much like the original game, albeit with a slightly dodgy objective guidance arrow.

Eurogamer also played Superhot, which once again looks and plays much like the original game, though it's not quite as visually impressive. This version of Superhot, Simpsons Hit and Run, and the PlayStation Experience as a whole was uploaded to Roblox by user Redwing Works, not Activision, not the Superhot team, and not Sony.

It is worth noting these are not Roblox games that are merely inspired by popular releases outside its ecosystem. Previously, a few popular Roblox games inspired by the game but not openly copying it exist. Counter Blox, for example, is a tactical FPS which uses Roblox assets and models, while Arsenal is another FPS inspired by Counter-Strike's Gun Game mode in which each kill progresses you to different weapons.

This is not the first time a popular game has been recreated on Roblox without the permission of its creators. The Peak developers urged people to pirate their game rather than play a "microtransaction-riddled ripoff slop" on Roblox.

Eurogamer has contacted Sony and developers for comment.

Journalist-led research found audiences increased trust when news videos identified their sources, such as government documents and interviews.

RELATED ARTICLETwo-thirds of news influencers are men — and most have never worked for a news organizationSarah ScireNovember 18, 2024About one in five U.S. adults regularly gets their news from news creators on social media platforms, but it can be difficult for audiences to suss out which personalities to trust amid an avalanche of misinformation, hot takes, and polarizing content.

New research spearheaded by a team of journalists and political scientists shows that giving audiences a peek into the reporting process can help bolster their trust in the news.

Proof News, a nonprofit newsroom focused on tech accountability, in collaboration with researchers at the French university Sciences Po, shared social news videos with 2,000 U.S. adults to measure their perceptions of trustworthiness. Participants were randomly assigned one of Proof’s yet-to-be-published short-form news videos.

Some participants viewed videos with labels describing the sources, others saw videos with labels describing the newsroom’s ethical standards, and some saw videos without a label. The Sources labels appeared on cards embedded at the end of the video, like a credits reel, or were placed into the video description, the text accompanying the video.

Participants then rated the videos on various trust metrics. We found that labeling videos did increase trust — particularly a label that described the video sources in the description and, to a slightly lesser extent, a label describing newsroom standards in the credits.

This effect was particularly prominent among survey respondents who identified as Democrats or reported having high trust in science. Those groups, when shown the sourcing label in the description of a general news video, reported higher assessments of transparency and trust in the journalist.

There were fewer statistically significant increases in trust metrics with labels for text articles, but Sally Lehrman, founder of The Trust Project, said the findings were still intriguing.

“I’m excited to see the results,” Lehrman said. “Methods and references really do resonate…People are asking, where did the journalist get this information, and how can I trust it.”

As audiences increasingly turn to social media for news, there is a growing public discussion about how trustworthy creators are, especially when it comes to disclosing their funding and relationships. In January, The Wall Street Journal wrote about corporate and foreign interests using influencers to push agendas. In August, The Washington Post reported that the Pentagon secretly hired conservative military veterans with large online audiences to amplify Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s messaging, and a New York Times investigation revealed online influencers received funding from political campaigns without disclosing the connection in their videos.

Some creators have begun trying to distinguish themselves from this type of behavior through their own disclosure efforts.

RELATED ARTICLEWith Local News International, Dave Jorgenson becomes his own TikTok guyNeel DhaneshaSeptember 9, 2025Journalist and creator Dave Jorgenson, who has nearly 1 million followers across TikTok, YouTube, and Instagram, has started publishing a transparency report on his website, Local News International, revealing his sources of funding alongside his editorial standards.

Ultimately, he thinks disclosures of political funding should no longer be voluntary but mandatory. “Platforms should be regulating themselves,” Jorgenson said in an interview. “You can’t really force that unless there are laws.”

Proof used Jorgenson’s ethics and transparency statement as a model for the newsroom standards label in video testing and found that it increased viewers’ trust in the news outlet and the likelihood that they would share a general news story.

New York City is already considering disclosure requirements in the wake of revelations in the Columbia Journalism Review that exposed the inner workings of New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s connections with influencers. The author of the article, Anya Schiffrin, a senior lecturer at Columbia University’s School of International and Public Affairs, testified at a September city council meeting that creator relationships — both paid and unpaid — should be disclosed to viewers.

“It’s important for democracy that we have standards,” Schiffrin said.

RELATED ARTICLEProof News is Julia Angwin’s attempt to bring the scientific method to investigative journalismNeel DhaneshaMarch 21, 2024Proof News, which launched in 2024, pioneered an Ingredients label that takes cues from the scientific method and names the journalists’ hypothesis, sources, limitations, and the story’s findings.

To test whether the label worked to increase viewer trust for short-form vertical videos, we stripped down the Ingredients label to a bullet list of sources followed by an asterisk denoting the reporting limitations. The survey showed the label had a consistent positive effect when placed in the video description, the text box that is often located under the video.

The team also tested how labels performed on online text news articles. The survey showed 1,600 U.S. participants original, unpublished Proof News stories without a label, with a Proof Ingredients label, a label modeled after the Trust Project that described the reporting process, and a label describing newsroom standards.

There were fewer statistically significant increases in trust metrics with the print labels. The most significant findings were an increase in perception of transparency when the reporting process label was used on a polarizing story and an investigative story.

One possible explanation for the differing results between labeling video and text articles is the “ergonomics” of the medium, said Damien Bol, a professor at the Center for Political Research at Sciences Po who helped lead the survey design and data analysis.

“When reading a text, for example, people can quickly skim through it or skip parts of it, whereas a video forces them to follow the story second by second, even when watching at double speed,” Bol said. Different formats, Bol said, shape the “experience of consuming a news story.”

Earlier this month, Julia Angwin, an author of this article, hosted a private convening of creators at Harvard Kennedy School’s Shorenstein Center on Media, Politics and Public Policy to discuss creating shared standards that could be conveyed through a label or other public statement.

The group of 30 leading content creators from news, science communication, education, history, and other evidence-based fields discussed how they could distinguish their work by signaling to viewers their commitment to standards that could include disclosing funding sources and the use of artificial intelligence. Creator-led efforts to bring this to fruition are ongoing.

San Diego-based journalist Lisa Remillard, who has more than 4 million followers on her TikTok channel “The News Girl,” said that making videos with verified facts can feel like a small club. But if she can’t independently confirm something, she’s “not putting that out there.”

Remillard’s audience appears to appreciate transparency. Jessica Yellin, the founder of the political podcast, YouTube channel, and social media brand “News Not Noise,” has also called for news creators to voluntarily sign a Creator Constitution, agreeing to journalistic standards such as citing sources, fixing errors, and disclosing financial relationships.

#2777. An Old-School Perspective on Finding Better Duck Hunting: Get Creative and Target the Hardest Places

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 27, 2026 3:00 PM EDT

Get the hottest outdoor news from Outdoor Life.

The way some hunters talk, you’d think good waterfowl hunting was a thing of the past. Sure, it was great 25 years ago. You could pick almost any field or pothole and shoot a limit of ducks. But millions of ducks and geese still sweep the flyways each fall, and a determined hunter can find good shooting. It’s just that you have to hunt the hard places.

That’s my name for spots that teem with ducks and geese but rarely see a hunter. Sometimes these areas demand too much effort, but more often hard places are open expanses that offer a hunter no place to hide. Pastures, the favorite haunts of Canada geese, are one example. How can you blind on a pasture as flat as a billiard table? A flooded field also is a problem. Hunters line the dikes of flooded stubblefields like fence posts, but few of them head for the middle because there’s no place to hide. The potential for open-water hunting on lakes, reservoirs, and bays, where waterfowl raft by the thousands, has hardly been touched. The windswept expanses of open ice where geese frequently rest has great possibilities too. In some parts of the United States a person has little choice about where to hunt. But in many regions, particularly in the prairie and Western states, hard places with excellent hunting abound.

Most hunters credit ducks and geese with too much intelligence. Some waterfowlers would have you believe that the Canada goose, for example, is second cousin to Einstein. That image of brilliance is ridiculous. Geese and other birds are smart just as dogs are smart. To teach a dog to sit, you push his rear down and say, “Sit!” After a few lessons he’ll sit when you give the command. Waterfowl learn similarly. On heavily hunted lands waterfowl avoid cover because they’ve learned that’s where the shots come from.

In much open country they’ve had no such conditioning. No one has shot at them, so ducks fall over themselves to land in decoys placed far from cover. In the hard places, waterfowl aren’t conditioned to be cautious. That is the basis for good bird hunting. It also accounts for the fact that you don’t need much of a blind in the open. Hunting hard places is more a matter of philosophy than of technique. Once you’ve decided to hunt a hard place you’ve cleared a major hurdle. Then you just get out there and start hunting. To show how simple it is, I’ll tell you about some ways my friends and I have hunted the hard places.

I usually don’t use a blind. In my part of Oregon, Canada geese spend a lot of time feeding and resting on pastures, and most ranchers don’t want their sod dug up for blinds. A blind above ground also is about as conspicuous as a high-rise apartment building. The only option is no blind. So you just lie on the ground.

Any hunter knows movement really spooks waterfowl, so being able to disguise yourself as a frozen clump of mud is the big step in making this work. I usually clump a few goose decoys around my body and put one in front of my face. The oversize shell decoys made today are especially good for this kind of hunting. A friend of mine has made silhouette decoys that are so large he can curl up behind one. In late season, when swans are present, I’ve used large swan silhouettes among my duck and goose decoys. This technique has worked especially well on ice, when I’ve worn a white suit and lain behind a swan silhouette.

Put your decoys as far from ditches and weed patches as you can. Set up where birds don’t expect danger. Look for small depressions, places where the grass is lumpy, or pressure ridges on the ice so you can hide. Even a slight variation will make you less obvious. And wear clothing that looks like the ground. The obvious choice after a snowfall would be to wear a white suit or to cover yourself over with a sheet. When the cover is green, pull a square of green camouflage cloth over yourself. Some of the pastures I’ve hunted in are muddy. My hunting coat is so impregnated with dirt it looks like part of the ground, so I often don’t cover myself with anything. An occasional roll in the mud keeps the blind fresh.

On windy days this simple approach is almost foolproof. The birds fly low and come directly in from the downwind side. Obviously you want to lie on the upwind side of a decoy. On calm days when waterfowl fly higher and sometimes circle, it’s not as easy, especially if you gawk at them.

In my region farmers flood their grainfields after harvest. Flooded fields draw ducks the way a candy store draws kids. For years I pondered the featureless expanses of water and mud, trying to figure out how I’d get out there without sinking out of sight forever. Then it struck me—an air mattress. Along with a huge bag of floating decoys, I dragged a well-inflated canvas air mattress into a partly flooded field. With the decoys downwind, I flopped the mattress on a clump of mud and lay on it, face up, and pulled a piece of camouflage cloth over me. Ducks and geese nearly landed on me. An air mattress will do the trick in any soggy spot where you can anchor it to some ground. But don’t try it in deep water.

A rubber raft is best for deep water. I use a two-man, rubberized, olive drab canvas raft. You can find these at big sports stores or surplus stores.

The raft can be dragged or carried to any location, and it often serves where a rigid, heavy duckboat won’t. I’ve used it in the same fields I used to hunt with an air mattress. It also works well in marshes where only a few inches of grass show above the surface of the water. I got this idea from watching a hunter one day in a flooded stubblefield. He had a low-profile boat, which he covered with chicken wire. He tied stubble all over the wire, then crawled under it into the boat. His simple blind looked like a mat of straw lying on the water. Ducks swarmed into his decoys as if he weren’t there.

From that example I made a chicken-wire cover for my raft, with a flap cut out of the middle so I could sit up and have an area to shoot through. I tied short pieces of decoy line on the chicken wire and used these to tie grass, stubble, lily pads, and other appropriate plants to the wire. In addition, I cut a floor for the raft made of 3/8-inch plywood. With some spar varnish for weatherproofing and a strip of outdoor carpet stapled around the floor’s edge to keep it from chafing the raft, I had a rigid bottom that made the raft stable. I can sit, kneel, or stand in it without much wobbling.

Another item that could be used in the same way as the rubber raft is a float tube, or belly boat. While these are made for fishing small waters, they’re well suited to hunting. I recently got one with a camouflage cover, and it looks like part of the marsh. With a chicken-wire cone covered with tules, it would look just like a muskrat house.

If the water is too shallow to use a float tube in the conventional manner—with your legs dangling in the water—you can use it as a shooting platform. One friend, Dave Hummel, did something similar with a tire inner tube. Dave saw lots of pintails on big marsh ponds. Those educated birds weren’t about to land within shooting range of the tule fringes, where a hunter could hide. They stuck to the middle of the huge ponds, where the only cover was a few patches of lily pads that stuck up a few inches above the water. There was no way to blind a boat out there, and a person couldn’t lie down in the foot-deep water. So Dave wired a round piece of plywood to the top of an inner tube. To hunt those smart pintails he threw his decoys onto the open water and put his inner tube table in the lily pads. He laid his gun and shells on the platform and camouflaged it with lily pads. Dressed in chest waders he was able to kneel behind it. To hide from circling ducks, he put his face down on the platform. When ducks were within range, he rose on his knees to shoot. Tough shooting? Yes. But it was the only way to get within range of those ducks, and Dave got his share of them.

I recently fished with a fellow who had a “boat” called a Bass Tracker Bantam. It’s a flat plastic hull about five feet long made for poking around backwaters for bass. It’s very stable and rises no more than six inches from the water. It looks to me like an ideal piece of equipment for hard-place hunting.

Another friend hunted from shallow water by using a piece of heavy brown plastic. The water, matted with weeds, was only three or four inches deep. He laid a 12×12-foot square of plastic on the water and jumped into the middle of it. The weeds held up the edges of the plastic to keep water out and he was able to lie down to hide from ducks without getting wet.

Open water may be the extreme example of a hard place to hunt. These days, with many huge reservoirs next to croplands, lots of ducks and geese rest on wide water. A sink box is the answer, but sink boxes are illegal. The next best thing is a low-profile boat. You can’t hide such a boat so you try to make it look like floating debris. My friend David Hagerbaumer hunts extensively on open bays along the Pacific Coast. He uses a Barnegat sneakbox, a flat boat that looks like a miniature hydroplane. Dave has a net for the boat that camouflages it, and he puts seagull decoys on the decks. On open water the boat just looks like a piece of flotsam drifting on the bay. Dave lies on his back in the boat, facing his decoys, which are downwind. This rig works well on diving ducks, and Dave often takes puddle ducks and brant while hunting from it too.

On an incoming tide, puddle ducks often work the shores of estuaries to feed on the flooding beaches and tidal marshlands. That’s when Dave hunts the edges, and he camouflages his boat by tying grass to the net covering, or by shoveling mud clumps onto the decks.

Dave has another rig, called an alligator, that should really do the job for you on mudflats or beaches. These are no longer available commercially, but you could easily make your own. The alligator is a fiberglass coffin, shaped like a mummy with an oblong hole cut out of one side. You drag it out to where you want to hunt, dig a hole, and bury the thing, except for the opening. Then you crawl in, lie on your back, and face up through the hole. It’s no more than a way to stay dry while hiding in a soggy place, but that’s all you need in the hard places.

Hunting hard places can be a little wet and dirty. A rugged, heavy pair of chest waders and a canvas coat that can withstand abuse are necessities. The right gun also is important. Guns get muddy in hard places, and most autoloaders I’ve used turn into single shots under these conditions. A pump gun is OK because you can grind shells through even if the gun does take on a load of grime, but the best choice is a double barrel, which is nearly impervious to dirt. Invariably in hard places you must shoot lying on your back, sitting, or from your knees. Your feet may be stuck in the mud, and you’ll feel like you’re caught in a spiderweb when you start trying to shoot through a chicken-wire cover. Add to that a good stiff breeze, which ducks can use to get quickly out of range, and you have a shooting challenge. A good idea is to practice shooting claybirds from awkward positions, also lie down and practice throwing your gun to your shoulder and swinging it. You may want to learn to shoot both right and left-handed so you can handle ducks coming in from both sides.

Hard places generally call for big decoy spreads. In open expanses, ducks in particular congregate in huge swarms. I can pile about five dozen inflatable ducks on my rubber raft so that’s about my maximum, but more decoys would be even better.

The last day of the season a couple of years ago typifies hard-place hunting. Ducks were streaming into a flooded stubblefield and hunters lined the dikes around the field. I waded out to the middle, found a small patch of mud to lie on, and threw out some decoys. Mallards broke their necks to get into that spread. But the mud was sticky and the wind was howling. By the time I could sit up to shoot, the ducks usually had flared to long range. The one box of shells in my pocket accounted for only four mallards and a honker. But it was an unforgettable afternoon of duck hunting. While hunters pass-shooting from the dikes only took an occasional high bird, I was at the center of a swarming, teeming mass of ducks.

Great hunting still does exist. It demands some ingenuity and hard work. But if you hunt the hard places, you’ll find the birds. And that’s what duck hunting is all about.

Even after a run once, I stood in the Starbucks line, waiting for my venti iced coffee while quietly fearing I was ruining everyone’s morning. So when I think about armpits, my brain goes to risk management before eroticism.

While noting those traits aren’t inherently masculine so much as culturally assigned, hairy pits and their natural smell can carry a specific erotic charge in the corners of gay male culture that are drawn to signs of manliness.

None of that was running through my head in Kevin’s bed. I felt curiosity mixed with mild panic. He created the brand with gay men in mind, developing products that complement their natural scent rather than completely covering it up. “At a gay bar, I don’t want to get close to a guy and smell nothing but cologne,” he says. “I want to smell him.”

Just to clarify, this is in no way an argument against showering. Gimenez distinguishes between bad body odor and a person’s actual scent. One needs handling. The other doesn’t have to be buried under six sprays of something called Ocean Rush. As he puts it, “The goal is a little fragrance, a little you, and no onion.”

The more I thought about it, the more I realized that I’ve always understood scent as part of presentation, just like clothes or hair, but I never extended that logic to my armpits, quite possibly because the entire deodorant aisle has been screaming at me since puberty to keep them quiet.

Gimenez thinks scent deserves the same consideration as the rest of how we show up. “There’s clothing style, and there’s scent style,” he says. “Your fragrance and how much you wear contribute to how people think of you, just like your clothes or haircut.” That includes sex, he adds: “When you smell right, he wants to get closer, and stay there longer.”

And because armpits are usually out of sight, there’s something mysterious and a little alluring about them. Kort says armpits bring together “body hair, sweat, scent, vulnerability and physical closeness,” and because they’re usually concealed, seeing, touching, or smelling them can feel especially intimate.

He also points out that eroticism often engages with the parts of our bodies or minds we are taught to hide. We’re told to sanitize and control our smells, so in a consensual sexual context, abandoning those rules can feel freeing. “Sweat may stop meaning ‘unclean’ and start meaning exertion, desire, and surrender,” he says. “Context changes the meaning of the sensation.”

Gay male culture has never had one grooming script anyway. There are smooth guys, those who present more polished, like they’re ready for a circuit party, and burly men who look like they just wandered out of a cabin with an axe. Kort says gay men may experience masculinity from both sides: what they desire in other men and how they want to be perceived themselves. A man who eroticizes body hair or musk in partners may preserve or display those same traits because they make him feel masculine and desirable.

Adding that removing hair doesn’t automatically make you cleaner. “You can have completely natural armpit hair and still have excellent hygiene,” he says.

Which is where a lot of men’s grooming advice starts to feel a little suspect. So much of it is built around subtraction. Remove the hair. Kill the odor. Dry the sweat. Become smoother, fresher, and less offensive. Useful, sure, especially if you’re sharing public transportation, but also a little bleak. At a certain point, it starts to sound like the goal is to smell less like yourself and more like a freshly wiped countertop.

Dr. Justin Lehmiller, scientific advisor and sex educator at adult toy company Arcwave, says scent can trigger emotion and memory in a powerful way, but the same smell can be pleasurable to one person and disgusting to another. And Kort makes a similar point, noting that someone’s natural odor may be neutral or unpleasant to a stranger, but deeply sexy to a partner because it signals familiarity and arousal.

Scent can be proof of closeness, but in terms of my moment with Kevin, I clearly wasn’t close enough yet for that kind of intimacy for it to feel natural.

In other words, I’m not a pit guy yet. But I’m much less dismissive than I was before. The next time a man asks me to smell his armpit, I may need a beat, but I’ll at least understand why he’s asking. Clean is good. But washed so aggressively that you smell like a bottle of Purell? I’ve heard plenty of answers to “What are you into?” and hand sanitizer has yet to be one of them.
