#2178. Manhattan’s Lost Its Screen Presence

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The other weekend, I pulled up Woody Allen’s Manhattan (1979) on YouTube because I’ve been starved for the highbrow and the funny, a rare marriage that movies used to achieve with some regularity, but now hardly ever bother to attempt. 

Let’s disregard the plot, here, and the characters, for there is a great deal about both that resists easy or simple discussion in the present American climate, and focus instead on the surface: the look of the film, the feel of it, the particular New York it conjures and holds up to the light like a jeweler with a loupe.

My feeling for New York City has always come in a peculiar Southern blend of love and affliction. I don’t care for the rudeness, or the flat, clipped music of the accent, the way a sentence up there lands like a screen door.

But some years ago—high school, through college, on into my early twenties—my family made a yearly trip north around Christmas, either before the holiday or just after it. Mother, father, sister, brother, and myself. We took rooms near Times Square. We saw the shows. We ate at the restaurants with the heavy menus and the heavier check.

My mother and sister went shopping. The first year, my father, brother, and I made the mistake of going along, and we stood there in Macy’s—the famous one—waiting among the racks like the accused waiting outside a courtroom, until finally we drifted, defeated, to the section where they sell the beds.

And there, without much discussion, the three of us climbed up onto the display mattresses and took our naps in full view of the shoppers, three Southern males asleep in a snowdrift of goose-down pillows while New Yorkers and tourists alike rushed past us toward their purchases. We never went shopping with the women again.

Manhattan opens with a voice reciting its own first sentence, over and over, dissatisfied, revising itself in real time: “Chapter One. He adored New York City. He idolized it all out of proportion…” What we are watching, before a single frame of story unspools, is the composition of New York, its narration into existence by a man who admits, within the first 30 seconds, that his version of it is a romance rather than a record.

Isaac Davis, played by Allen, drafts Manhattan, discards the draft, drafts it again, each revision a further layer of gloss over a city that by the film’s own admission was never quite there to begin with. Gershwin swells. The skyline assembles itself in black-and-white, denuded of the actual chromatic clutter of a real metropolis: no exhaust-brown, no bodega-awning red, no garbage-day green.

The result is not less than New York, but more: a hyperreal locus, a city more New York than New York, purified into its own advertisement. By the time the narration settles on its final version of the sentence, we have watched a myth being built in front of us, brick by adoring brick, and we have watched it happen with the confidence of a myth that knows it will outlast its subject.

Consider, in this light, why the film insists on monochrome: color would have been the honest choice, but the lesser one. The black-and-white suggests a setting already understood as the past. Allen may have shot it that way because that’s how he remembered the city from boyhood. The image does not become nostalgia, but arrives already nostalgic, already a simulation of an image of a city, twice removed from the referent before the referent has even appeared.

The formal choices reward the kind of patient gazing that asks not what a shot means, but what it does: how it organizes the eye, how it withholds and releases. Willis’s camera holds wide and still. And it is Allen—small, voluble, anxious—who is made to cross the frame, dwarfed by the buildings on either side. The city is never made to accommodate the man. The compositions insist on this dynamic with a kind of moral clarity: architecture as fixed, permanent, indifferent; the human figure as transient, comic, and a little absurd against so much stone. There’s no accident in the fact that the funniest lines are often delivered in the widest shots: the humor is small; the buildings aren’t.

The bench scene deserves a word of correction before anything else, since even devoted admirers of the film misremember it: the silhouette behind Isaac and Mary at dawn is not the Brooklyn Bridge, but the Queensboro—the 59th Street Bridge, the one Simon and Garfunkel made feel groovy—shot from a little park at Sutton Place, the bench itself (according to my Internet searches) trucked in by the crew for that single image and trucked out again after.

Which is, in its own way, the whole method of the film in miniature: even the most iconic, most seemingly eternal image of New York on offer here was a temporary prop set down in front of a bridge for one morning’s shoot. The permanence is manufactured.

The film’s two registers—the melancholy of the story and the euphoria of the imagery—do not cancel each other out so much as run on parallel tracks, and the gap between those tracks is where the picture does its real work. Consider the function of the montage sequences against that of the dialogue scenes. The montages are non-diegetic in feeling even when diegetically grounded: they exist to establish a rhythm of civic wonder, asking nothing of the viewer but admiration.

The dialogue scenes, by contrast, are shot with almost no cutting at all: long, static two-shots, characters talking past each other in real time, the visual monotony forcing attention onto the halting, self-undermining texture of what is actually being said. The film is, formally speaking, telling us two different things simultaneously and daring us to notice the seam: the city as sublime, continuous, orchestral; the people within it as fragmented, hesitant, unable to complete a sentence about their own feelings without doubling back on it, exactly as the opening narration doubled back on itself.

The visual grandeur is never undercut by irony—Allen does not mock the skyline—but it is placed in a kind of formal tension with human smallness that neither element resolves. That unresolved tension, held shot after shot without comment, is the melancholy, and it’s built into the cutting pattern before it is ever spoken aloud.

Now, alas, to the present city—the real one—as it stands to be reorganized, and to the politics of the mayor presently promising to reorganize it.

New York has been bleeding people for years. The arithmetic of it is no longer a matter of dispute: the city and state have shed net domestic migrants every year for over a decade, hundreds of thousands of taxpayers relocating to Florida, Texas, and the Carolinas, taking with them not just households, but the tax base itself, since a disproportionate share of those leaving sit at the top of the income ledger.

Economists have written this story many times over. They generally tell it thus: rent regulation suppressing housing supply, tax rates chasing away the earners who fund the very programs meant to help everyone else, business formation slowing as regulatory and labor costs climb, the tax burden left to redistribute itself across a shrinking and less mobile base.

It is, by now, a familiar and well-documented indictment, and further democratic-socialist experimentation of the kind Zohran Mamdani proposes (price controls, municipal enterprises, steeper taxation of capital) is likely only to accelerate a trend already well underway. What economists rarely pause to mention, in this careful accounting of bad economics, is the bad aesthetics: the fact that a shrinking, flattened, price-controlled city is not only a poorer city, but a less legible one, a city that stops producing the aspirational, ambitious textures that an environment needs to be worth writing a Manhattan about in the first place.

Rent freezes, city-owned grocery stores, free buses, government-run childcare, union-built housing pinned beneath the same frozen rents, higher taxation on the wealthy who have historically financed the city’s theater of distinction: this is a program that wants New York to stop signifying, to stop chasing visual excess, to level unnecessary beauty, and to disappear the glorified aesthetics that defined Manhattan (the film).

The free market is a machine for producing urban variety and diversity. It sorts neighborhoods into strata, forces competition among restaurants and shops and doormen and galleries to distinguish themselves, and out of that sorting comes precisely the texture Isaac Davis fell in love with: the specific bookstore, the specific delicatessen, the specific bench.

Price signals are semiotic: a rent freeze not only caps a number, but also removes the mechanism by which neighborhoods differentiate themselves through investment, turnover, and risk. A city-owned grocery store cannot, by definition, be a particular grocery store as Zabar’s is particular because its entire justification is that it’s the same everywhere, fungible, a public utility rather than a character.

Today’s Manhattan looks different from Allen’s black-and-white mise-en-scène partly because of the regulations governing its buildings: facade inspection mandates have wrapped whole blocks in sidewalk sheds for years at a time, and emissions caps are pushing owners to retrofit prewar towers with electric heat pumps, through-wall units, and rooftop equipment.

Universal childcare and free buses, worthy as they may sound to the ear of compassion, are administered goods, which, besides being economically harmful to the very communities they purport to help, do not accumulate the patina of specificity, tenacity, drive: the worn brass rail, the eccentric owner, the 40 years of small adjustments to changing taste. These details turn a mere service into a grand milieu that a person can photograph in black-and-white and mean something by it. You can’t rent-control your way to a Rizzoli’s.

The specific aesthetic New York that this film so lovingly constructs was the product of an economy that permitted radical unevenness, permitted some blocks to become Sutton Place and others to remain what they were. The old New York could be ugly in reality and beautiful in art because it left room for artists to look upward, to take its disorder and inequality and noise and transfigure them into myth. The danger of Mamdani’s New York is not merely that it becomes poorer or more regulated, but that it becomes aesthetically downward-looking, a city preoccupied with administering what is wrong with it rather than generating the beauty that makes people want to imagine it as something more.

A politics organized entirely around flattening unevenness in the name of universal provision will tend to produce a city whose surfaces mean less. Mamdani’s New York, should it fully arrive, promises a setting that is more equal and more affordable and, quite possibly, less quotable, a city no future Woody Allen would find himself compelled to open his picture with “Chapter one. Which brings me back to that Christmas Macy’s, my father asleep on a showroom mattress, three Southerners playing hooky from the shopping. I think that’s what I’m partially mourning when I mourn the New York of Manhattan: not the wealth, not even the skyline, but the specificity, the sense that the city was a collection of unique rooms rather than a portfolio of interchangeable services.

The people I know who have left for Texas, for Florida, for the redder counties of this reddening country, will tell you they left the taxes, the crime, the cost. But listen long enough, and what they are really grieving, underneath the ledger, is that the New York which could still surprise you is going, has been going for a while now, and no rent freeze, however kindly meant, will bring it back, because you cannot legislate a city into being irreplaceable. You can only leave it alone long enough, and love it hard enough, to let it become so on its own, the way, once, at dawn, on a borrowed bench under a borrowed bridge, it did.

AI is finding new holes in the infrastructure of the internet. Is anyone around to patch them?

For the past week, software engineers around the globe have been scrambling to update the backend of websites after a team using an OpenAI model discovered two major vulnerabilities in PHP, a critical piece of web software.

PHP, open-source scripting software that underpins as many as 70% of websites worldwide, can become outdated when website owners don’t update to newer versions of the software, making them vulnerable to attacks. Shortly after alerts were issued about the new vulnerabilities, new versions of PHP were issued, and site operators were urged to update. Some government websites went dark in Europe as these updates were applied, but for much of the web, it’s been left to individual website owners to apply the fixes.

No law compels them to do so, although if a website suffers a data breach, the owner could be liable to customers or clients. And for web surfers, it’s not obvious what PHP version a site is running without running terminal commands or using an online PHP checker.

Given that some websites are no longer actively managed — I own a website that was latent and hadn’t had a PHP update or updates of its WordPress software in years, for instance — the PHP story has shown that the web’s aging architecture is proving to be insecure due to neglect and dwindling resources to keep it safe.

One of the most shocking revelations that has come out of the last week’s race to get websites updated and safe is that an organization tasked with making sure security flaws in PHP are addressed is itself on shaky ground.

Reuters reported that the PHP Foundation, a nonprofit formed in 2021 to work with researchers and cybersecurity companies to ensure a crisis like this wouldn’t happen, has only one full-time staff member and lacks the resources to expand.

The Foundation’s Executive Director Elizabeth Barron told Reuters that she’s the sole staff member and works with about 14 part-time contractors and unpaid board members. As AI-generated threats increase, there’s twice as much demand to change PHP code to address them as in the past. “Yet the number of contributors hasn’t doubled,” Barron said. While companies and governments rely on PHP, “It is clear PHP is severely underfunded,” she said.

A representative for PHP Foundation did not immediately return a request for comment.

Nearly all small businesses have some sort of web presence, whether it’s a social media account, a Google listing or a website they own. But small business owners, a survey from Network Solutions found, may not be addressing the PHP issue because they don’t know it affects them.

The report, based on a survey of 121 small business owners with their own websites, found 45% of them had never heard of PHP and 71% didn’t know if their websites use it. A large majority of 71% of these site owners hadn’t heard about the recent PHP flaws.

Rick Radinger, principal systems architect for Network Solutions Group, said that the survey showed awareness and knowledge gaps, but that in addition to not knowing about the current PHP problem, they may not think it’s their responsibility to act on a fix. “An owner might know about the warning but could assume their hosting provider will handle it,” Radinger said. “Others might need additional help but aren’t sure who to contact or next steps.”

Some of the mobilization about the PHP alerts included updates from large web infrastructure companies like Cloudflare and web hosts such as GoDaddy. In a blog post, Cloudflare said it deployed new web application firewall protections to detect and fend off attacks. GoDaddy made updates to its managed hosting and Windows hosting services that didn’t require website owners to take extra steps, but said that domain customers who manage their own sites or run virtual private servers may need to update PHP themselves.

In addition to its security updates, Cloudflare has also introduced a PHP Capture the Flag contest as part of its Bug Bounty Program on HackerOne. “We’ve put a deliberately vulnerable PHP installation behind our (firewall), and we’re inviting security researchers on HackerOne to try to bypass the protections and capture a flag,” Cloudflare said in its blog post. Cash prizes range from $100 to $10,000.

For those who don’t own a website, the PHP vulnerabilities aren’t something you can protect against by updating your passwords; it’s up to websites to stay updated to protect their visitors.

“For users, it is frustrating that they can’t fix this, as it is an issue with the site’s server, not the browser,” Radinger said. “Simply updating your browser doesn’t address the issue, and a site being up and running normally does not mean the site has been fixed.”

The usual advice applies more than ever: Be careful about clicking on unfamiliar website links, don’t share sensitive information with websites you don’t trust and be especially cautious about links that will download software to your device.

The PHP crisis is a good wake-up call about what’s happening to the World Wide Web as it approaches its fourth decade of operation. This latest security scramble is just one of the threats websites face (AI-generated breaches are a huge one). But many of the dangers to come may stem from aging digital architecture; PHP is just one of myriad tools websites use, including JavaScript, SQL databases, content management systems (like WordPress or Wix), and the software that the servers themselves run. If those aren’t regularly monitored, managed and updated across the web, you can expect a lot more vulnerabilities to emerge in the years to come.

#2154. Azerbaijan’s Hijab Restrictions Exclude Girls From School

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Three Azerbaijani women wearing headscarves in Baku, Azerbaijan. © 2019 Getty Images

School officials across Azerbaijan have been turning away girls wearing hijabs following the introduction of new government dress regulations, sparking protests across the country. Police have detained dozens of people amid the protests, while Instagram and TikTok users have reported difficulties accessing the platforms as public criticism of the restrictions has grown.

Azerbaijan’s Cabinet of Ministers amended school uniform regulations on September 21, prohibiting clothing "inconsistent with the principle of secular education." Although the regulations do not explicitly mention hijabs, schools began refusing entry to girls wearing headscarfs when the rules took effect on October 12. News reports showed distressed girls after being turned away from schools.

Separate dress requirements adopted on October 6 also affected teachers.

Authorities responded to the protests with widespread detentions. The Ministry of Interior acknowledged on October 12 that they had detained 37 people in Baku, alleging disruptions to public order. Independent media reported that more than 200 people were detained near a central Baku metro station that evening. Activists also reported 88 detentions in Nardaran village, with criminal cases opened against many of those detained.

People criticizing the restrictions online have also faced detention. Unidentified individuals reportedly took activist İlkin Hüseynov from his home on October 14, a day after he launched "Let Leyli Go to School," an online initiative connecting volunteer teachers with girls excluded from classrooms. He was subsequently sentenced to 30 days detention for allegedly disobeying police orders.

In Nakhchivan, a man reportedly received a sentence for 25 days detention following a TikTok post about the restrictions. Social media users across Azerbaijan also reported difficulties accessing Instagram and TikTok on October 12; some were only able to connect via virtual private networks.

Excluding girls from schools because they wear religious headscarves interferes with their rights to education and freedom of religion or belief, and raises serious concerns about discrimination. While governments may regulate school attire, any restrictions must be lawful, necessary, proportionate and non-discriminatory. Azerbaijan should not use the principle of secular education to deny girls access to schooling.

Azerbaijani authorities should immediately ensure that students and teachers wearing hijabs can attend schools, release anyone detained solely for peacefully protesting or criticizing the restrictions, ensure access to legal counsel for those in custody, and investigate reports of interference with social media access.

#954. Why These Athletes Don’t Stress Over Sleep Scores

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

We can track almost everything about our health today—our pace on a run, our heart rate, our heart rate variability (HRV) and, of course, our sleep (or lack thereof). But what are fitness trackers actually calculating about shut-eye?

Most, including Whoop, don’t directly measure sleep (wearables don’t have the same capabilities as a kitted-out, in-hospital sleep lab). Instead, they use readings of things like movement, heart rate and HRV to estimate when we’re asleep and awake, then turn all of that information into a so-called sleep score.

That reading can help estimate how long we slept, how consistent our sleep has been, how much time we spent in different sleep stages, and how the night compares with what our body typically needs. Having all that data at your fingertips can be incredibly useful. But can obsessing over the number (especially before a big morning) sometimes do more harm than good?

We spoke to some of the world’s leading Hyrox and CrossFit athletes about their relationship with sleep tracking, and why they don’t always let the numbers have the final say.

According to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine 48% of adults in the US have used a sleep tracking device to monitor their sleep quality and duration. (And in fact men are more likely to do so than women.) But also: 76% of adults have been kept up at night worried about not getting enough sleep.

A poor sleep score doesn’t necessarily mean you’re going to have a poor day. So while sleep is extremely important, especially for high-performers, the pressure to achieve a “perfect” night can be counterproductive. There’s even a name for it: orthosomnia, which refers to an obsession with achieving a high rating on a smart device. You might wake up feeling fine, check your score, see that it’s not great, and suddenly start worrying that you actually slept poorly.

A 2024 study of 523 people found that people showing signs of orthosomnia also reported more severe symptoms of insomnia. That's a problem. If one bad score is enough to change your mood, knock your confidence, or make you rethink your training, the number is starting to have too much influence on your routine. For athletes used to training and competing under fatigue, and traveling across time zones, sleep is only one part of the recovery picture.

That doesn’t mean you should throw your Whoop in the bin. The number can tell you something about your sleep, but it shouldn’t be the deciding factor in how you start your day.

All this raises an obvious question: What if the best thing you can do with your sleep score is occasionally ignore it? Some athletes, it seems, have arrived at that solution.

We asked Aniol Ekai, the top men's CrossFit athlete in Spain this year, what he would do if he woke up to a poor sleep score. His answer was surprisingly simple: he wouldn't believe it. “I don’t want a number telling me how I’m going to feel or how I’m going to perform that day,” he says. “Whether it [is accurate] or not almost doesn’t matter.”

By refusing to let a sleep score dictate how he feels, Ekai creates a useful bit of distance between what his tracker says and how he actually performs. “I don't check my scores a week before a big competition, because I don't want another thing to think about, including any kind of stress, unless it's life or death," he says.

Charlie Botterill, currently ranked fourth in the world in Hyrox men’s singles, says sleep has become a much bigger focus for him this year, with a few simple habits making the difference: keeping his phone out of the bedroom for at least two hours before bed, waking up at the same time each day, and making his bedroom a better place to sleep. “Based on how much I train throughout the day, my cortisol will naturally be more elevated in the evenings, which is why keeping my phone away from the bedroom can help me sleep,” he says.

But even with those practices in place, staying asleep isn’t always easy. And while a disrupted night can affect his sleep score, Botterill doesn’t allow the number to determine how he approaches the day. He checks his score, but not every morning, and takes a deliberately relaxed approach to what it tells him.

People over 40 are now the fastest-growing group at Hyrox fitness competitions, reshaping what middle age looks like for the wellness-obsessed gym rat. One Midwestern regional sales director leads us through this year’s event.

Manuela García Caparrós, a Hyrox Pro athlete and former Elite 15 competitor, takes a similarly measured approach. She tracks her sleep with Garmin and Luna, but doesn’t let the data dictate her training. “If I get a bad sleep score, I always compare it with how I actually feel,” she says. Sometimes the data backs up her perception; sometimes it doesn’t. If she feels tired and the stats agree, she might reduce her training volume or prioritize recovery. But one bad night isn’t enough to change the plan. “I pay much more attention to trends over time than to a single bad night of sleep," she says. “I never make decisions based on the numbers alone.”

So yes, wearables can be hugely powerful—being able to track your recovery, strain, and sleep can give you a pretty good idea of what’s going on with your body, especially if you know what to do with the numbers. But after speaking to some of the world's top-ranked fitness athletes about how much they actually rely on their sleep scores, something else became clear: The data should never be the final word.

Instead, it’s best to look at the bigger picture. How do you feel? Are you focused? Do you have energy? And, perhaps most importantly, how does training actually feel? Tracking our sleep is useful, but it’s still just one piece of the puzzle.

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson – typically the court’s most frequent speaker at oral argument – began the term relatively quietly. In Suncor Energy v. Boulder County, the first argued case, she spoke the fourth-most among the participating justices, accounting for only 12.1% of the court’s words. But that relative silence did not last. Jackson led three of the next four arguments and finished the six-case sitting with 6,254 words, significantly more than any of her colleagues.

The arguments produced other patterns, some less predictable. Jackson and Justice Sonia Sotomayor repeatedly concentrated their attention on the same litigating side, but Justice Neil Gorsuch did so as well, despite the justices’ ideological differences. At the same time, their questions did not necessarily point toward identical legal outcomes.

The cases themselves – and how they were treated – revealed something more. Disputes concerning climate litigation, veterans’ constitutional claims, retirement-plan investments, and environmental review all raised questions about when, where, and under what conditions judicial intervention is available. Indeed, that essentially became the theme of the court’s October session.

Jackson’s dominance and some unexpected cross-ideological alignment

As described above, Jackson’s relatively limited involvement in Suncor initially appeared to vary from one of the Supreme Court’s most consistent recent patterns. During the 2025-26 term, she accounted for 22% of justices’ total speech and led in 41 of 58 oral arguments. Yet in the first argument of this term, her 829 words represented only 12.1% of substantive justice speech, placing her fourth among eight participating justices. Across the other five arguments, however, Jackson’s share averaged 21.0%. By the end of the sitting, Jackson had spoken 6,254 words, more than any colleague, and accounted for 19.3% of all substantive justice speech.

Jackson’s questioning also revealed a recurring alignment with Sotomayor and, more surprisingly, Gorsuch. Across several disputes, the three justices repeatedly directed a disproportionate amount of attention toward the same side.

In Johnson v. United States Congress, for instance, Gorsuch questioned whether the veterans’ benefits system possessed the competence to adjudicate constitutional challenges, while Sotomayor emphasized the burden of forcing veterans through an administrative process prior to obtaining constitutional review. Jackson similarly challenged the government’s account of how constitutional questions were handled in practice. The three justices’ approaches differed, but all three explored weaknesses in the government’s proposed review scheme.

In Suncor, the justices’ shared focus covered up larger doctrinal disagreements. Jackson questioned whether the case was sufficiently developed for Supreme Court intervention. Sotomayor asked about the courts’ authority and whether specific arguments had been preserved. Gorsuch explored how the Clean Air Act affected federal common law and potential state-law restrictions.

These alignments should not be conflated with emerging voting coalitions, however. In Beaird v. United States, for example, a Federal Sentencing Guidelines case, Jackson and Gorsuch proved the argument’s two dominant speakers while developing substantially different accounts of the U.S. Sentencing Commission’s authority.

The lesson is this: The justices who identify pressure points in an argument do not always group along familiar ideological lines, and agreement about which position deserves examination need not produce agreement about the possible controlling rule.

Jackson’s individual prominence did not translate into liberal dominance of the court’s collective conversation. Across the six arguments, conservative justices contributed 54.3% of substantive justice speech, compared with 45.7% from the three liberals. Conservatives supplied the majority of justices’ words in five of the six cases, with Johnson the exception. This represents a somewhat different balance (so far) from the 50.9% conservative–49.1% liberal division reported in a previous Legalytics analysis of the 2025-26 term.

Perhaps more revealing was the distribution of participation within those blocs. Justice Brett Kavanaugh finished second across the sitting with 5,031 words, followed closely by Justice Elena Kagan (4,998) and Gorsuch (4,920). Yet the three justices occupied markedly different positions from argument to argument. Kavanaugh supplied 23.7% of the justice’ speech in Suncor but only 6.0% in Johnson. Gorsuch ranged from 25.7% in Beaird to just 1.2% in Salazar v. Paramount Global. Kagan, who averaged 21.2% across the opening-day arguments, averaged only 13.6% across the next four.

Overall, the six arguments revealed neither the conservative or liberal bloc dominating the conversation. Instead, participation shifted substantially with the legal questions presented, often placing different justices at the forefront of particular disputes. Those changes become especially consequential when examining the competing theories of judicial review and interpretive authority developed throughout the individual arguments.

Four arguments about access to judicial review

Four of the court’s first six arguments presented different versions of a recurring question: what must happen before a federal court can intervene in a case? The doctrines varied considerably, from Supreme Court jurisdiction and veterans’ constitutional claims to pleading requirements and administrative finality. Nevertheless, each argument required the justices to consider how procedural and institutional/justiciable boundaries determine whether a legal challenge can proceed.

The sharpest illustration came in Department of the Air Force v. Prutehi Guåhan, where the justices examined whether the Air Force’s decision to seek a territorial hazardous-waste permit constituted final federal agency action, and how that determination interacted with environmental review under a federal statute. The government received 67% of explicit question marks but accounted for only 52.7% of advocate speech. Its counsel, Sopan Joshi, experienced a 60% cutoff rate, which was the highest among the 15 advocates appearing during the sitting.

The exchanges revealed different understandings of when an agency decision is settled enough to justify judicial intervention. Kagan questioned whether an agency’s completed deliberations could be rendered non-final simply because another regulator’s approval remained necessary. Jackson, typically on Kagan’s side, focused on the opposite possibility: if Guam’s permitting authority could impose conditions materially changing the Air Force’s project, perhaps the application remained a proposal rather than an operative federal commitment. Justices Clarence Thomas and Amy Coney Barrett called out the unusual institutional arrangement, with the federal government appearing as a regulated applicant rather than the permitting authority itself.

Gorsuch and Chief Justice John Roberts approached the problem from another angle, exploring whether postponing review could leave challengers without an effective opportunity to challengee the federal government’s environmental obligations. Kavanaugh explored the practical consequences of requiring successive rounds of review.

The relationship to the opening-day cases is telling. In Suncor, Thomas and Jackson questioned whether the dispute was sufficiently developed for Supreme Court review, particularly with other federal issues and several factual questions still unresolved below. In Johnson, the justices confronted a very different institutional barrier: whether constitutional challenges involving veterans’ benefits must proceed through the specialized administrative-review system rather than federal district court. Jackson, Sotomayor, and Gorsuch each tested aspects of the government’s proposed route, including whether it provided a practical means of resolving constitutional claims.

Anderson v. Intel Corporation Investment Policy Committee brought up a related yet distinct procedural issue. There, the question was what a retirement-plan plaintiff must allege to advance a claim that an ERISA fiduciary failed to use the requisite “care, skill, prudence, or diligence” under certain circumstances. Kavanaugh and Jackson distinguished underperforming investments from allegedly imprudent asset-allocation strategies, while Kagan and Barrett examined how rigorous a “meaningful investment benchmark” must be.

These cases do not rest on a particular judicial ideology. But they all deal with the availability of judicial review: whether the litigation has reached a proper point, the question of an adequate forum, the sufficiency of the allegations, and whether the court’s intervention is appropriate yet.

Beaird: competing theories of legal authority

No argument during the sitting generated more speech from the justices than Beaird v. United States. The justices contributed 7,791 words, more than twice their total in Anderson, with Gorsuch and Jackson accounting for 25.7% and 22.4% of these, respectively. Together, the two justices provided nearly half the court’s substantive speech. Yet their exchanges revealed a profound difference in how they understood the authority of the U.S. Sentencing Commission and the legal status of its commentary.

The dispute asks whether Stinson v. United States, the court’s 1993 decision requiring deference to Sentencing Guidelines commentary under specified conditions, continues to supply the governing rule. The case’s unusual alignment further distinguished the argument. Both Beaird and the United States supported vacating the judgment, and the court thus appointed a “friend of the court,” Anthony Dick, to defend Stinson. The petitioner-aligned advocates consequently accounted for 68.3% of advocate speech and received 80.1% of justice words. This imbalance partly reflects the unusual distribution of advocates rather than necessarily indicating hostility toward their position.

Jackson, who once served on the Sentencing Commission, began by challenging the premise that the Sentencing Guidelines and their commentary can meaningfully be separated. In her account, sentencing judges rely on both parts of the Guidelines Manual to calculate the recommended sentencing range. Commentary helps determine how provisions operate together as opposed to consisting of an unrelated agency interpretation. She returned to Congress’ decision to establish a sentencing framework intended to limit disparities among similarly situated defendants, questioning whether importing limitations from administrative deference doctrine would undermine that design.

Gorsuch’s questioning approached the problem from a very different institutional perspective. He examined where Congress expressly authorized binding commentary, why the Commission should be able to accomplish through commentary what it could instead accomplish through formal Guidelines amendments, and whether deference would weaken the procedural safeguards associated with that process. His concerns extended to the incentives created by allowing the Commission to make authoritative interpretations without necessarily following the same procedures applicable to the Guidelines themselves.

Other justices explored possible consequences of either approach. Kavanaugh repeatedly returned to the stability of a system operating under Stinson for more than three decades, questioning whether revisiting the doctrine might introduce new uncertainty into sentencing. Kagan examined whether Congress’ express recognition of policy statements complicated efforts to distinguish them from commentary, suggesting that differences in terminology might not fully resolve the question of authority.

The exchanges suggest several possible routes to a decision: reaffirming the current system, limiting the circumstances under which commentary receives controlling weight, or requiring a more explicit statutory basis for binding interpretations. They also illustrate a theme from earlier research on the relationship between oral argument and eventual opinions: The most revealing material may not be which advocate received the most questions, but the competing legal frameworks the justices developed as they questioned counsel.

The first six arguments also revealed substantial differences in how advocates were able to develop their answers. Across 982 advocate speaking turns, 420 ended in an explicitly identifiable cutoff by a justice, a rate of 42.8%. That figure was somewhat higher on the first day (45.2%) than during the four subsequent arguments (41.4%), but the variation among individual advocates was significantly larger.

Joshi, representing the Air Force in Prutehi, experienced the sitting’s highest cutoff rate at 60.0%, with 66 of his 110 turns ending that way. Kannon Shanmugam followed at 56.9% in Suncor, while Paul Clement was at 55.2% in Salazar. These were not merely differences between government and private counsel but arose in disputes involving separate statutes, procedural issues, and judicial argument coalitions.

The clearest within-case contrast occurred in Salazar. Joshua Hammack received numerous questions about his interpretation of the Video Privacy Protection Act, yet only 21.5% of his turns ended in cutoffs. Clement, advocating the opposite position, was cut off in 55.2% of his turns. Similarly, in Prutehi, Caroline Flynn’s 41.1% cutoff rate (arguing on behalf of Prutehi Guåhan) was substantially below the government’s 60.0%, and her median response was more than twice as long.

Even advocates supporting the same position had different experiences. In Suncor, Shanmugam’s median response was 13.5 words, compared with 35 for Principal Deputy Solicitor General Sarah Harris, who supported the petitioner and experienced a 40.0% cutoff rate.

The differences illustrate how much the structure and subject of individual exchanges can shape an advocate’s opportunity to explain a legal position. These observations also complement earlier Empirical SCOTUS findings about an increasingly active oral-argument bench.

Jackson’s unusually limited participation in the first argument of the term, Suncor, proved to be an exception rather than the beginning of a broader retreat. Across six arguments, she remained the court’s leading individual speaker, even as the distribution of judicial participation shifted considerably from case to case.

More surprising was the relationship between the justices’ attention and their ideological positions. Jackson, Sotomayor, and Gorsuch consistently concentrated on the same litigating side. But their exchanges also revealed why common questioning should not be confused with voting the same. In Beaird, for example, Jackson and Gorsuch developed competing accounts of institutional authority despite both dominating the argument.

The substantive disputes supplied another connection. From Suncor and Johnson to Anderson and Prutehi, the justices repeatedly considered when judicial review should be available. Despite some of the substantive issues in play, the defining theme of this term’s start was one of judicial power.

Called "The Light that Shines Through the Universe," the sculpture is the brainchild of Vietnamese American artist Tuan Andrew Nguyen. It is a tribute to the memory of the Buddhas of Bamiyan, a celebrated archeological site in Afghanistan. The Buddhas were carved into the massive cliff in the Bamiyan Valley 1,500 years ago. The statues were dramatically destroyed by the Taliban in 2001, leaving empty caverns where the Buddhas used to be.

In an interview with Tricycle Magazine (Fall 2026), Nguyen speaks about his vision and intention for this work of public art. “The Buddhas of Bamiyan have always carried for me a profound lesson about the impermanence and instability of what we imagine to be permanent.”

He witnessed the destruction on television when he was in his mid-20s. As he processed the event, he took a wide view; he didn’t focus only on the destruction or the loss of a heritage site. He took a more profound perspective, or what we call a “cognitive reframe.” He reflected on the idea of Buddhist idea of emptiness. “In Buddhist philosophy, emptiness is not simply about absence or loss—it’s a space for potential.”

What is remarkable about this unique work of public art (which follows in the tradition of Rodin’s "The Thinker" and Maya Lin’s Vietnam Veterans Memorial) is the focus on the potential for healing. The NYC Buddha has two parts—a sandstone body (carved by master stone sculptors) and brass hands, held by metal poles, which evoke prosthetic limbs. The metal for the hands came from artillery shells found in Afghanistan and Vietnam. They glow with light when the sun touches them. They are in the classic mudras (or postures) of fearlessness (Abhaya) and giving blessings and compassion (Varada). What is profound is that the artist takes the materials of war and destruction and turns them into objects of peace and resilience.

When we look at the sculpture, we are moved by the monumental presence, the heartbreak of loss, and the ubiquity of violence, but above all, the possibility of healing. The Buddha, in its component parts, holds the possibility that we have the imagination, energy, and courage to create a better future for all.

I feel that this corresponds to what Nguyen is doing in his sculpture—sending compassion, peace, and reconciliation to the world with his work of art.

I had not yet seen the High Line Buddha, but I was holding the meditation practice in mind when I was in the presence of this remarkable and visionary work of art. This was the prayer I offered, based on a traditional loving-kindness meditation:

“May all beings be Safe and Healthy. May all beings be free of suffering and ill-will. May we all be Peaceful and live in Wisdom and Compassion. May we treat each other with kindness and respect.”

Can we, guided by our artists and spiritual teachers, send blessings out to this suffering world?

And, whether you want to welcome spring into your garden with pristine white petals or drifts of carnival color, there’s a variety to suit. Plant Crocus vernus at a depth that’s equal to three times the corm’s height, in sun or partial shade in zones 4-8, adding ¼ inch of grit to the bottom of each hole, and the bulbs will quickly naturalize. A favorite spot for these crocus is under deciduous trees where bare branches give them the early spring sunshine they need to flower well.

Because these spectacular flowers sit atop a single slender stem, Dutch iris bulbs look better planted in groups, which makes them easier to bury, too. Iris x hollandica bulbs are hardy in zones 5-9, but in zone 5 they benefit from a mulch of shredded leaves thrown over the top to keep them cozy. You can also plant these bulbs in zones 3 and 4 in pots in an unheated greenhouse, ready to move outside when the worst of the winter is over. These ornamental onions are very relaxed when it comes to when they’re planted and, although most folk get their alliums into the ground in October, as long as the soil isn’t frozen solid, they’ll flourish from a November planting. There are dozens of allium varieties with flowers ranging from small pom poms to huge spheres, lasting for up to a month then drying into structural seedheads.

As the size of the bulbs ranges as much as the blooms, use the usual rule of thumb to plant bulbs at a depth equivalent to three times their height. All alliums need full sun, where they’ll thrive in zones 4-9, and be sure to line holes with a layer of grit. While there are alliums that flower in summer, all my recommendations are late spring varieties that bloom from April into June.

There’s one big reason why you should add hyacinths to your garden, and that’s their incredible scent. As the flowerbuds open, their perfume is fresh, reminiscent of cut grass. In full bloom, an intense and intoxicating floral fragrance takes over, warm with vanilla and spicy with clove. Then as the flowers fade, the aroma deepens and becomes more musky. The scent profile can differ by color, too, with purples and blues typically boasting the strongest perfume, while whites and pinks smell a little sweeter, and yellows and oranges spicier.

Emerging in late February, hyacinths add an extra dimension to crisp, cold spring days when their scent hangs in the air. Hardy in zones 4-8, hyacinths prefer to be planted once soil temperatures have dropped, so the bulbs remain dormant but still get the deep chill they need to flower. Choose a sunny spot and plant bulbs at a depth of three times their height, dropping a ½ inch layer of grit into the hole to prevent them rotting. Hyacinths also thrive in a pot, where you can pack bulbs so they’re almost-but-not touching for a fabulous fragrance cloud on a patio or front porch.
