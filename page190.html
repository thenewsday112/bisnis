📅 Oct 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Frustrated by refrigerator issues? Use these tips to handle common problems and routine maintenance.

Not usually. In older refrigerators, water is often stored in a reservoir near the crisper drawers. But in many modern models, water flows through tubing that runs up the back of the refrigerator to the water filter, then down to coiled tubing that serves as a storage tank, and back up to the door dispenser. Along the way, it’s exposed to warmer parts of the refrigerator. Because the water is constantly flowing through the tubes instead of chilling in a reservoir, it may not be as cold as you’d like. Here are a few ways to enjoy colder water:

If the ice from your refrigerator has an unpleasant smell or taste, it’s probably time to clean the ice maker. Do this once or twice a year, depending on how often you use it:

We all learned in school that water freezes at 32 degrees. While that temperature is fine for making ice cubes, it’s too warm for safely storing frozen food because mold and bacteria can continue to grow on food surfaces. According to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the U.S. Department of Agriculture, the ideal freezer temperature is zero degrees. This is also the recommended factory setting for most major refrigerator brands, helping keep food safely frozen while conserving energy and extending the life of the appliance.

Dirty condenser coils are a common culprit. When they’re clogged with dust, the refrigerator can overheat, causing the compressor’s overload protector to shut it down. The refrigerator may restart automatically once the compressor cools, but by then your food may already be warming up. Vacuum the coils regularly to clear debris and prevent overheating.

Also check the condenser fan. If it’s blocked by debris or not spinning freely, the fridge can’t release heat efficiently. Remove any obstructions and clean the fan thoroughly.

Finally, check the interior light. A stuck or misaligned door switch can leave the light on even when the door is closed, raising the temperature inside the refrigerator. To test it, close the door and use a butter knife to pull the door gasket slightly away from the frame. If light shines out, the switch may be faulty or out of alignment.

Dish soap is a trusty staple. Put a drop on a microfiber cloth with warm water and wipe with the grain of the metal. Wipe again with a clean damp cloth, then towel-dry to prevent water spots and soap residue.

For tough grease or fingerprints, use a stainless steel cleaner. These products are formulated to cut residue without leaving streaks, making them a good step up when soap and water aren’t enough.

For a quick polish after cleaning, a little olive oil can help restore shine. Apply it with a microfiber cloth and buff with the grain until smudges disappear. Follow with a dry microfiber cloth to remove excess oil and prevent a greasy finish; this leaves behind a thin protective layer that resists fingerprints.

Dust and debris can easily accumulate on refrigerator coils, forcing the appliance to work harder and use more energy. Fortunately, cleaning the coils is simple. First, unplug the refrigerator and access the coils (they’re usually behind the front kickplate or on the back of the unit). Use a shop vacuum to remove dust and debris. For hard-to-reach places, use an air compressor with a blower nozzle to loosen the dust, then vacuum it up. Remove any stubborn dirt with a soft-bristled brush or duster, then vacuum again. Finally, clean the floor around your work area, reattach any panels and plug the fridge back in.

First, listen. Do you hear anything? There should be a rumbling from the auger inside the ice bin. If there’s a rumble and no ice, ice is likely stuck in the door chute and needs to be cleared out. If you hear a hum but no rumble, the auger is probably stuck and can’t spin; emptying the bin and reloading the ice may fix it. If you don’t hear any sound at all, the dispenser switch may be broken and will likely need to be replaced by a pro. If you hear the auger turning and nothing’s clogging the chute, move on to these steps:

A great place to start is with the handles, which tend to yellow and collect grime over time. Instead of replacing them, clean and repaint for a quick, budget-friendly refresh. You’ll need spray paint formulated for plastic, plus a degreaser and a scrubbing pad.

Pry off the caps that cover the side screws, then open the door and remove the remaining screws. Take off the handles and wash them with degreaser and hot water, scrubbing any textured areas to remove buildup. Wipe them with a dry cloth and let them air-dry completely. Mask off any emblems or chrome trim, then spray-paint, coating the sides first, followed by the flat surfaces. Once the paint has dried, reinstall the handles.

Baking soda is a highly effective odor neutralizer. As bacteria break down food, they release smelly molecules that can be either acidic or alkaline (basic). Baking soda, aka sodium bicarbonate, reacts with these compounds to help reduce odors. Stash an open box in the refrigerator and replace it about every three months. Tip: Date the box so you know when to replace it.

#2401. When Should You Actually Use Your Hazard Lights? You May Be Using Them Wrong

📅 Oct 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Hazard lights can warn other drivers when a vehicle is stopped or disabled. Here’s when to use them, and when to leave them off.

Drivers are notorious for forgetting common traffic rules, and that can be especially true during extreme weather. A perfect example is knowing when to use hazard lights while driving.

Hazard lights are one of the simplest controls in a car, but drivers don’t always agree on when they should be used. Seeing hazard lights while driving is especially common during heavy rain, fog, sudden traffic backups, and other situations where drivers want to make their vehicles more noticeable. But should they be used during extreme weather?

Learn when hazard lights make sense, when drivers should leave them off, and what to do when bad weather makes it difficult to see.

There isn’t one nationwide rule for using hazard lights while a vehicle is moving. Some states allow them in certain situations, while others limit or prohibit their use.

AAA notes that hazard-light laws vary by state, so drivers shouldn’t assume the same rules apply everywhere. Anyone unsure about using flashers while driving should check the current driver handbook or motor vehicle agency guidance for the state where they’re traveling.

Hazard lights, also called emergency flashers or four-way flashers, make a vehicle’s turn signals flash at the same time. Their main purpose is to warn others about a stopped or disabled vehicle.

After a breakdown, flat tire, or similar problem, the driver should move as far from traffic as safely possible and turn on the flashers. The same applies after a crash if the vehicle has to remain near traffic. If it can move safely, getting out of an active travel lane should come first.

Extremely poor visibility is one situation where hazard lights may also help while a driver gets off the road.

“In extremely dense fog where visibility is near zero, the best course of action is to first turn on your hazard lights, then simply pull into a safe location such as a parking lot of a local business and stop,” according to the National Weather Service’s resources for driving in fog.

Hazard lights shouldn’t serve as a substitute for headlights during bad weather. AAA specifically advises against switching them on simply because heavy rain makes it harder to see.

“Do not drive with your hazard lights on,” according to AAA. “Hazard lights are meant to signify that your vehicle is disabled. Using them while driving could confuse other motorists.”

The flashing lights can also make a moving vehicle harder for other drivers to read. During heavy rain, drivers should instead turn on their headlights, slow down, and leave more space between vehicles.

“Hazard lights can actually reduce visibility making other drivers think you are stopped or stalled,” according to AccuWeather. “They make it difficult to see if motorists ahead are tapping on the brakes and they don’t allow you to use your turn signals.”

Avoiding dangerous road conditions can be safer than trying to manage them after a trip has already started.

“Check your local weather and traffic reports before heading out,” according to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration’s winter driving tips. “If your roads are not in good shape, consider postponing non-essential travel until the roads are cleared. If you do have to go out, make sure you are prepared in case you become delayed while traveling.”

“Disparaging journalism is a far more central part of Fox News programming than it is of either MSNBC or CNN programming.”

In 2025, trust in journalism reached a record low. According to Gallup, only 8% of Republicans expressed trust in the news media, compared with 51% of Democrats. New data show an uptick among Republicans, but overall trust remains near this record low, and the gap between Republicans and Democrats remains substantial.

One of the key drivers of this lopsided decline in trust in journalism may be the extent to which news outlets spend time bashing other news outlets, or bashing the news media in general — a phenomenon we call inter-journalism disparagement. Our research team at Duke University’s DeWitt Wallace Center for Media & Democracy recently completed a study of the prominence of this phenomenon on each of the “big three” cable news networks (CNN, Fox News, MSNBC/MS NOW); whether it has increased or decreased over time; and whether there are significant differences in the rate of disparagement across networks.

The results were stark. Fox News far exceeds the other networks, with inter-journalism disparagement taking place once every 14.5 minutes. In contrast, CNN engages in inter-journalism disparagement roughly every 140 minutes, and MSNBC every 61 minutes.

To conduct this analysis, we gathered 15 years of cable news transcripts (2011-2025) for each network. We gathered a random sample of one week’s worth of programming for each year.1 With this approach, we ended up with 3,543 transcripts and almost 19 million words across the three networks, which equates to roughly 62,000 pages of text.

Given the volume of text to be analyzed, we trained OpenAI’s GPT-5.5 to identify attacks against the news media. We developed a 20-page instruction document to help the model code instances of disparagement. GPT-5.5 consistently achieved 99.5% accuracy at identifying inter-journalism disparagement and 100% accuracy for avoiding false negatives, compared to human-coded samples. (You can read more details about the entire training and analytical process here.)

We defined inter-journalism disparagement as negative statements about the conduct or content of an explicitly identified journalism target (such as a specific news organization, or the news media in general). Our definition, which is described in greater depth in the full paper, needed to be quite detailed in order for ChatGPT to produce accurate results. What we found when comparing the three networks tells us a lot about the dynamics of contemporary cable news and partisan journalism.

First, Figure 1 shows the rate of journalism disparagement across each of the big three networks. Because the volume of text that we analyzed varies across each network, our primary metric was the rate of disparagement per 1,000 words of transcript text. In this figure, we show not only the rate per 1,000 words, but we also convert that rate into a time-based frequency, using the industry standard of 1,000 words equaling approximately six minutes of air time.

In Figure 2, we graph the frequency of inter-journalism disparagement over time. Here, we see that the massive difference between Fox News and CNN/MSNBC persists over the 15-year time period. Still, we do see some dips in the volume of disparagement on Fox News in the two-year periods leading up to the 2016 and 2024 elections, with journalism disparagement levels on Fox News rebounding to near-peak levels in 2025. We haven’t figured out yet what to make of this pattern.

CNN and MSNBC seem to converge in their disparagement activity starting around 2017, with MSNBC’s disparagement rate decreasing somewhat, and CNN’s disparagement rate increasing somewhat. This pattern aligns with recent research that has shown, in other contexts, that CNN and MSNBC programming are converging in ways that suggest diminished differences between the more “centrist” CNN and the more left-leaning MSNBC.

A key takeaway here is that we should not think about Fox News and MSNBC as flip sides of the same partisan journalism coin. At least in relation to inter-journalism disparagement, these left- and right-leaning cable networks behave very differently. Disparaging journalism is a far more central part of Fox News programming than it is of either MSNBC or CNN programming.

In order to get a deeper understanding of these differences, we also identified the targets of the disparagement. These results are presented in Figures 3 through 5.

Figure 3 presents a breakdown of the most common targets of disparagement on Fox News. By far the most frequent target of disparagement on Fox News is “the media.” Following in a distant second is CNN, with the “mainstream media” rounding out the top three targets. Other relatively frequent targets include The New York Times, MSNBC, and The Washington Post.

As should be clear, Fox News devotes far more attention to disparaging journalism as an institution than it does to attacking individual news sources. This stands in stark contrast to what we see when we look at the results for CNN and MSNBC. Looking first at CNN, Figure 4 shows that the network’s most frequent target is Fox News (though with a frequency that wouldn’t crack the top three of the Fox News list).

Similarly, as Figure 5 shows, Fox News is also the number one target on MSNBC. Both CNN and MSNBC have the same top three, with Fox News followed by “the media” and the “right-wing media.” These instances of institutional disparagement happen far less frequently on CNN or MSNBC than they do on Fox News.

Finally, we were also able to identify which hosts or anchors were the most frequent disparagers of their fellow journalists. Those results can be seen in Figure 6. Given the lopsided amount of inter-journalism disparagement on Fox News, it is not surprising that this graph is dominated by Fox News personalities, with Sean Hannity taking the top spot, followed by Greg Gutfeld and Tucker Carlson (note: our graph for Howard Kurtz reflects his move from CNN to Fox in 2013).

This study didn’t distinguish further in terms of the nature of the disparagement across the three networks (for example, in terms of severity or legitimacy). This is something we are exploring in the next stage of this work. Nor did we examine whether viewer exposure to inter-journalism disparagement reduces trust in specific outlets or the institution of journalism. That certainly seems like a reasonable assumption, given the dramatically lower levels of trust in journalism among Republicans that we noted at the outset. We plan to investigate this relationship directly in future research.

But what we do see in this research is that disparagement of the institution of journalism, and of particular news outlets, is a much more prominent component of Fox News programming than it is of either CNN or MSNBC. Some researchers have proposed that this kind of high-volume disparagement of news sources that don’t tow the same partisan line takes us beyond traditional notions of partisan journalism and into the realm of “anti-media media.” The goal of anti-media media is to “weaponize the facade of journalism against the roles assigned to journalism” in a democracy. Based on our findings, Fox News fits this characterization.

#2407. I Tried To Renovate Everything at Once. Here’s What Went Wrong…

📅 Oct 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Taking on too many renovation projects at once can leave everything half-finished. Here’s how to know when it’s time to slow down.

Home improvement isn’t the classroom: there’s no extra credit for taking on more work, and you’re not going to make the honor roll for having the most unfinished projects.

It can be easy to fall into the familiar cycle of jumping from one major renovation to another and finding your to-do list growing day after day without actually whittling it down. I know because I’ve done it. One project turns into two, then something else suddenly feels more important, and before long, several rooms are half-finished at once.

At one point during my current fixer-upper I had so many projects going that the house felt less like a home and more like a construction site.

Before you hit a metaphorical wall (continue as planned if you’re doing demo), take a step back and see if this is starting to sound familiar:

You’ve got several rooms torn apart, tools and materials everywhere, and you keep starting new jobs before finishing old ones. Or maybe working on the house just isn’t fun anymore and every weekend feels like another shift.

It’s much easier to fix a bad plan early than it is after you’re already in way over your head. Spacing projects out gives you time to finish, clean up and actually enjoy the work you just did before starting all over again.

I consider myself a bit of an expert in this area because, well, I’ve been guilty of overcommitting to projects more times than I can count. While I still find myself occasionally doing too much at once, I’m in a better position to recognize those warning signs/red flags earlier.

Recently, I wrote a column for Family Handyman about how I’m adding a 95% day to my DIY routine. Basically, I set aside a day every few months to finish all those little things I’ve been putting off. Missing trim, nail holes, outlet covers and other jobs that keep a “finished” project from actually being finished all go on the list.

I’m also getting better at utilizing my project planner to see projects through and stay on schedule. Writing everything down makes it pretty obvious when I’m trying to cram too much into one week and helps me decide what actually needs to get done first.

Learning how to triage projects will also go a long way. What do I mean by that? Instead of doing a little bit of each project every day, focus on finishing one and then move on to the next one. You can either pick the one that takes the least amount of time to complete so you can check things off your to-do list quicker, or the project that has a more time-sensitive deadline.

For people who think in financial terms, this is like the snowball vs. avalanche debt payment strategy. Or for a sports metaphor, decide whether you need the easy points or should focus on the bigger play first. I’m out of metaphors, but I think you get the general idea.

Also, remember there’s no shame in hiring a professional to finish a job you’ve started. Life gets busy and contractors are often happy to take an easy paycheck on a job that’s already been started. They might even appreciate that some of the early legwork is already done for them. If bringing in someone to do one or a few jobs allows you to finish other things, it might be worth it.

An important DIY skill is learning when to walk away. If you’re currently in this situation, or find yourself overcommitted down the road and are thinking back to this column, just remember it’s totally normal and you’re certainly not alone. If anything, it means you’re working on making your home a little better, and that’s definitely something to celebrate.

But not for too long… there are still projects to finish, after all.

📅 Oct 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic's new Claude release, Sonnet 5.5, brings cybersecurity safeguards that can change which model handles a request during a Claude conversation. Higher-risk tasks can fall back to Sonnet 5.

According to Anthropic's explanation of model switching, automatic fallback is enabled by default in Claude. Users receive a notice, and the answer is labeled with the model that provided it. The chat then stays on Sonnet 5 unless the user changes the selection.

The company identifies penetration testing and exploit generation among activities that may trigger cybersecurity fallbacks. Source-code vulnerability scanning remains supported. Biology and reasoning-extraction safeguards can block requests outright, rather than route them elsewhere.

The distinction matters operationally. A block stops the request; a fallback allows an attempt to continue with a different model. Neither outcome should be treated as interchangeable with completion by the model the user originally chose.

Anthropic says Sonnet 5.5 generates output more than 30% faster than Sonnet 5 and can reduce cost per task by up to 30%, based on its testing. Token prices remain unchanged.

Those claims describe performance under the company's test conditions. An organization evaluating the service also needs to examine what happens when its own assignments encounter safeguards.

An internal pilot could record how often work is interrupted, whether fallback answers are usable, and when users need help. Such measurements would give buyers a more relevant basis for deployment than assuming every task receives the advertised model's capabilities.

Anthropic has announced plans to expand its Cyber Verification Program with tiered access for Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5, and Mythos models. Its current program guidance explicitly excludes the two new 5.5 models and says the expansion is forthcoming.

Organizations should distinguish those planned arrangements from access available today.

The broader enterprise issue is accountability. When an AI service changes models during an assignment, someone still needs to determine whether the resulting work is suitable. A visible handoff helps, but organizations must decide how employees should respond to it.

An authentic wheel of Grana Padano carries a signature brand burned into its rind—so you know that if you see a diamond pattern and the cheese’s name, it must have been produced in the Valle Padana (Po River Valley) of Northern Italy according to strict rules with roots in a 12th-century Cistercian monastery. At the time, the region surrounding the city of Milan was primarily soggy marshland. The monks who built the monastery—Chiaravalle Abbey—were followers of the Rule of Saint Benedict, which, along with devotion to prayer, mandated that they practice agriculture with a focus on responsible, sustainable use of land. They first figured out how to turn the marshy landscape into arable fields so they could grow high-quality feed for dairy cattle. Since milk (and fresh cheese) didn’t last long, the next challenge for the monks was how to preserve this nutritious, valuable food source to nourish themselves and their community.

The method they developed—heating the milk in copper cauldrons, adding rennet (a coagulating enzyme), cutting the curds into tiny pieces, and molding, salting, and aging the cheese—remains largely unchanged today. By producing the first iteration of what is now known around the world as Grana Padano, the monks of Chiaravalle Abbey earned the distinction of operating the world’s first cheese dairy.

Easy to like, portable, and a good source of protein, the cheese became an emblematic food, enjoyed by Italians at all levels of society through the Renaissance and into the modern era. Its iconic name, meanwhile, developed over time. The monks called it “caseus vetus”—Latin for “old cheese”—but since the peasants didn’t know Latin, they referred to it as “grana” for its distinctive grainy texture. As production spread well beyond the monastery, each province had its own grana: Cheese made in Milan was “grana Milanese,” and “grana Mantovano” was from Mantua.

It wasn’t until 1954 that the Italian government codified the name Grana Padano to unite the different varieties of grana cheese under a single wider geographical indication and that the Grana Padano Protection Consortium was established. In 1996, Grana Padano was awarded the European Union’s Protected Designation of Origin (PDO) designation. Today the consortium incorporates 134 member dairies, who together produced over 6 million wheels of cheese in 2025. In addition to safeguarding the name, the organization is also responsible for the global promotion of Grana Padano PDO and governs every detail of production to ensure that each wheel meets the strict standards established under the PDO.

The specifications start with the cows’ diet: All fresh forage and at least 75 percent of dry fodder (such as hay) must be grown within the cheese production area. Raw milk must be collected within 24 hours of milking and be partially skimmed, allowing for natural separation of the cream. After a natural whey starter is introduced to the copper (or copper-lined) milk vat, calves’ rennet is added for coagulation. The cheese is then broken into rice-size curds using a spino, which resembles a large whisk, and packed into round molds, where it sits for at least 36 hours. The wheels are immersed in brine for anywhere from 14 days to a month and aged for at least nine months and up to 24 months or longer. Wheels aged over 20 months can be labeled Riserva.

Bite into a chunk of Grana Padano PDO, and you taste the sweetness of fresh milk coupled with a savory hint of toasted nuts. Riservas may be flakier and more crystalline in texture than younger versions of the cheese but never dry or crumbly, and they must still have a delicate flavor. Grana Padano PDO is an exceptionally versatile cheese, equally delicious on a cheese board, wrapped with a slice of Prosciutto di Parma PDO and drizzled with aged balsamic for a sophisticated appetizer, as a filling for ravioli, or grated over soup or pasta.

It’s good for you, too. Lactose- and gluten-free, Grana Padano PDO is low in fat and an excellent source of protein, calcium, and vitamin B12. A 30-gram piece (just over one ounce) has as much nutrition as a pint of milk, making it a beneficial as well as tasty solution to the “afternoon slump” for both kids and adults. Whoever you share Grana Padano PDO with, be sure to tell them what they have in common with Italians from more than 1,000 years ago—that they’re savoring a taste of European culinary history.
