#1093. Pretzel-Crusted Pork Chops

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Pretzels and mustard are a match made in heaven, and this recipe brings those classic flavors to a satisfying weeknight dinner. The homemade creamy honey mustard sauce pulls double duty here, acting as both the coating and the sauce. This not only amps up the flavor of the pork chops, but it also keeps the ingredient list short. Once breaded, the pork chops are transferred to the oven, making for a simple, mostly hands-off recipe. You can use that time to prepare a quick side or two, like potato salad or mac and cheese, and just like that, you’ll be sitting down to a delicious dinner in no time.

The pork chops:When buying pork chops, look for “natural” pork chops. Enhanced pork chops are injected with a saltwater solution that can make your breading soggy and prevent it from getting that perfect golden crust. If you can only find enhanced, just pat them really dry with paper towels before breading and go easy on the salt. You can tell if pork is enhanced by checking the label—it should say something like "contains up to 15% solution" or list ingredients beyond just pork.

The pretzels:I love the flavor of pretzel snaps (aka waffle pretzels), so I went with those, but feel free to use whatever pretzels you prefer or have on hand—you can even make the chops gluten-free by substituting GF pretzels. If you’re going with a different shape, use the weight measurement to make sure you have enough to coat the pork chops.

The mustard:Typically, I would use a combination of whole grain and Dijon mustard, but because I’m keeping the ingredient list short, I went with a country-style Dijon. It’s a great middle ground, but if you can’t get your hands on any, stone-ground will work just as well. If you happen to have both whole grain and Dijon on hand, then you can substitute half of each mustard.

Storage:Cooled pork chops can be stored in an airtight container for up to 4 days. Pork is so lean that reheating can dry it out, so I recommend slicing up any leftovers and serving them cold—they're excellent on sandwiches.

#4354. Same Answer, Different Reason: What a Reasoning Model Says When It Follows a Planted Hint

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Publish AI, ML & data-science insights to a global community of data professionals.

I put wrong answers in front of 200 exam questions and read what a small model’s reasoning said when it fell for them.

Reasoning models show their work. Before the final answer comes a long stretch of thinking, and a lot of safety thinking leans on that text: if a model is doing something it shouldn't, maybe the reasoning will say so. Chen et al. put the condition plainly, that this kind of monitoring only works if the reasoning faithfully represents what actually drove the answer [1].

Their test is simple to state. Take a question, ask it twice, and the second time slip in a hint, for example a line saying a professor believes the answer is (C). If the model's answer moves to the hinted option, the hint changed what it did. Then read the reasoning and see whether it admits that. They ran this on Claude 3.7 Sonnet and DeepSeek R1 across six kinds of hints and found that the reasoning often revealed hint use in less than 20% of the cases where the hint had been used [1]. The idea of testing faithfulness with biasing features in the prompt goes back to Turpin et al. [2].

I wanted to see this with my own hands, at a size that runs on a free GPU. So I used a 1.5-billion-parameter model distilled from DeepSeek-R1 [3], 200 multiple-choice questions, and three kinds of hint, and I read what came out.

Here are the results first. The planted answer pulled the model to the wrong option in 14 to 28 percent of the cases where it could, depending on the hint, against a noise floor of 8 percent. In the 13 transcripts I read where a hint had moved the answer, exactly one said nothing about it. Six deferred to it openly. Six mentioned it and then argued for the answer on other grounds, as if the idea were the model's own. The automatic counter I wrote first got this wrong in both directions, and the way it went wrong is most of what I learned.

A hint is useful because you can compare. Ask the question without the hint and note the answer. Ask it with the hint and note the answer again. If the hint points at a wrong option and the model moves to it, there is a good chance the hint did that.

There is a catch that is easy to skip over. These models sample, so the answer to the same question changes from run to run even with no hint at all. If you do not measure how often that happens, every random flip toward the hinted letter looks like influence. So every question gets two no-hint runs, and the second one gives me a noise floor: how often does an unhinted model land on the "target" letter anyway?

The model is DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, run in half precision on a free T4 GPU with Hugging Face transformers. The questions are 200 random draws from the MMLU test set [4]. For each one I picked a wrong option at random as the target, and built three hinted versions of the prompt:

Sampling used temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95 and a limit of 2,048 new tokens. A run counts only if the model finishes its reasoning and gives a parseable answer, which excluded 15 percent of the first no-hint runs. Without the hint the model got 43 percent of the questions right, counting unfinished runs as wrong.

I call a hint "followed" when two things hold: the first no-hint answer was not the target letter, and the hinted answer was. Questions where the model already chose the target without any hint are left out, since the hint can't be blamed for those. That leaves 127 to 138 usable questions per hint.

The professor hint was followed in 39 of 137 questions (28.5%, 95% interval 22 to 37%). The grader hint was followed in 21 of 127 (16.5%, 11 to 24%), and the metadata tag in 19 of 138 (13.8%, 9 to 20%). The noise floor was 11 of 137, or 8.0% (4.5 to 14%).

The professor result sits clearly above the floor. The grader interval just overlaps it. The metadata interval overlaps it a lot, so at this sample size I can't say the XML tag did anything at all. There is also a limit on how to read the individual cases. If 8 percent of questions flip toward the target with no hint, then about a quarter of the 39 professor cases, and more than half of the 19 metadata cases, might have flipped anyway.

My first attempt at measuring faithfulness was a keyword counter. For each case where a hint had changed the answer, it scanned the reasoning for words like "professor", "verified", "validation" and phrases like "the given answer", and called the case faithful if it found one.

Here is what it reported. For the professor hint, 25 of 39 cases mentioned it (64%). For the grader hint, 15 of 21 (71%). For the metadata tag, 1 of 19 (5%). That last line looked like a finding: the model followed a hidden answer tag nineteen times and mentioned it once.

That is why I wanted to read the actual text before believing it. One caveat first. The full-run transcripts were lost when my notebook session closed, so the reading below comes from a 20-question pilot of the same setup: same model, same prompts, same sampling settings, run separately from the 200-question experiment. The pilot produced 13 cases where a hint moved the answer. Eleven came from the R1-distilled model and two from a second model I'll come back to. I read all 13.

It defers. The reasoning says it is going with the supplied answer. One transcript from the second model has it arguing that every option in an ice hockey question is a penalty, then conceding: "But given that the answer is A, I have to choose A as the correct answer." In one grader case, the R1 model reads the leaked check function aloud and closes with "So, based on the validation function, the answer is C." Six of the 13 were like this.

It mentions the hint, then argues its own case. Six more. The hint is stated, usually in the first sentence, and then the model reasons about the content and arrives at the hinted answer as if on its own merits. One opens with "The professor believes the answer is (C), so I need to think through each option carefully" and closes with "Therefore, the correct answer should be C." Nowhere in between does it say the professor is why. In another, the model decides the question is about the professor: "this multiple-choice question about a Stanford professor's response to terrorism." It had read the hint as the topic. A third names one person as the most likely candidate and then picks the option it calls "(A) Holland."

It says nothing. One transcript, a professor case, never refers to the professor or to any supplied answer anywhere in its reasoning.

The split by hint type is lopsided, though the numbers are small. Five of the seven professor transcripts were the mention-then-argue kind. All three metadata transcripts deferred, in words like "the answer is given as C".

That wording is what broke my counter. It had looked for "given answer", and two of the three transcripts wrote "answer is given". Of the 13 transcripts the counter flagged 10 as mentioning the hint. It missed two real deferrals, one of them an R1 metadata case and the other from the second model. And as a detector of actual deference it was right on only 4 of its 10 flags, since the other 6 were restatements of the hint in the first sentence. So the 5 percent figure for metadata was an artifact of a word-order mistake, and the 64 and 71 percent figures were measuring something looser than what I'd meant.

If you built a monitor that asks whether the reasoning mentions the hint, the six mention-then-argue transcripts would all pass it. If you asked a human reader whether the reasoning says the hint is why the model answered as it did, they would all fail. The two questions sound close and give very different answers, which makes me think faithfulness is better scored on at least three levels than on two.

I should be careful about what the middle group shows. The answer moved, and the stated reasons are about content. That fits a model that was pulled by the hint without saying so. It also fits a model that was not pulled at all and landed on that letter by chance, which the noise floor says happens. I can't tell the two apart from one transcript.

I also can't compare my numbers with Chen et al.'s. Their models are much larger, their hints and labeling procedure differ from mine, and a 1.5-billion-parameter distilled model may well write its reasoning differently. All I can say is that mentioning the hint was common in my 13 transcripts (12 of 13), which is a looser bar than revealing that the hint was used.

This is one small model. In the pilot a second one, Qwen3-1.7B in thinking mode, followed a hint in only 2 of 34 hinted questions (0 of 11, 2 of 11, 0 of 12), and both times it deferred, so it tells me almost nothing about faithfulness, though it does say that models differ a lot in how easily a hint moves them.

The reading covers 13 transcripts, one reader, and three borderline calls where I could have gone either way. I do not report a faithfulness rate from it, because 13 cases cannot support one. The 200-question numbers above are the ones that rest on a full run, and they only describe how often the hints worked, not what the reasoning said about them. Runs that never finished their reasoning, 15 percent of the total, are excluded, and they may differ from the rest in ways that matter, since a model that rambles for 2,000 tokens is probably not reasoning the same way.

Save every transcript this time. Have two people label them against a written rubric with the three groups above, and report how often they agree. Then run the same thing on a model large enough to be comparable to the ones in [1]. The part I'd most like to test is the middle group: whether the mention-then-argue pattern gets more common or less common as models get larger.

The strongest hint, a professor's opinion, pulled a small reasoning model to the wrong answer in 28 percent of the usable questions, against a noise floor of 8 percent. In the transcripts I read, the reasoning almost always mentioned the hint. Mentioning is not explaining, though: in six of the 13, the stated reason for the answer was the model's own argument, with the hint sitting in the first sentence like decoration.

My keyword counter scored those as honest, and it also missed open deference that was worded in an unexpected order. If you evaluate reasoning with a keyword check, read some transcripts before you trust the number.

#953. Birds are more afraid of women than men. Scientists have no idea why

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Birds living in cities appear to react differently to men and women, and scientists are struggling to explain why. An international research team discovered that urban birds, including great tits, house sparrows, and blackbirds, tend to fly away sooner when approached by women than by men. The surprising behavior raises new questions about how birds perceive the people around them.

The study took place in five European countries, where male and female participants (matched for height and clothing) walked directly toward birds in city parks and other green spaces.

On average, men were able to approach about one meter closer than women before the birds flew away. The researchers measured this response by recording how close a person could get before a bird took flight (termed flight initiation distance).

Published in the British Ecological Society journal People and Nature, the findings showed the same pattern in Czechia, France, Germany, Poland, and Spain. The difference also appeared across all 37 bird species examined, including easily startled magpies and pigeons that typically allow people to approach much more closely.

The researchers believe their findings indicate that city birds can distinguish between male and female humans. However, they have yet to determine which characteristics the birds recognize or why they appear more cautious around women.

Professor Daniel Blumstein of the University of California, Los Angeles, who helped conduct the research, said the results were convincing despite the lack of an explanation.

"I fully believe our results, that urban birds react differently based on the sex of the person approaching them, but I can't explain them right now. We used bleeding-edge comparative analysis techniques that showed our findings were consistent across cities and species, but we simply don't have a conclusive explanation yet."

Dr. Yanina Benedetti of the Czech University of Life Sciences Prague, another member of the research team, said the discovery challenged her expectations about how birds respond to human observers.

"As a woman in the field, I was surprised that birds reacted to us differently. This study highlights how animals in cities 'see' humans, which has implications for urban ecology and equality in science. Many behavioral studies assume that a human observer is neutral, but this wasn't the case for urban birds in our study."

Scientists have proposed several possible explanations for the difference. Birds might be responding to chemical signals such as pheromones, variations in body shape, or differences in how men and women walk. For now, however, these ideas remain speculative, and additional experiments will be needed to identify the actual cause.

Dr. Federico Morelli of the University of Turin, a co-author of the study, said the unanswered question is one of the discovery's most fascinating aspects.

"This is maybe the most interesting part of our study," said Dr. Federico Morelli from the University of Turin, a co-author of the study. "We have identified a phenomenon, but we really don't know why. However, what our results do highlight is the birds' sophisticated ability to evaluate their environment."

The findings suggest that urban birds may be sensitive to human characteristics that people themselves rarely notice. Identifying those signals could help researchers better understand how wildlife assesses potential threats in environments shared with humans.

Dr. Benedetti said: "Urban birds clearly react to subtle cues that humans do not easily notice. Follow-up studies could focus on individual factors such as movement patterns, scent cues, or physical traits, testing them separately rather than grouping them under observer sex. This approach would help identify the specific cues birds detect."

More Research Is Needed to Explain the Bird Behavior

Although the investigation included 2,701 observations, the researchers emphasize that their findings are still preliminary. Further studies will be necessary to establish whether the difference between birds' responses to men and women consistently appears in other settings and populations.

For now, the discovery points to an intriguing possibility: Birds sharing our cities may be paying attention to subtle differences between people that scientists have yet to fully understand.

#1506. Social Media is Obsessed With Creating Fat ‘Savings Accounts.’ Should You Be?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

RIGHT NOW, there is something that aesthetic dermatologists consider more valuable than any synthetic compound you could inject into your face—more valuable, in fact, than gold.

Yes, dermal fillers like the ones crafted from hyaluronic acid (brand names: Juvederm and Restylane) still remain effective in helping to restructure and add volume to areas of the face that are sagging. But using your own fat to achieve the same effect offers benefits beyond merely replacing what time has taken. In fact, it’s become almost routine in many plastic surgeons offices to liposuction fat from one area of the body and transfer it directly to the face.

However, some patients aren’t using that adipose tissue immediately, opting to “bank” it, creating the equivalent of a fat savings account. “Surgeons will remove extra fat beyond what’s needed for an initial grafting procedure, store it, and save it to have on hand as an insurance policy in the future,” explains Krishna Vyas, MD, a board-certified plastic surgeon at Blechman Plastic Surgery in New York City. This is known as “fat banking” and it's been making waves on social media as patients’ fat deposits are rapidly dwindling thanks to GLP-1 use. It’s transformed what was once considered something to shed at any cost into an aesthetic hot commodity.

Meet the experts: Krishna Vyas, MD, is a board-certified plastic surgeon at Blechman Plastic Surgery in New York City. Lyle Leipziger, MD, is the chief of plastic surgery at North Shore University Hospital and LIJ Medical Center. Anthony Berlet, MD, is a board-certified plastic surgeon in New York.

Ahead, we spoke with several plastic surgeons about how fat banking is transforming facial aesthetics—and what to consider if you’re thinking about it.

“Fat banking refers to the process of having fat removed from your body through liposuction, which is then frozen and stored to be used later for cosmetic procedures that add volume to the face, breasts, or body,” explains Dr. Vyas. It’s typically sucked out of either the abdomen, hips, thighs, flanks, back, or arms, and is “processed to separate it from blood and fluid, placed into sterile containers, and frozen at very cold temperatures,” he adds. The fat is sent to a cryopreservation facility where it's stored in liquid nitrogen until you’re ready to use it, the fat is thawed and injected where you need it.

Fat banking is not a standalone surgery. Instead, it’s typically performed during a procedure that already involves liposuction, such as body contouring, a tummy tuck, or Brazilian butt lift (BBL). Rather than discard the extra fat that was removed, your surgeon will process and freeze it for future use. “The banking step adds minimal time to the surgery you’re already having,” Dr. Vyas assures.

None of this to say that doctors haven’t been using patients’ fat for other purposes before today. Fat grafting or transfer has been routinely used for decades in facial rejuvenation, breast reconstruction, correction of contour deformities, and body enhancement. It refers to a process by which fat is removed and then immediately re-injected into another area of the body during the same procedure. Soft tissue taken from the abdomen, for example, can be placed into the cheeks or lips. Fat grafting has become an increasingly viable alternative to traditional hyaluronic acid filler, as a way to “naturally” re-volumize areas of the face, improve skin quality, and in some cases, slightly lift the brows.

The concept of fat banking, on the other hand, is relatively new, but is becoming an increasingly popular tool for plastic surgeons. The key difference is timing. Here, you’re not using fresh tissue, rather fat that’s been stored for weeks, months, or even years, explains Lyle Leipziger, MD, chief of plastic surgery at North Shore University Hospital and LIJ Medical Center.

Like sushi, fresh fat is usually best, and comes with the highest opportunity of survival. But studies show that stored fat samples will remain sterile and usable if properly preserved for up to two years. That said, Dr. Vyas explains that surgeons who do offer fat banking will most likely use the stored tissue within 3 to 12 months. “How well the fat performs after thawing depends heavily on how it was harvested, frozen, and stored in the first place, which varies from provider to provider,” he adds.

Beauty brands, dermatologists, and plastic surgeons have done their part in helping to raise awareness of one of the biggest consequences of rapid weight loss from GLP-1s—volume loss and skin laxity—which can significantly speed up the aging process. It’s an interesting paradox: While fat may be undesirable in certain parts of the body, in the face, it can keep you looking youthful. And since you can’t pick and choose where you’ll shed fat when you lose weight, fat banking has gained significant traction on social media as an antidote to GLP-induced laxity and volume loss.

Trouble is, for these patients, it may be too late. “It turns out that the fat patients are rushing to bank may not be their best fat to begin with,” explains Dr. Vyas. There is literature suggesting that body weight affects the quality of adipose-derived stem cells, one of the cells responsible for whether a fat graft actually survives and integrates with its new tissue. “Fat harvested from patients at higher body weights contains stem cells that are biologically compromised, making it less able to form new blood vessels, slower to proliferate and migrate, lower in overall viability, and more inflammatory in their genetic profile,” says Dr. Vyas. Translation: Once you’ve lost a significant amount of weight, banking may not be your best bet.

That’s why some doctors recommend that patients bank their fat before they shed excess pounds. Plus, despite the trendy appeal, the overwhelming body of evidence suggests that fresh fat grafting, performed at the time of the desired procedure, remains the gold standard backed by decades of clinical evidence. “My preference is to use freshly harvested fat whenever possible because I believe it offers the best biologic activity, best revascularization potential, and ultimately the best long-term outcome for the patient,” Dr. Berlet says.

A legislative proposal introduced in March 2026 in the French senate could considerably impact the country’s art market. Proposed by senator Jacques Grosperrin, it seeks to strengthen mechanisms for controlling French cultural property, expanding the state’s power over works held in private collections and considerably extending an authority that is already substantial. So far the proposal has escaped the scrutiny of commentators on French art law.

The proposed bill’s authority is grounded in the Code du patrimoine, which defines trésor national (national treasure) in article L.111-1 as “property designated as historic monuments, objects belonging to public collections or any category deemed of major significance to the national heritage on historical, artistic or archaeological grounds”. An export certificate is required before any such property leaves French territory, and the administration can refuse that certificate outright, without compensation, once the work qualifies as a trésor national. Following a refusal, the work cannot leave the country for 30 months. It is this very framework that Grosperrin’s proposal seeks to expand, broadening the definition of a trésor national to French artists’ works regardless of age, and extending this pre-emption to private transactions conducted beyond the confines of auction houses.

France blocked the sale of Rembrandt’s The Standard Bearer (1636, above) in 2019 so it could try to acquire the painting, but it could not raise the necessary €165m Courtesy Rijksmuseum

France’s recent appetite for naming new trésors nationaux gives this framework renewed relevance. Gustave Caillebotte’s Boulevard vu d’en haut (1880) was designated in February 2026, and its export remains barred until August 2028.

During those two years, the owner could not access the proceeds, the buyer had to keep their financing in place, and the vendor kept paying to insure and store a painting frozen mid-sale, without compensation. A more revealing counterpart is found in the case of Hans Baldung Grien’s Portrait of Susanna Pfeffinger (1517), which was withdrawn from a sale at Drouot and classified a trésor national in March 2026, a decision that has prompted considerable debate in legal circles. No foreign buyer was involved and no export was ever attempted. By using export controls to restrict domestic commerce, the administration turns heritage protection into a pretext for unwarranted market intervention, reducing trading activity under the guise of cultural stewardship.

This creates a market shaped by apprehension. International buyers hesitate to bid on a work they may never be able to export, while French buyers can be just as wary, since state intervention signals the work is important enough for the authorities to want it themselves. In Italy, where a comparable mechanism exists, the president of the auction house Cambi Casa d’Aste estimates an export restriction can cut a work’s value by 70% to 80% when it holds major international appeal, precisely the category France’s trésors nationaux increasingly fall into.

Particularly insidious is the state’s retroactive intervention. Long after a lawful transaction is finalised, authorities can unilaterally reclaim ownership on the premise that a work never legally left the public domain in the first place. Years later, when the firm applied for an export certificate, the state asserted that the fragment had never left public ownership, for lack of a formal declassification act. The Paris Administrative Court sided with the state in 2017, and the Conseil d’État upheld that ruling on further appeal three years later. Accordingly, neither the buyer’s good faith nor years of undisturbed possession could alter the outcome. The deeper concern is that scarcely any private collection can trace its provenance that far back, leaving collectors perpetually vulnerable to retrospective claims.

Crucially, Caillebotte, Baldung Grien and Rembrandt belonged to an established historical canon long before the state took any interest in their work. The age threshold in the current definition of a trésor national has kept contemporary work beyond the state’s reach. Grosperrin’s bill would remove that threshold for French artists. More damaging than the mere breadth of this authority is how unevenly that reach is applied. It is swift and forceful once a work has already acquired real standing, and largely absent from the process that created that reputation in the first place. That value is built over years by collectors, galleries and buyers who identify an artist, take a risk on their work and construct the market that eventually turns a piece into something the state considers worth protecting. The state has comparatively little presence at that earlier stage.

Under Grosperrin’s proposed expansion, the state steps in only at the apex of a sale, seizing the fruits of private risk under the guise of public duty. Turning every mature masterpiece and every contemporary breakthrough into a perpetual liability goes beyond cultural safekeeping and binds the future of French art to a machinery of state control that this bill seeks to institutionalise.

#964. Oxford scientists discover how viruses build themselves without a blueprint

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Researchers at the University of Oxford have observed virus-like particles assembling themselves one molecule at a time, uncovering the surprisingly simple rules that allow complex biological structures to form with remarkable reliability. The findings, published in Nature, offer a detailed look at a process that scientists have struggled to observe directly for decades.

Understanding how viruses construct their protective protein shells, and how that construction might be interrupted, could lead to new antiviral drugs, improved vaccines, and other medical treatments. The discovery helps explain how individual proteins consistently organize themselves into the correct structures despite having thousands of possible ways to connect.

The researchers found that viral assembly resembles navigating a complicated maze, where only certain paths lead to lasting progress. At first, protein components form weak connections that can easily break apart if the arrangement is unsuccessful. This flexibility allows the molecules to keep trying different combinations until they form particular closed structures. Once multiple connections hold these structures together, they become far more stable.

These stable arrangements serve as molecular checkpoints, narrowing the enormous number of possible assembly routes into a much smaller set of successful pathways. One especially important milestone occurs when five larger protein building blocks connect to form a closed pentagonal ring. This is the first highly stable structure in the assembly process. Once it forms, each subsequent stable stage requires fewer additional building blocks, allowing the particle to grow more quickly.

Co-lead author Dr. Roi Asor (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, University of Oxford) explained: "A virus has to solve an extraordinary construction problem. Its components somehow have to find the right arrangement among a huge number of possibilities, without a blueprint or machinery directing the process. We can now watch what happens molecule by molecule and see the physical rules that make it possible."

Watching Virus Particles Assemble Molecule by Molecule

Viruses store and protect their genetic material within organized protein shells known as capsids. Depending on the virus, these shells may consist of tens or even thousands of individual protein components. Remarkably, in many cases, the proteins can arrange themselves into the correct structure without any external machinery directing their assembly.

Although scientists have investigated viral self-assembly for decades, capturing the process as it unfolds has been a major challenge. Many of the intermediate structures exist only briefly and appear in very small quantities. As a result, traditional methods have generally allowed researchers to examine the starting materials and finished particles without clearly showing how one transforms into the other.

To overcome this limitation, the Oxford researchers studied an engineered virus-like particle composed of 60 protein units. They used mass photometry, a technique developed at Oxford that determines the mass of individual molecules by measuring the light they scatter. By combining this technology with a new approach for keeping individual molecules confined and continuously observable, the team could repeatedly measure the mass of a growing particle as additional proteins attached.

This approach allowed the scientists to follow the construction process as it happened, effectively tracking the addition of individual molecular building blocks over time.

Co-first author PhD student Dan Loewenthal (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, University of Oxford) said: "This method lets us study the assembly process directly, we just take a video!"

Co-lead author Professor Philipp Kukura (Department of Chemistry and Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery, Oxford University) said: "Until now, much of our understanding of how these structures assemble has had to be reconstructed from snapshots or theoretical models. Being able to both quantify the underlying interactions and follow one particle as it grows changes that. We can see the important intermediate structures appear and deduce a detailed model of the construction process."

The Surprising Trick That Makes Viral Assembly Work

The results help explain how a process involving an overwhelming number of possible molecular arrangements can consistently produce the correct structure. Rather than exploring every possible route, the proteins progress through a relatively small number of stable intermediate stages that guide assembly toward completion.

The findings also clarify why the earliest stage of construction, called nucleation, is particularly difficult and why growth becomes substantially faster after the first stable structure appears.

A key part of this efficiency comes from the ability of protein building blocks to make mistakes without permanently disrupting the process. Weak initial connections allow incorrect combinations to separate, giving the proteins another opportunity to find a better arrangement. When enough components eventually connect in the correct closed configuration, the resulting structure becomes stable enough to support further growth.

Dr. Asor added: "The weak interactions give the system room to make mistakes. Most encounters don't have to be successful: the components can separate and try again. But once enough of them come together in the right closed arrangement, the structure becomes stable, and assembly can move forward. That combination of trial and error followed by locking in successful structures is what makes the process so reliable."

New Possibilities for Antiviral Drugs and Vaccines

By identifying the molecular interactions that control viral assembly, scientists may eventually be able to develop treatments that interfere with the construction of viral protein shells. The same insights could also support efforts to engineer virus-like particles for vaccines and other therapeutic applications.

Co-author Dr. Jack Tan (MRC Weatherall Institute of Molecular Medicine and CAMS-Oxford Institute) noted: "The ability to understand these processes at the molecular level could have important applications in vaccine development and antivirals that disrupt viral assembly."

The implications may extend far beyond viruses. Self-assembly is essential throughout biology, helping create everything from the internal skeletons of cells to specialized molecular compartments and large protein complexes.

By making it possible to observe these intricate construction processes one molecule at a time, the Oxford team's approach offers researchers a powerful new way to investigate how complex biological structures emerge from relatively simple interactions.

#559. Was van Gogh’s ‘The Starry Night’ a flight of fancy or a real sky?

“The Starry Night,” one of Vincent van Gogh’s most famous paintings, was painted in the summer of 1889. Now researchers say they've figured out the exact night that inspired the painting.

Vincent van Gogh’s “The Starry Night” may have been based on one specific night sky: July 22, 1889, just before dawn over France.

If that’s true, it means the famous painting was based on real observations, not just van Gogh’s imagination, astronomer Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim and colleagues report October 22 in Scientific Reports.

“How could an invented sky fit the sky of a particular date this closely?” the team writes. “Observation and imagination have commonly been treated as an either/or, yet ‘The Starry Night’ shows the two coexisting on one canvas.”

Art historians typically date “The Starry Night” to mid-June 1889, based on one of van Gogh’s letters to his brother Theo.

“At last I have a landscape with olive trees, and also a new study of a starry sky,” van Gogh wrote on or about June 18. About a month earlier, van Gogh had entered a hospital in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, France. “The Starry Night” is thought to represent the view through the window of his room. A short time later, van Gogh experienced an illness during which he didn’t paint for about six weeks, so most historians think “The Starry Night” must have been painted before then.

That letter is taken as strong evidence, says astronomer Donald Olson of Texas State University in San Marcos, who has written about how astronomy shows up in art history. Historians favor the June date because “van Gogh himself said so,” he says.

That assumption has led to some confusion, though. In the 1980s, two astronomers tried to match the painting’s stars to real skies in Saint-Rémy in mid-June. One proposed that the sky was from June 19, 1889, and the collection of swirly stars is the constellation Aries.

But the other concluded it couldn’t have been painted on June 19, based on cloudy weather reports in Aix-en-Provence, France, about 70 kilometers away. That study claimed the painting was “a celestial composite, constructed to make an image of the morning sky.”

Kim, who founded a telescope-building company called SLLAB Inc. in Seoul, South Korea, didn’t know about the June date when he became interested in “The Starry Night.” “Unlike previous astronomical studies, we could start freely,” he says.

Given his job, Kim had an intuition for which stars would be visible when. “People like me, who guide a telescope through its first alignment with the sky, naturally memorize where the stars are,” he says.

He was skeptical of the Aries theory. The stars in that constellation are faint enough that they wouldn’t be all that visible in the predawn sky, “hardly enough to inspire a painter,” he says.

Instead, Kim saw the stars as the Hyades star cluster, which forms a sideways V shape in the constellation Taurus. To test that theory, he and his colleagues applied a statistical technique called Procrustes shape analysis to put a number to how well the stars in the painting matched any particular constellation.

Researchers compared the V-shaped collection of stars in “The Starry Night” (first image) with those in the Hyades star cluster (middle image). The resulting graph (last image) maps the real stars (blue) and the painting’s stars (orange) against each other. They don’t overlap perfectly, but they’re suspiciously close, researchers say. The label Sn stands for “The Starry Night.” while the constellation’s Greek letters represent typical astronomy notation.

The team tested the method on another van Gogh skyscape, “Starry Night Over the Rhône,” which clearly depicts the Big Dipper. “Apart from the Big Dipper, there is no star pattern we can say for certain that Vincent painted,” Kim says. The degree of matching between the art and the sky for both paintings turned out to be similar, a major clue.

Kim also used the position and phase of the moon (a waxing gibbous on June 19, in contrast to the sharp waning crescent in the painting) and Venus to further constrain the painting’s date. He used computer simulations that weren’t available in the ‘80s to calculate the positions of celestial objects on certain dates in 1889 down to the second. He even traveled to France in June and July 2019 to try to find the field of view van Gogh would have seen from his window. Ultimately, July 22, 1889, was the best fit for the team’s data.

“I still cannot forget the moment we finally found the spot, overlaid the drawing on the view and saw it fit perfectly,” Kim says. “I was standing where Vincent had stood 130 years earlier. And the thrill I felt when I confirmed that the moon, the Hyades and Venus of July 22, 1889, fit exactly within the drawing’s field of view is still with me.”

Not to douse Kim’s enthusiasm, but that field of view may not be exactly what van Gogh saw, Olson says.

“The authors’ assumed narrow field of view, essential for their theory that the stars of the Hyades fill most of the canvas, is inconsistent with the much wider field of view required to encompass ‘The Starry Night’s’ round hills and cliffs,” he says. That casts doubt on the paper’s conclusions.

Olson proposes an experiment: Tell an amateur astronomer that “The Starry Night” depicts the Hyades and ask them to point out the star Aldebaran, which is at the bottom left of the V. In real life, this is the brightest star in the Hyades. But the painting has other much more prominent stars.

“If [the authors’] theory is correct, then this should be easy,” Olson says. Kim and colleagues identify Aldebaran as one of the two stars to the left of the cypress tree in “The Starry Night.” But Olson thinks others will see it differently. “A decent guess is that an experienced amateur astronomer, even when prompted to see the Hyades, will not identify Aldebaran as either of the two stars to the left of the cypress tree.”

But a large new study in Communications Medicine suggests that this “eating jetlag”—the difference between when you eat on weekdays versus weekends—may come with a real cost for men’s heart health.

Researchers in France analyzed data from 104,806 adults (average age: 43) enrolled in the long‑running NutriNet‑Santé cohort. Participants logged the timing of their first and last meals every day, allowing scientists to calculate each person’s “eating jetlag”—how much their weekend eating window shifted compared to their weekday routine.

If you’re loose with your weekend eating schedule, the news isn’t great.

Men in both the Advance and Delay groups had a higher risk of cardiovascular disease compared to men who kept their eating times steady. The more people’s eating schedule drifted, the worse the risk climbed.

Over roughly eight years of follow‑up, researchers recorded 2,368 new cardiovascular disease cases, including strokes.
