#2099. The Supreme Court’s next major case on the free exercise of religion

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

On Nov. 3, the Supreme Court will hear argument in St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy. This case raises important questions about how far the court will go in expanding the Constitution’s protections of the free exercise of religion. One of the most dramatic changes in constitutional law in recent years has concerned the religion clauses of the First Amendment. For decades, the Supreme Court took a robust approach to the establishment clause, seeing it as best described by Thomas Jefferson’s metaphor of a wall separating church and state. At the same time, the court took a much weaker approach to the free exercise clause, as embodied in its decision in Employment Division v. Smith, which held that the First Amendment does not provide a basis for religious exemptions from general laws.

But, in recent years, the court has completely reversed this. It has greatly lessened the protections of the establishment clause; a majority of the current justices reject the idea of a wall separating church and state. In its most recent case about that provision, 2022’s Kennedy v. Bremerton School District, the court declared: “‘[T]he line’ that courts and governments ‘must draw between the permissible and the impermissible’ has to ‘accor[d] with history and faithfully reflec[t] the understanding of the Founding Fathers.’” Given this, it is likely that only government coercion to worship or adhere to a particular religion will be found to violate the establishment clause.

By contrast, the Supreme Court has aggressively expanded the protections of the free exercise clause. In 2021’s Fulton v. City of Philadelphia, the court held that it violates the free exercise of religion for the city, in contracting with private entities to place children in foster care, to insist that contractors not discriminate based on sexual orientation when they had a religious reason for doing so. In 2022’s Carson v. Makin, as well as 2018’s Trinity Lutheran v. Comer and 2020’s Espinoza v. Montana Department of Revenue, the court ruled that it violates the free exercise of religion for the state to provide aid to private secular schools that it denies to religious schools. And in 2025’s Mahmoud v. Taylor, the court held that it violates the free exercise of religion for a school system to fail to give parents of elementary school students notice and a chance to opt their children out of a curriculum that presented “LGBTQ+- inclusive” storybooks.

Colorado’s universal preschool program funds preschools – public, private, and religious alike – that sign an agreement to give children an “equal opportunity to enroll” regardless of, among other things, religious affiliation, sexual orientation, or gender identity. Two Catholic parish preschools and the Archdiocese of Denver say that they have a religious belief that they must consider a family’s sexual orientation and gender identity in admissions, and they sued under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 for an injunction against the preschool requirement.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the 10 Circuit upheld the Colorado law, applying Employment Division v. Smith and explaining that the Colorado law is a neutral law of general applicability; it was not motivated by a desire to apply to religion and applies equally to all. The court also explained that Colorado was not denying funding to religious schools that it provides to secular institutions: No preschool taking state money can refuse to admit children because of the race, religion, sexual orientation, or gender identity of the parents.

The Supreme Court granted certiorari on two questions: (1) Whether proving a lack of general applicability under Smith requires showing unfettered discretion or categorical exemptions for identical secular conduct; and (2) whether Carson v. Makin (that the government violates the free exercise clause if it denies funds to religious schools that it provides to private secular schools) displaces the rule of Smith only when the government explicitly excludes religious people and institutions.

There are many underlying issues that could make the case enormously important. First, will the court overrule Employment Division v. Smith and, if so, what will replace it? The court did not grant review on this question, but it certainly underlies the case. Five years ago, in Fulton v. City of Philadelphia, Justice Samuel Alito wrote a lengthy opinion concurring in the judgment, joined by Justices Clarence Thomas and Neil Gorsuch, urging the overruling of Smith. Justice Amy Coney Barrett wrote a concurring opinion, joined in full by Justice Brett Kavanaugh, in which she said that she agreed with much of Alito’s opinion, but that overruling Smith would raise many difficult questions and there was no need for the court to resolve them in the case before it.

Petitioner St. Mary Catholic Parish clearly is urging the court to jettison Smith. The first argument in its brief is that the “Court rarely applies Smith.” It says: “Out of the eighteen free exercise cases this Court has resolved since Smith in 1990, this Court has relied on Smith only twice to rule against the religious objector,” and one of these was Smith itself. But the brief says that lower courts, like the 10th in this case, constantly rely on Smith.

The ideological shift on the court with regard to free exercise claims has been dramatic. The majority opinion in Smith was written by Justice Antonin Scalia and it was liberals at the time that were critical of this holding. Now, it is the most conservative justices who are urging Smith’s overruling and it is the liberal justices who are defending it. Smith was about whether Native Americans should have a constitutional right to a religious exemption from a law prohibiting consumption of peyote – a hallucinogenic substance. Now, the cases are about Christians, such as the Catholic Church in St. Mary Catholic Parish, that are seeking religious exemptions.

Second, there is the question of when religion is infringed. If the court overrules Smith, this issue will become much more salient, as will the court’s need to define what is “religion” and a “religious belief.” Under Smith, with no exemptions for any religion, there is far less need to face these questions.

For instance, in St. Mary Catholic Parish, there is a question of whether the Colorado law infringes the church’s religious beliefs. The Colorado statute does not limit the ability of the Catholic schools to teach whatever they choose. The law simply says that a school receiving government funds should not discriminate against children, including based on the sexual orientation or the gender identity of the parents. The Catholic Church certainly may say that its teachings condemn same-sex sexual activity and gender transitions. But their contention in the present case is that their religion requires that they discriminate against children because of their parents being gay, lesbian, or transgender. This seems a much more difficult argument unless the court is going to accord considerable deference to religious claims in applying the free exercise clause.

Third, even if the court overrules Smith, does the government have a compelling interest in preventing discrimination against children on account of the sexual orientation or gender identity of their parents? In 2018’s Masterpiece Cakeshop v. Colorado Civil Rights Commission, the court considered the question of whether a baker had a right under the free exercise clause to violate a Colorado civil rights statute by refusing to design and bake a wedding cake for a gay couple. The court ruled narrowly, holding that members of the Colorado Civil Rights Commission had expressed impermissible animus towards religion. In 2023’s 303 Creative v. Elenis, the court held that it would violate the freedom of speech to apply a civil rights statute to force a website designer to provide her wedding services for same-sex couples. The court did not consider her claim based on the free exercise of religion.

There always is a tension between liberty and equality. Any law that prohibits discrimination limits the freedom to discriminate. For decades, the Supreme Court was clear that prohibiting discrimination was more important than protecting the right to discriminate. In 1968’s Newman v. Piggy Park Enterprises, Inc., the court summarily rejected a claim that a person’s religious beliefs were a basis to discriminate in violation of civil rights statutes. If the court overrules Smith, or says that strict scrutiny should be used under Smith, the question will need to be addressed: should the free exercise of religion provide a right to harm others by discriminating against them?

Fourth, if the government provides funding for schools, may it deny funding to schools that discriminate based on their religious beliefs? In a series of cases culminating most recently in Carson v. Makin, the court has held that the government violates the free exercise clause if it denies funds to religious schools that it provides to private secular schools. St. Mary Catholic Parish argues that Colorado is doing just that: denying it funding because of its exercising its religious beliefs and excluding children whose parents are gay, lesbian, or transgender.

But Colorado argues that its law does not discriminate based on religion. It will pay for children to attend preschools that are religious so long as they do not discriminate against children based on their parents’ sexual orientation or gender identity. In Carson, the Maine statute paid for children in areas without public schools to attend private schools but denied funding for children attending religious schools. Colorado says that its law is different because it does not exclude such institutions.

The Supreme Court could rule in favor of Colorado by applying Employment Division v. Smith. But the conservative majority – and especially Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett – want to expand the protections of the free exercise of religion. St. Mary’s Catholic Parish v. Roy could provide them the opportunity to do so and thereby dramatically change the law.

Erwin Chemerinsky is the Dean and Jesse H. Choper Distinguished Professor of Law, University of California, Berkeley School of Law.

Ben and Erin have been building the Home Town brand for a little over a decade, taking viewers into the heart of small-town America, and highlighting the all-too-often overlooked gems, including a maternity ward in their hometown, Laurel, Mississippi, a bowling alley in Fort Morgan, Colorado, and an Art Deco theater in Sebring, Florida, where fans got to see the Napiers tag team with the new hosts, Bryan and Sarah Baeumler.

Bryan and Sarah are hardly strangers to seasoned HGTV fans. Before landing their own hit series, Renovation Island and Renovation Inc., Bryan hosted four shows solo, with Sarah often joining as his design and business partner. Their latest spin-off breaks down the 12-week project into eight episodes, showcasing the small island community, which faces mounting natural disasters including tsunamis and, more recently, forest fires, which have caused quite the economic downturn, closing mills and damaging resources. While the hosts and locations may change across episodes, Home Town’s core principle will always remain the same. “Let the townspeople tell their own story,” say Ben and Erin. “That has been something that we learned in Laurel long before TV. #3054. Wisconsin Angler Lands Record-Breaking Muskie on Green Bay

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 20, 2026 12:43 PM EDT

The hottest fishing news, tips, and tactics

Captain Nolan Koepp and David Grisar started fishing out of Green Bay, Wisconsin at 6:30 a.m. on October 7 and they would go on to break the state’s record for longest muskie. They launched Koepp’s 21-foot Ranger boat into the Fox River, motored into the Bay, and started trolling multiple lures from stout rods.

They’d caught nothing by late morning, and Koepp knew a strong west wind was forecast as the sun rose.

“I ran from the southeast shore of the bay to the west side so we could fish in the leeward part of the bay,” Koepp tells Outdoor Life. “It was a little bumpy, but good for trolling there.”

Koepp, from Two Rivers, Wis., is a 19-year charter captain on Green Bay, and David Grisar, of Saukville, is his dentist. The two have fished and hunted together, but never for muskies. Grisar had never caught a muskie, and he knew Green Bay was prime water for big muskies. Koepp is a top muskie guide on the bay.

“We hadn’t been trolling long – it was just after noon,” Koepp, age 38, says. “We were trolling in 7 feet of water, and suddenly a fish slammed one of the lures – a 6-inch-long ‘Blue Water’ muskie diving crankbait in perch color.”

Grisar grabbed the bucking rod while Koepp cleared the other trolling rods from their holders.

“He fought the fish for about 10 minutes on an Okuma trolling outfit with 50-pound braided line, and an 80-pound fluorocarbon leader,” Koepp says. “The fish never jumped, but it sure fought hard and deep.”

Grisar got the big fish close and was stunned at its size. Koepp told his dentist-angler that the fish was huge, likely the biggest he’d ever seen.

Koepp netted the massive muskie, brought it aboard his Ranger and quickly measured it on a bump board.

“I’ve had some 55-inch muskies come aboard before, and some heavier, but never one that length – 57.5 inches,” the experienced Green Bay guide says. “I didn’t get a measured girth on the fish, but I’d estimate it to be about 25 inches around. I think it weighed about 40 pounds.”

Koepp unhooked the fish, measured it, took a few photos and released it in just two minutes, he says. He wanted to be sure to get the prized muskie back in the water as quickly as possible for sure survival.

“The fish was in great shape and swam away strong,” he says. “Green Bay muskies are feeding up hard for the coming winter. They’re gorging on shad and perch, and will soon start moving into feeder rivers.”

Grisar submitted photos of his muskie’s length to Wisconsin’s Department of Natural Resources fisheries officials, who have certified his catch as a new Badger State record catch-and-release muskie.

Grisar’s fish tops the previous catch-and-release muskie measuring 57 inches, caught by Illinois angler Sam Becker in August 2025. Three catch-and-release muskie records were set in Wisconsin in 2025 – all from Green Bay.

With Grisar’s fish, that makes four consecutive state muskie records for Green Bay.

Koepp says Grisar will have a replica mount made of his record muskie.

“I still have the Blue Water plug that he used to catch his fish, and it’s got some large muskie teeth holes in it,” says Koepp with a chuckle. “If he wants it, I’ll sure give him the lure. It would look pretty good hanging in that mounted muskie’s mouth.”

#4001. Found objects and fall-outs: publication reveals new sides to Donald Judd and his work

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Donald Judd’s To Susan Buckwalter (1964) art: © 2026 Judd Foundation / ARS, New York; Photo: Alex Marks © 2026 Judd Foundation

The US artist Donald Judd (1928-94) remains one of the most celebrated and studied figures of post-war American art—and one of its most difficult to pin down. In a new book, The Sculpture of Donald Judd, the art and architectural historian Neil Levine offers a fresh reading of the Minimalist artist’s work, tracing its development from painting to three-dimensional objects in the first chronological analysis of the artist’s practice. A friend of Judd’s from roughly 1965 to 1975, when the two had a falling out, Levine draws from decades of following the artist’s practice, as well as anecdotes from their friendship.

The Art Newspaper: A lot has been written about Judd—and by Judd. Why did you decide to write this book?

Neil Levine: I’ve had a long-standing belief that theory and art practice are very different. What artists write is writing, and what artists make is art, but the two do not necessarily correlate. A lot of Judd’s writings are about other people’s work. They give clues to what Judd was thinking about his own art, but these writings are not directly about his work. I love the language he used, but writings, like his often-quoted [essay] Specific Objects are totally opaque. If we keep using Judd’s writing to discuss his work, does it illuminate anything about the objects? I feel there are other meanings to discover.

Did you come across any discoveries while writing the book?

I had not been as aware of the importance of the found object on Don’s work. I always knew of the famous black painting with a baking tin inset that is in the Museum of Modern Art [in New York], but I never put it together how significant found objects were to Judd’s early work. I think there’s a clue to some of the meaning: that in its relationship to real objects, Judd’s work stands as a form of concrete art rather than abstract art. In its concreteness, it’s critical of the abstractness of modern life.

Judd's Untitled (1961) is in the collection of the Museum of Modern Art in New York art: © 2026 Judd Foundation / ARS, New York; Photo: MoMA, New York. Gift of Barbara Rose

Did having a personal relationship with Judd affect the way you approached writing about him?

Yes and no. Before even meeting Judd, I had a challenging relationship with writing about him. I wrote for Artnews after college and was assigned to review a group exhibition at Green Gallery that included Judd. This was the spring of 1965, and there was a sculpture called To Susan Buckwalter, of projecting volumes hanging from the wall, that just knocked me over. It scared me, it was strange. I’d never seen anything like it. I was so nervous that I was unable to review the show. That piece is on the cover of my book now.

Being friends with Judd didn’t mean getting to know him in the way you might think. One of the reasons I was keen to take the trip to France in 1975 was to get into his head. In the evenings, we’d sit outside and I’d ask him questions, but the answers were never very forthcoming. My comments struck a nerve, and this trip, as I share in the book, was the last time Judd and I spoke. There’s a reason he’s known as having been taciturn.

Some of London's leading cultural institutions are joining forces with the Indian organisation Serendipity Arts to stage a major festival celebrating South Asian creativity.

The Victoria and Albert Museum (V&A), Science Museum and Natural History Museum are among the partners for Serendipity Arts Festival London, which will take over Exhibition Road and South Kensington's cultural quarter between 19-27 June 2027.

The festival, which is in partnership with the Great Exhibition Road Festival led by Imperial College, will also be joined by the Royal Albert Hall, Royal Geographical Society, Royal College of Art (RCA) and Royal College of Music, which are all participating.

The festival will feature established and emerging artists from India and its diaspora, with a programme spanning visual art, music, dance, new media and food. Events will take place in galleries, concert halls, cafés and outdoor spaces.

Shefali Munjal, the co-founder and patron of Serendipity Arts, said: “South Asian creativity cannot be understood through a single discipline, tradition or geography. It lives as much in food, sound and movement as it does in a gallery.”

Six women curators have been appointed: Veeranganakumari Solanki and Nancy Adajania (new media), Latika Gupta (visual arts), Preethi Athreya (dance), Sneha Khanwalkar (music) and Elizabeth Yorke (culinary arts).

Kristian Volsing, a senior curator at V&A South Kensington, tells The Art Newspaper the partnership is “an incredibly timely collaboration”, ahead of the museum's exhibition South Asia Now: Fashion. Art. Design. at V&A East in spring 2027 and the reopening of its South Asia Gallery in 2028.

He added that the festival would create an “opportunity to centre South Asian creative talent”, with the programming “reflecting London's diversity and creating a welcoming space for all communities”.

The festival builds on Serendipity Arts’ annual event in Goa, India, which celebrated its tenth anniversary in 2025. Since launching in 2016, it has attracted more than one million visitors and participants and supported over 10,000 artists. The organisation introduced its work to London in June this year through two public projects with the Science Museum and Great Exhibition Road Festival. Other international partnerships this year include collaborations with the National Pavilion of India at the Venice Biennale, Rencontres d'Arles, the RCA and the Centre national des arts plastiques (Cnap) in Paris.

The Royal Geographical Society will develop an exhibition inspired by objects in its collections relating to the Indian subcontinent. Its director, Joe Smith, tells The Art Newspaper: “Applying creative methods and cross-cultural perspectives to these items will uncover different stories and learnings, deepening the connection between past and present and helping us see the world differently.”

He added: “We hope visitors to the exhibition find the experience enriching and leave considering how they can learn from times, places and cultures that may be different from their own.”

The partnership will also extend to India, with London's Natural History Museum presenting its Wildlife Photographer of the Year exhibition at the Goa festival this December. Further programme details will be announced in the coming months.
