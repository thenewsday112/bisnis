One bleak English winter a long time ago, wandering through London in the wake of a relationship that had just unraveled, I walked into an antiquarian bookstore and chanced upon a rare century-old edition of the Brothers Grimm fairy tales — which I had grown up with — illustrated by Arthur Rackham.

All fairy tales deal in enchantment, but the Grimms’ are especially powerful cautionary tales about the treacherous line between true and false enchantment in love — something Rackham’s art captured exquisitely.

One particular illustration stopped me up short, unnerved me, captivated me with its disquieting fusion of terror and tenderness:

The image haunted me for years. I kept returning to it, puzzling over what it was telling me. I lived more, I loved more, and eventually came to see it as a perfect and terrible metaphor for what happens when two people with attachment trauma mistake their trauma for love. Most relationships fail and falter out of this confusion, whatever its manifestation. Here, in this century-old illustration, was a key to an elemental truth: You cannot hold, or hold on to, anyone else unless you can hold yourself — hold your own heart, hold the roots of your pain, whatever it may be — with absolute golden compassion.

I wanted a response to Rackham, a counterpoint, something to visualize the power of that truth, so I set out to make it.

I began wandering forests and shorelines, looking for driftwood and windthrow trees, exerting inordinate joules taking off their centuries-old roots and giant burls with my Japanese hand-saw, spending inordinate hours sanding them and polishing them with urushi lacquer, then making for them small ceramic hands covered in kintsugi gold powder.

They now live in the Museum of Delight as large-scale reminders that we spend our whole lives learning how to hold our own hearts.

Odors, stuck-on food grime, and garbage bag juices can make your trash can a disgusting chore to clean out, especially if you have a habit of simply changing the bag without cleaning the can. Instead of just grimacing and looking the other way, you should be cleaning your trash can regularly to prevent mold, reduce the risk of pest infestations, and kill odors. Though the most basic reason is simply so that it isn’t so gross to use. Ahead, we’ll explain how to clean a trash can properly—both the smaller indoor types and the larger (and generally more disgusting) outdoor bins..

The best way to clean a trash can differs slightly depending on whether you are cleaning an indoor or outdoor trash can. However, regardless of trash can size or location, it’s essential to regularly clean the bin to remove stuck-on food, wash away grime, rinse out garbage bag juices, kill odors, and get rid of any pests that may have taken up residence in your trash.

Remove the trash bag, and transfer any loose debris or crumbs from the bottom of the can. If the bag is torn, you will need to remove loose debris and food with your hands. For this reason, it’s highly recommended to wear rubber gloves to protect your hands.

Even the best trash cans can sometimes become overrun with maggots if flies get into your trash. As gross as maggots are, dealing with them is relatively easy: Pour boiling water onto the maggots for an immediate kill. Alternatively, you can use a solution of 1 part white vinegar to 3 parts boiling water, or a 1:1 mixture of bleach and water.

If your trash can is maggot-free, take the can outside to the driveway or lawn to wash it—or, if you live in an apartment or condo, you can use your bathtub. (Make sure to sanitize the tub afterward.) Spray or rinse the inside and outside of the can thoroughly with hot water from a garden hose, faucet, or shower head to loosen surface grime.

Pour or spray all-purpose cleaner inside the can, and use a sponge or microfiber cloth to scrub it. For stubborn, dried-on food, sprinkle baking soda over the wet spots or spray on a mixture of equal parts dish soap and white vinegar, and let the solution sit for 5 to 10 minutes to break down the buildup.

Disinfecting is an essential part of the cleaning process. Spray a disinfectant spray or a mix of hot soapy water and vinegar inside your trash can. The disinfectant will kill any microbacteria and viruses that may be lingering inside the garbage can. After disinfecting the trash can, wipe it clean with a microfiber cloth.

Finally, rinse the trash can thoroughly to remove all soap residue, then pour out the soapy water. Let the can air dry before putting in a new trash bag to prevent moisture buildup. Once it is dry, sprinkle baking soda at the bottom to absorb future smells.

The process of how to clean an outdoor trash can vs an indoor trash can have essentially the same steps, but there are some differences in how you can approach the cleaning process. For outdoor cans, you’ll want to use a long-handled scrub brush instead of using a microfiber cloth, and rinse the trash can with a pressure washer instead of a simple garden hose.

#3590. She Didn’t Start Running Until Her 40s. At 47, She Finished Her First Marathon in 3:17—and Then Got Even Faster

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“I’ve only been running since 2022, so for me it’s all very exciting and great fun,” Vikki Robertshaw says. “And I feel like I’ve got a bit more left.”

Children are often inspired by their parents, but Vikki Robertshaw’s motivation was sparked by her two daughters.

“They ran cross country and track, so I would be taking them to events nearly every weekend, and thinking, ‘oh my gosh, they’re amazing,’” says the 47-year-old from Barrowford, Lancashire, in the U.K. As she watched them bravely show up to the starting line again and again, often getting excessively competitive and hard on themselves, she realized she “wanted to show them that it’s not all about winning,” she says. “It doesn’t matter how you do; it’s just about giving it a go.” What’s more, she wanted to set an example for her girls, showing them they can keep doing hard things at any stage of life.

Vikki swam competitively as a teenager, but she had never tried running before. And while she always made it a priority to take her daughters to athletic clubs and races, as a full-time working mom, she never prioritized her own fitness journey.

By the time she got the running itch four years ago, her daughters were teenagers, old enough to be left on their own. “I started very slowly, doing a little bit here and there with no intention of doing anything major. I just wanted to do something for myself, really,” she says.

Vikki pushed herself to run farther and farther, and at some point signed up for a few half marathons—that’s when she discovered she absolutely loved racing. At the time, “I was running with no real routine or plan at all, just seeing how it went,” she says.

But that all changed when she joined a running club and signed up for her first marathon.

“I had one pair of trainers that did everything; I had no idea what a tempo meant; I’d never done intervals, and never run on a track,” Vikki says. Through the club, she started working with a running coach, George Woodburn. “He’s been really instrumental,” says Vikki, emphasizing the importance of having a human coach who understands that, as a working mom, she has other priorities. “As much as I love running, it’s not my whole life. So he made it more realistic, and for me to be able to achieve my goals rather than compare myself to other people.”

Her coach kept things very simple, advising Vikki to add one weekly track workout to help build her speed, on top of her weekend long run, and outside her easy runs. She kept up this two-key-runs-per-week routine for the four months leading up to her first marathon. “Because I started to get more of a structure, that goal became achievable.”

She stood at the start line of the 2024 MBNA Chester Marathon with an ambitious goal for her first race of that distance: to finish in 3:30. “I’d kind of worked out at that point, with a bit of support, what I was going to aim for,” she recalls.

Vikki remembers taking off a bit too fast, propelled by the excitement of her first marathon. As she was running out of steam in the final miles, she serendipitously bumped into her friend Katie—who was running the metric marathon (26.2 kilometers instead of miles, or 16.28 miles)—at the point where the two race courses converged. “She kind of almost dragged me to the end,” Vikki laughs. “So I did actually pick my pace up in those last few miles. I’m not sure it would have happened otherwise, but it was like fate that she was there.”

At the end of the race, Vikki was overjoyed at her accomplishment—before she even learned about her finishing time of 3:17:20.

She was excited to learn that the time qualified her for the 2026 London Marathon. Leading up to it, she became even more intentional about her training, researching nutrition and including strength training to better support her overall strength and recovery. Before the race, she wrote her splits on her arm—in case her GPS glitched—which helped her maintain a steady pace through each mile. She finished in 3:13:08.

Vikki has more ambitious running goals, however, with so much messaging about women simply maintaining speed or getting slower in their 40s, she admits there are times she questions whether she’s already hit her peak, and wonders if it’s possible to keep increasing speed. “I’ve only been running since 2022, so for me it’s all very exciting and great fun,” she says. But then she looks at stories about achievements of women her age and older and feels hopeful that together they’re rewriting what’s possible for them. That their prime is far from over. “I look at other women that are faster, so it is feasible—whether it’s realistic for me, I don’t know, but I’m enjoying trying,” she says. “And I feel like I’ve got a bit more left.”

You don’t have to sign up for a full marathon right off the bat. “Start with 30 minutes, three times per week, and just build consistency from there,” Vikki says. “You never know where it’ll take you or what you’re capable of achieving until you try—so just give it a go.”

When Vikki started her running journey, strength training was the farthest thing from her mind, but now she sees it as vital. “My intention is to make sure I do like a couple of sessions of strength work each week, to prevent injury and things like that going forward,” she says.

In a similar camp of taking care of your body, Vikki is a big advocate for proper fueling, plus plenty of rest and recovery. “There is no one key thing, but it is always about getting enough sleep.”

Vikki didn’t find running until her 40s: It’s never too late, and there is no perfect time to start. “Just know that you don’t have to have all the gear, and you don’t need the perfect conditions,” she says. “You literally just go out there and put one foot in front of the other.”

#4337. Does Agentic RAG Actually Retrieve Better Evidence? I Tested It on 298 Questions

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Publish AI, ML & data-science insights to a global community of data professionals.

I let an AI agent search again whenever it chose. It found some papers plain search had missed, lost some plain search had found, and gained too little to trust.

In January 2025, Aditi Singh and coauthors published a survey titled Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation: A Survey on Agentic RAG, revised in April 2026. They describe agentic retrieval augmented generation, or agentic RAG, as a search system with an AI agent inside it. The agent can plan its next step, check its own results, and use other programs, such as a calculator or a search box, while it looks for information. The same survey lists evaluation and efficiency among the open challenges. That is the part I wanted to measure.

Retrieval augmented generation, or RAG, means that a language model, an AI system that writes text, answers a question using text it first retrieved from a collection of documents. RAG with plain search looks the question up once, as written. Agentic RAG adds an agent. A language model reads the first results and decides whether to stop or to write a new search.

More searches look like they should find better evidence, meaning the paper that actually answers the question. Each extra search also needs another language model call, and whoever runs the system pays for it. Each one also makes the user wait longer and gives the agent another chance to write a worse search than the one it started with.

So I ran one test. I designed it and used an AI coding assistant, Claude Code, a tool that writes and runs code from instructions, to build and run it. I tried four ways of searching, with the same 298 questions over the same 2,000 recent arXiv papers. The four ways were these.

Learn this step by step with the interactive AI Engineer roadmap.

Plain search looks the question up once, as written.

A reranker makes the same search, then uses a small scoring model to reorder the first 50 results.

A question rewriter asks a language model to rewrite the question three ways, searches with all four versions, and merges the results.

An agent makes a search, then lets a language model read the first results. The model decides whether to stop or to write a new search, up to three searches in all.

All four are ways of doing the first half of RAG. RAG has two halves. The first half retrieves text that is relevant to the question. The second half gives that text to a language model, which writes the answer. I tested only the first half, so none of these four writes an answer.

Here is how each one fits. Plain search is the retrieval half of ordinary RAG in its simplest form. The reranker is the same, with a common extra step that reorders the results. The question rewriter is the retrieval half of RAG with question rewriting, another common technique, and it follows the same fixed steps every time. Only the agent is agentic, because it reads the results and chooses whether to stop or to search again.

For each way, I counted how often it put the right paper among the first five results it returned, its top 5. The question rewriter and the agent use a language model, so they do not return exactly the same answer twice. I ran each of them three times.

The agent put the right paper in the top 5 for 96.87 percent of questions, against 95.64 percent for plain search. That gap is +1.2 percentage points, and the range of plausible values runs from -0.8 to +3.5. A range that includes zero means this test cannot tell the two apart.

Plain search left the right paper out of its top 5 on 13 questions. The agent found the right paper for 7 to 8 of them in each run, and lost 3 to 5 papers that plain search had found.

A reranker reorders the first 50 results with a second model and needs no language model call. It put the right paper higher in the list by a clear margin, with a score of 0.959 against 0.895 on the rank measure explained below. It cost nothing in model fees.

Rewriting the question into three variants and merging the results did worse than plain search, at 93.6 against 95.6 percent, though that gap also sits inside its range.

The short answer to the title is that, on this test, giving the agent more searches did not clearly find better evidence. It found some papers plain search missed and lost some plain search had found, and the gain it left was too small to tell from chance. The one clear improvement came from the reranker, a cheap step that reordered the first 50 results and moved the right paper higher.

Everything below shows how I got there, what I changed along the way, and where the result stops being reliable.

I needed to measure the searching alone, so the quality of a final written answer could not hide it. The test therefore stops one step before the answer. For each question, there is exactly one paper whose abstract, its short summary, answers it. A way of searching succeeds if that paper appears in the first five results. From here on I call each way of searching a design.

Here are the four designs, named dense, rerank, multiquery, and agent. The first one, dense, is the plain search and the baseline, which is the version the others are compared with. Each of the others adds one thing. The one that rewrites the question first is multiquery, and the one that can search again is agent.

Dense is the plain search and the baseline. It turns the question into an embedding, which is a list of numbers that represents what the text means. Every abstract has its own embedding too, made beforehand by the same model. The search returns the abstracts whose numbers sit closest to the question's, much like finding the nearest points on a map.

It makes one search and calls no language model. I used the embedding model BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5, which is released under the MIT license.

Rerank is the same search with one extra step. The dense search first returns its top 50 abstracts. A second model, called a cross encoder, then reads the question and each abstract together and gives each pair a score for how well they fit. The 50 are reordered by that score, so the abstract that fits best moves to the top.

It reads each pair in full, so it runs only on the 50 and not on all 2,000 abstracts. I used cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2, released under Apache 2.0. It is a small scoring model that never writes text.

Multiquery is the search with rewriting. Claude Haiku 5.5, a small and fast model from Anthropic, writes three different versions of the question. The system searches with the original and the three versions, which gives four ranked lists. It merges the four lists into one with reciprocal rank fusion, or RRF.

RRF gives each paper 1 divided by (60 plus its position) for every list it appears in, and adds the scores up. A paper in position 1 of a list earns 1/61, and a paper in position 10 earns 1/70. A paper that sits high in several lists therefore ends up on top. Cormack, Clarke, and Büttcher introduced it in 2009 and fixed the constant at 60.

Agent is the search that can try again. The first search uses the question as written. Claude Haiku 5.5 then sees the titles of the top 5 results and replies either "stop" or with a new query, which is the words to search for. It can search at most three times. The lists from all its searches are merged with the same RRF setting, so it ends with one ranked list like the others.

A librarian is a fair picture of the agent. Ask a plain question and a plain search returns what sits nearest on the shelf. A librarian who rephrases it, perhaps using the words researchers in that area actually use, may find the right book. The same librarian may also guess words from the wrong area and walk away from the right shelf. Which of those happens more often is what I measured.

One fairness point matters here. The agent and multiquery search more than once, and dense searches once. The results table below prints the average number of searches next to every design, so more searching is visible and not hidden.

The test is only as good as its questions and its collection, so here is how I built both, and what I checked.

I collected 2,000 papers from arXiv, the open server where researchers post papers before peer review. They come from four categories. cs.AI is for artificial intelligence, cs.CL for computation and language, cs.IR for information retrieval, and cs.LG for machine learning.

The papers were submitted on 92 different days between 1 July and 30 September 2026. I chose that window because Anthropic's model overview lists a June 2026 training data cutoff for the models I used. That is the last date their training data covers, so the papers should not be in it. The title and abstract of each paper are the only text any design could see.

Before running any search, I picked 300 papers at random to write questions about. I call them source papers. For each one, Claude Sonnet 5.5 read the title and abstract and wrote one question that the paper answers. The instructions told it never to copy the title or a stretch of four or more words from it.

The questions rotate through three styles. A specific question names the method or setting in Claude Sonnet 5.5's own words. A problem question describes the problem without naming the method. A vague question is short and imprecise, like something half remembered.

The table shows three of the 298 questions, one of each style. Each is the first of its style in my list, so I picked them by a fixed rule and not because they looked good. For each one, it gives the paper the question was written from and the place that paper came in when plain search ran, where 1 means first. All 298 questions are in the repository.

I set the rules, and Claude Sonnet 5.5 wrote each question from a paper's abstract. Each question was then checked against that abstract, as the section on checking below describes. The questions here are long and detailed. Even the one labeled vague is 28 words and names specific ideas, such as "two separate measures" and "ROC curves". A person typing into a search box might write only a few words, such as "clustering evaluation ROC", which gives a search much less to work with.

In a small trial run first, which I call the pilot and describe below, plain search was too good, so I added a harder condition. For each source paper, I put its five most similar papers into the collection, measured by shared words (term frequency inverse document frequency, or TF IDF). I used shared words and not the embedding model, so the lookalike papers were not chosen by the same model that does the searching.

These neighbors are only loosely similar. The median top neighbor had a similarity of 0.15 on a scale where 1 means identical. That means the neighbors share some topic words but are far from copies. This test is therefore harder than a random collection and still resembles ordinary lookalike papers on the same topic.

A question can be unfair if the abstract does not actually answer it, or if another paper answers it just as well. The coding assistant first read every question against its source abstract and the three nearest other papers and marked each one pass or fail. I then spot checked 30 of the passes against their abstracts and found no unsupported answers. That check covers 30 questions, and the other 268 rest on the assistant's pass alone.

Two questions failed because they asked for a result the abstract does not report, which left 298. For example, one asked how much labeled data is needed, which the abstract never states. Their IDs are q064 and q088 in the repository.

I ran a small pilot first, because a test is useless if the plain search already gets everything right. The pilot used 300 papers and 20 questions, of which 17 passed review.

Plain search put the right paper at rank 1, which is first place, for all 17 of them. No design could have looked better, so the pilot could not separate anything. That is why I added the lookalike papers and grew the collection to 2,000.

The pilot also exposed a mistake in my first agent. It merged its searches by keeping each paper's best raw similarity score. On one pilot question, a rewritten query pushed the right paper from rank 1 down to rank 4. The reason is that a score of 0.8 for one query does not mean the same as 0.8 for another. I changed the agent to merge by position with RRF, the same setting multiquery uses.

I did not tune the constant of 60, and I did not try other values.

Here is everything that changed along the way, and what triggered each change.

The collection grew and gained lookalike papers, because the pilot was too easy.

The agent's merge changed from best score to RRF, because of the pilot question above.

Three questions changed after review and before any search that calls a language model ran. Two repeated four or more words of their paper's title, so I regenerated them. One question's source paper had also been a pilot source, so I replaced it with another paper.

Five questions initially came back without a question because Claude Sonnet 5.5 replied with plain text, not the format I expected. I accepted the plain text, so all 300 got a question.

After I saw the plain search and reranker results, I read again all 13 questions that plain search missed, along with the papers it returned instead. I changed no question, and I removed none because of a result. I also did not drop the questions that plain search already ranked first. Removing them would have meant choosing the test after seeing the answer.

Two measures carry the results, and both are higher when the design is better.

Recall at 5 (recall@5) is the share of questions where the right paper appears in the top 5. If it is 95 percent, then for 95 of 100 questions a reader looking at five results would see the right paper.

Mean reciprocal rank at 10 (MRR@10) rewards putting the right paper first. A paper at rank 1 scores 1, rank 2 scores one half, rank 5 scores one fifth, and a paper outside the top 10 scores 0. The score is the average over questions.

A single average hides how much it could move with a different set of questions. So I report a range for each difference. Imagine drawing 298 questions from the pile again and again, putting each one back after it is drawn, and recomputing the difference each time. I did that 2,000 times and took the middle 95 percent of the results. This is a bootstrap range, and it shows how much the answer moves when the set of questions changes.

The difference is paired, meaning each design is compared with plain search on the same questions. When a range includes zero, the data are compatible with no real difference.

The two designs that call a language model, multiquery and agent, ran three times each. Dense and rerank return the same result every time, so they ran once. Claude Haiku 5.5 does not let me set the temperature. That setting is the control that makes a model repeat itself more or less exactly, so its replies vary a little from run to run.

I turned off its thinking option, where the model reasons at length before it answers. That way the reported cost and the delay, which is the time from the question to the ranked list, reflect the retrieval design alone, with no hidden reasoning added. Claude Sonnet 5.5, which wrote the questions, does not allow thinking to be turned off, so I used its lowest thinking setting instead.

Plain search already finds the right paper in 95.6 percent of questions, and it ranks it first for 253 of 298. That leaves 13 questions where an extra search could help. Recall can rise by at most 13 of 298 questions, which is 4.4 points, so no design can gain more than that on recall. I come back to this under the limits.

This section matters because a higher number in the table does not mean a better design. The chart shows each design's change from plain search, with its range.

The reranker gained +1.3 points on recall@5 (range -0.7 to +3.7), which includes zero. On rank quality, which is MRR@10, it gained +0.064 (range +0.037 to +0.093), which clears zero. It is the only clear improvement in the test. It moves the right paper higher, and that costs one extra scoring pass and no model fees. It also adds about 1.5 seconds of delay on my laptop.

The agent gained +1.2 points on recall (range -0.8 to +3.5). On rank quality it gained +0.011 (range -0.006 to +0.029). Both ranges include zero. The honest reading is that the agent may be a little better, may be no different, and may be slightly worse, and this test cannot say which.

Multiquery changed recall by -2.0 points (range -4.6 to +0.4), and on rank quality by -0.026 (range -0.057 to +0.002). Both ranges include zero, but in every run it lost 11 to 13 of the 285 papers plain search had found, which also suggests it is worse.

The three runs differ by 0.3 points for agent and 1.3 points for multiquery on recall. Both are smaller than the 4 to 5 points of width in the ranges above. That width comes from having 298 questions, so repeating the runs more would not change the picture. More questions would.

The averages hide what actually happened inside the searches, so this section looks at specific questions. I saved every query the agent wrote, and the queries quoted below are exactly as it wrote them, including the technical shorthand.

The agent turned plain wording into the words researchers in that area actually use. One question asked how to send incoming requests, in real time, to several servers that generate text, each with limited capacity, while balancing response time against total output. Plain search did not return the right paper in its top 10. Rewrites that guessed words from the agent's memory of the area often named something else. The merge then placed those other papers above a paper plain search had already found.

Only 2 questions were lost in all three runs. The other 6 were lost in just one of the three runs, which is ordinary variation between runs. That is 12 losses in total, split 4, 3, and 5 across the three runs.

The agent stopped after one search on about 59 percent of questions, after a single model call to decide. It used all three searches on about 26 percent. On 7 to 8 questions per run it used all three and still missed. Five questions were never found by plain search or by any agent run.

A result without its limits is easy to overread, so here are the limits, in the order I would worry about them.

There is little room to improve. Plain search is right on 285 of 298 questions, so only 13 questions can show a gain on recall. The ranges above reach about 2 to 3 points on either side of each estimate, so a smaller real gain would not show. A harder collection, or many more questions, would be needed to detect a small real benefit.

It is one collection and one kind of question. The papers are recent AI preprints from one quarter, and a model wrote each question from the abstract. Questions written from an abstract share its wording, which helps any search that matches words. Every question here has between 14 and 54 words, with a median of 32. A real user's questions would be messier and may be much shorter, which gives a search less to work with. I make no claim about enterprise documents, other languages, or longer texts.

The agent is one model with one set of instructions. I wrote the agent's instructions once and did not tune them. A stronger model, better instructions, or a different merge could change the result in either direction.

The review covered only part of the questions. The coding assistant did the first pass over all 300 questions, and I checked 30 of them. Some unfair questions may remain. The questions plain search misses are the ones that matter most here, and I read those 13 again by hand.

There are a few smaller issues. One replacement question has no lookalike papers planted next to it and may be easier. Of the pilot's source papers, 12 also appear in the full collection, so the pilot and the full run overlap. The test also measures retrieval only, so it says nothing about whether the final written answer is better.

The finding is "no clear improvement on this collection, at the cost of extra model calls and delay". That is the useful result, because it tells you where not to spend first.

If you run a RAG system, count first. Take a few hundred real questions, run plain search, and see how many it misses. If it misses very few, an agent has little to fix. Plain search missed 13 of my 298 questions.

Add a reranker before you add an agent. It was the only change here with a clear gain, and it needs no language model calls, though it adds about 1.5 seconds of delay on my laptop. Then, if you add an agent, read the queries it writes. Cap the number of searches, merge by position and not by raw score, and watch for new terms that were not in the user's question. In the examples I read, those added words were where papers were lost.

I left one question open. The reranker gave the clearer gain for free. What would have to be true of your questions for an agent's extra searches to be worth their delay and their failures?

Mix 4 cups (500 g) all-purpose flour, 1¾ cups apple juice, 1 Tbsp. Diamond Crystal or 1¾ tsp. Morton kosher salt, 1 Tbsp. sugar, and one ¼-oz. envelope instant yeast (about 2¼ tsp.) in a large bowl just until flour is hydrated and a very shaggy dough forms, about 1 minute. Cover bowl with plastic wrap and let dough sit at room temperature 15 minutes.

Set a small bowl of room-temperature water on work surface for easy reaching. Uncover dough, dip your hand in water and stretch one side of dough up and over to meet opposite side; turn bowl 90°. Repeat 3 more times, turning bowl 90° after each fold. (This constitutes 1 full fold.) If dough doesn’t always stretch to meet opposite side, just aim for the middle. Wet your hands and shake off excess water any time dough begins to feel sticky. Cover dough and let sit 30 minutes (set a timer). Repeat folding process to make 1 full fold. Re-cover and let dough sit another 30 minutes. Repeat folding process and resting one more time. By the last fold, dough should be significantly smoother and tighter.

Transfer dough to a generously floured surface, dust with flour, and shape into a tight ball. Dust dough with more flour. Dust a kitchen towel with flour and place inside a high-sided bowl to line. Arrange dough, seam side up, in bowl on top of towel. Tightly cover bowl with plastic wrap and let dough sit in a warm spot until puffy, about 45 minutes.

Place a rack in middle of oven, then set a large ovenproof Dutch oven with a lid on it; preheat oven to 400°. Place a 12" square of parchment paper on a flat surface. Cut a 2"-long slit in each corner toward the center. Very carefully, tip dough out onto center of parchment, trying your best not to deflate it or ruin its shape. (It’s easiest to place parchment on a flat plate so you can easily turn dough out of bowl.) Working very quickly and using a pair of kitchen shears lightly coated in vegetable oil, cut an X in center of bread. Remove pot from oven and very carefully lower dough into pot. Cover pot and return to oven.

Bake bread 35 minutes. Uncover pot, reduce oven temperature to 375°, and continue to bake bread until crust is deeply browned, 35–40 minutes more. Carefully tip loaf out onto a wire rack and let cool at least 1 hour before slicing.

We stand up, back up a couple hundred yards as we’re screaming at the bear to get away. I do a quick mag check and see I have nine rounds left, eight in the mag and one in the chamber. I can see the bear sitting on its back haunches around 90 yards away, and I just Hail Mary one in its direction. The bear goes on its haunches and walks back into the trees where it came from, real slow with its head down, and then starts to moan.

I’ve killed two black bears with my bow and have never head the death moan. When I heard it this time, I said, ‘Jerrad, I think that’s the death moan.’ He tells me we’re not sticking around to find out. We backed out into the middle of a meadow where I could wash his eyes to get that pepper spray out of his face, and I took a selfie video explaining what had just happened, and then a video of washing Jerrad’s eyes out. We hiked back to the truck, collected ourselves, and then drove out to get cell service and call the wardens. We went into town and got a burger and a hotel room. I texted a few people, including my wife, to tell her that we’re okay, but that we got charged by a grizzly bear.

Next morning we met with the wardens, a state and a federal warden, plus Wyoming’s big-game biologist. I was kind of nervous, but I knew it was the right thing. The night before, the state warden had told me the process. He told me, ‘You have the right to defend your life, but we’ll treat this as a federal investigation because you may have killed an endangered species. You will be under federal investigation.’ I mean, I was hot about that. Here I had just about been killed by this bear, and I’d be treated like a criminal?

But I have to say, the wardens were professional and really competent. They first recorded my version, and then they recorded Jerrad’s version. Then we drove up to the trailhead, and they were totally cool with us loading our weapons. They had sidearms plus shotguns and three dogs. We get back in there and they went through a full investigation. There had been a little snow overnight, and you could see where I had dropped my bow—it was still there—and there were paw prints all over, from the bear’s cub. That’s why she was roaring when we encountered her; she was telling her cub to take off. The wardens ask me to stand by the bow, which is where I was when she came out of the trees.

Here’s the thing, in my initial statement to the wardens, I told them that the bear came out of the trees maybe 40 yards from me, and got as close as 15 or maybe 20 yards. The wardens ask Jerrad if that’s the way he remembered it, and he just looks at me like I’m crazy. He says, ‘Sam, what the f$#k is wrong with you? That bear wasn’t 40 yards. It was more like 10 yards, and maybe closer.’ So I stand at the point where I dropped my bow and take out my rangefinder and range it: 17.1 yards. Then the wardens ask how close she got. I can see there’s a rock with a spot of blood on it, which is where she must have turned when Jerrad shot. That was pretty much right where my bow was, and where I started back-pedaling and where I fell back right against a tree. I find that spot and range the rock with blood. Six yards.

The federal game warden looks over the situation. He looks at the range. He looks at me, then looks around and then back at me. He says something like, ‘Holy shit. How did you get off so many shots so quickly?’ I tell him, I don’t know. I just did. Then the state warden asks the biologist, how quickly could a bear close the distance from 17 yards? He says less than a second at full speed. That’s when it hit me, just how close and quick everything was, but also how slow it was in my memory. I about lost it, about threw up on a rock. It seemed so much longer to me, but the warden told me that this is common. People remember things differently in the moment than they actually were. The opposite is also true.

Then the wardens [found the bear and] did a necropsy on her. She was 18 to 20 years old, and had just the one cub. She was lactating, which was the worst thing. That cub was probably not going to make it. It had come back around its mom after we left, and maybe had a last meal off her, but would probably get killed by other bears or starve in the winter. But the wardens also found that the sow had a fresh small-caliber rifle wound in her right quad that went into her guts. It was probably only 2-3 weeks old. So some jackwagon out there took a pot shot at her to scare her away or to kill her. The wardens said that’s why she was so pissed. She was old. Had a cub sucking on her. And was wounded.

Then the wardens destroyed the bear, on purpose. They destroyed the hide, the paws. They were definitely not going to let us take photos, and obviously we couldn’t take any part of the animal. They also encouraged us not to share our account on social media, certainly not until the case was closed.

They figured that I hit the bear three times out of the seven times I shot, twice in the mid-chest and one shot grazed her right shoulder and broke her back femur. They said that Jerrad had hit her once broadside, right behind the shoulder, a perfect double-lung shot. The way they framed it in their report, was that the bear was coming right at me and had I not been prepared to shoot it a couple times, and had my buddy not made a good shot to the lungs, that the bear would have gotten on top of me. And that very well could be true. But I didn’t even remember drawing my gun, so…

But there are a couple things that I do know. One, I found out that my fight-or-flight response is to fight. My first instinct wasn’t to turn and run or to freak out. My instinct was to act. To shoot back. And to make sure I was protected, which I don’t think is a conscious choice but now at least I know that. Also, I never knew what fear was until I was on my butt with a grizzly bear one stride away from landing on top of me. There was a very small split-second period from when that bear was that close and I’m on my butt that this feeling that I’ve never felt before in my life started to well up inside of me. I was about ready to scream like a little girl, bawl like I was 5 years old, and call for Mom. And then, as fast as it came, it went away, but it was definitely there, and later thinking about it, I realized that this is what people in battle must know.

And I learned the value of practicing with purpose. I kept hearing from people—on social media and in videos—that you have to work on your mechanics. It made sense to me. You work on your mechanics with your bow. I don’t go out to just shoot my bow; I go out there to make purposeful shots to get better. I focus on my form and details. I did the same thing with my Glock. You can do everything you need to do with a gun by dry-firing that you can do with a live round, and I did that, to the degree that when I needed it, it became instinctive, a muscle memory. I think that saved my life.

I have this thing on my Instagram profile. For much of my professional life, I was an arborist instructor, teaching people how to run chain saws and climb trees. There’s a lot of safety considerations; you don’t want to drop a big tree on yourself or fall out of a tree. So I developed this motto: ‘How you do anything is how you do everything.’ That’s exactly how I went about my preparation for this elk hunt. I knew bears were a possibility—as unlikely as getting hit by lightning, but definitely a possibility—and figured that I’d do everything I could to be prepared. If I hadn’t taken my preparation as seriously as I did, I can almost guarantee it wouldn’t have worked out the way it did. I’d have either been really hurt or you’d have read in the papers about a couple of hunters getting killed by a grizzly bear.

I wish it hadn’t happened. I never wanted to encounter a bear, and it sucks for that bear and her cub. But I also think that those bears in the Yellowstone area are the apex predators, and they’re not backing down, so these kind of encounters are probably inevitable until we get approval to hunt them and restore fear of humans.

But that was a life-changing event. I’m happy it worked out, for me and Jerrad, at least. And I’m happy my practice made a difference. But the intensity is something that’s part of me now. In fact, just last week Jerrad and I got tattoos. Mine is a grizzly bear paw on the inside of my right forearm, where I can always see it.

#1076. Costco Is Making A Major Change To Kirkland Signature Butter—And It Costs $4 Less Than Kerrygold

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The dairy is coming from a very familiar place.

Costco members, check your fridge—Kirkland is making a major change to its butter recipe.

A post on r/Costco revealed that a new version of Kirkland Signature Grass-Fed Butter is starting to hit shelves. The size and price remain the same, but there’s one major difference: where the dairy comes from. The previous version was made in New Zealand, while the new one comes from Ireland.

This news doesn’t exactly come as a surprise. News broke this summer that Irish dairy co-op Tirlán had struck a deal to supply grass-fed butter for Costco’s Kirkland Signature brand. The warehouse has carried Tirlán’s own brand, Truly Grass Fed, since 2025. The deal earlier this year, though, expanded the producer’s presence by establishing it as a supplier for Costco’s own store brand.

Other dairy farmers in the country were calling foul when the deal was announced, accusing Tirlán of directly competing with Ireland’s flagship brand, Kerrygold. Tirlán, which also supplies dairy to Kerrygold’s parent company, pushed back against the criticism. “It should be noted that those developments are market-led and extend beyond any single supplier, source country or brand,” a representative said.

But the price difference and impending competition from Costco’s recipe change are undeniable. The warehouse price for four eight-ounce sticks of Kerrygold is $14.99. The private-label version, by contrast, is $10.99.

While some members are looking forward to a more affordable Kerrygold dupe, others are sad to see the New Zealand version go. “Noooooo I like the New Zealand stuff,” one Reddit user wrote. Another added, “RIP Kiwigold.”

Whether members will prefer Kirkland’s new Grass-Fed Butter over Kerrygold remains to be seen, but it’s still a great deal regardless.
