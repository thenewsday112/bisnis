#895. How to Make Better Pie Crust, According to a Pastry Chef

📅 Oct 20, 2020  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

There is a lot of subtlety that goes into making a good pie crust, the types of intuitive moves or small tips you pick up in a professional kitchen (or by baking with an experienced relative). But a few principles make all the difference: keeping the butter cold, avoiding overmixing, letting the dough rest, and baking the finished pie long enough.

Below, and in my book A Good Bake, I cover the little things that matter, from making and rolling out pie dough to crimping the crust and knowing when a pie is done. These are the techniques I use to make delicious, professional-looking pies, no matter what recipe I’m following.

A Good Bake by Melissa Weller with Carolyn Carreno

In a professional bakery, cooks prepare pie dough up to several days in advance. Set yourself up the same way at home. Making the dough ahead gives it time to rest and chill, so it’s ready to roll when you need it. You can also freeze pie dough up to one month in advance; wrap it well and defrost it overnight in the fridge.

The essential ingredients in pie dough are flour, water, a pinch of salt, maybe a very small amount of sugar, and some fat. Many traditional American pie dough recipes call for lard, rendered pork fat, which makes for an exceptionally flaky crust.

I love lard, but quality lard is not always easy to find, so I use good old-fashioned unsalted butter for my pie and tart crusts. With good technique, you can achieve plenty of flakiness and get that great buttery flavor.

Some big pieces, some smaller pieces, just right.

Many pie recipes call for cutting the butter into ¼" cubes. I prefer largish ½" chunks. After cutting the butter into pieces, I arrange them onto a plate and freeze them for 10 minutes so the butter gets as cold as can be without turning rock solid.

When mixed into the flour, some larger chunks remain intact. When you roll out the dough, those chunks form striations of butter. As the crust bakes, the water in the butter turns to steam, creating microscopic flaky layers—and a light, tender crust.

Lots of recipes call for adding ice water to the dough. I find that cold water from my refrigerator (like from a water filter or door dispenser) works fine. That said, if you want to use super-chilled water, it certainly won’t hurt.

Add water only after you mix the flour and butter. When the water joins the party, gluten starts to form. Too much gluten development toughens pie dough, making the finished crust heavy and dense rather than light and flaky. Mixing the flour with cold butter first coats the flour’s proteins, impeding the interaction between the water and the flour and reducing gluten development.

It’s okay if there are still some floury bits!

After bringing the dough together, I shape it into a round disk or brick, depending on the shape I’ll roll it into. Then I wrap it in plastic wrap, run a rolling pin over it to compact and flatten it, and put it in the refrigerator to rest.

A good rest lets the flour fully absorb any moisture so that the dough comes together completely. Resting the dough also relaxes the gluten, making it easier to roll out and less likely to shrink in the oven.

If your kitchen runs warm, work quickly and give the dough extra time in the refrigerator whenever it starts to feel soft or tacky. You can also chill your mixing bowl, bench scraper, and rolling pin before you start.

Take the dough out of the refrigerator, put it on the counter, and let it sit until it feels malleable but still firm (10 to 15 minutes) before rolling. The goal is for the dough to be just pliable enough to roll but not so soft that it starts to stick. Conversely, rolling our cold dough straight from the fridge can cause it to crack.

Once you start rolling, move quickly. Even the firmest dough block will soften quickly under the friction of the rolling pin and the heat of your hands. The whole process shouldn’t take more than a minute or two.

It may be unconventional, but I always butter a pie plate before arranging the dough. The butter helps hold the dough in place as I shape it and provides insurance for removing it from the pan after baking. I smear enough butter on the bottom and sloped sides of the plate to leave a visible layer. I usually use cold butter, but slightly softened also works.

There are two ways to “line” a pie plate—that is, to make sure the dough fits snugly inside. Either fold the dough in half, lift it from the surface you rolled it out on, and place it in the pan. Or roll the dough onto your rolling pin and carry it over on the pin. I fold the dough when lining a round pie plate, and I roll it onto a rolling pin when using a jelly roll pan, which requires a larger piece. Use whatever method you find easiest.

In either case, carry the dough until it hovers over the pie plate, tart pan, jelly roll pan, or other vessel, centering it as much as you can. Then gently lay it onto the dish and unfold or unroll it. Adjust the dough so that it’s centered on the pan.

Ease the pie dough into the bottom corners of the pan without stretching it.

Gently lift one edge of the dough with one hand and use the other to press it into the bottom of the pan, working your way around the perimeter. Use a delicate touch, and resist pulling the dough, which will cause it to spring back when baked and create thin spots in the crust.

Next, gently run your finger along the rim to press the dough onto it, just enough to ensure it adheres. This allows you to see how much dough is overhanging. Make sure it is even all the way around and sitting nicely on top of the rim.

Use kitchen shears to trim the overhanging dough so there is an even 1" hanging over the perimeter of the pie dish. (Discard the trimmings or smush them together into a disk and refrigerate or freeze the dough to make a pie another day.)

If you are making a double-crust or lattice-topped pie, trim the top sheet of dough or lattice so it is flush with the outside edge of the pan.

Keep it even: Place your thumb in the indent from your index finger just made.

In pie making, “crimping” means forming the decorative, wavy edge around the perimeter of a pie. To crimp, tightly roll the overhanging dough toward the center of the pie to form a thick lip that rests on the ridge of the plate. While many recipes call for folding the excess dough under, I prefer folding it up and over. When you’re finished, the dough shouldn't hang over the rim.

The process is the same whether you are making a single- or double-crust pie. For a double crust, roll the bottom crust over the top crust, sealing the filling inside.

As you form this ridge, use your fingers to shape the edge of the rolled crust so that it is rounded and even. Once the entire edge is rolled, place the index finger and thumb of one hand on the inside edge of the ridge and use the index finger of your other hand to push the outside edge toward the center of the pie, forming a V or U shape. Continue around the perimeter until you have crimped the entire crust.

There is a lot of chilling when making pie. First, you chill the butter. Then you chill the finished dough before rolling it out. You’ll also want to chill the pie dish after lining it, and again after crimping and filling the dough, before baking.

If you’ve ever wondered why pies in professional bakeries look so much prettier than your homemade ones, it’s because they chill them—or even freeze them—before baking. Chilling the uncooked pie helps it hold its shape better during baking.

In my kitchens, we often freeze a fully assembled fruit pie (overnight is best), then bake it directly from frozen. To bake a frozen pie, put it in the oven straight from the freezer and add 10 to 20 minutes to the total baking time, using the recipe’s visual indicators for doneness in the recipe.

Always put a baking sheet under pies before baking. This is especially important with fruit pies, to prevent the bubbling fruit juices from spilling over the edge of the pan onto your oven floor.

Also called pre- or par-baking, blind baking refers to baking the crust before adding the filling. For many custard pies, which cook faster than fruit pies, you’ll partially blind-bake the crust to keep it from getting soggy or even staying raw. For pies and tarts whose fillings are not baked at all, you’ll blind-bake the crust until it’s fully golden brown

To blind-bake a crust, line the shaped, crimped, unbaked pie shell with parchment paper (crinkle the paper first to fit the pie shell better), then fill the parchment-lined dough with pie weights to prevent the crust from puffing while it bakes. BA’s favorite pie weights are inexpensive steel ball bearings, but many cooks use dried beans or rice; label any food items you use as “pie weights” when the pie is done, since they will not be good for cooking after baking.

For the full technique, see BA’s guide to blind-baking pie crust.

Depending on how cold the pie is when you put it in the oven—and depending on your oven’s hot spots and other quirks—baking time can vary, so I always rely on the recipe’s visual cues for doneness.

The crust should be a rich shade of golden brown. For a fruit pie, the filling must be bubbling through the lattice in the center of the pie or the vent cut into its top. If the filling isn’t bubbling in the center (not just around the sides), the fruit hasn’t cooked down enough. Remove it too early, and you risk a filling that weeps when you cut a slice.

Custard pies must bake until they are set. A set custard will wobble slightly when you jiggle the pan, but it shouldn’t slosh around like liquid. Avoid baking a custard pie so long that it’s fully set, which can cause surface cracks as the pie cools.

Cooling time is essential to pie making. Custard pies need to set (and sometimes chill for several hours), and fruit pies need ample time to gel and firm. For beautiful slices that hold their shape, the wait is worthwhile.

Overmixing or overhandling the dough encourages gluten development, which can make the baked crust tough rather than tender. Mix only until the dough begins to come together; it should still look a little shaggy, with visible pieces of butter.

Pie dough can shrink if it hasn’t rested long enough or if you stretch it while fitting it into the pan. Let the dough rest in the refrigerator before rolling, gently ease it into the pie plate without pulling, and chill the shaped crust again before baking.

Slumped crusts can happen if you remove the pie weights too early or you don’t fill the crust enough. Add weights to fill the crust to the rim of the pie dish, and don’t remove them until the crust is set (it should look dry and the parchment will no longer stick).

The butter may have gotten too warm or worked too thoroughly into the flour. For distinct flaky layers, keep the butter cold and leave some larger pieces intact. If the dough becomes soft or tacky while you're working, return it to the refrigerator until firm.

Why does my pie dough crack when I roll it out?

Dough that’s too cold can crack when you roll it, while dough that hasn’t hydrated enough may crumble. Let chilled dough sit until it’s pliable but still firm before rolling. If it remains dry and won't hold together, the problem may be insufficient hydration.

Navigation is key to how well a robot vacuum performs its job. Suction matters little if the vacuum fails to cover every part of your floor. Just about all models utilize three fundamental types of sensors to help them navigate.

This navigation method involves a laser that locates features by sending out pulses of light and measuring how long they take to return. LiDAR is especially useful for creating accurate maps and is the most powerful and most common navigation tool for robot vacuums.

With this navigation style, the vacuum travels in one direction and then changes course when it hits an object. A lot of models are programmed to optimize their paths and make them more efficient. By running into a wall in a couple of places, the robot vac can verify the location of the wall and move parallel to it, turning 180 degrees each time they hit an end wall.

Visual Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (vSLAM)

This triangulates position by tracking multiple points in a room in successive camera frames. Over time, the vacuum learns and becomes more efficient by relying on points that remain constant and do not change.

Suction power, which indicates how strongly a vacuum pulls in air and debris, is typically listed by the manufacturer in pascals (Pa). The bulk of modern vacuums offer anywhere from 4,000 to 10,000 Pa, but with each new model that comes out, we’re seeing those numbers continue to rise, with some upwards of 20,000 Pa. Just be aware that higher suction impacts a robot vacuum’s battery life and noise levels.

A brand’s claims about suction power may not provide the full picture, either. Manufacturers don’t tell us exactly where or how they’re measuring—they may take measurements at the impeller or without passing the filter, or using the vacuum only in a controlled environment. It’s similar to how automakers disclose horsepower ratings on cars as an engine spec. The transmission, differential, and other components all leech some of that horsepower, so what’s actually available at the rear wheels differs from the number listed in the specs.

#699. Cornbread Dressing With Sausage

📅 Oct 15, 2019  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

This is not your typical Southern cornbread dressing recipe (or cornbread stuffing, if you prefer). Sure, it has plenty of melted butter, onions, celery, and sage-rich breakfast sausage. And yes, the top bakes up golden and crisp, while the interior stays tender and almost-but-not-quite custardy. But in our Making Perfect: Thanksgiving series, Rick Martinez and Chris Morocco gave the Thanksgiving staple a wake-up call via hot chiles and something perhaps even more unexpected: corn nuts.

But don’t worry about hard little nuggets in your dressing; we’re after the flavor, not the crunch. Pulverized into a fine powder and bloomed in butter with the aromatics, the corn nuts bring an earthy sweetness and seriously amp up the corn flavor.

You can use store-bought cornbread or a box mix, but we developed our Buttermilk Cornbread recipe specifically with stuffing in mind. Whichever you choose, drying the cubes in the oven helps them hold their shape as they soak up the stock. If the liquid pools when you first add it, let the cornbread absorb it for a few minutes before transferring everything to the baking dish.

What kind of cornbread is best for dressing?

A sturdy cornbread that holds its shape when soaked with stock is ideal. Molly Baz’s buttermilk cornbread recipe was developed specifically for making stuffing, but store-bought cornbread works too. Just don’t skip the oven-drying step, which helps keep the dressing from turning mushy.

Are there any substitutes for the corn nuts?

If you can’t find corn nuts, try finely ground Fritos, which have a similar earthy flavor, or another plain corn chip. Several readers have reported good results with this swap. You can also leave the corn nuts out altogether, though you’ll lose some of the concentrated corn flavor that makes this dressing distinctive.

Can I make this cornbread dressing without sausage?

Yep! But you’ll need to make a few other adjustments. If going for a totally vegetarian stuffing, you can easily swap in vegetable stock, but since the sausage provides a host of savory flavor, you’ll need to compensate. Add 1–2 Tbsp. soy sauce or a pinch of MSG in with the wine. You can also double up on the dried herbs—especially the sage—since breakfast sausage brings in those flavors as well.

Preheat oven to 325°. Divide 1 batch Buttermilk Cornbread or 3 lb. store-bought cornbread, cut into pieces (about 14–16 cups of ¾" cubes) evenly between 2 large rimmed baking sheets and bake, tossing occasionally, until dried out and lightly browned around the edges, 40–50 minutes. Let cool at least 10 minutes. Increase oven temperature to 350°.

Meanwhile, cook 1½ lb. breakfast sausage, casings removed if needed, in a large skillet over medium-high heat, stirring and breaking up with a spoon, until lightly browned and cooked through, 6–8 minutes. Transfer to a plate.

Reduce burner to medium heat and melt 1 cup unsalted butter (2 sticks) in same skillet. Add 2 medium onions, chopped, and 4 celery stalks, chopped; season with kosher salt. Cook, stirring occasionally, until softened but not browned, 8–10 minutes. Add 3 garlic cloves, finely chopped, and 2 Thai chiles or 1 jalapeño (with seeds), chopped. Cook, stirring once, until very fragrant, about 1 minute. Add ¾ cup corn nuts, finely ground in a food processor, blender, or mortar and pestle (about ½ cup) and cook, stirring often, until very fragrant and vegetables are starting to brown around the edges, about 3 minutes. Add ¾ cup dry white wine and cook, stirring occasionally, until almost completely evaporated, about 3 minutes. Add 1 Tbsp. finely chopped sage and 2 tsp. finely chopped thyme and toss to combine. Remove from heat.

Combine cornbread, sausage, and sautéed vegetable mixture in a large bowl. Whisk 3 large eggs and 3½ cups turkey stock or low-sodium chicken broth in a medium bowl until very well combined. Pour over cornbread mixture and let sit, gently stirring every minute or so, until cornbread has absorbed all or virtually all of the liquid. Season with kosher salt  (about 1 Tbsp. Diamond Crystal or 1½ tsp. Morton kosher salt) and freshly ground black pepper.

Lightly butter a 3-qt. or 13x9x2" casserole dish. Transfer stuffing to dish and tap dish lightly against counter to distribute and compact stuffing. Cover tightly with aluminum foil and bake until stuffing is very hot throughout and bubbles appear around sides, 40–45 minutes. Increase oven temperature to 425° and remove foil. Continue to bake stuffing until top is lightly browned, 15–20 minutes longer.

Do ahead: Stuffing can be baked at 350° 3 days ahead. Let cool, then chill. Reheat in a 350° oven before increasing temperature to 425° and removing foil.

What are the benefits of wearing compression socks?

Compression socks can help improve circulation, reduce swelling, and alleviate discomfort or pain in the legs that may result from sitting in the same position for an extended period of time, like on a long flight. By promoting blood flow, compression socks can also help prevent blood clots, including deep vein thrombosis (or DVT), a very rare and dangerous condition in which a blood clot forms in a deep vein in your body and travels to your lungs. Compression socks can also aid in muscle recovery, making them a nice option for athletes too.

Talk to your doctor about your pain points to see if compression socks might be beneficial to you. The right pair can help travelers, as well as pregnant women, athletes, and anyone prone to swelling in the legs or feet. Compression socks can also help relieve swelling and pain caused by varicose veins.

What compression level should I look for?

To benefit from compression socks, it's important to find a pair that fits well and offers the right millimeters of mercury, or mmHg, for you. The mmHg measurement calculates how much pressure is being exerted. Medical-grade socks sit in the 20-30 mmHg range, while most casual compression socks are still tight enough to encourage circulation at 15-25 mmHg. For reference, socks that are 30 mmHg are extra tight, while pairs that are 10 mmHg are relatively loose.

While shopping for a pair of compression socks, you might notice that some are labeled as graduated compression socks. These pairs apply the greatest amount of pressure at the ankle and gradually decrease in tightness towards the top of the sock, helping blood flow back up towards the heart more effectively. Graduated compression socks are a comfortable choice for travelers who prefer a pair that aren't extremely restrictive all the way through.

Orbital sanders are available in both corded and cordless models. Corded sanders are your go-to for extended shop work. Cordless sanders have improved dramatically over the past few years—thanks to more powerful brushless motors and lithium-ion batteries that run longer and deliver more consistent sanding power. Just keep in mind that sanding draws quite a bit of power, so batteries tend to drain faster than they do with tools such as drills and drivers. Keep a spare charged battery or two on your person—either in a tool belt or jacket pocket—for quick changes on the go.

Sander speed is measured in orbits per minute (OPM). Most random orbital sanders run somewhere between 7,000 and 13,000 OPM. Higher speeds remove material faster, but they also generate a lot more dust and heat. That’s why sanders include variable-speed controls, allowing you to slow things down for fine finishing or more delicate work as needed.

Some good uses of variable-speed controls include:

Higher speed: paint removal, rough shaping, leveling surfaces

Lower speed: finish sanding, veneer, between coats of finish.

Most sanders use 5-inch sanding discs, which are inexpensive and easy to find. Some larger models use 6-inch pads, which remove material faster and are more common in professional shops. For general DIY work, a 5-inch sander is perfectly capable and usually lighter and easier to handle, while 6-inch sanders are typically favored for those working on larger surfaces such as floors, tabletops, doors, and cabinetry, where the bigger pad speeds up material removal.

Anyone who’s sanded indoors knows that dust control matters. Even a short sanding project can leave a room filled with fine particulate matter. Most orbital sanders include a dust bag or canister that captures debris through holes in the sanding pad that line up with predrilled holes in sanding discs. And while these work reasonably well for small jobs, the bags and canisters fill quickly, and it becomes easy to neglect dumping them as you work.

The best solution is connecting your sander to a shop vac or dust extractor to ensure your own personal safety and a cleaner workshop. An added bonus is that sanding discs last longer as debris is cleared from the abrasive surface with proper extraction, which will save you money over time.

Orbital sanders might not look intimidating, but vibration can add up quickly during long sessions, leading to fast hand and arm fatigue. Sanders don’t need a lot of care, but you can’t just ignore them either. The best thing you can do for a sander is to blow the dust out of it using compressed air (or a can of compressed air purchased at an office supply store). You can also use a shop vacuum and work over its exterior, especially its air vents.

To protect yourself, most notably your lungs, wear a dust mask when sanding. Better models have a foam strip to help the mask make a better seal to your face. They may also have a vent that reduces moisture buildup under the mask.

Clean up as you work to prevent large piles of talc-like dust from accumulating. Having a floor mat outside the shop to wipe your feet is great, too—better yet, take your work shoes off. This cuts down on tracked dirt, which is more than just a nuisance. Shoes with a wider base also provide more stability, and are recommended for walkers with wider feet.

You can also look for shoes that have earned the American Podiatric Medical Association’s Seal of Acceptance, which have been independently verified by APMA testers and found to promote foot health.

Breathability is essential, especially on hot summer walks when the temp keeps rising up. Look for woven mesh uppers or open-knit for airflow. Check the fit too as feet tend to swell the longer you’re out.

Finally, a note on upper aesthetics: Although it might seem superfluous, make sure to pick a shoe with an upper design and color that you like and that works with your everyday wardrobe. You won’t wear them as much if you don’t like how you look.

Finding a dependable pair of shoes—the multipurpose ones that you wear for standing all day at work, running errands, traveling—can change your life. Think about it: because of gravity’s pull, your feet are putting in a lot of work standing still and when in motion.

The padding of a good pair of shoes can help you feel light and supported. Just remember to rotate your shoes out at least once a year. Just like running shoes, midsole foam wears down, which decreases cushioning.

Because trail shoes are specifically designed for durability and gnarly terrain, we wouldn’t recommend them for an everyday walker. Trail shoes tend to be stiffer and heavier due to thicker rubber outsoles and lugs that help you get better traction on dirt and rocks. Some shoes also have built-in rock plates to keep roots and debris from poking up into your feet, which adds to their weight and stiffness.

We recommend these for long hikes, but not as your go-to walking shoe, especially if your daily walk takes you across city sidewalks or paved suburbia.

Drop refers to the difference between the height of the heel and the forefoot in a pair of shoes. Shoe companies make the heel taller than the forefoot to cushion it as you touch down. A taller heel also minimizes how much energy and effort you use to take each step, reducing fatigue and allowing you to walk for longer distances. Most walking and running shoes have a drop somewhere between 6mm and 10mm.

However, by demanding less from your feet than a totally flat shoe, shoes with high drop can mask deficits in strength and flexibility that can lead to injury if left unchecked. For that reason, brands like Hoka and Altra make specialty shoes with little or no drop to help runners build strength in their feet. If that sounds like something you want to try, keep in mind that switching to zero-drop shoes comes with a bit of a learning curve. Don’t throw out your old shoes, and give yourself time to adjust.

You’ve most likely been running in shoes with 8 mm to 12 mm of drop your entire life, so progress gradually if you’re making the switch to low or no drop.
