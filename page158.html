Cardboard mulch is being championed as a sustainable, low-cost method to suppress weeds and lock moisture into soil, but did you know it can boost your harvest, too? A USDA-funded trial found cardboard mulching was not only highly effective at reducing weeds, but increased some vegetable crops by as much as 97%. Yep, you read that right.

A variety of crops were grown over different seasons in the two-year cardboard mulch trial, a collaboration between the University of Wyoming and the USDA’s Western SARE (Sustainable Agriculture Research and Education) program. The growing conditions weren’t easy, either: the trial site of Sweet Hollow Farm in Victor, Idaho, sits in Zone 4 with a super-short growing season. Yet alongside impressive yield increases, results showed weed coverage in cardboard-mulched beds at just 15%, compared to 45% in beds that were tended using traditional tilling methods.

Impressed, I decided to test out the findings for myself and back in the summer I used old cardboard boxes to mulch my vegetable bed and sat back to see what happened. Now it’s October, and I can report that my veg bed is not only 95% free of weeds but I’ve enjoyed my best-ever harvest, I'm happy to vouch for using cardboard as a mulch if you're growing vegetables. I also discovered a few vital details that make a huge difference. So, if you want to try cardboard mulching yourself – and you absolutely should, because now we know a cardboard mulch does work – here’s what you need to know for great results.

While you want to block sunlight to suppress weeds, it’s vital to allow both water and air through for healthy soil and happy roots, and corrugated cardboard makes the layer less dense. In the USDA-funded trial, two layers of corrugated cardboard were used to create a dense barrier to exhaust aggressive perennial weeds such as field bindweed. I only used one layer of corrugated cardboard and it still suppressed weeds well, and the soil beneath it still smells sweet.

I was concerned that water wouldn't penetrate the corrugated cardboard but sliding a long-probe moisture meter like this from Amazon into the soil reassured me that it did. In my very unscientific tests between this mulched bed and the unmulched bed beside it, I was pleased to see the cardboard layer kept the soil moisture level far more consistent, too.

An uncoated finish also helps soil and root health as the cardboard layer is more permeable and breathable. Another reason to avoid coated cardboard is that glossy or water-repellent finish is typically made up of synthetic polymers – use it as a mulch and you’ll inadvertently be adding microplastics to your soil.

It’s not always easy to spot coated cardboard as some have matt finishes, and you need to watch out for cardboard that’s had water-resistant additives mixed directly into the pulp during production, too. To test, put a drop of any cooking oil onto the cardboard: if it soaks right in, go ahead and use the cardboard; if it sits on the surface, that’s a sure sign the cardboard contains compounds you don’t want in your soil.

While plain cardboard is best, it’s fine to use cardboard that’s been printed with black ink as that’s likely water- or soy-based and will break down safely in soil. You’ll be happy to know that the black ink used on the 20 million standard Amazon packages sent out every day is plant-based. However, highly pigmented colored inks can contain synthetic compounds that you don’t want leaching into your soil.

Similarly, skip white cardboard as this goes through chemical bleaching processes, whereas standard brown cardboard is typically made from recycled paper fibres or raw wood pulp.

If you’re recycling boxes that arrived by mail, it’s vital to remove all tape and shipping labels before using. These products are designed to withstand weather and rough handling so don’t think they’re just paper. Standard thermal shipping labels contain synthetic chemicals that soil microbes struggle to break down, and research shows can be taken up by root crops. Some types of paper tape are reinforced with fiberglass, so remove every last bit.

Make it easier to peel off labels and tape, and their adhesive backing, by hosing the cardboard down first and leaving it to sit for five minutes.

You could also take a tip from the USDA-funded trial which re-used cardboard from a local craft brewery down the road, and ask local companies if they have some to recycle. Like the large cardboard slip-sheets used by the brewer to separate layers of beer cans during transit before being recycled in the trial, this packaging is likely to be free of labels or tape.

Running perennials like bindweed can travel horizontally in search of light, so overlap the edges of cardboard sheets by at least 6 inches to ensure they don’t find their way through the barrier.

In the USDA-funded trial, a 4-6-inch layer of compost was laid over the top of the cardboard sheets before planting began. A planting hole was then punched through the cardboard, into which a seedling was transplanted. So, while taproots and the lower root system grew into the ground to access moisture, the upper feeder roots grew into the compost layer that was rich in nutrients.

As well as the lack of competition from weeds and the extra moisture locked into the soil by the cardboard layer, trial yields were undoubtedly boosted by the added compost. Standout results included winter squash, where kabocha squash saw a 28% higher yield and spaghetti squash saw a 40% higher yield than the same crops in tilled control beds. Onions and shallots also fared exceptionally well with red onions seeing a 62% higher yield and shallots showing an increased yield of 97%.

Such a thick layer of compost wasn’t practical for my backyard veggie bed or my budget, so I experimented with leaving half of the bed with only the cardboard mulch, and half with a 2-inch layer of compost covering the cardboard layer. While weeds were effectively suppressed in both sides, and all plants cropped exceptionally well, the half with compost-covered cardboard was more productive when it came to crop yield.

If your garden is exposed, galvanized steel staples will secure cardboard in place if you're not immediately covering the sheets with compost.

I’m all for creating a low-maintenance garden with maximum reward for minimal effort, so I’ll definitely be using this method again. Though it took a little while to lay the cardboard mulch, I easily got that time back several times over because of the reduction in weeding and watering. It's also an easy way to increase the abundance of harvested crops in a small veggie bed like mine.

Cardboard boxes are a great resource for gardeners and can be used for all sorts of tasks from adding cardboard to balance plant material when composting to laying out sheets of cardboard to kill grass in preparation for a new border. Overall, a cardboard mulch made growing backyard veggies much easier, and as I usually mulch with compost once a year anyway, there was no cost but plenty of reward, and I recommend you give it a go.

#2525. Nobody told me I’d be too busy making the memories to enjoy them

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

I thought I was giving my kids everything. Instead, I was missing the moments I worked so hard to create.

By Jessica Koosed Etting Updated Oct. 21, 2026

A few weeks before my son Oliver turned eight, planning his birthday started taking up residence in my brain.

We weren’t aiming for anything particularly extravagant. We were having extended family over for dinner and cake, and enjoying the same traditions we’d built around birthdays over the years. Breakfast in bed with balloons. Presents. Time with grandparents. His favorite meal for dinner. And at the end of the night, we’d go around the table and each say three things we loved about him.

These little rituals have become the essence of our family’s birthday magic.

In the weeks leading up to the big day, I was thinking about what Oliver wanted, figuring out the food, doing the shopping, wrapping the gifts, finding cute decorations, coordinating with family about wish lists, ordering the cake, making sure his brothers had something to give him, and remembering the special cereal he wanted. I was doing all of that alongside work and school and dinner and everything else our family already had going on.

Then the birthday came, and it all went off as planned, for the most part. Oliver was thrilled. His brothers were excited. Our family was there. At one point, the boys were all playing and laughing together with their grandparents, and I remember wanting to go sit with them.

Instead, my brain took over. If I didn’t start clearing dinner now, we’d get the cake too late, and we still needed to have time for presents.

So I got up. Set up the cake and dessert. Found the candles I’d bought. Rounded everyone up. Took pictures. And then came more dishes and wrapping paper to clean up and wrangling three tired kids into bed.

Later that night, I sat down and looked through the photos on my phone of everyone smiling together. They were exactly the kind of pictures that make you think, This. This is the good stuff.

But, I couldn’t remember a single moment where I had really enjoyed it or even felt present with my son the whole night. And, unfortunately, birthdays weren’t the only time I felt like this.

I always thought I’d be the type of mom who loved creating fun memories for my kids. Until I became a parent, I never realized how much invisible labor it took to create those magical moments of childhood.

Tooth fairy rituals, holiday traditions, family vacations and weekend outings… They all require someone planning things out in advance. And, usually, that someone is Mom.

To be clear, I love creating fun moments for our family. I know it’s not everyone’s cup of tea, but it wasn’t something I personally wanted to give up. But something needed to change.

Because, while I was busy planning and managing the entire experience, I wasn’t participating. Instead of enjoying my time on the beach with the kids, I was already thinking about who needed sunscreen re-application, or the groceries we’d need to grab for the next day, or the email I needed to send back to their coach.

Some of that is unavoidable. But all of it didn’t have to be mine.

I wish I could say I simply decided to be more present, but it took years to build better habits around how our family plans and shares the work, and I still catch myself slipping into cruise-director mode sometimes.

But as the founder of Jam, an all-in-one calendar and organization app to reduce the mental load of modern family life, I’ve also spent a lot of time thinking about how to better manage the invisible labor that piles up. And over the years, I’ve found some tried-and-true ways to alleviate it, especially during those special magical moments.

Over time, I also realized that some of the most special moments with my kids require absolutely no planning at all. A slow Sunday morning when nobody has anywhere to be. A few extra cuddles and arm tickles at bedtime. The last ice cream cone of summer, before “real life” starts again.

But those moments are also so easy to miss when half your brain is already in tomorrow.

Now I’m better at recognizing that constant pull of the mental load, and pressing pause on it, too.

There will always be something that needs to happen next. I’m a working mom with three kids, after all, so it’s unavoidable.  But I am more aware now that I don’t want to spend their childhood constantly preparing for the next part of it. Sometimes Sunday morning is a good time to get a jump on a few things that need to get done… and sometimes, it isn’t.

When I look back at the pictures, I want to see more than the beautiful moments I created for my kids.

I want to remember how it felt to be there with them, too.

Arc, which ServiceNow and Nvidia call “a personal long-running AI agent that can take on everyday work,” is generally available as of Wednesday. The companies originally released the idea for the agent back in May. Arc is available now on Mac, but Windows support is still on the way.

OpenShell is an open-source tool that controls runtime. The Control Tower is ServiceNow’s governance framework for AI agents, which aims to be a catch-all service that monitors what agents are doing, vets their access, checks for security risks like prompt injection, measures agent performance, and more.

Like many agents built for enterprise use, Arc is designed for daily tasks that rely on productivity tools such as email and Microsoft Teams. Designed to run efficiently on Nvidia’s new RTX Spark devices, it can run locally on an employee’s desktop or within Nvidia OpenShell on its own device. Similar to other agents that work off of accumulated context, ServiceNow and Nvidia said Arc builds memory and skills over time so that its performance continually improves.

The companies also said Arc is efficient, handing more complex tasks off to frontier models only when necessary and keeping data as local as possible otherwise. ServiceNow and Nvidia also said that Arc “proactively surfaces work and action items” by scanning an employee’s inbox and meeting notes.

But more than any new or highly competitive capabilities overall, what sets Arc apart, according to the companies, is the multi-level security framework surrounding it. ServiceNow and Nvidia said their approach offers more reliable security by “moving enforcement beyond the software layer into the runtime and hardware layers.”

Essentially, while the Arc agent is working on tasks, ServiceNow’s Super Intelligence Control Tower enforces company policies, while Nvidia OpenShell restricts the agent’s behavior where necessary.

“IT defines policies in the Super Intelligence Control Tower and NVIDIA OpenShell can be directed to enforce them at runtime,” the announcement explained. “Users obtain granular control over which systems AI agents can access, APIs they can call, and data they can read.”

AI Passport is another layer of agent security that the two companies announced last week. It holds an enterprise’s set of rules, which the Control Tower sets, about risk tolerance and the services Arc and other agents are allowed to access. That lets Nvidia’s features like OpenShell intervene efficiently if an AI agent takes action it’s not supposed to, the companies explained.

With the other pieces of ServiceNow and Nvidia’s security puzzle, AI Passport functions like a bridge between ServiceNow infrastructure and Nvidia’s checkpoints. The entire system is designed to continuously monitor an agent’s actions and permissions to prevent breaches, unsanctioned actions, and other fallout from using agents.

If the Control Tower tells OpenShell an agent’s passport has been revoked, OpenShell will keep the task the agent was trying to execute contained within a sandbox, where reviewers can audit it later. That interruption is also task-specific, meaning other, approved agent actions can continue without restarting.

“When a passport is revoked in the Super Intelligence Control Tower, Nvidia BlueField can enforce that revocation at machine speed before the AI agent can proceed,” the companies said. “This creates enforcement in silicon designed to prevent overrides from compromised software.”
