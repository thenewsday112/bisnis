#1393. Frank Lloyd Wright’s Taliesin West Reopens After a Freak Accident Caused a Visitor's Death

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Taliesin West, Frank Lloyd Wright’s winter home and studio, is reopening following a death at the site on Friday, October 16.

The victim, identified as Brother Rogério Renato Mateucci, the rector of Pontifical Catholic University of Paraná in Brazil, was touring the UNESCO World Heritage site in Scottsdale, Arizona, when a piece of debris from the building’s roof fell and hit his head, the New York Times reported. The accident occurred around 2 p.m. following strong winds—local weather service reported gusts up to 32 miles per hour—which dislodged the a piece of the structure. According to the Scottsdale fire department, he died instantly. No other injuries occurred.

“The Foundation mourns the loss of Rogério Renato Mateucci, who tragically died on October 16 in an accident at Taliesin West,” Joseph Specter, president and CEO of the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation, which owns and operates the building, shared with AD in a statement.“Taliesin West has been closed to the public to allow the Foundation to conduct a thorough safety inspection of the site, including its roofing and structures, and to cooperate fully with local authorities, who have determined the incident was weather-related.” The Scottsdale Police Department confirmed to AD that the tragedy was weather-related and no foul play was suspected; however, their investigation is not complete.

Wright designed the residence in 1937 as his winter home and studio, imagining it as a desert camp that emerged from the arid grounds. Originally, the building was topped with a canvas roof, which offered protection from the hot sun while still allowing light to pass through the covering. “These were lightweight, translucent, and could be opened and closed to modulate airflow and temperature,” Rebecca Barron, director of preservation at Taliesin West, shared in a previous blog post from the Foundation.

However, the material wasn’t waterproof, and over time the building transitioned to year-round use (currently, it serves as the headquarters for Wright’s foundation in addition to other public programming), prompting the Foundation to add fixed acrylic panels in the 1990s in certain areas. According to the blog post, the Foundation is in the process of replacing the acrylic roof with a more resilient material that more closely resembles Wright’s canvas. Police confirmed to the Arizona Republic that a piece of acrylic glass hit Mateucci, but the Foundation did not comment on the condition of the roof when the accident occurred.

The Foundation says it will now monitor the weather more closely as part of the reopening plans. “Weather station technology has been added to enhance our existing safety procedures at the site, to assess temperature, wind speed, air pressure, and rain. This will offer the Foundation localized safety data to further inform the daily practice of opening our site to the public,” Specter said. “With these actions and safety assurances in place, we feel confident that Taliesin West is safe for visitors, and we will reopen the site to the public on Saturday, October 24, at 10 am. We continue to hold Mr. Mateucci and his loved ones in our hearts and continue to offer our deepest condolences.”

Following his passing, those who knew Mateucci have expressed their deep sadness at the accident and spoken highly of the late rector. “Brother Rogério spoke and acted with a rare gentleness and had a remarkable ability to listen, always making room for others to be heard,” Paulo Mussi Augusto, PhD, Kent State’s associate vice president for global education, who worked closely with Mateucci through a partnership between their universities, said in a statement. “He loved beauty and found it where art and science met. As a man of faith, he was steadfast, slow to judge, and always willing to give people another chance. His legacy will continue to inspire all of us who had the privilege of knowing him.”

#3038. Animal Attack Roundup, Oct. 20

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As we pointed out last week, most victims of animal attacks around the world are not tourists but the people who live amid potentially dangerous animals all the time. Here are some incidents that have come to light recently.

Chamois in France. Photo: Ludovic Laude/L’Est Republicain

A normally timid mountain ungulate has been attacking runners on the outskirts of Besançon in eastern France.

One victim, a 30-year-old woman jogger, was knocked unconscious and suffered a punctured shoulder and serious scalp wounds requiring stitches and staples. The incident occurred just a few hundred meters from the outskirts of the city.

“I didn’t hear it coming,” the woman, known only as Milena, told Le Monde. “I only had time to see its head 10 centimeters from me, and then, nothing, total blackout…”

The woman was unconscious for about five minutes.

Another runner had been attacked days earlier. The incidents occurred near La Chapelle des Buis. Wildlife authorities described the behavior as highly unusual and authorized the animal’s removal. The cause of its aggression remains unclear.

A 71-year-old Winter Garden, Florida fisherman, Steven Hamilton, was posing for a photograph with a fish when a large alligator lunged from the water, seized his leg, and pulled him into a pond.

Emergency crews responded, but the alligator refused to let go of his body, according to Fox News. By the time they released him and killed the alligator, Hamilton was dead. The medical examiner identified drowning and blunt-force injury to his left leg as causes of death, with alligator tooth marks on both sides of the leg.

Alligator attacks are rare but occur occasionally. According to the Florida Fish & Wildlife Conservation Commission, there have been a total of 500 alligator bites against humans since 1948, including 32 fatal encounters.

Vladimir Odulo, a fisherman from Primorye in Russia’s Far East, survived an attack by an Asiatic black bear, also known as a Himalayan bear.

The bear attacked while Odulo was fishing. He tried to defend himself with his fishing rod, but the animal knocked him into the water. Odulo continued fighting as it “beat his face like a boxer,” Russian media reported the fisherman saying. Suddenly, the bear stopped the attack and fled into the forest.

Odulo suffered several wounds and a serious eye injury, but survived. Doctors in Vladivostok were able to save his eyesight.

A venomous Caspian cobra, which now occurs high in Himachal Pradesh. Photo: Shutterstock

Two deaths in the Indian Himalaya have been attributed to Caspian cobras. Native to Central Asia, this venomous species has expanded its range eastward into the higher reaches of Himachal Pradesh. It now also occurs in Pakistan and Kashmir.

The first death occurred when a 40-year-old man accidentally stepped on a Caspian cobra while spraying an apple orchard. Wearing open-toed footwear, he sustained two bites on his leg. He was taken to a local faith healer before being shifted to the hospital, where he was declared dead, according to the Tribune of India.

In the second case, a 62-year-old man stepped on a snake while on his way to work and was bitten on the leg. He collapsed within half an hour and was taken to a health facility, where he died.

These fatalities occurred some time ago, but the report on them was published just this week in the journal Epidemiology International. The Caspian cobra generally inhabits higher, open, rocky terrain at elevations of 2,000m or higher, where both incidents occurred. Researchers suggest its range may now include lower elevations.

Two men died in separate elephant attacks in northern Kenya.

On October 11, Eisimgai Gorow Ltarakwa, 41, was herding goats near Nguposorogin village when a lone elephant attacked him. He suffered severe injuries and died at the scene.

Two days later, Mukhtar Abdoy Hussein, 64, was grazing livestock near Benane village when a herd of elephants trampled him to death.

The incident highlights the risks faced by herders in areas where livestock grazing activities bring communities into close proximity with wildlife. Under a conservation initiative, the families are entitled to receive compensation, according to Kenyan media.

#2839. Adam Ondra Just Downgraded the World’s First Proposed V18. We Should Have Seen This Coming.

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

(Photo: Courtesy of Petr Chodura via Adam Ondra)

About our reporting: Associate editor Sam MacIlwaine pulls from a prior interview with Elias Iagnemma, a Testpiece Climbing podcast episode, Adam Ondra’s public posts, several YouTube videos, and records from Climbing’s digital archives to explain the history of Ondra’s downgrades and why the world’s first V18 was no exception.

On Sunday, October 18, Czech climber Adam Ondra announced that he made the second ascent of Exodia, the world’s first proposed V18, in Piemonte, Italy. The 33-year-old Olympian, who is arguably the most accomplished rock climber of all time, downgraded the boulder from V18 to V17. His downgrade ended nearly a year of speculation over whether the first-ever proposed V18 would hold up in future repeats.

“It’s very much my style,” Ondra wrote in an Instagram post caption, adding that the climbing was “extremely cool and very fun to project.” He took four days in total to send it, noting that he was in “possibly my best ever” shape for climbing it.

Exodia marks Ondra’s second V17, after Soudain Seul in Fontainebleau, France. However, he remarked that it “felt harder than Soudain Seul,” making Exodia his most difficult bouldering send to date. Read on for a history of Ondra’s biggest downgrades.

Last November, Italian climber Elias Iagnemma shocked the climbing world when he finally sent his 211-session, four-year project, Exodia. He then proposed it as the world’s first V18. Prior to Exodia, Iagnemma had sent Burden of Dreams, the world’s first V17 and a benchmark for the grade, in 25 sessions. He also put up his own first ascent, The Big Slamm, in 35 sessions, proposing V17; however, it has not been repeated. Exodia took him six times as long as either boulder. After his send, he told Climbing that it was “the hardest thing I’ve ever done in my life.”

In broad terms, Iagnemma described Exodia in two parts, a V14 and a V16, with an inverted kneebar rest between them. It contains approximately 25 hand moves across a blocky overhang traverse. Iagnemma compares the boulder visually to “the face” of the Exodia Yu-Gi-Oh card, which inspired the problem’s name.

Since November, several elite boulderers, including Soudain Seul third ascensionist Camille Coudert, have traveled to Piemonte to try Exodia, according to a Testpiece Climbing podcast episode interviewing Iagnemma. But it wasn’t until Ondra showed up, fresh from a spree of V14 and V15 first ascents in Italy, France, and the Czech Republic, that Exodia saw a second ascent.

When Ondra visited the boulder in September, Iagnemma showed up and offered all his beta. “Adam thinks the first part is 8B+/V14 hard,” the Italian said on the September 28 podcast episode. “For me it’s just 8B+. But for the second part, Adam doesn’t think it’s 8C+/V16.”

In Iagnemma’s send video, he jumps to a pinch and cuts feet shortly after exiting the kneebar. But for this move, he claimed, Ondra did not need to cut feet. “When I jump to the pinch, Adam’s still with the toe hook to the pinch, and maybe this way, it’s a bit easier than 8C+,” Iagnemma said. “I’m a little bit smaller and I lose my feet.”

Ondra confirmed this advantage in his send announcement. “The second part is even more my style,” he wrote. “I can reach one hold from one toe hook without cut[ting] loose.”

Iagnemma responded to the announcement with congratulations. “You’re not only the strongest climber in the world, but an amazing person as well,” he wrote publicly in Ondra’s comments. “I hope we get to climb together someday.”

With Exodia, Iagnemma joins a long list of elite climbers to have experienced an Ondra downgrade of their first ascent, including Stefano Ghisolfi, Jonathan Siegrist, Magnus Midtbø, Will Bosi, Dave Graham, and Aidan Roberts.

In 2003, Spanish climber Barnabé Fernández claimed the first ascent of the 270-foot Chilam Balam in Málaga, Spain, calling it the world’s first 5.15c. However, he did not provide proof of his ascent, and the 5.15c grade was called into question by Alexander Huber and many others. Eight years later, Ondra swept in, made the first confirmed ascent, and downgraded it to 5.15b, a grade that had been already established by then.

One of Ondra’s most memorable downgrading sprees occurred back in 2012, during his first visit to the U.S., when he quickly disposed of three of the hardest routes in the Red River Gorge. He onsighted both Jonathan Siegrist’s Pure Imagination and Adam Taylor’s The Golden Ticket on the same day, then downgraded both from 5.14d to 5.14c. A few days later, he flashed Taylor’s harder route, Southern Smoke Direct, and downgraded it from 5.15a to 5.14d. “Adam Ondra pretty much wrecked the Red,” wrote routesetter Aubrey Wingo in a blog post about the sending spree.

One of the reasons that Ondra is known for downgrades is his geo-agnostic philosophy on grading, which adheres to a universal scale instead of bending to the subjectivity of local standards. “What is a grading system worth if we are not even trying to make it objective?” he told this magazine after his Red River Gorge massacre. “We’re just lying to ourselves to get the highest number possible. I am always trying to compare my ascents with other benchmarks of that grade, so climbing said grade feels the same in the Red, the Alps, Norway, or Spain. It seems to me that grades were really stiff in the 1980s and `90s, but then, strangely enough, inflation occurred.” He also noted, “I wouldn’t call it downgrading—I would call it a personal suggestion or opinion.”

But while Ondra considers his downgrades “opinions,” nearly all of his suggestions tend to stick. Others have acknowledged that Ondra’s ascent of their route is what defines the grade. In 2025, Magnus Midtbø jokingly pointed out that Ondra’s 2012 downgrade of his boulder Blood Redemption from V15 to V14 came one day after day after Midtbø beat Ondra in a competition in Norway. “I almost had a V15 under my belt until Adam Ondra ruined it,” Midtbø mock-complains in a YouTube video. However, later in the video, he admits that V14 is “probably a more accurate grade.”

Ondra has acknowledged his power over the community, too. In recent years, he’s become more careful to stress that his downgrades are not personally motivated. In 2021, he downgraded Stefano Ghisolfi’s Erebor, then considered the hardest route in Italy, from 5.15b/c to 5.15b, right after Laura Rogora redpointed it and became the first woman to climb 5.15b/c. “The fact that Laura did Erebor is one of the most impressive achievements in climbing ever,” Ondra wrote in an Instagram post caption, “but my suggestion is just my honest opinion and not a way to put down her achievement.”

At this point, when Ondra comes to town, it’s normal for any first ascensionist to prepare for the re-evaluation of their life’s biggest accomplishment. In 2017, when Ondra tried Steve McClure’s Rainman (5.15b), the hardest sport climb in the U.K., McClure himself showed up to watch. A video published by the British Mountaineering Council shows him visibly nervous.  Rainman was McClure’s seven-year obsession; it took him 127 sessions to finally get it done. But Ondra didn’t send that day. “You can be relaxed,” Ondra told McClure, laughing, after he fell from high up on McClure’s route. The 47-year-old grinned in obvious relief. Today, Rainman remains 5.15b.

Given Ondra’s unique reputation as possibly the best climber in the world, British climbers Tom Randall and Pete Whittaker have jokingly invented a new grading scale based on Ondra’s ability level. “The O-grading system starts at 0, so if Adam flashes anything, it’s just O-zero,” said Randall in a 2023 YouTube video of Ondra visiting his famous Cellar in Sheffield. “O1 will be, it will take him one session. That could be the second go, or it could be the 25th go of the session. It’s always O1. O2 is two sessions; O3 is three sessions, and so on.”

Ondra joined in the joke in 2024. On April Fool’s Day, he posted the O-grades for a few of his biggest accomplishments, adding the plus and minus effects to note effort and to narrow the scale. Silence (5.15d) and La Dura Dura (5.15c), two of his most groundbreaking redpoints, were labeled O60 and O42+ respectively.

But at this point, the O-grade system is probably worth taking seriously, if only to get inside Ondra’s head and anticipate a future downgrade. Like any other climber, Ondra relies on his past experiences on various routes and boulders to determine the grade for his current one. Yet unlike most other climbers, his opinion carries significant implications for us all. One may wonder whether Rainman survived a downgrade simply because in 2017, Ondra hadn’t yet flashed 5.15a, the benchmark for O-zero; therefore it had to be at least O1, which was (and is still) 5.15b.

By contrast, Exodia took Ondra four sessions, which means it’s O4—slightly lower on the O-grade scale than Ondra’s only other V17, Soudain Seul (O5). In that line of logic, the downgrade suddenly makes perfect sense.

#3688. The Best Tune-Up Workouts to Help You Absolutely Crush This Year’s Turkey Trot

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

These two coach-approved tune-up sessions build 5K speed and sharpen your finishing kick.

Many runners sign up for a Turkey Trot 5K with no goal other than to enjoy the day and the company of their fellow trotters. Other runners take this annual race more seriously. Very seriously.

Either way, full support! But we have exactly what you need to rip a blazing-fast time this season, thanks to Jeff Gaudette, founder and head coach of RunnersConnect.

The key to running your fastest 5K? Structure the right workouts ahead of race day that will help you build and sharpen your speed. Gaudette shares the tune-up workouts that check both these boxes, plus he shares tips for racing your best Turkey Trot to date.

First, you’ll want to include a session that prepares you for what your goal 5K pace will feel like so you can maintain it through the finish. Next, as you get closer to race morning, you’ll benefit from some faster intervals that help sharpen the speed you’ll need to reel in competitors over the final stretch.

Why it works: When November begins, you’re close enough to race day to get a pretty good sense of the fitness you’ll bring to the starting line. To gauge exactly where you stand, Gaudette recommends a dose of 5K-specific training with what he calls a “speed endurance” session. Aim to do this workout about two to three weeks before your Turkey Trot.

The number of 400-meter repeats you complete will depend on your current weekly training volume, Gaudette says. Higher-mileage runners should shoot for 12 reps, while lower-volume runners will want to aim closer to eight. And if you’re still out of breath when it’s time to start the next interval, don’t worry—that’s exactly the point.

“The rest is short enough so you are not going to be fully recovered before you begin every single interval,” Gaudette explains. “This trains your aerobic system and will give you a pretty good gauge of whether you’ll be able to maintain this pace for a 5K race.”

Why it works: The second workout adds some running at speeds faster than your 5K goal pace. But rather than “speed endurance,” the goal of this effort is “speed sharpening,” which is what helps you prime a strong finishing kick. Aim to do this workout either just before or during the week of your Turkey Trot.

Unlike the first workout, you don’t want to skimp on rest between intervals. Make sure that you allow yourself a full recovery, says Gaudette. “The purpose of the long rest is so we’re not producing any type of soreness or fatigue. This workout is more about getting a sense of what these paces feel like.” For example, the 400-meter repeats help get your legs turning over faster and make your goal 5K pace feel a bit more comfortable by comparison.

Keep in mind that these workouts get more challenging as you progress through them. “Runners will usually find that the first third of the workout feels pretty easy. The middle third is hard but comfortable, and then the last third reaches that RPE of 8 or even 9,” Gaudette says.

A seasoned trotter, Gaudette toed the starting line of some of the largest and most famous Thanksgiving races, including the Manchester Road Race and the O’side Turkey Trot. The most common mistake he sees? Runners sprinting right out of the gate.

“Really focus on your pacing,” he says. “Going out too fast is the mistake most runners make, especially in turkey trots, because these races tend to draw less experienced runners. These runners don’t yet have that sense of pacing, and they don’t realize how hard the 5K can get in the second half.”

So, if you’re new to the 5K, don’t hammer with everything you’ve got right off the starting line. And if you’re an experienced runner, don’t get rattled if you find yourself farther back in the field during the first mile.

“A runner who typically finds themself in the top half of most of their races might end up in the back third initially since the entire field has gone out so fast,” Gaudette explains. “Just stay focused on your own internal pacing and make sure that first mile is within range: either at your 5K pace or maybe even a little bit slower.”

Why? Because that controlled start sets you up for a huge advantage in a 5K. Conserving your energy over the first mile or so allows you to race hard in the second half. “It’s a double-whammy,” says Gaudette. “You get the benefit of feeling strong, plus the extra motivation that comes from catching a ton of runners through the finish.”

#3917. Rippling water informs wildlife pavilion by Desai Chia Architecture

Intersecting circles make up the Chatham Pond Pavilion, which was designed by US studio Desai Chia Architecture to be a wildlife-viewing station on a private property in Upstate New York.

Located in the rural town of Chatham, the pavilion sits within an 80-acre (32-hectare) property belonging to a couple with grown children.

New York City-based Desai Chia Architecture has designed other structures for the site, including a main house, guest dwelling, livestock barn and large greenhouse.

A meandering path leads to the pavilion, which is perched on the edge of a spring-fed pond formerly used for agricultural purposes.

“Once used as a livestock watering hole, it has now been remediated and restored as a natural wetland haven for wildlife, birds, fish and pollinators,” said Desai Chia Architecture.

In addition to wetland creatures, the area is populated with bears, foxes, deer, raccoons and possums.

To minimise disruption to the habitat, the architects conceived a viewing structure lifted above the ground.

The 380-square-foot (35-square-metre) pavilion is shaped like the number eight in plan and is composed of two platforms of differing heights.

The higher platform extends over the water, allowing visitors to “observe natural wetland habitats at the pond, as well as activity in the forest beyond”.

The structure is topped with a sculptural canopy made of three intersecting, hollowed-out cylinders.

“The intersecting forms were inspired by overlapping concentric ripples of water created when raindrops fall on the pond’s surface,” the team said.

One of the cylinders has a solid roof with a solar-powered ceiling fan, with a PV array atop the roof.

The pavilion is constructed of galvanised steel columns and curved steel beams, which were fitted together on-site. The canopy is clad in standing-seam panels made of 98 per cent recycled aluminium.

“The exterior of each cylinder is finished in a luminous satin grey, reflecting the surrounding forest and sky, while the interior is coated in a copper finish that casts a warm glow within,” the team said.

The pavilion sits on the southwest corner of the pond, enabling it to be shaded in the afternoon and to capture dramatic views at dusk.

“As the sun sets, the light rakes across the pond from the west and lights up the forest canopy to the east, giving the pavilion an ideal sight line to the glowing row of trees and through the illuminated understorey,” the team said.

Other projects by Desai Chia Architecture include a New York home with crisp silhouettes and cedar cladding, and a Connecticut residence with courtyards for exhibiting artwork.

You’d almost forgotten last deer season, hadn’t you? Then a few weeks ago, your buddy got his shoulder mount back from the taxidermist and was texting you trail camera photos of all the good bucks under his stands. Looks like he’s in for another awesome year.

But all of his bragging reopened the old wounds from last November. Now you have flashbacks of bucks snorting in the distance and dreams of arrows stuck in trees. Those haunting words from your cousin — “I don’t see no blood” — are stuck in your head like the lyrics to a bad pop country tune.

Spend more than a weekend in the deer woods and you’re bound to screw up a few opportunities. You can make excuses and then repeat the mistakes this year. Or you can learn from them. We’re going to help you have a season without excuses, because we’re facing them down right here and now.

I’d lay odds that a hit high in the chest results in more lost deer with a bow than all other marginal hits combined. Contrary to the popular “no man’s land” myth, there is no gap between a whitetail’s lungs and its spine, but the spine does sit lower in a deer’s chest than many hunters realize. That’s why at first, this hit can look pretty good. But the arrow zips either through the backstraps or the upper edges of the scapulae (which are surprisingly easy to penetrate). The telltale sign of a high hit will be bits of sticky meat on the arrow and a bright-red blood trail that fades quickly. Deer usually survive this type of hit (read our ultimate guide on where to shoot a deer).

Most high hits are the result of nervous, string-jumping whitetails and steep shot angles taken from a high treestand. So, remember this: Bad as a high hit is, a low hit usually means a dead deer. As long as your broadhead penetrates just above the white fur line, it will find vitals, and the shot is usually fatal.

I like to aim at whitetails as if they were 5 yards closer than the actual range. Some bowhunters call that “cheating low,” but it helps me put more venison in the freezer, and I don’t consider it cheating at all. —Will Brantley

I don’t know your neighbors, but I know this: The lengths of BS spun at a buck pole will usually out-measure the inches of antler hung on it. Deer hunters love to tell tales and, dare I say it, stretch the truth. It’s easy to assume that big buck you’ve been watching is dead when you aren’t seeing it anymore. But big bucks get big because they have played this game before. Odds are much higher that the deer isn’t dead. It could be tucked away with a doe or on a walk looking for one. There’s plenty of data highlighting how little some bucks actually move during the rut, but that same research shows that some bucks travel a long, long way from home.

The fact is this: Killing a big buck is hard, and so the odds of your neighbors killing every single one in the area are slim to none. Until you see a dead buck with a neighbor’s tag on it, keep hunting him. —Tony Hansen

With hunting pressure at ridiculous levels on every neighboring parcel to the ground I hunt, I knew I needed to do what I had written about so many times before: Let the hunting pressure on nearby land push deer into my area. I would only hunt when “prime time” arrived. That thinking made me miss my window of opportunity entirely.

I pulled a trail camera card on October 28, my first foray into the thick of the timber. It was jammed up with daylight photos of three different shooter bucks that had been regular travelers past my favorite stand over the previous 10 days. The last couple of photos recorded the bucks as blurs as they dashed by in hot pursuit of some early-cycling does.

Those bucks spent the next two weeks in various stages of hot doe heaven (known to us sorry saps as “lockdown”), and I never laid eyes on any of them. Gun season opened, hunting pressure tripled, and daylight movement ceased. My season was over before I had even started hunting.

It is sage advice to pick and choose those times when you hunt your very best spots. But I had taken that to an extreme and left myself without enough time to hunt. I’d also ignored an important fact: The very best hunting of the rut happens when the first few does begin to cycle. That can occur any time after the second week of October — especially in areas with a skewed doe-to-buck ratio.

Yes, it makes sense to exercise caution when you’re hunting a mature buck. But to kill the deer you’re after, you have to go hunting. —T.H.

Mowing those woolly fields so you can shoot across them is standard preseason prep for a lot of hunters. It’s also one of the single worst things you can do for the wildlife habitat on your hunting property. “Daylight deer movement in a field is most consistent when the vegetation structure is 4 to 5 feet tall, and less if the structure is less than 2 feet tall,” says Craig Harper, professor of wildlife management for the University of Tennessee, and a frequent speaker for the National Deer Association.

In addition to cover, old-field habitat can provide an incredible food source. But that’s only if the right stuff is growing. Repeated mowing stimulates the growth of grasses like tall fescue and brome grass, which are of virtually no use to wildlife. Periodic burning or disking, to maintain early successional growth and keep a field from becoming a young forest, tends to stimulate the growth of forbs.

“In most fields, you can almost count on at least 500 pounds of dry matter [forage] per acre,” Harper says. “We have recorded as many as 4,000 pounds of forage per acre. There’s potentially a lot for a deer to eat in an old field throughout the season, including the winter rosettes of various forbs like old field aster and goldenrod, and especially blackberry leaves.”

If you can’t burn or disk and must mow, do it in the winter. In the late summer, mowing a few strategic shooting or travel lanes through your field might not hurt — but you’ll have better hunting if you turn the tractor off and leave the rest of that thicket right where it is. —W.B.

You got caught taking a treestand selfie, didn’t you? It seems these days, everyone is trying to film their hunt or update their Instagram feed from the tree. That means they’re not sitting still. “People underestimate how well a deer can see,” says Kerry Wix, a professional videographer who’s filmed hunts for some of the biggest names in outdoor television. “That’s why I think it’s easier to teach a hunter to run a camera than to teach a videographer how to hunt. If you can’t read a deer’s body language, you’re going to screw up and move at the wrong time. I like to follow the 10-foot rule: If a big deer is walking through the timber, you can plan on him going about 10 feet before he stops again to look and listen. That’s when you make your adjustments, because when he’s standing still, you’d better not move a muscle.”

If you just want to kill a buck, leave the camera at home and the phone on silent in your pocket. —W.B.

Late one September evening last season, I watched the 10-point I’d been hunting ease through the timber toward my stand. By the time he was 20 yards away, I had eight minutes of legal light left — but it was too dark to see through my peep sight when I drew.

We might practice shooting from long range, while seated, and from treestands, but few of us practice shooting in the low light whitetails prefer. To be clear, I’m not talking about Hail Mary arrows in the dark — but if a big buck is standing in bow range and it’s legal to shoot him, don’t you want every advantage? Try this.

Take a Look: Large-aperture peep sights might give up some long-range precision, but they allow for maximum visibility in low light. That’s what a whitetail hunter wants. As you’re positioning your peep before the season, draw your bow and close your eyes. When you open, you should be staring right through it, at your pins. If you have to move your head at all to see it, keep tweaking its placement—and don’t serve it in until it’s perfect. This way, you can skyline the peep aperture, hit your secondary anchor point (see below), and take the shot, confident you’re lined up, even if the peep itself is tough to see.

Double Anchor: In good light, a peep sight makes shooting easier. But many bowhunters are too dependent on them. It’s perfectly possible to shoot good groups without a peep, especially inside 30 yards. I prefer to establish a couple of consistent anchor points (a kisser button on the string helps) that become instinctive.

Practice Late: Wait until 15 minutes after sunset to begin your evening practice. Move your target out of the open front lawn and into the woods. As it gets darker, check your peep against the skylight and keep on shooting until it’s too dark to see the target. That’s usually about 30 minutes after sunset. Another benefit of this drill is that it trains your eye to pick out detail on a dark target.

What about that 10-point I mentioned? I did just this: raised my bow up to find my peep in the skylight. Then I lowered it slowly, and hit my secondary anchor point. I put my fiber-optic pin, which I could see clearly, behind the buck’s shoulder, which I could also see. I released the arrow and listened to that buck fall dead 75 yards away. —W.B.

And what route did you take when walking that mile in? Did you stomp through multiple bedding areas on your way? Did you leave early enough that you weren’t pushing deer out as they returned to their beds?

Distance is nothing more than a measurement. Walking a mile in just for the sake of distance doesn’t guarantee that you will be hunting in a spot with more deer. If you really want to kill deer on public land — especially those that see heavy hunting pressure — you must walk with a purpose. You have to be heading to an area that others aren’t pressuring and that deer are utilizing, and you have to get there without alerting the deer that you intend to hunt. Probably because social media has skewed our idea of what a big deer looks like because it’s caused us to assign arbitrary minimum scores for social acceptance. I usually stay in a cheap rental cabin with a kitchen because making meals is cheaper than paying someone else to do it. If you can put aside $1,500 a year, you can enjoy a weeklong hunt in a prime location. I hunt plenty of public land, but I also have had decent luck gaining permission with a simple knock on a door and a handshake. —T.H.

Compared to centerfire rifles, shotguns aren’t all that accurate. So why would you make them even less so by using iron sights? A low-magnification scope atop a full rifle slug barrel is perfect for sending slugs precisely through baseball-size holes in the brush. A scope is far superior to open sights in low light, and for most hunters, it’s just as fast. There’s no good reason not to use one. —T.H.

If you intentionally limit yourself to a recurve bow and hunting from the ground for the added challenge, then props to you. But you probably won’t kill as many deer as the guy using an aluminum treestand and modern compound bow. Equipment and tactics evolve and improve, and the hunters that evolve with them are more successful. Food plots and trail cameras work. Planting a food plot requires a lot of effort, though, and a new stable of trail cameras isn’t cheap. Savvy hunters do what they’re able. Maybe it’s planting a quarter acre of turnips instead of 2 acres of alfalfa, or buying three base-model cameras in lieu of five cellular ones.

Of course, if you really would rather avoid all that newfangled “crap” that makes hunting easier and more enjoyable, go for it. It’s a convenient excuse to have alongside that bowl of tag soup. —T.H.

We’re all busy in the fall. But hunter numbers are declining nationally, and it’s going to require veteran hunters taking new adult hunters out into the field to preserve our hunting culture and heritage. And taking someone new doesn’t meant that you have to sacrifice your own hunting. It’s not like you have to give up your planned week of vacation during the rut. Start by finding the right mentee. This should be someone who’s really driven to get into hunting, but isn’t quite sure how to get started. Your prospective mentee should be willing to scout, spend time at the range, and most importantly, have passed hunter safety class. You shouldn’t be holding this new hunter’s hand through the entire process, simply advising them along the way. Then take them along with you to hang treestands or check trail cameras or scout a new piece of public land. Even better, get them to help you out on some food plot or habitat work.

You’ll have a lot more fun mentoring a new hunter than you’d probably expect. Then set aside a weekend to get your mentee on a hunt that has a high success rate. Does and young bucks are ideal for new hunters. In a perfect scenario, you’d hunt a spot that the two of you had scouted together.

If all that still sounds too time intensive, do what you can. Even the small things, like giving a new hunter your old riflescope, can help recruit more people into our ranks.
