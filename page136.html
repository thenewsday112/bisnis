#3787. Accordion Icons: Which Work Best?

📅 2026-10-23  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Vitaly Friedman loves beautiful content and doesn’t like to give in easily. When he is not writing, he’s most probably running front-end & UX … More about Vitaly ↬

Weekly tips on front-end & UX.Trusted by 182,000+ folks.

So what icon is the most reliable to show expansion and collapsing for accordions? Is there really any noticeable difference between carets, chevrons, arrows, and triangles?

Well, research shows that there is some difference indeed. But what’s more interesting is that split menus are harmful, why it’s better to not have icons and text labels link to different actions, and why the caret is the safest icon choice. Let’s dive in!

The findings aren’t very surprising: without any icon in use, users expect that tapping on the menu item would take them directly to a new page. Users often struggle with custom icons as they don’t clearly signal that they’ll open an accordion. And people tend to tap fairly equally on the icon and on the label for most icons.

Many designs (like the one below) tend to slightly overcomplicate things by adding arrows and carets pointing everywhere, depending on the state of the accordion. But typically we don’t need to use an arrow to indicate a link to the category.

The rule of thumb is very simple: if it’s a link, the easiest and most effective way is to simply underline it — without any arrow, caret, or other icon. Perhaps it’s not particularly exciting, but it works flawlessly every single time.

Using a right-facing arrow icon ▶ isn’t associated with an expectation of going directly to a new page. And having the same icon pointing down ▼ doesn’t necessarily imply that an accordion is going to open on tap/click.

It’s a good idea to avoid split buttons for accordions — where the text label links directly to a page, but the icon opens the accordion. Instead, it’s much more reliable to allow users to tap on a main category and expose the menu; and to access the category page, we add a link to it inside the menu.

For accordions, we typically use +/− or arrows ↓/↑ to signal expanded and collapsed states, and typically it works well. However, both have many different interpretations. Plus + can mean anything from saving for later to adding to cart to zooming in, and can be particularly confusing for multi-level navigation, with many levels open at the same time.

Arrows ↓/↑ can mean scrolling or sliding or finding the next match, but also a jump to a particular portion of a page. Both plus and arrows are well-understood and work, but depending on the design, can be confusing — mostly because they are overloaded.

The safe choice is to use the caret/triangle pointing down ▼ by default, and change it to a caret pointing up or a caret pointing sideways ▶ on interaction. If you need an alternative, then +/- typically work well, too.

But we certainly don’t need any further icons or arrows to indicate where the links go. The same goes for split menus, which often produce more mistakes than help. Instead, we have one button or dropdown that does exactly one thing — shows all options and allows users to drill down.

And if you need a direct link, we link the title directly to the page and underline it. More often than not, simpler is better.

#772. I’ve Spent Years Testing Benchmade Pocket Knives. Here Are the 7 Best, Ranked

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

These American-made blades aren’t cheap, but they’re worth their price tag for quality and performance.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. Why Trust Us?

Benchmade is one of the great American knife brands, with an expansive collection of blades made entirely in its Oregon, U.S., headquarters. Founded in 1987, the company has a long history of pushing the boundaries of production and design.

Today, Benchmade is best known for its modern everyday carry (EDC) folders kitted out with the brand’s Axis lock, first put into production in 1999. This once-patented ambidextrous folding mechanism is now employed by nearly every major knife competitor worldwide.

It was the release of the Osborne 940 in 2000 that established Benchmade as a brand worth paying attention to. And in 2017, the debut of the Bugout 535 cemented its distinguished reputation. Both of these pocket knives proved that ultralight materials could still be shockingly strong and durable. In time, Benchmade’s folders, fixed blades, and automatics gained devotees among mountaineers, backpackers, and hunters.

The Bugout 535 was my first Benchmade knife, which I added to my collection upon its release, and it’s become my go-to blade. Over the years, there have been others I’ve tested, and I certainly have my favorites, which I’ve ranked in order below.

I was late to the Benchmade party, as my first of its knives was the Bugout 535 released in 2017. At the time, its 2-ounce weight and stiff, secure lockout was a confusing novelty for people like me who equated “heavy” with “strong” and “durable.” It took me years to fully trust the Bugout because of how light the Grivory handle scales, aluminum liner, and thin-yet-tough CPM-S30V steel make it. Now it’s my go-to for backpacking and backcountry hunts in addition to everyday carry.

Despite a relatively high price tag, the Bugout has sold more than a million knives in less than a decade of production and is arguably the defining pocket knife of the explosion of modern EDC blades. I’ve yet to chip the edge or break the tip despite being a known knife abuser. The slim and light build is barely noticeable in your pocket or pack. Sure, the materials and specs can be found elsewhere today, but the Bugout still sits in my hunting pants and Benchmade continues to push the design with knives like the Bugout Vapyr featured below.

Benchmade makes a whole range of hunting blades, from the new wood-handled Slipridge folder to the Taggedout, which is basically a beefed-up Bugout built for hunters. I always keep a folder in my pocket while hunting, but also have a fixed blade or three in my pack along with a pocket sharpener in case there’s field dressing to be done. The Benchmade Raghorn is a midsize modern hunting fixed blade that rarely leaves my pack and rarely needs sharpening.

Despite being almost nine inches long, the Raghorn weighs just 3.5 ounces thanks to a relatively thin blade and lightweight G10 scales. (My older Raghorn has carbon fiber scales and a blaze orange blade coating but isn’t in production anymore, sadly.)

As with many Benchmade blades, high-end S30V stainless steel is used for durability, edge retention, and toughness. I’ve butchered dozens of animals with mine and it hardly shows.

The Bugout Vapyr released in 2026 stands as evidence that Benchmade isn’t content to rest on its laurels. Nine years after the original Bugout debuted, Benchmade designers revisited the iconic ultralight EDC and found a way to make it even lighter and thinner. And it’s arguably more robust, employing thin aluminum scales instead of lightweight synthetics for an all-metal build.

Considering the featherlight 1.72-ounce weight, it’s an impressive feat of engineering and production. The Vapyr uses an upgraded version of the Axis lock with a slide that’s ridged and easier to grab, but the action isn’t quite as free-swinging as the original Axis.

The Infidel might be Benchmade’s most influential automatic and the Shootout its most popular, but the pricey Auto Fact is my personal favorite. The Italian stiletto-styled side-opening automatic is one of the most distinctive automatics I’ve seen. It’s not as fast as most out-the-front (OTF) switchblades, but the spring-loaded Axis lock deployment is smooth, not jarring like many more powerful side-opening automatics.

The long, narrow blade has a dagger look, but is single-edged, making it safer and more practical for everyday tasks. Still, this is more of a novelty than a workhorse and one of the few knives I keep in rotation for EDC despite its limitations. The action is awesome, but it needs to be kept clean and well-oiled or the action can slow down or fail entirely if you get it too gummed up.

The Osborne 940 isn’t my all-time favorite Benchmade, but it’s arguably the most important Benchmade knife. The Osborne debuted in 2000 and it paired Warren Osborne’s ranch-ready design with the then-new Axis lock. The blend was a hit with a wide range of knife enthusiasts and working people, thanks to a capable build and the intrigue of the Axis.

Since 2000, there have been dozens of Osborne variations and limited releases. The original 940 used 154CM steel and aluminum scales, but today you find countless versions of the original plus a Mini Osborne, Auto Osborne, and customizable Osbornes.

The design has stood the test of time and there are plenty of similar blades from other brands, especially since the Axis lock patent expired. If there’s a downside to buying an Osborne, it’s that the price has climbed to $300 since 2000 and many of those capable imitators (the Kershaw Iridium, as one example) cost significantly less.

I love flipper actions on pocket knives. They rival the speed of an automatic, snapping open fast and locking out securely as a result, but most rely on liner or frame locks, which aren’t as easy to open one-handed, making them less practical everyday tools. Benchmade doesn’t make a ton of flippers, but the Lowden is one of the relatively few Axis lock flippers combining one-handed convenience with the fidget-friendly speed of a flipper.

The Lowden sits deep in the pocket and is easy and fast to deploy. I still prefer the Bugout overall, but the slightly heavier Lowden has an all-metal build and a thicker blade, which makes it feel more robust for everyday abuse. And if you generally prefer a flipper like I do, but also want the ease and security of the Axis, this is one of a handful of knives with that particular combination.

The Benchmade Triage has the distinction of being the rescue knife carried by the crew of the Artemis II for their recent orbit around the moon. Even if you’re not preparing for a space mission, the Triage is a simple folding rescue knife for emergency preparedness and first responders.

The blunt-tip blade helps avoid accidental punctures in high-stress situations and the fold-out belt cutter adds functionality without the complexity and bulk of a multitool. The N680 steel prioritizes corrosion resistance so it’s a knife you can stash in an emergency kit or bugout bag without worrying about it holding up.

#2780. Hiking is Losing a Piece of Its Soul as Boots Become a Thing of The Past

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

You can’t stop progress, and that’s as true for hiking as for everything else. Athletes today are pushing further, breaking records and setting FKTs on long-distance trails every year; fabrics are getting lighter and stronger; mapping, tracking, and monitoring are increasingly digital and device-based; and footwear is getting lighter, sprightlier, and more expendable.

But as we move forward, something is getting left behind that was once a core piece of this sport’s soul. It was every hiker’s best friend, well-loved and well-used, a part of the uniform no one ever forgot, the ticket to freedom that carried you out your front door: the hiking boot.

Boots have fallen out of fashion in recent years. More and more hikers today prefer low-cut, lightweight trail shoes or runners. Some surveys suggest that on the Appalachian Trail, as many as 90 percent of hikers are wearing trail runners, while just 10 percent are wearing boots. And there’s good reason for that. Boots are heavy; they’re stiff and less nimble; they’re typically more expensive; they don’t breathe as well, and they always take longer to lace up. Why shouldn’t hikers prefer a lighter, typically cheaper, more comfortable alternative?

Because (and I already feel sappy typing this) boots tell a story. They last for years instead of seasons. The scratches, stains, dog-eared tongues, weathered soles, and dirty laces of a seasoned pair of boots don’t just give them character, but make them a character. I greet my hiking boots when I open the door of my tent in the morning like an old friend. I curse at them from time to time when they unlace or trip me up. Occasionally, I’ll thank them after we’ve traveled many miles over rugged terrain.

My boots are my buddies. And I fear that’s a relationship many modern hikers might never get to experience. So, here are the (mostly sentimental) reasons why I love boots and I’ll never be swayed to start wearing shoes or runners instead.

A good pair of hiking boots tells its owner’s story. You can translate what’s inscribed on them and recite the associated tales from memory. They bear the story of your trails and adventures on their soles (and mids, and uppers).

For example, when I proposed to my now-wife, we were on top of a mountain, and I did not have a ring. But the moment was upon me, and I had to seize it. My solution? I cut a length from one of my hiking boots’ extended laces and literally tied a knot around her finger. Of course, I replaced that lace eventually. But the cut one was long enough that I could still tie my boot and hike safely down the mountain. Now, every time I look at that pair of boots and see the unmatched laces, I think of that very special moment. When people ask about them, I get to tell the story.

On another occasion, in the German Alps, I’d just hiked to a refuge with my parents, and we were enjoying spectacular views, great conversation, incredible weather, and a basket of bread with olive oil. A drop of the oil spilled on my leather hiking boots and stained them forever. Whenever I notice that little discolored blotch, that fond memory flashes across my mind.

Eventually I’ll replace the boots that tell these stories—nothing, especially hiking footwear, lasts forever. However, those boots are in their fourth year of hard use. When the soles finally do give out, the brand, Zamberlan, offers resoling services. That could extend their lives another three or four years.

Which brings me to the next reason I love boots.

Part of the reason boots can tell a story is that they last longer than a typical pair of lightweight trail shoes or runners. Backpacker has interviewed athletes who burned through as many as 14 pairs of shoes in a single thru-hike. These days, trail runners are almost becoming disposable. Like those good old No.2 pencils you used in high school, they wear down quickly and go straight in the trash when they burn out.

People do the same with boots; it just tends to take longer. And, as mentioned, some brands will offer services like resoling to extend the life of the boots and mitigate waste. Very few, if any, footwear brands offer that kind of service for hiking or running shoes.

Environmentally, that isn’t great. Yes, some shoes are made with recycled materials or even made so they can be recycled at the end of their useful life. But if you’re burning through numerous pairs of shoes in a year (or even a summer), that’s a lot of waste whether it’s Global Recycled Standard (GRS) certified or not.

Finally, there’s the microplastics issue. Some studies have suggested that soft-soled running shoes are “significant contributors” to microplastics contaminating remote bodies of water, like lakes, creeks, and streams. That hasn’t been scientifically observed with harder-soled shoes and boots.

This will seem like a negative to many people, but I think there’s something to be said for boots slowing you down in nature. No one is running long distances in full- or mid-height hiking boots. They’re too heavy, don’t breathe well enough, and aren’t very agile. When I’m wearing my boots, I’m moving slower. That forces me to look around, take in the views, focus on the sounds and smells around me, and generally be more present.

There’s nothing wrong with wanting to move fast or crush miles. It’s just not how I want to hike every time I hit the trail. Wearing boots forces me to go slow and pay attention to the place I’m exploring.

My final point comes down to functionality. I love my boots because they protect the crap out of my feet. I can stomp through scree fields, splash through puddles, accidentally kick rocks or roots, and I don’t have to worry about hurting my foot. Leather boots are especially good in this regard.

I also tend to be prone to rolling my ankles. If I’m not paying full attention to the trail, I will sometimes stumble, and my ankle gives out at a weird angle. In low-cut shoes, I know I’d easily sprain or maybe even break something in one of those moments. The extra protection boots offer gives me peace of mind that my feet and ankles are safe.

If you swear by trail shoes or runners, I’m not going to convince you to switch to boots, nor am I trying to. There are a lot of good reasons to use trail runners. I’d never blame or fault anyone for liking them more.

To me, though, the hiking boot is as important to the soul of hiking as a cowboy boot is to life on the ranch, or as a pair of Xtratuf boots are to an Alaskan fisherman. It’s not just a tool of the trade. It’s an embodiment of the lifestyle, a piece of the wearer’s identity, and a trademark of sorts that lets the world know who you are and, more importantly, where you’ve been.

Europe has dropped the ball on the prototype for its first reusable spacecraft. Or, rather, the prototype itself was dropped — on purpose — but the outcome of its fall was not what engineers had hoped for.

The incident occurred during an Oct. 6 drop test of the full-scale Space Rider model, from the European Space Agency (ESA). The robotic vehicle's initial drogue parachute deployed successfully, but its main parafoil detached during deployment, leaving the prototype unable to slow its descent before crashing into the ground, according to ESA officials. The cause of the failure remains under investigation.

"Following several successful tests using representative vehicle masses, the latest system-level drop test in October was not completed as planned due to an anomaly during the parafoil deployment sequence," an ESA spokesperson confirmed in an email to Space.com. "An independent investigation is ongoing to determine its root cause and inform the next steps."

Space Rider is being developed as Europe's first reusable orbital spacecraft — an uncrewed vehicle designed to carry scientific experiments to orbit for up to two months, then return them to Earth for analysis. Its method of return, though, differs from that of any other spacecraft in operation today. Space Rider''s wingless, lifting-body design relies on a steerable parachute, or parafoil, to maneuver toward its landing area. This allows it to perform runway-style touchdowns, as opposed to other, less accurate parachute-aided recoveries in current practice.

This month's drop test was intended to demonstrate that ability, which is a critical requirement for Space Rider's planned reuse after missions in orbit, but the impact of its failure may not end up disrupting its ultimate development timeline. According to Géraldine Naja, ESA's director of space transportation, the vehicle's other remaining qualification tests aren't holding back its path to launch.

"We hope that this test will not damage, or let's say lead us to modify, the remaining schedule and qualification activities," Naja said during an ESA Council media briefing on Oct. 22. "In any case, the remaining qualification activities are not on the critical path of the development of the Space Rider."

Naja described the incident as an unfortunate but important event. "We have had many successful tests before, but testing and validation is part of the development of a space system," she said.

The lost vehicle was a full-scale test model assembled to evaluate Space Rider's descent and landing systems. The minivan-sized mockup reproduced the size and flight characteristics of its reentry module, with avionics to control its autonomous guidance and navigation systems, but the vehicle was far from a space-ready article.

The Oct. 6 test took place at a military range on the Italian island of Sardinia, where the mockup was released from a helicopter at an altitude of approximately 2 miles (3 kilometers). According to Naja, the vehicle's initial drogue parachute triggered as expected and began slowing the Space Rider mock-up's descent before its larger, steerable parafoil was supposed to unfurl. The parafoil, however, detached during deployment, leaving the vehicle in freefall before impacting the ground. ESA has subsequently recovered all the craft's components.

This month's event was the first drop test since an unsuccessful attempt in May, when a sensor flagged an issue during preparations, prompting engineers to abort the test before the model was released. Following that, Space Rider's testing range, operated by Italy's Ministry of Defense, was unavailable from June through September.

Space Rider's descent and landing qualification campaign began in 2025, with previous successes demonstrating its parafoil deployment and autonomous capabilities. Beyond its landing systems, Space Rider also underwent separate thermal protection testing earlier this year, exposing its heat-shield materials to temperatures around 2,900 degrees Fahrenheit (1,600 degrees Celsius) in plasma wind tunnel trials.

ESA has targeted Space Rider's first orbital mission for later this decade.

#2030. Trump administration and Texas urge court to leave their agreement on deportation proceedings in place

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Trump administration and Texas urged the Supreme Court on Thursday afternoon to leave in place their consent agreement abolishing a Biden-era regulation that had allowed immigration judges to suspend some deportation proceedings. U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer told the justices that the request from the challengers in the case – three cities, led by Baltimore, and a group that provides legal services to immigrants – to pause a federal district court’s ruling “attempts to improperly leverage a clear-cut dispute over” the challengers’ efforts to join the case “into a far-reaching attack on the merits of” the court’s approval of the agreement. For its part, Texas argued that granting the challengers’ request “would revive a rule the district court declared unlawful, renew the fiscal and sovereign injuries Texas alleged, and unsettle a final judgment accepted by the actual parties.”

The Executive Office for Immigration Review, which is part of the Department of Justice, adopted the regulation at the center of the dispute, which provides what is known as “administrative closure,” in 2024. The rule gives immigration judges the authority to suspend deportation proceedings in some scenarios – when, for example, a crime victim or witness is seeking immigration relief, such as asylum – while the immigrant’s appeal in the lower federal courts is ongoing.

Texas went to federal court in Wichita Falls, Texas, in June, arguing that the EOIR does not have the power to adopt the administrative-closure rule. The case was assigned to the only federal judge there, Chief U.S. District Judge Reed O’Connor.

On the same day that the case was filed, the U.S. Department of Justice and Texas filed a motion to have O’Connor enter a final judgment for the state. The DOJ also asked O’Connor both to throw out the rule altogether and to issue an order making clear that the EOIR could not adopt a similar rule in the future. O’Connor granted those requests.

Three cities with substantial immigrant populations – Baltimore, Maryland; New Haven, Connecticut; and Columbus, Ohio – sought to join the case to defend the administrative-closure rule on appeal, along with Centro Legal de la Raza, a group that provides legal services to immigrants. O’Connor denied that request, holding that the challengers lacked a legal right to sue, known as standing. In particular, he rejected the cities’ contention that the elimination of the rule would cost them money because their taxpayers are likely to be deported. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit declined to pause O’Connor’s decision while the challengers appealed, prompting them to come to the Supreme Court on Oct. 9.

In their application to pause O’Connor’s decision, the challengers told the justices that the consent judgment was “indefensible.” “If a judgment procured through such open collusion is allowed to remain in effect,” they argued, “any agency can avoid the strictures of the” federal law governing administrative agencies “by advertising its unwillingness to defend a disfavored regulation and by immediately acquiescing to nationwide relief.”

In their briefs opposing the challengers’ request, both the Trump administration and Texas emphasized that the challengers do not have standing to intervene on appeal and therefore cannot challenge the consent judgment. Sauer noted that although the consent agreement throws out the administrative-closure rule, it does not require cases that have already been administratively closed to be returned to an immigration judge’s calendar. As a result, Texas said, any injury to cities would hinge on “a cascade of discretionary and unidentified events”: “an administratively closed case must be re-calendared; the alien must lose on the merits; a final removal order must issue; the Department of Homeland Security must execute it; and the person otherwise must have remained employed and paying a municipal income tax. No declaration identifies even one taxpayer for whom that chain is likely.” Any such effects on the cities “are too ‘indirect’ and ‘attenuated’ to” give them a right to sue, Sauer similarly contended.

Moreover, Texas added, “[i]f any local government could establish standing whenever federal immigration policy might eventually change the number of resident taxpayers, nearly every immigration measure would be judicially challengeable by municipalities on both sides.”

#785. Reality Might Be ‘Leaking’ Into a Fifth Dimension—And Dark Energy Could Be the Smoking Gun, Scientists Say

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

If we find that gravity starts acting weird at the scale of a micron, it could trigger a process of discovery that could finally explain the biggest mysteries of the cosmos.

Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

Imagine you are standing in a dark, quiet kitchen at midnight. You’re reaching for a glass of water, confident that the counter is where it’s always been, that the floor is solid, and that the air around you is, well, just air. You feel like you have a pretty good handle on how the room—and the world—works.

But what if there was a trapdoor? Not a physical one in the linoleum, but a hidden dimension tucked into the very fabric of space, no larger than a speck of dust, yet governing everything you see.

That’s a good analogy for a cosmic puzzle that scientists have been trying to solve for decades. It stems from the fact that our best approximation of how the universe works just doesn’t fit with reality. On one hand, we have the standard model, our remarkably successful “instruction manual” for subatomic particles. On the other, we have dark energy—that mysterious, ghostly pressure pushing the universe to expand faster and faster. The problem is that our math says dark energy should be enormous, but our telescopes show it’s incredibly tiny. It’s a mismatch of epic proportions.

So, how do we fix a universe model that doesn’t add up?

In a paper published on arXiv in September 2024, researchers Ignatios Antoniadis, PhD, from Sorbonne University and Dieter Lüst, PhD, from the Max Planck Institute have proposed a radical “top-down” explanation. They aren’t just tweaking the dials; they are suggesting that our entire four-dimensional reality is actually “leaking” into a fifth dimension.

They call it the “Dark Dimension” scenario.

In the world of string theory—our best guess at a “theory of everything”—we usually imagine extra dimensions as being curled up so tight they are practically invisible, like the thickness of a thread viewed from miles away. But Antoniadis and Lüst suggest something different. Using a framework called “weakly coupled string theory,” they’ve derived a model where one of these extra dimensions is actually quite large.

That might sound small, but in the world of particle physics, a micron—the size of a single bacterium—is gargantuan. It’s a mesoscopic scale, a middle ground between the quantum and the cosmic. If this dimension exists, it’s like finding out your studio apartment actually has a massive, hidden ballroom just behind the wallpaper.

This is called “UV-IR mixing.” Usually, we assume that the physics of the very small (the ultraviolet) and the physics of the very large (the infrared) don’t talk to each other. They stay in their own lanes. But the authors argue that in quantum gravity, these scales are tethered together. The tiny value of dark energy we observe is actually a signal—a breadcrumb trail—pointing directly to the existence of this hidden, micron-sized dimension.

It is a bold claim, but the math is compelling. By looking at how strings “slosh” around on a worldsheet (the two-dimensional surface a string traces through space), the researchers found that if you want to keep the universe stable and explain dark energy without “fine-tuning”—which is physicist-speak for "fudging the numbers"—you almost inevitably end up with this dark dimension.

If this fifth dimension is real, it changes everything about our future in the stars. It means that gravity isn’t just a weak force; it’s a force that is “diluted” because it’s spreading out into that extra space. It also suggests that there is a “tower” of light particles—a cosmic ladder of new physics—waiting for us just beyond our current reach.

Experimentalists are already looking for “short-range” gravity deviations. If we find that gravity starts acting weird at the scale of a micron, we’ll know Antoniadis and Lüst are onto something. We would be looking at the first potential physical proof of string theory.

But for now, it remains a beautiful, mathematically rigorous hypothesis. We are still peering through the keyhole, trying to see the shape of the room beyond.

And that’s the thrill of it. We are finally learning how to ask the right questions. The dark dimension might be a mirage, or it might be the foundation of everything. Who knows what we’ll see when we keep peeking around corners?

#1006. I've Maxed Out My Bookshelf, But These 10 Cookbooks Are Worth Making Space For

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A recipe developer’s picks for the season’s most exciting new releases.

Delish editors handpick every product we feature. We may earn commission from the links on this page.

Fall is prime cookbook season, and this year I’ve been especially flooded with new releases featuring everything from high-protein to baking to weeknight dinner—all competing for a spot on my (totally loaded) bookshelves. As a recipe developer who regularly reviews cookbooks, I’ve read hundreds of cookbooks over the years, and I’m not easily impressed by beautiful design or gorgeous photography—and I’m allergic to anything generic.

That said, from the never-ending list of options, these 10 cookbooks stood out to me. They range in subject from the expected (weeknight dinner, baking) to the less common (cookbook as memoir, cookbook as paranormal storyteller). But regardless of subject matter, each has a distinct voice and point of view—and that’s what makes them worth a second flip through.

Here are the 10 cookbooks I’m making shelf space for this season.

Caroline Chambers’ first cookbook delivered a full slate of approachable, innovative weeknight meals—and her second does not disappoint. From creative mashups (Ramen Vongole) to classic standbys (The Only Egg Salad You’ll Ever Need), each recipe left me excited to cook it (and, as a recipe developer, just a little jealous that I hadn’t come up with the idea first!)

Beyond the smart ideas and execution, I especially appreciated the book’s practical structure and organization: the recipes are grouped into chapters based on cook time, so you can easily find a recipe that suits your time frame, and the ingredient prep is included in the instructions, so you know you won’t get slapped with prep time in addition to the advertised cook time. A 15-minute recipe takes 15 minutes, start to finish—and isn’t that refreshing?

I can’t wait to make: Green Curry Wontons

I dabble in cocktail-making, but I LOVE understanding the how and why behind them, so Good Spirits is my idea of a good read. Here, master cocktail-maker Tiffany Barriere is sharing a comprehensive, concise overview of the world’s most classic cocktails, from how they were created to how you can whip up a killer version yourself.

Interspersed between the cocktails are sidebars that offer deeper dives into specific spirits, bar tools, and mixology—the nerd in me absolutely loved these!—along with stories highlighting significant Black bartenders and their contributions to cocktail culture over the decades. Whether you’re here for these stories or want to level up your own bartending, this book promises a good time.

Today’s cookbooks usually try to solve a problem or explore a culture, but Carla Lalli’s latest is doing neither. More a collection of personal essays than a cookbook, Food Is A Feeling pushes the boundaries of what I believed a cookbook could be—in a way that both surprised and impressed me.

Weeknight cookbooks are only successful if the author truly understands their audience—and Alex Snodgrass has clearly listened to hers. Packed with prep-ahead breakfasts, fast lunches, and pantry-staple dinners, this cookbook is tuned into the reality of everyday life, and focused on meeting the 21st-century home cook where they are.

The photographs are beautiful, and the recipes promise big, bold (approachable!) flavors, but beyond that, I especially appreciated the tiny practical design details that make this book both helpful and accessible. The dietary sidebar highlights recipes you can make ahead, and the side “bursts” with freezer tips and other kitchen info. Print space is valuable real estate, so their presence shows real dedication to making the book as useful as possible—no matter what restrictions you have.

I can’t wait to make: Indian Butter Chicken Meatballs

Carla Hall’s first baking book has been a long time coming, but I’m here to say it was totally worth the wait. Though she’s mostly known as a savory chef, Carla definitely knows her way around cake, pastries, and biscuits (in particular!), and, if anything, her savory expertise only makes her bakes more unique (Chocolate Pumpernickel Pancakes? Rustic Glazed Carrot Tart with Loaded Crumble? Sign me up.)

As someone who bakes a lot, I especially appreciated Carla’s precise (and sometimes brilliantly unorthodox) technique. She’s not afraid to tell us to temper chocolate in the microwave or take the internal temperature of our baked goods. And she’s equally eager to offer new approaches to traditional recipes (did you know you can make curd in the microwave?!) Carla respects our ability to learn and bake at her level, and through that, she instills us with the confidence to believe we actually can.

I can’t wait to make: Butternut Squash Beignets with Spiced Sugar

I’ve flipped through dozens of Mediterranean cookbooks, but Ayesha Nurdjaya’s exuberant, Mediterranean-by-way-of-Brooklyn is so compelling I couldn’t put it down. Filled with unapologetic takes on Mediterranean favorites (sweet potatoes with feta butter and chocolate, anyone?), this book is exploding with so much cooking joy, you’ll find yourself reaching for it every weeknight.

Ayesha’s restaurants emphasize communal eating, and I especially love how apparent that is in her book. There’s a whole chapter titled “My Dips Don’t Lie” featuring (you guessed it!) a full gamut of dips from beet tzatziki to “Gravesend Onion Dip.” There’s also an index at the front of the book which organizes recipes into “playlists” so you can easily find the perfect recipe for any occasion. The musical chapter titles continue throughout the book, by the way—just another detail that makes this book so fun.

I’ve been dying to eat at a cha chaan teng in Hong Kong since I developed a recipe for the iconic diner French toast, and while I still haven’t convinced my husband to brave the long plane ride, flipping through this cookbook almost transports me there. Part historical culture study, part cookbook, Cha Chaan Teng offers an immersive deep dive into this unique genre of Hong Kong restaurant, along with an array of intriguing recipes to make at home.

Reading through the recipes, I was struck by how familiar yet different many of them were. The food of the cha chaan teng is a blurred fusion of British, Chinese, Western, and working-class food, and the resulting dishes reflect that. There’s pork chops baked into rice, beef brisket in a curry sauce, and spaghetti bolognese made with cream of chicken soup. There’s also hot dogs served in milk buns with a smear of Miracle Whip—apparently quite traditional. I won’t knock it till I try it!

I can’t wait to make: Perfect Scrambled Egg Sandwich

Food is more than food: it’s cultural history and storytelling, and Amy Bruni’s Cuisine To Kill For is a powerful (and in some cases, gruesome!) reminder of just that. Filled with dozens of historical and modern recipes accompanied by the true crime stories that inspired them, this cookbook is a thrilling read—and if you dare to cook from it, it promises to be an even more thrilling eat.

Like Food Is A Feeling, Bruni lets storytelling guide the recipes. Each deeply dark (and possibly totally fantastical) story is rooted in a place said to be haunted, and the accompanying recipe for each story is either a historical food that’s been re-engineered for a modern audience, or something modern that’s somehow connected to the location the story took place. Whether or not you decide to make anything in it, you won’t be able to put it down.

I can’t wait to make: Lemon Toast (H.H. Holmes’ Murder Castle)

I love the recent Ottolenghi cookbooks for the way they push the envelope while staying totally weeknight-friendly, and this latest one takes that tricky balance to a whole new level. Filled with smart, beautiful recipes that deliver big flavor for minimal effort, Simple Too is taking ease and simplicity in the kitchen to the extreme—with brilliant results.

I can’t wait to make: Butternut “Parmigiana”

I’ve never been to Georgia, but after reading this cookbook, I want to book a flight immediately so I can eat there. Written in partnership by Georgian chef Meriko Gubedladze and American food writer Benjamin Kemper, this culinary love letter is filled with vibrant, intriguing food, paired with plenty of helpful culinary facts and history so we can better understand it.

One thing I especially love is that this cookbook doesn’t overcomplicate the food—but it also doesn’t attempt to tone it down or assimilate it into the broader American culture. The pantry section succinctly describes a few Georgian staples—dried marigold, barberries, and tkemali—and then uses them throughout the recipes. The book describes unfamiliar dishes in clear terms without unnecessarily comparing them to American dishes. And while Khachapuri makes an appearance, make no mistake: there’s so much more here than just cheesy bread.

#2436. No First Amendment Right Not to Sell Bridal Outfit for Same-Sex Wedding

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From N.Y. State Div. of Hum. Rts. V. D. Auxilly NYC LLC, decided today by New York appellate judges Sallie Manzanet–Daniels, Manuel Mendez, Barbara Kapnick, Bahaati Pitt–Burke, and Matthew Parker–Raso:

Complainant, at the time a Missouri resident, emailed D. Auxilly, a New York based business that produces and sells attire, including bridal outfits, to inquire about a bridal jumpsuit she had seen advertised on one or more of D. Auxilly's social media pages. Specifically, complainant requested information about payment options for the jumpsuit. Complainant also informed D. Auxilly that as her fiancé had "fallen in love" with it after struggling to find anything she liked, complainant wanted to purchase it for their upcoming wedding.

Dominique Galbraith, D. Auxilly's owner and operator, responded on behalf of D. Auxilly advising complainant of the payment requirements of the business but that she would not be able to make the garment for a same-sex wedding because it would go against her religious beliefs. She also urged complainant and her fiancé to reconsider getting married….

The First Amendment does not shield D. Auxilly from liability for this discrimination. Unlike the baker in Masterpiece Cakeshop v. Colorado Civil Rights Comm (2018), D. Auxilly was not asked to design and create customized items which might constitute an "expressive statement." Nor was it tasked with creating a customized wedding website, as in 303 Creative LLC v. Elenis (2023). Instead, D. Auxilly refused to sell the wedding garment it had previously designed and which it had marketed to the public. While the garment likely would need to be adjusted for sizing, this potential sizing alteration would not constitute an expressive statement. Neither would D. Auxilly's "speech" be compelled here by requiring it to sell the previously designed and marketed garment.

Enforcement of the [law] here also does not violate D. Auxilly's right to free exercise of religion. The statute is "neutral, both on its face and due to a lack of evidence that it targets religious conduct for a distinctive treatment."

{[T]he Division had jurisdiction in the instant matter because the discriminatory conduct occurred in New York and its impact was felt in the state. Complainant reached out to D. Auxilly, a business which markets its clothes as "made in New York," seeking to purchase goods from this New York business. D. Auxilly's discriminatory conduct based upon complainant's sexual orientation deprived complainant of an opportunity to engage in commercial activity here. Indeed, by affirmatively labeling its products as being "made in New York" but then refusing to sell those same "New York" products to people of a certain sexual orientation, D. Auxilly implicitly associated the City and State of New York with a discriminatory act, and the impact test is satisfied. Additionally, the fact that complainant communicated with D. Auxilly online through social media rather than traveling to a New York storefront does not deprive petitioner of jurisdiction over this matter.}

Finally, we find the awards of compensatory damages [$5,000] and civil penalties proper. The award to complainant is reasonably related to the wrongdoing, supported by evidence before the Division, and in line with other awards for similar injuries. Likewise, the civil penalty of $20,000 is neither disproportionate to the offense nor does it shock the conscience.

Melissa Franco and Michael K. Swirsky (New York State Division of Human Rights) represent the state.
