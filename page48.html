#4026. Why the Left Brain Controls the Right Body

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A theoretical neuroanatomy paper published in Frontiers in Neuroanatomy by researchers at the University of Minnesota proposes an evolutionary explanation for why the brain is cross-wired. The authors argue that contralateral organization, where the left side of the brain governs the right side of the body and vice versa, was not necessarily an independent adaptation for limb control or somatosensation. Instead, the ancient optical physics of the eye and the contralateral mapping of the midbrain optic tectum established a structural template over 500 million years ago that subsequently biased the evolution of sensory and motor circuits in four-limbed animals.

Source: Frontiers / University of Minnesota

One of the most enduring enigmas in neuroanatomy is contralateral organization: why did vertebrates evolve central nervous systems where the left cerebral hemisphere monitors and directs the right side of the physical body, and the right hemisphere commands the left?

While medical students and neuroscientists routinely memorize the intricate crossings of the brainstem and spinal cord, known as decussations, the evolutionary pressures that led to this complex “cross-wiring” across sensory and motor modalities have fueled debate for centuries.

Now, in an analysis published in Frontiers in Neuroanatomy, neuroscientists Daniel Wang and Martin Wessendorf, PhD, associate professor emeritus at the University of Minnesota, propose a unifying evolutionary hypothesis.

They suggest that the brain’s widespread cross-wiring is not a collection of independent functional adaptations. Rather, it represents an ancient evolutionary path-dependency initiated by the simple optical physics of primitive eyes and the ancient midbrain circuits built to interpret them.

The evolutionary story begins with sensory input from the visual apparatus. Because a spherical eye lens physically reverses an incoming image onto the retina, light originating from the right visual hemifield falls onto the left side of each retina, and vice versa.

To process this inverted sensory data efficiently, early vertebrates evolved the optic chiasm—an anatomical intersection where retinal ganglion cell axons cross the midline so that visual inputs from the right half of the world are routed entirely to the left side of the brain.

These visual signals terminate primarily in the optic tectum (termed the superior colliculus in mammals), an ancient midbrain structure present across all living vertebrate lineages. The optic tectum constructs an internal spatial map of the contralateral environment to coordinate rapid defensive and predatory orienting responses.

Crucially, in its deeper cellular layers, the tectum also receives somatic touch and auditory information aligned in exact spatial registration with the overlying visual coordinates.

Given that all surviving vertebrates possess an optic chiasm and a contralaterally organized optic tectum, the authors deduce that this architecture was already firmly established in Cambrian-era jawless fishes roughly 538 to 486 million years ago.

As ancient aquatic creatures transitioned into four-limbed terrestrial animals (tetrapods), the lineage encompassing amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals, new somatic sensory and voluntary motor pathways were required to control limbs and navigate dry land.

Rather than viewing each crossing pathway as an optimal adaptation designed from a clean slate, Wang and Wessendorf argue that evolution operated as a conservative engineer building with pre-existing modular materials.

Because the optic tectum had already coupled a contralateral visual map to deeper somatic sensations to coordinate survival reflexes hundreds of millions of years prior, any novel sensory or motor tract that evolved later had to align with this established coordinate system to prevent sensorimotor mismatch.

“Since evolution is conservative in its innovations – it often retains and builds upon existing structures, pathways, and mechanisms rather than inventing new ones – the organization of the visual system (and in particular, the optic tectum) may have defined the organizational principles required of the sensory and motor pathways that evolved later,” the authors explain.

Under this framework, contralateral wiring is an evolutionary spandrel: an architectural consequence anchored to the optics of the vertebrate eye. The authors note that future analyses examining the internal neurocranial fossils of early Cambrian vertebrates could provide physical milestones tracking how this visual blueprint laid the groundwork for the human central nervous system.

Why is the right side of the brain responsible for the left side of the body? The evolutionary history of a puzzling trait

In humans and most other mammals, the right motor cortex controls the spinal motoneurons that cause the limbs on the left sides of our bodies to move.

Similarly, the right side of the cerebral cortex predominantly receives sensory information about the left side of our bodies and the left side of the outside world. Although there have been numerous papers (dating back at least to Cajal) speculating on the adaptive value of this contralateral organization, to date none appears to have examined its evolutionary history.

Our paper examines the major contralaterally organized circuits found in extant vertebrate brains and proposes a novel hypothesis regarding their evolution. We found that vision is contralaterally organized in all vertebrates reported: input from each visual field is carried by the optic tract to the contralateral optic tectum (i.e., the superior colliculus in mammals) and is retinotopically mapped there. In addition, in most tetrapods, somatosensation and hearing are carried rostrally to forebrain by contralaterally organized circuits. Stem amniotes (−-the predecessors of reptiles, birds, and mammals) appear to have had contralaterally organized motor systems including the tectospinal tract and rubrospinal tract.

Finally, the last common ancestor of mammals itself appears to have had a contralaterally organized neocortex, including a corticospinal tract. We conclude that the mammalian neocortex evolved in brains in which many major motor and sensory circuits were already contralaterally organized. By adapting contralateral organization itself, the neocortex was able to integrate directly with those circuits without the “wiring cost” incurred by altering them.

When the twenty-two-year-old Sylvia Plath (October 27, 1932–February 11, 1963) wrote to her mother one bleak January day, both women were wading through a darkness of the soul. Science was only just beginning to hone the tools with which to begin dissecting the elemental mystery of what makes us who we are: how much of our psychology and what we experience as our personhood — whether we call it self or soul or spirit — is a product of our physical constitution, of the particular biochemical processes coursing through our particular infrastructure of matter that we experience as our body. Neuroscience was then an infant science — it still is — and the helix of heredity had just been discovered, hinting at the promise of new clarity on the ancient puzzlement of nature versus nurture, new insight into how much of our psychology is a biological inheritance and how much an ongoing composition continually revised by the confluence of chance and choice we call experience.

Several years earlier, the teenage Plath had begun contouring her consciousness and mapping its psychological promontories, its luminous surfaces and its dark edges, rhapsodizing about the joy of living, thinking deeply about free will and what make us who we are, and composing her first tragic poem in response to a minor domestic accident. Two years before she shaded in what was becoming an all-consuming darkness in “The Disquieting Muses,” she wrote to her mother in a letter included in the posthumously collected Letters Home: Correspondence 1950–1963 (public library):

I don’t know whether it is an hereditary characteristic, but our little family is altogether too prone to lie awake at nights hating ourselves for stupidities — technical or verbal or whatever — and to let careless, cruel remarks fester until they blossom in something like ulcer attacks — I know that during these last days I’ve been fighting an enormous battle with myself.

But then, with the presence of mind and the triumph of spirit that allowed her to live through the remaining years of her life — years she filled with some of the most timelessly exquisite poetry ever written — she adds:

But beyond a point, fighting only wears one out and one has to shut off that nagging part of the mind and go on without it with bravo and philosophy… Your present life is the important thing.

There is a dangerous fallacy — a biological falsehood, a feebleness of empathy, an ethical failing — in the view that people who die by suicide after living with mental illness have somehow failed at life. It is one thing to feel deeply the tragedy of that loss, to rue the help not available to them in their time of struggle; it is quite another to fault the faulty instrument itself. It is impossible for any one consciousness to truly know what living inside another is like in the first place — we make art and poems and songs to try to show each other what it is like to be alive in this body-mind. But it is especially unfathomable for a mind coursing with fairly ordinary biochemistry, housed in a brain with fairy ordinary neurophysiology, to grasp what it might be like to live with a mind inflamed by ceaselessly misfiring neurotransmitters or a mind housed in a brain with a large tumor pressing against the amygdala at every moment of every hour. To survive even a single day with such a mind is no small feat. To have not only survived thirty-one years, as Sylvia Plath did, but to have filled those years with works of staggering beauty, with poems that irradiate generations of lives — that is a rare triumph of the spirit.

Complement with May Sarton on the cure for despair and Lorraine Hansberry — another visionary artist of Plath’s generation, who also lived and made in the darkest depths — on the most reliable antidote to depression, then hear Meryl Streep read Plath’s stunning “Morning Song” and savor a rare glimpse of the poet’s inner world through her little-known paintings.

#1049. The Art of Deadzoning: 6 Retreats Where You Can Really, Truly Disconnect

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Another year, another travel trend—though this one might actually warrant the hype. Life nowadays is fast and far too busy. We are switched on to the point of no return, and as a result, most working folk are on a one-way ticket to burnout-ville. Enter deadzoning: the practice of seeking out places so unreachable that the modern world simply can’t find you.

Increasingly, intentional isolation has replaced sightseeing at the top of the travel agenda. From desolate deserts to cut-off civilisations, wild and remote destinations are emerging as the places travellers will be talking about most in 2027. Why? Travelers are seeking the kind of nervous‑system reset and awe‑led emotional experience that help lower stress-inducing hormones like cortisol and aid a good night’s sleep.

Younger generations, particularly Gen Z and Millennials, are also actively seeking ways to reduce their screen time. This has led to an unstoppable rise in digital detox retreats, wellness-first spa menus, and “slow travel” marketing jargon over the last few years. But only a handful of global refuges truly offer the distance these travelers truly crave.

From Western Papua to the steppes of Mongolia, here’s Vogue’s pick of the world’s best deadzoning retreats where you can really disconnect.

Astonishingly remote and exclusive, this is one of the few hotels in the world that has no roads leading to it. Reached only by helicopter, the setting is one of total isolation from the outside world. The 50,000-acre working high-country sheep and cattle farm owned by the Wallis family is set deep within a dramatic glacial valley and is bordered by Mount Aspiring National Park near Lake Wanaka, one of the country’s largest lakes. The alpine lodge features just four private timber and stone-hewn chalets, all kitted out with sheepskin carpets and possum fur-throws. Outside, a private deck overlooks the mountains, as does a hot tub filled with fresh water directly from the surrounding glacial streams. As for all the great outdoor adventures? They’re what travel folk dub “once in a lifetime.” Heli and bike skiing expeditions take place across 17 peaks over 800 diverse runs, the highest at 2,585m above sea level. All of the sky-high expeditions are private, and as everything is accessed only by helicopter, you are certain to have whatever slope you visit entirely to yourself.

This not-for-profit remote retreat set in the heart of the UNESCO-protected Orkhon Valley first set up camp over 25 years ago as the private summer refuge of German filmmaker Christopher Giercke, as a place for his Mongolian wife, Enkhe, and their children to reconnect with their heritage. Since then, it has evolved into one of the world’s most exclusive word-of-mouth and off-grid polo retreats. Guests stay in traditional handmade gers (with no internet) crafted by local artisans, all featuring intricate handicrafts as well as beds adorned with soft cashmere made in the family’s factory, which also produces cashmere for Hermès. Designed as a place to totally switch off, there are yoga classes in the morning, a massage ger, a resident shaman, and a sauna that looks over the dramatic landscapes of the Orkhon River. The horses in these parts are semi-wild, and all expeditions are led by skilled local horsemen who guide you over the steppes which, since the days of Genghis Khan, have been revered as the gateway to heaven.

Most Westerners have never heard of Tinajani. Why? For the simple fact that it rarely features on any standard Peru itinerary. Set quite literally in the middle of nowhere, Tinajani is one of the Peruvian Andes’ lesser-known wonders, headlined by a high-altitude, luxury-safari camp managed by indie hospitality brand Andean. The retreat, set in a nature reserve dubbed the “Canyon of the Guardians” 14,600 feet above sea level, is home to six canvas tents pitched beneath a hulking strip of anthropomorphic red rock formations carved by wind, water, and an ancient lake—some dating back a colossal 15 million years. Days are spent hiking and exploring formations that stretch for miles into the rocky steppes. The vast Puya de Raimondi forest is on your doorstep, where thousands of giant bromeliads—the largest on the planet—rise like sentinels over the highlands. Come nightfall, the sky transforms into a galactic amphitheatre, offering thousands of shooting stars and some of the clearest views of the Milky Way anywhere in the world.

If you really want to go AWOL, give Silolona Sojourns a call. The luxury charter outfit was founded by the late American anthropologist and long-time Indonesian resident Patti Seery, who spent decades immersed in the archipelago. Today, the company continues her legacy, crafting bespoke itineraries that navigate some of Southeast Asia’s most isolated waters. Built by master Konjo shipwrights in South Sulawesi using dense tropical teak and ironwood, Silolona and its sister phinisi schooner, Si Datu Bua, venture where few travelers have sailed before. Among their most remote expeditions are voyages to Papua, Halmahera, and the Forgotten Islands. Here, it’s all about encountering a wild, ancient world alongside the Asmat people, whose artistic and cultural lineages stretch back millennia. A realm of sweeping river deltas, dense rainforests, thousands of wild orchid species, and distant snowcapped peaks, voyages here are often paired with Cenderawasih Bay—a protected expanse celebrated for its pristine reefs and thriving population of resident whale sharks.

In 1972, Bhutan’s fourth king, Jigme Singye Wangchuck, declared that Gross National Happiness was far more important than Gross Domestic Product. It’s a guiding philosophy that endures today, lending this tiny Himalayan kingdom its famously gentle rhythm. Go anywhere in the country and a warm, sincere welcome is virtually guaranteed—especially in the Punakha Valley. Tucked along the riverbank beside quiet hamlets, andBeyond Punakha River Lodge offers an uncommon sense of complete seclusion. Crafted from traditional Bhutanese timber and stonework, six luxury safari tents sit framed by the towering Himalayan peaks beyond. Tall grasses, subtropical forest, and birdsong envelop the property in 360-degree wilderness, encouraging an instinctive shift down a gear. The daily tempo here is hyperlocal: morning walks wind through emerald rice terraces toward cliffside monasteries and nunneries, balanced by afternoon yoga, traditional hot-stone herbal baths, and lazy riverside picnics.

Picture this: a vast, sun-baked landscape where golden granite kopjes rise dramatically against the horizon. Between them, acacia trees snake through arid valleys and rocky ridges home to scattered pastoral settlements. This is Samburu—the gateway to Kenya’s untamed northern frontier, and the setting for Sasaab, one of the continent’s most striking wilderness lodges. Blending airy Moroccan arches with coastal Swahili details, the open-sided tented suites look down onto the Ewaso Nyiro River, where local herdsmen bring livestock to drink and bathe. As the only property in this particular conservancy, you share the vastness solely with your companions, along with pairs of dik-diks (the world’s smallest antelope), rock hyraxes, hornbills, and basking lizards. Daily life outside the camp remains deeply rooted in tradition. The semi-nomadic Samburu people live in circular manyattas protected by acacia thorns from wandering lions and leopards, and guests are frequently welcomed into neighboring villages for an afternoon of storytelling, laughter, and an invitation to try their hand at goat milking.

#4374. EU privacy advocates accuse US data annotation firm of snooping on contractors

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Noyb files five complaints in four member states over alleged tracking of workers on Scale AI's Outlier platform

US data annotation operation Scale AI faces five GDPR complaints in four EU countries over allegations that it unlawfully monitors European contractors.

Privacy campaigner noyb alleges that Scale tracks contractors who perform data annotation work on its Outlier AI platform. Like workers on Amazon's Mechanical Turk platform of yore, Outlier contractors label data, evaluate AI-generated responses, and perform other tasks to improve AI models. Scale counts companies including OpenAI, xAI, Anthropic, and Google, as well as the US government, among its customers.

Scale AI faces allegations similar to those noyb has made against other US tech firms since the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) went into effect in 2018, namely violations of said regulation.

With all that work being done digitally and remotely, Scale has extensive data collection practices to not only verify the identity of Outlier workers, but also to extensively track their activity.

“Platform work seems to be fertile ground for a whole new type of workplace surveillance,” said Thomas Le Bonniec, sociology researcher at French privacy research group DIPLab, who co-authored a report earlier this month examining Outlier's data collection practices. “This would be seen as unacceptable in any other form of employment in the European Union.”

Among the issues that raised the hackles of noyb's lawyers was identity verification conducted by Persona, a company that has faced scrutiny over an exposed test environment associated with its efforts to secure US government business. Researchers speculated that the system could be used to build a people tracker, but Persona rejected that interpretation and denied having active US government contracts. Separately, Persona's relationship with chat app Discord soured ahead of the platform's broader age verification rollout, with Discord saying the provider failed to meet its requirements for on-device facial age estimation.

According to noyb, Persona collects biometric data to verify Outlier contractors are who they say they are, as well as gathering IP addresses, device info, geolocation data, addresses, and credit data of Outlier users.

In addition to the biometric identification data, noyb accuses Scale of tracking Outlier workers by collecting system data and device activity (including activity outside of Outlier work), and transmitting that to third parties including LinkedIn, TikTok, Facebook, and other analytics services as often as every few seconds. Noyb warns that the information could be used to profile individuals and serve targeted advertising.

“Such constant and extensive tracking while working really goes against all reasonable expectations,” noyb data protection lawyer Lisa Steinfeld Leiser said of the allegations. “Why should LinkedIn or Facebook know which tasks you're working on?”

Noyb has filed complaints in France, Italy, the Netherlands, and Austria against Scale, arguing that it lacks a legal basis to install tracking software on contractors’ devices. The US company also stands accused of violating GDPR rules on data minimization, which requires that companies only collect data that’s strictly necessary for specific purposes. The complaints also assert Scale violated GDPR rights to data access after ignoring complainants’ requests to access the records of what data Scale had stored on them, as well as country-specific laws in the nations where the complaints were filed.

Noyb is asking data protection authorities in those nations to conduct a full investigation and, if necessary, fine Scale for its alleged violations.

""The report’s claims are unfounded and inaccurate," Scale told us after this article was first published. "Outlier’s practices are disclosed to contributors and require their consent. We use standard tools to verify identities, prevent fraud, protect confidential project information, and ensure accurate payment."

The complaint is likely to give further fuel to the digital sovereignty movement sweeping the EU, which has begun to realize in the past couple of years that it may not be in the best interest of the bloc to be entirely beholden to US tech companies, even if such a separation may be painfully difficult. ®

#4032. Lines, Bars, and Understanding Data

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Updated October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Data visualization is one of the final steps of the scientific process. As researchers, we are trained to develop a hypothesis, design the right experiment to test it, apply the correct statistical tests to evaluate the results, and finally produce data visualizations that capture the main effects. This final step is often left to the discretion of the authors, since there are many different ways to depict results visually.

For example, the two charts below depict the same exact results. Let's suppose the data represents average grades on a math exam of two groups of students, before and after particular types of instruction. In the bar chart on the left, the x-axis represents time (before or after the intervention), the y-axis represents average exam scores, and the bar colors represent the type of instruction the groups receive (blue represents video instruction and red represents a standard lecture). If you take the data at face value, the bar chart suggests that while both groups improved, the group that received the standard lecture improved more.

Now, the line graph on the right represents the same data, but visualized differently. Instead of individual bars, each instruction group is shown by a single line (blue or red) that represents the type of instruction they received, where each line connects the data from before and after the instruction manipulation. For most researchers, the choice of whether to present data in the form of a bar graph vs. a line graph feels completely arbitrary, and the decision might come down to aesthetics. For example, I might choose the representation on the left because the colors are clearer, and the data points are separated from each other, making the values easier to read out. Someone else might choose the line graph because they prefer to see the two time points connected.

A new study published in last month's issue of Journal of Vision suggests that the choice of visualization matters more than we think. Nestor Matthews and colleagues designed a series of bar graphs and line graphs depicting eight possible types of effects and interactions. For example, if we think about the exam performance data above, the different graphs could show a main effect of instruction type (e.g. the red line always above the blue line), a main effect of time (e.g. both lines go up after instruction), different types of interactions (e.g. the red line goes up while the blue line goes down), or a number of other possibilities. In the study, participants completed blocks of trials in which they had to categorize each graph as a "target" or "non-target". At the beginning of each block, participants were not informed what the target was, so initial responses were random, and participants received feedback after each response. After several trials, participants eventually improved in their ability to classify the graphs, and the rate of improvement was tracked over time across the eight different types of graphs and compared between bar graphs and line graphs.

The results were quite surprising: first, participants showed much faster learning when categorizing line graphs compared to bar graphs. This was true whether the data depicted main effects or interactions. Second, participants really struggled to categorize graphs that depicted main effects of the factor along the x-axis, regardless of graph format. For example, in the example above, the x-axis represents time (before and after the instruction manipulation). Participants were much more likely to attend to the factor shown by the two colors (blue vs. red) and their interaction (the different slopes of the lines) than the factor of time represented by the x-axis. This was most pronounced for bar graphs; even after 24 trials, participants assigned to the bar graph condition were no better than chance at identifying bar graphs that depicted the correct changes in the x-axis factor.

As a researcher, these results highlight two main issues to consider when choosing my next data visualization. The first is that bar graphs seem to be inherently less clear than line graphs. Despite my own visual preference for bar graphs, it may be more effective to communicate some results using line graphs. Why that is remains a question for further investigation. Perhaps an eye-tracking study can help pinpoint what aspects of graphs people attend to and what aspects they ignore.

The other issue is that, even when using line graphs, people might take longer to incorporate information that varies along the x-axis. In the examples above, time was always depicted along the x-axis, but I could have made a different choice. For example, the line graph below depicts the same data as the line graph above, except that the meaning of the x-axis and color assignment swapped. Now, the x-axis represents the format of instruction, and the colors represent the time of the exam (before or after instruction). Even though all the same data is depicted, the research study by Matthews and colleagues suggests people might ignore the new x-axis factor and focus more on the colors.

Scientists may also base their visualization decisions on whether the x-axis actually represents a continuous or discontinuous variable. In the second line graph, the x-axis represents the two groups, which is not a continuous variable. I would personally be reluctant to use a line graph this way, because to me, line graphs implicitly suggest the x-factor could vary continuously between the two endpoints of the line. But there is no intrinsic continuity between a video lecture and a standard lecture. Therefore, my preference would be to depict time (before and after) along the x-axis, as in the line graph in the first figure. One could imagine extending the x-axis to represent future time points, for example to show whether instruction effects are maintained over longer periods of time. Nevertheless, this choice of how to designate the meaning of the x-axis can have unintended effects on the salience and understandability of the results from the reader's perspective.

Communicating clearly is a top goal of scientific writing. The new research by Matthews and colleagues suggests that, beyond the typical factors scientists consider when deciding how to visualize results, we need to also consider how readable different graph elements are across the general population.

Remembering the late singer’s style, as her Back to Black album celebrates its 20th anniversary.

With her swoop of jet-black beehive hair, sharp-as-a-knife cat-eye eyeliner, and skinny jeans, Amy Winehouse defied the neat, overly curated pop star style of the early aughts. Rather than looking like everyone else, she was a purely individualistic amalgamation of vintage and her own curated, undone personal style pulled from the aesthetic of her local Camden in London as well as her favorite style stars like her grandmother and Brigitte Bardot.

Twenty years after the release of her second and final album, Back to Black, it’s impossible to ignore the late singer’s influence on music and fashion. With a new exhibition in London showcasing her iconic looks, a massive tribute concert, Back to Black’s special 20th anniversary re-release, and 2008 fashion on the rise, Winehouse’s style is as important as ever.

She wore white tank tops, little denim shorts with black leather studded belts and tiny gingham dresses. Winehouse’s tattoos, visible black and red bra straps, and thick gold chains were as key to her look as her hair and makeup (the cat-eye eyeliner, which she proudly did herself). Beyond winning five Grammy Awards in one night, she defined a short but impactful era. “I don’t want to be the prettiest or the sexiest or whatever. I just want to look different and to look like me,” she told The Guardian in 2004. Early on, Amy Winehouse worked with the stylist Lou Wood for photo shoots. “One day she turned up in a massive pair of high heels and hot pants, carrying a guitar,” she recently told The Independent. “We were walking for five hours,” she says, laughing. “She wasn’t a manufactured artist. She had her own mind definitely, and her own style.” Winehouse also collaborated with her longtime friend Naomi Parry as a stylist.

#2764. Elk Hunting from ATVs Has Gotten Out of Hand

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 27, 2026 5:24 PM EDT

Get the hottest outdoor news from Outdoor Life.

I’ve always been a pretty “go with the flow” type of hunter with a general mindset that if it’s legal, there’s nothing wrong with it. But then I went elk hunting on public ground during Colorado’s first rifle season.

I made the 28-hour drive from my home in Pennsylvania to Colorado with my cousins for their first-ever elk hunt. I had warned them of the side-by-side presence I’d experienced on a previous archery hunt in this very unit. It was manageable then. Plus, we’re from PA so we’re used to hunting pressure.

But as our elk hunt progressed, I felt no small amount of anger and sadness as the backcountry we were trying to hunt became a side-by-side racetrack.

We arrived several days before the opener to set up camp, scout the nearby creek drainages and finger ridges, and find the elevation band the elk desired. After a long hike up and into some national forest ground, to my elation, we found a pile of elk. We spotted a few raghorns, a couple smaller satellite bulls, a bugling herd bull, and his harem of cows. They were all using a knob near a meadow for their mid-day bedding area.

We watched them for a bit, then backed out of the area to continue our scouting mission. After zeroing in on the terrain features and elevation a few more times over two days of scouting and finding new elk in each location, we were feeling good about our opening day plan. When the alarm went off in my tent at 3:00 a.m. that morning, we were ready to get started on our 4-mile hike into the target area. As daylight set in, we were already at the bottom of the creek, letting the thermals pull our scent down and away from where we expected the elk to be.

We spent the next three hours meticulously crawling through the creek bottom, checking the north and south facing slopes on each side of us. To our surprise, we never laid eyes on an elk by the time we crested the knob mid-morning. I was a bit confused, until we rounded the corner and a mere 300 yards from where we had seen the large herd was a guide camp with generators, side-by-sides, a large tent with woodsmoke rolling out of the stovepipe, and several easy-ups.

I was frustrated, but I kept my composure and assured my cousin that what those guides were doing was legal and this kind of thing happens. They had accessed the spot via a legal access road that snakes up and around the basin we were hunting. My guess is, they had no idea they had effectively spooked an entire herd of elk by setting up their camp in that location. We hiked away from their camp in search of the elk, but were unable to locate them that day. I counted it as a learning experience for us — if a place is legally accessible by vehicle, we should expect vehicles to be in there.

The next morning, we executed a plan I’d devised lying in my sleeping bag the prior evening. After some obsessive map study, I felt as though I might know where that herd of elk was pushed to. We made our approach and found the herd. Unfortunately, my cousin’s scope had been knocked off zero and he ended up making a non-fatal hit on a nice bull at only 125 yards. His shot resulted in only a few drops of blood and broken spirits. We later found out that his rifle was shooting a foot low.

After re-sighting the gun and mentally recovering, we made our way to a nearby trail to begin our hike to the next pin on my map. The trail had a big sign that read, “Administrative Route — Motorized use allowed only by Forest Service Authorization.” As our trekking poles squelched down into the fresh, muddy UTV tracks, I gave a quizzical look to my cousins. Moments later, a side-by-side came ripping up the trail behind us with two hunters hauling a dead bull. We congratulated them, and they regaled us with their hunt.

“We were just driving around when all of the sudden a whole herd took off in front of us, I threw the gun out the front window and ‘BOOM’, dropped this guy on the run!”

I’m sure my face said a whole lot more than I could muster in that moment, because after that, they quickly said goodbye, good luck, and drove off. Aside from shooting out of a vehicle being illegal in Colorado, it’s just unethical and lacks any respect for wild bull elk, which is a critter that I hold in high regard.

As we continued down the trail shaking our heads in disbelief, three more side-by-sides ripped past us. Our boots became caked in mud as we walked out of the basin, contemplating our next move. That afternoon, we loaded our truck to find a new area. To our dismay, we counted more than 50 side-by-sides either out and about, or parked near tents or campers along the main road through our unit. Every trail was rutted up, and the national forest had seemingly become a UTV mudders’ paradise, except these mudders were pursuing elk.

We also encountered certain trails that were closed only to certain motor vehicles, with a width restriction in place. In essence, any vehicle under 50” was allowed through, and any wider would be stopped by two large metal poles on each side of the path. Unsurprisingly, the poles were bent out to high hell, allowing vehicles much wider than 50” to baja their way through the forest.

By the end of the third day of our hunt, we were disheartened. We had been in elk every day, and able to evade the side-by-side pressure. At times we were even able to use it to our advantage. But we were not enjoying anything representative of a wilderness hunt. The lack of respect for the place and the animals that lived there really started to eat at me.

Read Next: Roadless Areas Provide 10 Times Better Elk Hunting and Support the Best Trout Streams in the West, Study Shows

As someone who grew up in the most hunter-dense state in the United States, I’ve experienced a lot of folks taking shortcuts and not wanting to put in the physical effort to go hunting “the old fashioned way.” However, driving their four-wheelers to their stands on private land is a whole lot different than ripping side-by-sides for miles on supposedly closed national forest roads in pursuit of an elk near the trail.

What draws Eastern hunters like me to the West and to elk hunting is the wilderness. The untouched, unkempt backcountry where humans don’t frequent. Where you can walk up on blue grouse and where the creeks are devoid of cairns and boot prints, where you can witness animals in their natural habitat and their natural state. We dream about silence that almost hurts your ears, the yellow aspen leaves trickling down in a slight breeze, and the snow-capped peaks looming on the horizon. Most of us want our Western hunt to be wild and we want elk hunting to be hard.

Elk deserve to be hunted by woodsmen who read the terrain, watch their wind and thermals, and stalk carefully into range. Elk should not be relegated to some trailside target practice.

I look forward to, train for, and dream about my annual elk hunt for 11 months and 3 weeks every year. Maybe I’ve over-romanticized the beauty and wildness of elk hunting in my mind. But I also know I’m not alone.

There are many of us who welcome the hardships that come with a lack of sleep, long miles, steep climbs, and heavy packs. After all, that’s what makes filling an elk tag so rewarding.

So take the perspective of one Eastern elk hunter for what it’s worth. We should be prioritizing enforcement of off-road rules in our national forests and not letting the off-roaders run amok. We should not be cutting new roads into the little remaining backcountry that we have left, simply because we can. And we need to refocus on what elk hunting, real elk hunting, is supposed to be about.

#4040. Anxiety Is Often a Symptom, Not a Diagnosis

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Why did people come to see me in my psychiatric practice? Yes, bad things happen. Divorce, sickness, etc. But most people to whom these things happen do not seek psychiatric help. When people came for help it was because they felt bad. My soon-to-be patients found themselves nervous, overwhelmed, sad, irritable, or in some similar state. Psychiatrically speaking, they were either anxious or depressed.

Here I’ll focus on anxiety as it is such a common occurrence. Our goal here is not to make a diagnosis for anyone, but rather to construct a map to guide you towards important areas of consideration. This is the same map used by psychiatrists. Keep in mind that I’ll be making generalizations that apply to most people. There are always exceptions, which is why a mental health professional is key in this process.

There are two other things to keep in mind: one is that being upset about difficult things is not a psychiatric issue. It only becomes so if it persists beyond normal expectations and makes your life unusually difficult. Second is that any of the descriptions below is infrequently a prelude to severe mental illness. Again, this is why having a mental health professional on your side is so important (and will be another post).

So, let’s begin with a definition of anxiety. Anxiety is the response of a mature brain to a perceived threat, but one that is not clearly defined. I say “mature brain” as a matter of brain development past childhood. And “not clearly defined” is when the specific threat is not obvious. When it is, we experience fear.

Children may have anxiety (despite their non-matured brains), but it is much less common than in adults. Children’s response to perceived threat is usually emotional outbursts. This is also the case in people with significant developmental disabilities or brain injuries.

Anxiety is experienced in one of two patterns: generalized anxiety or panic. In brief, generalized anxiety is worry, sweaty palms, and tight muscles; while panic is sudden attacks of multiple bodily symptoms such as palpitations, dizziness, chest pain, numbness, and shortness of breath along with feelings of dread.

The important thing to know about anxiety in psychiatry is that it is ubiquitous. All psychiatric disorders can, and often do, bring anxiety with them. Generalized anxiety and panic may exist on their own and then become a diagnosis of an anxiety disorder, but more commonly they are part of another diagnosis. The exact diagnosis depends on the person’s age, what other symptoms they have, and what is happening in their life. (I have left out medical reasons; aside from hyperthyroidism, medical problems are rarely the explanation for anxiety).

I am not a child psychiatrist, and this issue is big enough for an article on its own. But we can summarize a few points. Anxiety is less common in children than in teens and adults. But it still has a significant prevalence, which is sadly growing. Early anxiety, such as separation anxiety and phobias (e.g. fear of the dark, dogs, bugs), is not uncommon in children, especially preschoolers, and these can be part of development and usually pass with time.

This is contrasted with temperamental anxiety, a part of their personality, which also begins in early childhood. This is more characterized by behavioral inhibition, avoidance of new things, general nervousness, and social anxiety. These are all much stronger predictors of anxiety later in life and this temperament is usually lifelong. Panic disorder, though, is rarely seen in childhood.

It is also important to consider environmental issues, such as family discord, problems in school, such as bullying, and related issues. Family history of anxiety or other psychiatric issues can also play a role.

School-age children with some of the problems listed above, such as temperament, along with OCD and tantrums, are even stronger predictors of other types of psychiatric problems later in life.

Good predictors of problems later in life are important, but do not solve problems at hand. Irrespective of what they may predict, a qualified clinician should evaluate and treat whatever is present. Some pediatricians have had strong training in this, but many do not. Usually, a child psychiatrist or psychologist is a better approach.

Anxiety in teens presents the same questions: age of onset, environmental life issues, and type of symptoms. I will just mention a few pathways. One is with new-onset panic attacks. This usually suggests panic disorder, often with accompanying agoraphobia. This includes many different fears such as fear of open spaces, closed spaces, distance from home, crowds, etc. These all represent a fear of being away from safety should a panic attack occur, as panic produces the sense that you are very ill.

The other pathway, which may present the same way, is into depression. In teens, as with younger children, expect schoolwork and social life to change for the worse when depression rears its head. This will be what stands out to parents and teachers, so a specialist must ask the right questions to find out about depression.

My own view, which is not the official psychiatric view currently, is that panic disorder is a prelude to depression. Current data finds that over half of panic patients will develop depression. I believe the actual number is higher.

Lastly, adolescence is when severe mental illness usually begins to show. This is the exception not the rule, but a good clinician will know when to investigate further.

Anxiety and depression are in many ways two sides of the same coin. They occur together, are inherited together, and are treated in very similar or identical ways. Patients with panic attacks virtually always become depressed in my experience.

This is a good bridge into adulthood, with the 20s being a crossover where patterns from adolescence or patterns from adulthood can occur. Moving into adulthood and middle life, the 30s through 50s, new anxiety is associated with depression unless it is a symptom of some significant life stressor.

Such stressors are sadly common in this period of life and include divorce, unemployment, financial problems, new medical problems, and death of parents. These could be extremely stressful times emerging from normal feelings of loss to significant anxiety and often, depression.

Lastly, moving into the 50s, 60s, and 70s, new anxiety is still associated with depression, but there is an important caveat. Namely, this depression is biologically not like the depressions occurring in all the younger periods of life. It does not include the family history of depression that is more common in these other patients. The question for psychiatrists then is, what is this late-life depression if it is not like earlier depressions?

Part of the answer is that it is the beginning of dementia. The risk is roughly 25-35% of these patients will develop dementia, but this varies. The higher risk is when the first depression is later in life (age 75 is higher risk than 55), and other dementia risk factors are present.

So, if you or someone you care about develops anxiety, the first thing to do is not to search the internet for an article on anxiety disorders. As we have discussed, look at the age of onset of the anxiety, the type of anxiety, what’s happening in the person’s life, and other potentially important symptoms (especially depression or cognitive symptoms in older people). Anxiety is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Understanding the full context of the anxiety will lead you to a better idea of what is happening to you or someone you care about and thus more helpful treatment.

#2170. Why MAHA Must Keep Conservative Roots

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For over two years now, the MAHA movement led by Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. has attracted some unlikely bedfellows. Everyone—from religious homeschool moms to more hippy food-as-medicine advocates—has filled rooms to try to find solutions to America’s chronic disease epidemic. But sometimes the tent grows so big that it invites people whose ideas are completely at odds with the movement’s original priorities.

The goal of healthier Americans should be fairly bipartisan, but the means to achieve it, and the underlying philosophies that drive it, can differ. Technocrats and transhumanists offer interesting solutions to the mechanisms of our dysfunctional healthcare system, such as updated systems for patient records and data collection. However, true and lasting reform requires solutions rooted in more conservative moral worldviews that maintain the natural order of God’s design.

Nowhere were the divergent approaches to making America healthy again more apparent than at last month’s MAHA Summit in Washington, D.C.

Bryan Johnson, the man doing his best to “not die,” headlined the Summit, touting his new religion, which claims the “body is God.” His followers embrace an “opening ritual” (like prayer, communion, or the sign of the cross), and a “mantra” (like the Our Father).

They even have a path to repentance. Rather than confession and forgiveness from God, there is “a part where people apologize to their body for something they’ve done that has inflicted harm upon themselves,” Johnson explains. Apparently, they absolve themselves of their own sins.

Rather than accept a view of human nature rooted in the theological principles of the Judeo-Christian tradition, Johnson wants to “reimagine human nature.” He discussed revolutionizing the way humans age and extending man beyond his “biological limit.”

His fellow panelists, Alex Colville and Max Marchione, agreed that the foundations of good health—diet, sleep, and exercise—can go only so far. Genomic medicine, including gene-editing technology, must be embraced.

One need not be a devout Christian to understand the danger of Johnson’s rhetoric. We are already seeing discussions of selective gene editing and “made-to-order” babies.

And while Johnson occupies the extreme end of the longevity movement, he does represent a concerning faction of the MAHA zeitgeist that believes humans can overcome any limitations placed on them—and will go to extraordinary lengths to do so.

This progressive narrative is in direct opposition to the more organic, grassroots uprising of those who believe that medical sovereignty, healthy soil and food, and meaningful human relationships are the fundamentals for human flourishing; those who believe that freedom is not found by removing all limitations, but through embracing struggle, pain, and even death to grow, change, and become the image bearers of Christ. Again, even if one does not embrace Christianity, a human-centered approach requires humility, a virtue often lacking in the technocrat’s vocabulary. Scientific progress can be good, but it should not be fueled by hubris or come at the expense of rejecting the natural order.

Thankfully, not all at the Summit embraced Johnson’s new, dystopian religion.

Michael Boes, the first chief MAHA officer of Steak ’n Shake, echoed the cries of those who embrace the real world, with all its obstacles, rather than one built on artificial intelligence and gene manipulation.

For Boes, the solution is much simpler. Eating real food and returning to the fast food of our grandparents’ generation—cooking fries in beef tallow and using buns without seed oil—can help restore vibrancy and order to the body.

For others, like the martial artist Michael Chandler, it’s about performing well, having a good attitude, and setting an example for his family. “My gratification is my children,” he said on a panel with MAHA-focused athletes.

Chandler also commented on the failures of striving for perfection, saying, “If we strive for perfect, we will never reach it … if perfection is the goal, you’re always going to be let down,” and intimated that doing so can lead to mental health disorders. He encouraged the audience to “enjoy themselves like a human being, sometimes.”

There is no such thing as moral neutrality. Any position or policy, whether it prioritizes American farmers or creates genomic medicine, has an end in mind, and ultimately a why that motivates it.

The MAHA movement has the potential to return to what is good, true, and beautiful, a harkening back to the food and medicine of our forefathers. Or it can lead to transgression, not merely progression, of the limits that God has placed upon human biology.

If conservatives fail to remember the why of MAHA, it will soon be co-opted by dystopian mad scientists who eat sandy green mush and clone themselves.

The future of the MAHA movement doesn’t have to be binary. Technological advancement and AI can drive meaningful change in how Americans reclaim medical sovereignty and access their own health data. But it must not abandon its roots in the fundamental truths of human nature.

One can and should pursue a healthy life, but death will never be optional. MAHA is about redeeming the time we’re given, not trying to outlive our expiration date.

#4047. 3 Core Horror Genres for Helping Find What to Watch

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Regular readers of this blog know I love horror films. However, I often feel burdened by having to figure out what to watch based on convoluted descriptions and classifications. Perusing streaming sites is a nightmare with respect to descriptions not being specific enough or getting my head around super-specific categorisations, like Lovecraftian-body horror, found-footage folk horror, or some six-way hybrid. Sometimes that kind of thing can be off-putting, especially if you just want to watch a run-of-the-mill monster movie. So, here’s a three-component genre framework discussing what makes horrors scary, which might help make searching less daunting.

Despite initial disbelief in the existence of a monster (typically for tension-building purposes), common within these films is that the monster is eventually presented to viewers and is both observable and scary. Sometimes they possess human-like features (though definitely not human), like vampires, mummies, werewolves, and lagoon creatures—as in most classics. Alternatively, the monsters can be "special" animals, like "Jaws," "Cujo," and the spiders in "Arachnophobia." Other times, the monsters are just people who do bad things (e.g., "Silence of the Lambs," "Misery," and "The Texas Chainsaw Massacre"). What they all share is that a corporeal antagonist is "out to get you."

Regardless of whether they’re a person, animal, or a classic "monster," they all reflect something "abnormal" that elicits some form of fear, nervousness, or anxiety. Such abnormality can be implicit or explicit, the latter exemplified by some monsters having a scary appearance. Design goes a long way—the first time I saw Freddy Krueger as a kid, I couldn’t sleep for days. I even look at "Terrifier"’s Art the Clown, as an adult, and get the heebie-jeebies.

Implicitly speaking, what about monsters that don’t "appear" scary? Often the lack of a physically scary appearance is used to subvert expectations. A classic example is Jack Nicholson’s character in "The Shining," chasing his family through a hotel. Instead of drastically changing his appearance, the perceived decomposition of his "sanity" helps elicit fear in audiences.

With that, some people don’t get scared by "monsters" because once they see, observe, and, potentially, make predictions about them, then they cease to be scary. Depending on the story’s build, observation of the "monster" after its reveal may yield the opposite of the desired effect. For example, in "Predator," Dutch classically states: "If it bleeds, we can kill it."

One interesting exception to this point about "observation" is "It Follows," which focuses on a "cursed" character being pursued by a slowly moving shape-shifting entity for its duration, not unlike the "Immortal Snail" dilemma. In its exception (i.e., shape-shifting), it subverts the notion of what’s in pursuit (similar to "Drag Me to Hell" and "The Ring"); thus, representing a genre overlap of sorts, in that it really pushes this "out to get ya" trope, but the "monster" does not conform to the limitations above. Instead, arguably, it plays more by the rules of the paranormal genre.

The paranormal genre doesn’t depend on a visually presented villain (e.g., "Poltergeist"); rather, it works on atmosphere (e.g., "The Others"), tension (e.g., "Hereditary"), and compelling world-building (e.g., "Evil Dead II"), where events conform to specific rules with a clear internal logic (e.g., "Resolution"). What you don’t see can scare you as much, if not more than what you can. So, for a paranormal horror to work, it relies crucially on good storytelling.

Despite this sub-genre potentially being the hardest to master in terms of filmmaking, the reasoning behind its longevity is simple. People tend to fear the unknown and unexplainable. As I discuss in terms of critical thinking, we like understandable information and certainty. If we don’t have that, we get confused, anxious, even frightened. We do our best to avoid that or fill in the blanks ourselves (explaining away the uncertainty with potential, though typically unevidenced, rationales). We see this in ghost stories all the time.

When items go missing or start moving on their own, characters try to explain away the fear until they can no longer ignore the inevitable. Someone always jumps to the conclusion that it’s ghosts, while another uses reason to refute such a claim. As it’s a ghost story, the former is generally right, but this doubt facilitates the tension building. Of course, by the time agreement is reached, it’s too late. Examples are many and, yes, it is pretty formulaic, even cliché; but, when done right—with tension, atmosphere, and all of the above—it can do very well to scare the hell out of us.

Though similar to the paranormal genre in that it focuses on uncertainty and settings outside character experience, the "unfamiliar environment" genre doesn’t require the paranormal to be scary. Instead, the horror focuses on mystery and/or social interactions as the driver(s) of tension and suspense. Consider many zombie films, like "Dawn of the Dead." Though zombies are a key feature of the film, akin to the monster movie genre (again indicating potential for useful genre overlap), they are not the focus of the narrative; rather, the focus is the interpersonal relationships among the characters cooked up in a deserted shopping mall, surviving the apocalypse. The zombies simply amount to a plot device. The "Resident Evil" series represents another good example, as does another video game-based horror, "Silent Hill," which at its core is focused on a strange town and its troubled history, not necessarily the bizarre monsters that inhabit its dark corners.

In monster and paranormal movies, characters rely on what they know to get through the ordeal (e.g., experience and any survival skills they may have, be it running, moving house, learning history for a solution, etc.). But in unfamiliar-environment films, the characters are in unknown territory. Often, things don’t work the same way they do outside—be it in terms of physics or logic (e.g., "Cube" and "Backrooms") or sociocultural interaction (e.g., "Midsommar" and "The Wickerman"). In such films, it's often the case that poor decisions are made, not because of disastrously bad thinking (e.g., splitting up the group in a monster movie), but typically because the characters’ "moves" are not facilitated or do not conform to the rules of their distinct setting. Tension is created by the fact that there’s nothing the characters can do to escape unless they somehow figure out the strange logic and workings of the unfamiliar environment. Often, they don’t even know they need to escape before it’s too late.

I wouldn’t claim that this framework will fit every horror movie, but it certainly works on those I’ve applied (e.g., classics and favourites). See Figure 1 for an example. With that, the purpose is not perfection—it’s about more easily identifying types of horrors without having to be too broad or too specific. Indeed, some films overlap these "genres," be it in the case of fitting into two or perhaps even all three—and I think that’s a strength. If a film can successfully integrate monsters into a paranormal backdrop with a unique setting or population, that just enhances the types of scares possible. Moreover, these three overarching types don’t exclude key features of more niche sub-genres. For example, psychological thrillers can fit in well with any three here. Indeed, I find the best horrors, regardless of category, are those that really exploit psychological elements. It’s such specific features that help the scares succeed—genres are a preference, not what makes it good; rather, it’s the execution of storytelling and filmmaking.
