#2906. Te Araroa blog: in which snoop dog does not die

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Great news everyone, Snoop Dogg did not, in fact, die! This would make sense to you if you were in the kitchen on day 3 of walking Te Araroa and someone announced he died in the three days your phone had been on airplane mode. You’d also have had the relief (mostly for Martha Stewart) of finding out mere moments later that that was fake news.

If that’s not an indication of what it’s like to walk on an expansive-lacking-in-landmarks beach for three days , I don’t know what is.

Section one of Te Araroa is a slog, a hard-packed sand beach (if you’re lucky) that exposes every single tiny part of your shoe that rubs.

Day one was the shortest, only 12.5 km on the map. In the lead up to my start day Fraser and I had spent a really lovely couple of nights in Kerikeri sleeping in an orange grove and walking to little beaches and viewpoints. We fed kunekune (a type of pig) and I played knuckle bones for the first time. I also, of course ran my last minute errands including buying gas, a lighter and some tortillas. My pack was feeling very large and heavy, and even more so when we picked up another walker and he was carrying a bag that looked to be about half the size of mine.

We drove up the last little bit of Northland to Cape Reinga, my nerves and excitement growing. We had been here about five years ago on a family holiday, back when I had no idea about Te Araroa. It was sort of surreal to be doing the drive again, but this time with a very different goal. We took our obligatory start photos at the northern terminus of Te Araroa, and then made our way back up the path to the actual trailhead. This was the hardest part of the day for me, saying bye to Fraser. He stood on the ridge and watched me walk away for the first kilometre or so, all the while I was crying and trying not to fall down the steps. It was all so beautiful and big and overwhelming.

After a few hours I arrived at the twilight beach camp, where the people who would become my constant companions for the next 85 mind-numbing beach kilometres gathered for the first time. We admired one person’s homemade tent, talked about the food we had brought, and ate our dinners. By the end of the night I was feeling a whole mix of things, sad to be away from Fraser and home, daunted by the next 85km of beach walking (and then the other almost 3000km), excited and giddy at the fact that after a year of planning this was it, it was all real. For the next few days I had two goals:

Day two was the first of the big days, with 5km up and over a hill followed by 23km on 90 mile (actually not 90 miles long) beach. The first three quarters went well, I walked quite a bit of it alone after excitingly taking off at my “normal” walking pace. This was a slight mistake as by the time I got to camp my legs were getting very sore and I was approaching wrecked. I knew that the camp this night, at Maunganui Bluff, had cold showers and I was desperate to get there and have a shower before it got chilly for the night.

I managed to snag what I reckon was the best campsite, tucked behind a harakeke (flax) bush to block the wind. I showered and dinner was a pasta feast rehydrated in the bag from night one’s rice and beans. I could not wait for bed!

Despite being tired I didn’t manage to fall asleep until a little later than everyone else, and then was woken in the night by a strange noise. I lay there listening, wondering if maybe a possum had turned up to ransack our food bags. But then I suddenly realized that hey! I knew that sound! That’s the sound of a horse grazing! As quietly as I could I found my headtorch and poked my head out the fly of my tent and there they were, three or four horses just a couple of metres from my tent. It was magical.

Day three was our second of three big days of beach walking and our first day entirely comprised of beach. I wasn’t hungry this morning but choked down a meal replacement shake and then a couple hours later a banana chocolate bumper bar. We left shortly after high tide to make sure we’d be walking in the hard sand all day instead of the dunes. The first 15 or so kilometres sort of disappeared under our feet and then we dragged ourselves through the next 2km to a lunch spot.

Lunch on the beach is so important, even though we were managing to keep our feet dry on the stream crossings, airing out your feet for a while makes a big difference to your blister count. We stopped beside a big truck towing a digger with a basket that looked like a sieve. One of my hiking-mates had a chat with the driver who explained that that’s how they harvest mussel seed – I have to admit that I have no clue what that really means but he seemed to think it was very cool.

Lunch on the beach is also when you start to get in your head. You think “okay I’ve done half the day’s walk so I’ve got this” but then you also think “oh god I’ve only done half the day’s walk! I can’t do this!” I think this is when having walking companions really helps. I can’t imagine what it would have felt like to walk this section alone, the expanse of the beach stretching out in front of me, the constant crashing of waves and the occasional sudden appearance of a car or truck zooming past. As it was it was mind numbing, but also meditative and the sort of experience that makes you feel like you’re really doing something once in a lifetime.

Getting to camp on day 3 was a massive relief. The camp was a much more established, privately owned site. The horses were grazing all around the camp as we hobbled up a gravel road off the beach to the site. The first thing we all did was buy a bag of chips each ($5 each but we would have paid more) and cans of fizzy drinks. There was a kitchen, gas stoves, dishes, cutlery and all the luxuries that you normally only get in town. Most importantly there were hot showers! In private rooms! A significant upgrade from the previous night. I’m already developing a real fondness for the minty smell of my castille soap because it means a warmer, more comfortable sleep.

I slept terribly this night, waking up every couple of hours. Even though I had gone to bed feeling really happy I woke up anxious at around 1am and didn’t fall back asleep until about 3:30am. This whole thing is definitely a journey in accepting the unknown and in being daunted but continuing on anyway. I also drafted the first half of this blog when I gave up on falling back asleep quickly so if you find typos in it, kindly ignore them.

“Somebody get me the HELL off this beach”

That was the sentiment on day four. We had all had enough of the beach, enough of the blisters and the same terrain for 30km a day. We also knew that ahead of us was a campground with showers, laundry, a shop and nice, flat tent sites.

Day four was a mental game. Walk 17km to Ngapae, a campground where we could stay if we wanted. We made it there by 1:30pm and stopped for a long lunch. There’s normally an icecream truck here, but it’s currently broken so the owner sold us fizzy drinks and gave us cushions to lie around on in the shade. She brought out a sliced up orange and banana and showed us where we could fill our water. It felt amazing to lie down. I briefly considered staying here but could not fathom having to get up the next morning and do more beach walking. So after lunch we packed up again and set off for our final 14km.

The second landmark of the day was almost at the end of the beach: the 100km mark. This milestone dangled in front of me like a carrot for the whole afternoon as we trudged down the beach. This end of the beach was much busier so there were more things to look at and more people to chat with. We met so many nice dogs!

By about 6pm we were approaching Ahipara, and I looked at my phone and shouted “oh! A hundred!!”

We took our milestone photos and continued on to the end of the beach where we were able to mercifully get a lift to the holiday park’s reception building. I was in a foul mood by the time I got my tent up but actually ended up sleeping really well and felt a lot better in the morning.

My first zero! I got a lift to resupply in Kaitaia (I bought way too much food) and then to my hostel where I had booked a private room. It felt so weird to be alone but also a little like a relief.

I took full advantage of my space. I pulled everything out of my pack, broke down all my food purchases, ate a real meal that I cooked and was given a glass of wine from some fellow hikers staying at the backpackers. They told me that they had been there for days as they all got injured while walking the beach, which freaked me out and made me worried that maybe I was injured and didn’t realize it yet.

A two hour phone call with Fraser helped me feel less paranoid about that, and I then crawled into bed for another mediocre sleep.

Next stop, Northland Forests. I’m hoping to complete these in four days but have heard that it is a slog up through the Raetea forest so may have to extend it to five. Stay tuned!

Before I left, Fraser added a whole bunch of albums to my phone and i purposely didn’t look at what he added. Each section I plan to pick a song of the section: this time around it’s Night Swimming by REM. This is because Fraser knows REM-induced nostalgia is important to me. I listened to it while unable to sleep at 3 am and had a little cry, it was good and healthy. (Honourable mention is the whole entire Grouper album called dragging a dead deer up a hill which got me to sleep that night).

#3955. This Vitamin Reduces Depression Symptoms By 50%

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Taking a high dose of vitamin B6 for a month reduced depression symptoms in people already experiencing them.

The vitamin helps the brain produce chemical messengers involved in mood regulation.

The findings suggest a potential role for B6 in treating depression, although larger clinical trials are needed.

Almost 270 adults, mostly young women, took part in the research.

They were randomly assigned to take either 100 mg of vitamin B6, vitamin B12 or a placebo each day for around a month.

Among almost 60 people with at least mild depression symptoms at the start, those taking B6 improved more than those receiving B12 or a placebo.

Average depression scores fell by about 54 percent in the symptomatic B6 group, compared with 24 percent in the placebo group.

The benefit remained after accounting for differences in how severe their symptoms had been at the start.

The findings come from a new analysis of data collected in the same research programme that previously reported reduced anxiety after B6 supplementation.

Vitamin B6 supports several processes involved in regulating mood.

Dr David Field, the study’s first author, said:

“Vitamin B6 plays a crucial role in the production of several chemical messengers in the brain, including serotonin and GABA, both of which are central to mood regulation.”

GABA helps restrain nerve cell activity, contributing to the balance between excitation and inhibition in the brain.

Increasing B6 availability could boost their production, potentially explaining the improvement in depression symptoms.

Inflammation may also play a role, potentially affecting both depression and how the body uses B6.

Vitamin B6 occurs naturally in foods including poultry, fish, chickpeas, bananas and fortified cereals.

However, the trial tested a high supplement dose, rather than a change in everyday food intake.

High doses of B6 can cause peripheral nerve damage, so the researchers say supplementation at these doses requires medical supervision.

Larger trials now need to establish the most effective dose, monitor safety and determine whether B6 could help alongside existing depression treatments.

The study was published in the journal Depression and Anxiety (Field et al., 2026).

#3303. Post-Race Weight Gain: Why You’re Heavier After Finishing a Race (And Why It’s a Good Thing)

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

You wake up feeling sore and puffy the day after a race, step on the scale, and confirm what you intuitively feel: you’re a few pounds above your pre-race weight.

But why? Logically, a massive calorie burn (up to 10,000+ calories for an Ironman!) should tip the scale the other way, so something must be wrong.

Your first impulse may be to cut calories or jump right back into training. Please don’t! What you’re seeing is your body’s normal physiological response as it goes into full recovery mode.

Post-race weight gain isn’t the same as the off-season weight drift many athletes experience. That weight tends to build over months and is a separate conversation about energy balance. The jump you see on the scale in the first 24 to 72 hours after the race is mostly water. Specifically, your body is hard at work replenishing glycogen, rebuilding plasma volume, and retaining water and sodium as part of its hormonal stress-and-repair response. It’s most noticeable after a half or full iron-distance triathlon, where fueling, fluid intake, and stress load are highest.

Let’s dig into what’s going on, and why this temporary bump is actually a good sign.

Long-course races drain your muscle glycogen. As long as you eat plenty of carbs, your body starts refilling those stores within the first few hours after you finish. But the longer the race, the more muscle damage, which slows the process.

After a long-course race, full restoration can take several days, rather than the roughly 24 hours we see after shorter events. Here’s the kicker: each gram of newly synthesized glycogen is stored with approximately three grams of water. So, replenishing 400–500 grams of muscle glycogen adds about 3.5–4.5 pounds, mostly from water, packed in your muscles. In other words, much of the bump you see on the scale in the first few days is restored fuel.

Glycogen isn’t the only reason the scale climbs. It’s normal to finish a long race dehydrated, since even a solid race hydration strategy can’t fully offset hours of sweat losses. In the 24 to 72 hours after the race, fluid-conserving hormones (aldosterone and vasopressin) remain elevated, signaling your body to hold onto fluid to restore plasma volume and electrolyte balance. The extra water weight won’t stick around long, so don’t cut back on fluids or salt to bring the number down. Your body needs both right now.

Racing 70.3 or 140.6 miles puts your body under more stress than a typical training day. During and after the race, cortisol, your primary stress hormone, spikes, signaling your body to retain water. Prolonged physical exertion causes muscle microdamage and inflammation.

The longer, hotter, and tougher the race, the longer recovery may take. As your body begins repairs, fluid moves into damaged tissues, which can explain your tight, swollen legs. You’ll notice improvement when your kidneys start clearing the excess fluid, leading to more frequent bathroom visits. A little puffiness for a couple of days post-race means the repair process is underway.

As training volume comes down after a race, you might expect your appetite to follow. And, for the first several hours, it often does, since hard effort can temporarily suppress hunger. But over the next day or two, it returns with a vengeance. That’s because a slower-moving hormonal system (primarily ghrelin and leptin) signals your brain to respond to the energy deficit you built up on race day.

You may feel hungrier for several days, often craving energy-dense, salty, carb-rich foods (think fries, burgers, and pizza). And all that yummy food, along with the water and sodium that come with it, adds to the scale number too. It’s not a lack of willpower or a total loss of control; it’s your body refueling.

Following an endurance race, some swelling is expected, but I’d be remiss not to flag a few scenarios that, although rare, require medical attention.

If one leg swells, especially with calf pain, warmth, or redness, it may indicate a blood clot. Swelling with shortness of breath or chest pain is an emergency; seek immediate attention.

Dark cola-colored urine with severe muscle pain or weakness could signal rhabdomyolysis: a condition involving severe muscle breakdown that can lead to kidney damage.

And if you finish the race heavier than you started and experience confusion, headache, or nausea, it could be hyponatremia (low blood sodium); seek medical help right away.

For most athletes, a few extra pounds in the days after a long-distance race signals a healthy recovery. Still, it can be tempting, especially if you keep close tabs on your weight, to see the number go up and immediately want to cut back or restrict.

Resist the urge. Your body needs carbohydrates and protein to rebuild glycogen and repair tissue; your fluid and electrolyte balance needs restoring, and your immune system needs adequate energy while under duress. Restricting food and fluids now won’t get your weight back on track. It will delay glycogen restoration, prolong fatigue and soreness, and potentially increase your risk of illness.

As your fluid levels normalize and inflammation subsides, most of the extra weight comes off naturally, typically within three to seven days. You’ve demanded a lot from your body in training and on race day, so give it what it needs: plenty of nutrient-rich food, adequate hydration, rest, and quality sleep. Then step back and trust your body and the process.

#624. Why Do We Have Necks? Scientists Uncover a 380-Million-Year-Old Evolutionary Twist

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

The first necks may owe more to the beginnings of walking than to looking around, according to models showing how little our early ancestors could move their heads.

In fish, the shoulders attach directly to the skull, so looking left or right means turning the whole body. Some of the animals making the transition from water to land about 380 million years ago began to develop a separate neck. Yet new research suggests that this change did not give them much more freedom to move their heads.

Models combining new fossil evidence with mechanical tests on living fish indicate that these early necks remained stiff, challenging the long-standing idea that they evolved to let the head move more freely. The international study, which included Tom Stewart, an assistant professor of biology at Penn State, was published October 14 in Nature.

Fossils from the Devonian Period, about 420 million to 360 million years ago, capture animals with a mixture of aquatic traits and features associated with life on land. Some had robust fins that might have supported their weight, allowing them to prop themselves up or push along the bottom in shallow water. Distinct necks also began appearing in these transitional fish.

“This is a really interesting time in Earth’s history where some of the first forests are forming, and there’s a really radical reorganization of the Earth,” Stewart said. “So, understanding exactly when our lineage made that transition from water to land, how it was achieved, and which anatomical systems were changing is an important part of that story. What’s cool is that many of the changes that were occurring in the skeleton at this time, about 380 million years ago, are still reflected in our own bodies today.”

One of those transitional fish was Tiktaalik, whose fossils come from Ellesmere Island in Nunavut, Canada, in the northern Arctic.

“The materials have been really important because Tiktaalik is one of the closest relatives to limbed vertebrates,” Stewart said. “It helps us to reconstruct a lot of intermediary steps in the transition to life on land, such as the move from suction feeding in water to biting on land or from swimming to walking.”

For the ancestors of four-limbed animals, known as tetrapods, the ability to look down or from side to side could have helped them find food and avoid predators in shallow water or on land. Scientists reasoned that these environments might make independent head movement more useful than it was in deeper water.

“Tiktaalik is one of these fossil fish, and it was first described in 2006,” Stewart said. “It has been reasonably assumed for decades that this separation of head and shoulders must have allowed the head to move more freely, so the hypothesis that neck mobility was an evolutionary driver for this change in the tetrapod body plan has prevailed, at least until now.”

Testing that explanation required reconstructing a backbone unlike those of most vertebrates alive today. Early tetrapods and their close relatives had a long rod of tissue called the notochord running through the entire vertebral column. Their vertebrae were small and arranged around this large core of soft tissue, which behaved differently from the joints between vertebrae typical of modern animals.

“The fish that were undergoing these changes had a vertebral column that was largely made up of soft tissue, which, unlike bone, is rarely preserved in the fossil record,” Stewart said. “In these cases, we can look to modern animals to serve as analogs for the past, like how living birds can teach us about now extinct dinosaurs.”

Backbones built around a notochord are rare today, although they were common throughout the history of life on Earth. Sturgeon, distantly related fish sometimes called living fossils because of their primitive features, retain this kind of backbone. That made them a useful way to investigate the mechanical properties missing from the fossils.

“We want to understand how these early tetrapods were living and how that is reflected in changes to their bodies,” Stewart said. “Trying to reconstruct where an animal lived, how it moved, what its behavior was, is a really difficult thing. The cool part of this story is that it combines data from fossils and studies of living animals in a mechanical, functional perspective to reconstruct what the changes we see in the animal’s anatomy mean for their behavior.”

The researchers measured the stiffness of modern sturgeon notochords, then applied those material properties to models of the necks of early tetrapods and transitional relatives such as Tiktaalik. The models allowed them to test whether separating the shoulders from the skull would have freed the head to move despite the backbone’s structure.

“I undertook this study to find out how big of a difference this change in anatomy made for these animals,” said Dana Korneisel, a postdoctoral researcher at Uppsala University in Sweden and first author of the paper. “The vertebral columns of sturgeon are built a lot like those of early tetrapods, but they didn’t seem terribly stiff. So, after doing the modeling, the lack of significant head mobility for almost all of these animals was a huge surprise.”

The estimates considered only the backbone. Muscles, skin, and, in many of these animals, bones embedded in the skin could have restricted movement further, meaning the living animals may have had even less neck mobility than the models suggest.

“Head elevation in these animals was no more than what you would expect in fish and, sometimes, even less,” Korneisel said. “We found that the kind of head twisting that we can do when we shake our heads was not feasible in any of these animals.”

With little extra head movement available, the researchers suggest that a distinct neck likely arose instead from anatomical changes associated with using the front limbs for locomotion. Greater flexibility appears to have been a later benefit, rather than the advantage that initially drove the separation of head and shoulders.

“We know that tetrapods did eventually gain higher neck mobility,” Korneisel said. “Clearly it was advantageous eventually, but it was not an advantage available to these early tetrapods yet because of the limitations of their anatomy.”

#1031. What Causes Bad Breath?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Bad breath is embarrassing enough. Not realizing you have it is worse. The cupped-hand breath check and a trusty pack of mints aren’t exactly foolproof—and the last thing you want is your breath to sabotage an important meeting or a first date.

Here’s the annoying part: You might have your oral hygiene routine fully dialed in and still deal with some funk. “You can have beautiful teeth, brush religiously, and still have breath that isn’t doing you any favors,” says Arthur Glosman, DDS, a restorative and cosmetic dentist in Beverly Hills, California. So what gives?

Ahead, experts explain what causes bad breath, how to get it under control, and when it’s time to call in a pro.

About 80 to 90 percent of bad breath (aka halitosis) originates in the mouth. The most common culprit? Excess bacteria. “Bacteria naturally exist throughout the oral cavity, but when they accumulate on the tongue, between teeth, or beneath the gumline, they produce volatile sulfur compounds responsible for odor,” says Graig Fischgrund, DMD, a periodontist at Apa Aesthetic in New York City. The back of the tongue—which many people don’t clean thoroughly—is prime real estate for buildup. According to Glosman, this area “is practically a five-star resort for odor-producing bacteria: warm, moist, and an endless buffet.” How appetizing.

Saliva—your mouth’s built-in cleaning crew—also helps determine how fresh or funky your breath smells. As Fischgrund explains, it helps regulate bacteria, neutralize acids, and wash away food debris. When saliva production drops, odor-causing bacteria can thrive. Mouth breathing, dehydration, smoking, and certain medications can all contribute to dry mouth. It’s also why morning breath is pretty universal (and perfectly normal): “Saliva production drops while we sleep, giving bacteria more opportunity to misbehave,” says Dr. Glosman.

Your diet can also leave its mark, though it’s usually temporary. Familiar offenders include coffee, alcohol, garlic, and onions. But if odor sticks around, it could point to a bigger problem, like gum disease or an untreated dental infection, says Fischgrund. Your brain uses this nutrient for memory, but its relationship with dementia risk is more complicated than “more is better”.

Moderate intake of choline, a nutrient found in eggs, fish and legumes, is linked to a lower risk of dementia.

Older adults consuming moderately high amounts had a 32 percent lower dementia risk than those consuming the least.

However, the highest intake was not associated with a clear benefit, suggesting that more is not necessarily better.

The research followed over 5,000 American adults, whose average age was almost 69, for around eight years.

Participants were initially free of dementia but around 500 developed the condition during follow-up.

After dividing participants into five groups according to choline intake, the researchers found a 32 percent lower dementia risk in the second-highest group than in the lowest, after accounting for other factors.

However, there was no statistically clear reduction in the highest-intake group.

The result therefore points towards a potentially beneficial range, rather than a steady fall in risk with every increase in consumption.

The researchers then combined their results with four previous studies to assess the broader pattern.

Together, these followed almost 138,000 people and recorded around 2,500 cases of dementia.

In the combined analysis, dementia risk fell as choline intake rose to approximately 465 milligrams per day, with no further reduction beyond that level.

Choline helps the brain produce acetylcholine, a chemical messenger involved in learning and memory.

It also supplies building blocks for the membranes surrounding brain cells, helping maintain their structure and communication.

These roles could help explain a link between choline and cognitive ageing, although they do not prove that consuming more prevents dementia.

The nutrient also participates in processes that regulate homocysteine, a substance linked to problems with blood vessels and cognitive function.

Eggs, meat, poultry and nuts were among the main sources of choline in the American participants’ diets.

People consuming more choline obtained a greater proportion of it from eggs.

Crucially, the analysis assessed choline from food and did not include intake from supplements.

The findings therefore cannot establish whether choline supplements would lower dementia risk.

The study was published in the Nutrition Journal (Chen et al., 2026).

Effective training can be pretty boring—and that's okay. You don't need a completely different workout every time you train.

Repeating key movements allows you to practice technique, build strength, and track progression, both experts say.

“If you're doing completely different workouts every week, you're not building strength,” says Bentley.

“Constantly changing everything makes it much harder to see whether you're actually getting stronger,” agrees Campus.

There is no magic number of reps that suddenly makes a workout effective, whether that's eight, 10, or 12.

“What matters more is that you are challenging your muscles and working with enough effort to create a stimulus,” says Campus. Your final few reps should feel difficult and bring you close to failure while still allowing you to maintain good technique.

Make sure your training also becomes more challenging over time, Bentley adds, by gradually increasing the weight, reps or training volume—otherwise known as progressive overload.

Doing random workouts can keep you active, but they'll usually deliver random results.

“A structured program gives your training purpose and allows for progressive overload,” Campus says.

In midlife, consistency and progression are far more valuable than constantly chasing the latest fitness trend, which may even increase your risk of injury, she adds.

Bentley recommends sticking with a program for at least six months to a year.

Particularly if fat loss is your goal, increasing your Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis—or NEAT—can help reduce body fat.

NEAT includes everyday movement such as housework, walking, carrying shopping, gardening, taking the stairs instead of the elevator, and even fidgeting.

Walking, in particular, is one of the “most underrated tools for fat loss,” says Bentley.

“It increases daily energy expenditure without adding a ton of fatigue or interfering with recovery.”

“You don't have to spend hours in the gym to increase your daily energy expenditure,” Campus adds. “Taking the stairs, playing with your children or simply breaking up long periods of sitting all count.”

Your training only works if your body has the opportunity to recover and adapt.

“More training isn't always better, especially when life is already demanding,” says Campus. “Prioritize sleep, rest days, nutrition and lighter sessions when needed.”

Bentley also recommends resting for at least two minutes between challenging sets so you can perform at your best.

Consistent habits over weeks and months matter far more than one meal or one day.

“A meal out, birthday cake, or a weekend treat isn't going to undo weeks of regular training and healthy eating habits,” Campus says. “The goal is balance, not perfection.”

Bentley agrees, urging women to “eat the pizza” and “enjoy dinner with your friends.” Have fun, she says, then simply “get back to your usual routine” without dwelling on it.

It's rare to feel motivated all the time, but you don't need to, say both experts.

Building habits and routines that fit around your life—and sticking with them even when motivation dips—is a much more reliable approach.

“For fat loss, your overall calorie intake matters more than obsessing over whether those calories came from carbs or fat,” says Bentley.

When considering macros, she says to focus on food in this order: calories first, followed by protein and fiber.

Campus agrees. “Energy balance is important, but that doesn't mean calories are the only thing worth considering,” she says. “Protein, fiber, food quality, portion sizes, sleep, and activity all influence how sustainable your diet feels and how well you can stick to it.”

#1369. How to Winterize a Garden: Ready Your Plants to Weather the Cold With These Expert Tips

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

As fall winds down, you’ll pull sweaters out of storage and crank the thermostat, but what about your outdoor plants? Knowing how to winterize a garden is key to welcoming spring with an abundance of thriving greenery—as opposed to a sad brown patch of lawn. If frost, snow, rain, and freezing temperatures are in the forecast, know that your outdoor spaces don’t have to face the elements without a little assistance.

To prepare your garden for winter, you’ll need burlap; straw; garden tools like pruning shears and a pruning saw or lopper; a compost pile or ready source of nutrient-rich matter; and, ideally, some winter-ready plants that can take the place of flowering annuals and edible plants.

One more item to add to your list (in potentially large quantities): mulch. “One of the biggest things we do is mulch, and then mulch some more,” says Christina Stembel, founder and CEO of D2C e-commerce flower company Farmgirl Flowers. “We bring in what feels like an absurd amount of mulch every fall and use it just about everywhere. It helps insulate roots, suppress weeds, protect the soil through rain [and snow], and eventually adds organic matter back into the ground. We mulch our garden roses, peonies, perennial beds, [and more].”

Amy Enfield, who has more than 30 years of experience in the lawn- and garden-care industry and is currently a senior horticulturalist at Miracle-Gro, offers thoughts on a mindful first step ahead of digging in: “Before pulling out tools or buying materials, walk your garden and take stock of what you have. Note which plants are annuals that will not return, which are perennials you want to protect, where the soil looks tired or compacted, and which areas might benefit from a cover crop over winter. This assessment keeps you from doing more work than necessary or missing something important.”

Burlap adds a layer of protection against the elements for this outdoor plant in the wintertime.

“Do not begin with applying mulch,” says Jose Lopez, the founder and owner of Williams Landscaping Inc. “First, walk through your fall garden to pull up dead foliage. Pull any foliage that shows signs of disease as well. Next, apply appropriate care to the plants that will remain. Finally, cover your soil. If you do not complete these first three steps, you’re simply burying the problems of the growing season.” To cover that soil, there are three primary materials, each of which plays a different role, Lopez says. “Compost supplies nutrients to the soil. Mulch serves as insulation [and you can] use the leaves that are readily available, shredding them for mulch. Straw has no place in most flowerbeds but is suitable for vegetable gardening.” Just be mindful of where you are placing covering materials, and “do not layer large amounts of wet leaves over a newly planted shrub because the water can cause root rot at its base,” Lopez notes.

Raised garden beds tend to be used for annual flowers and edible plants, and these typically need to be removed, says Dr. Enfield. “Leaving their debris in the garden over winter creates a cozy habitat for overwintering pests and fungal disease.” Pull any spent annuals and finished vegetable plants, disposing of or composting them. “Diseased plant material should go in the trash rather than the compost pile,” Dr. Enfield says.

Once the beds are cleared, Dr. Enfield says to “amend the soil before it freezes. Working organic matter, like compost, composted manure, or a bagged soil into garden beds in fall gives it the entire winter to work into the soil structure, so it is ready to support planting in spring. Spread three inches over cleared beds and work it into the top six inches of native soil before winter sets in.”

Some plants are tough enough to make it through the winter without much support, but others can benefit from some TLC in the autumn. For those plants that aren’t hardy enough to withstand winter in your climate, you’ll need to break out some of the aforementioned materials: burlap, straw, frost cloth, and horticultural fleece. Amanda Webb, head gardener at Windy Hill Farm and Preserve, notes that “burlap is particularly useful for protecting exposed evergreens from winter wind, while straw can provide insulation around the crowns of marginally hardy perennials. I prefer breathable materials over plastic, which can trap moisture and create problems when temperatures fluctuate.”

Shrubs and other borderline hardy plants also need to take cover from the cold. “Some plants, particularly broadleaf evergreens like rhododendrons, hollies, and boxwoods, as well as any shrubs or perennials that are on the edge of their cold hardiness zone, benefit from physical protection over winter,” says Dr. Enfield. Burlap is the most commonly used material for this purpose; it can be wrapped loosely around individual plants or used to construct a simple windscreen that protects evergreens from the desiccating winter winds and sun, which can cause browning and leaf scorch even in plants that are otherwise cold-hardy. “Avoid wrapping plants in plastic, which traps moisture and heat and can do more harm than good. Burlap breathes, which is what makes it the material of choice.” For young or newly planted trees, wrapping the trunk with tree wrap or burlap protects against sun scald, “which is the cracking of bark caused by rapid temperature changes on the south and southwest sides of the trunk during winter thaws.”

Frost protection will help shield these potted plants from freezing temperatures.

Gardening doesn’t have to stop in winter; you just have to focus on plants that thrive in the cold. “I can’t stand seeing a vegetable bed sitting bare all winter,” says Mirela Bajic, senior garden designer at House Designer. “If you’re not growing anything in it, put something in for the soil’s sake. Winter rye is brilliant for that, [as] the roots hold everything together through the bad weather and then you just cut it back come spring.” She adds that many plants are more than rugged enough to make it through frosty, even snowy seasons. “Kale, cabbage, leeks, chard, [and more] all keep going through the cold if you let them.” Winter-ready crops like these help hold the soil together, provide some lovely greenery amid the gray backdrop of wintertime, and can even be used in hearty cold-weather meals.

It’s imperative that you tend to your property’s sprinkler systems and hoses, says Webb. “Drain irrigation systems and hoses if freezing temperatures are expected,” she says, or you risk catastrophic damage once water in the lines freezes and expands.

First, you’ll need to shut off the water supply to your sprinklers and hoses, removing the latter and putting them away for winter. Then, blow out sprinkler lines using compressed air to expel any residual water from the pipes. You should use a compressor set to 80 PSI or lower to avoid damaging sprinkler heads in the process. Once the lines are clear, leave the water shut off at the source inside your home (or garage or greenhouse or wherever the main water connection point is found) while leaving external valves partially open so last drips can fall and trapped water has a place to expand if it freezes.

Just because it’s too cold outside to grow colorful flowers or tasty tomatoes, peppers, or herbs doesn’t mean you can’t keep the growing going on indoors, and no dedicated greenhouse is needed, either. With a self-watering hydroponic growing system like a Gardyn Studio 2, you can grow full-sized plants indoors, including the same flowers and edible plants you’d place in a raised garden bed outdoors. If indoor space is at a premium, you can also use a smaller countertop system like a LetPot that can be used with a smaller number of flowers or food plants.

All those leaves you painstakingly rake or blow into a pile need not be bagged up and sent off to the dump; you can recycle it all to make your own mulch, which can preserve and nurture your garden throughout the winter. It’s best to use a leaf shredder to break down fallen leaves into smaller bits, rather than simply spreading collected leaves out atop your beds. You can also use a lawn mower to break down leaves, though this will dull the blade with extended use. If you have a wood chipper, you can use branches and brambles to make mulch as well. Even grass clippings can make great mulch, provided you dry it in the sun first and mix it with some thicker material, like those shredded leaves or chipped branches.

You don’t have to remove every dead flower, be it a truly finished annual or a dormant perennial, says Stembel, of Farmgirl Flowers. “One thing I’m trying to get better at is not cleaning everything up just because it looks messy. There are flowers that went from stunning to eyesores everywhere, seed heads, and so many perennials that are better off being left alone for a while so the foliage can die back naturally and the plants can store energy for next year. A lot of those seed heads and stems also feed birds and give beneficial insects places to overwinter.” Sometimes, the best thing you can do to winterize your garden is to leave it alone.

You should plant bulbs in the autumn when soil temperatures are consistently holding at around 55 to 60 degrees Fahrenheit, and about six weeks before you expect the first frost. “[It’s] my absolute favorite fall ‘chore,’ planting the bulbs,” says Stembel. “This fall we’re planting thousands of daffodils, tulips, and drumstick alliums around the farm. I know thousands sounds excessive [but] bulbs might be my favorite kind of delayed gratification. By spring, I’ll mostly have forgotten that we trudged outside on cold, rainy fall days and dug what felt like endless trenches to plant all of them until the little green sprouts start popping through the soil. Those first flowers bring us so much joy right when we need it most [after] months of cold, gray weather. In fall, I wonder why we planted so many, but by spring I’ll wonder why we didn’t plant more.”

#2934. Tick Disease Nightmare: If You Became Allergic to Venison Would You Still Go Hunting?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 2:14 PM EDT

Editor’s Note: This story was originally published in September 2019, before Alpha-Gal disease was as widespread as it is today. Since then, the writer has recovered from his malady and is back to eating red meat. (See the epilogue).

It’s mid-August and my dad, a buddy, and I are tucked into our layout blinds as another flock of Canada geese swings in low toward our spread. When they line up perfectly, I holler “Take ’em!” and we greedily get to shooting, dropping four birds out of the flock. We’ve already piled up about 15 geese by now, but the early-season limit here in North Dakota is a generous 15 birds per person.

Many hunters—many waterfowlers, even—would look at so many honkers with a tinge of reservation. Goose meat isn’t valued as much as, say, a venison backstrap or elk tenderloin. But to me, our growing pile of geese looks like a mound of gold. That’s because Canada goose is one of the few red meats I can eat without getting sick. I have Alpha-gal syndrome, a tick-borne illness that makes its victim allergic to any mammal-based food product. The disease is carried by the Lone Star tick, and it’s becoming more prevalent all around the country (more on that later). For hunters like me, contracting Alpha-gal means no more elk backstraps, no venison cheeseburgers—not even a fried squirrel leg.

“Get down and load up,” I holler, watching another flock of geese appear over the horizon. “Here come some more.”

I started getting sick in December. I’d get hit with a crippling stomachache that would start an hour or two after almost every meal. Sometimes I’d get sick enough to spend a few hours vomiting in the bathroom. Other times I’d feel a tightness in my chest and a rash of hives would break out on my skin.

After a few days of this, I went to a doctor who ran a series of blood tests (all fine) and an ultrasound of my gall bladder (also fine). After narrowing my diet to only chicken and rice, and more tests from my doctors, it became clear that I have Alpha-gal syndrome.

I did some research and learned my reactions are relatively mild on the spectrum of this condition. According to the Mayo Clinic, symptoms of an Alpha-gal allergy include: hives and itching, swelling of the lips, face, tongue and throat, wheezing or shortness of breath, runny nose, abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea or vomiting, headaches, and anaphylaxis—a severe, potentially deadly allergic reaction that restricts breathing.

I read stories about Alpha-gal victims who were rushed to the emergency room as their entire body ballooned up, or others who passed out from throat swelling. I also learned that this is a relatively new condition: The meat allergy was first formally described in 2009, according to The New York Times Magazine. On its info page, the Centers for Disease Control describes Alpha-gal syndrome as “a serious, potentially life-threatening allergy to alpha-gal that can develop after a tick bite.” There is no cure. But I also found out that if I am not bitten by a carrier tick again, there’s a chance that my allergy could fade over time.

So I cut red meat out of my diet for the rest of the winter and spring. By mid-summer I summoned the courage to try a few slices of grilled venison backstrap. I was cooking the meat for my wife, but the sizzling cut looked and smelled too good to resist. So I gobbled down three thin, medium-rare slices—and waited. A few hours went by, and I felt fine. Progress.

So what is this allergic disease, exactly? And how are ticks transmitting it?

Technically, I’m allergic to a complex sugar called galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose, or Alpha-gal for short. The New York Times Magazine published an in-depth report in July on mammalian-meat allergy that explains how the allergic disease is spread. This is an excerpt from the Times report:

“When the lone-star tick feeds, alpha-gal leaks from its mouth into the wound, exposing the victim’s immune system to the sugar, prompting the immune system to remember and pursue alpha-gal. But exposure to alpha-gal alone probably doesn’t achieve this feat. [Dr. Scott] Commins, who is at the University of North Carolina, at Chapel Hill, has identified a candidate, an enzyme in the tick’s saliva called dipeptidyl-peptidase that works as an adjuvant. It’s also common in bee and wasp venom. This enzyme, Commins argues, is what tells your immune system to see alpha-gal as the type of threat that warrants the itching and swelling of the allergic response.”

Lone Star ticks are most common in the southeast, where Alpha-gal syndrome is more prevalent, but large numbers of Lone Star ticks have been found as far north as Maine and as far west as Oklahoma, according to the Mayo Clinic. I live in Minnesota, where there aren’t established populations of Lone Star ticks, but even so, Alpha-gal cases have been cropping up here since 2017.

Some researchers think it might not just be the Lone Star tick that spreads the disease.

“I think most of these allergic responses are due to the Lone Star tick, but that doesn’t account for problems that we’re having in Australia,” Commins says in a podcast on his website. “People that we know about in southern Sweden…have this same allergy, but they don’t have Lone Star ticks. So one of the myths… is that Lone Star ticks are the only cause, and that’s certainly not true.”

As tick numbers continue to grow around the country, researchers expect to see more Alpha-gal syndrome cases in even more locations.

In some ways, contracting Alpha-gal disease is better if you’re a hunter. I have access to large quantities of wild duck and goose meat, which (when cared for and prepared properly) can be a killer substitute for red meat. Don’t believe me? Try this recipe for a sous-vide Christmas goose, this recipe for wild-duck ramen, or this tutorial on how to take your Canada goose cooking to the next level.

On the other hand, my condition makes big-game hunting trickier. I’ve been a deer hunter since I was 12, and one of the things I’ve always loved about that is the freezer full of venison after a successful hunt. So am I going to take this deer season off? Hell no. Sure, I hunt deer for the meat, but even more so, I do it for the experience. I’ll fill my freezer with ducks and geese, and I’ll spend my deer season enjoying the hunt itself.

Plus, I’ve got a big extended family of wild-game eaters, plus some friends from the city who would love a few venison roasts and steaks. And there are all kinds of great venison donation programs that turn deer into food for folks who need it. One of the reasons that researchers believe ticks and tick-borne diseases are spreading is because of growing populations of whitetails (which carry breeding ticks) around urban and suburban areas. If I kill a couple deer in high-population areas and donate the meat to folks who need it, that’s my small way of helping reset the natural balance of things. Maybe it will even earn me some good karma with the deer gods.

I still consider myself a meat hunter, but this fall I’ll just be passing the meat along to someone else. And when I throw the next venison backstrap on the grill for my wife, I might even try four slices.

As the months and years went by, my sensitivity to red meat subsided. I’m now able to eat beef and pork without any problems. Unfortunately, this is not the case for everyone who is struck with Alpha-gal. Some folks remain allergic no matter how much time passes. So while I had a few tough years of passing on ribeyes and pork-chops, I still consider myself one of the lucky ones.

One of the biggest myths in all of turkey hunting is that male turkeys respond to calling only in the spring. The fact is, toms gobble all fall, too, and are sometimes more vocal in autumn than in spring. And they can be very responsive to a call.

Just as in the spring, a hunter’s job is to get gobblers excited, because that’s when they make mistakes. And to do that, you have to sound like a gobbler when you call, imitating a newcomer that’s challenging the old boys’ club. It’s a pecking order thing. They come to the calling because they want to see who you are, and because they want to kick your behind.

Unlike in spring, turkeys are driven by food in the fall rather than the need to reproduce. Therefore, scouting food sources is one of the best ways to find fall toms. In early autumn, look for soft-mast foods such as hawthorn, dogwood berries, wild grapes, persimmons and crab apples.

Rounding out the fall smorgasbord are a variety of insects, assorted seeds, grasses, cherries and agricultural crops of clover, beans, small grains and waste grain that’s left on the ground in freshly harvested fields.

Later in the autumn, turkeys feed primarily on hard mast dropped by a variety of oaks and maples, as well as other trees that produce nutlike seeds. However, trees don’t produce these every year, so the trick is to know the foods turkeys prefer and to find which foods are available in your hunting area.

As long as the habitat and food sources don’t change, neither will fall roost locations, and these bedrooms are the best places to begin your fall hunt.

Always set up tight on a roost way before first light and on the same side or in the same direction as the previous evening’s fly-up. Listen for the first turkey to call, and then duplicate whatever the turkeys do, but with more enthusiasm. If turkeys yelp, you yelp; if they cluck, you cluck, but with more feeling. Work them into a frenzy.

When it comes to cutting, putting, purring and other sounds, there is no reliable difference between a hen and a tom; they all do it. When a gobbler gets excited, it will sometimes yelp 30 times in a row and do a lot of high-pitched putting.

One way to tell the difference between hen and gobbler yelps is the rhythm. A hen’s note is relatively faster and shorter in length. When a gobbler is yelping, each note is more drawn out, with a slightly slower rhythm. It can also sound coarser, or “throatier,” than a hen’s.

A quality slate or glass call is one of the best devices to use on fall turkeys. The slate will produce great soft tree talk, purrs and hen yelps, as well as realistic gobbler yelps. My favorite is the mouth call. I can imitate almost any turkey sound with the diaphragm call, including jake kee-kees, gobbler yelps and cutting.

#992. No-Knead Pumpkin Focaccia

This version doesn’t require any kneading, making it straightforward enough to pull off in a single afternoon. Bake focaccia, rotating pan front to back halfway through, until deep golden brown, 23–28 minutes. Transfer pan to a wire rack and let focaccia cool slightly in pan. Using a metal spatula, turn out focaccia onto rack and let cool completely.

#1011. Salt Bread (Shio Pan)

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Welcome to the Bon Appétit Bake Club, a community of curious bakers. Each month senior Test Kitchen editors Jesse Szewczyk and Shilpa Uskokovic share a must-make recipe and dive deep into why it works. This month we welcome deputy food editor Hana Asbrink to be our guest baker. Come bake and learn with us—and don’t forget to join the Bake Club group chat over on Substack.

The version of this recipe that appeared in our November 2026 print issue has the incorrect bake time listed. The recipe that follows features the correct total bake times of 15–18 minutes (please see step 8).

Salt bread is a wondrous buttery roll known as shio pan in Japanese or sogeum ppang in Korean. It originated in Japan in 2014 and quickly became popularized in Korea soon thereafter, taking off in the post-Covid era around 2021. It’s formed by rolling each ball of dough around a nub of salted butter. As it bakes, the butter melts and oozes out of the bun, “frying” and crisping up the bottom. If you can imagine a warm dinner roll meeting a flaky croissant, you’re getting closer.

You’ll often find a range of salt bread, some leaning closer to sturdy bread with a tighter crumb and others resembling a softer milk bread. In all salt bread cases, there are a few key identifiers: (1) a deeply golden exterior, with the aforementioned crisp bottom; (2) the signature hollow in the middle (where the pat of butter once stood) when you tear the bun in half; and (3) a sprinkle of flaky or coarse salt atop the bun.

In this riff, I use an enriched dough, bringing in qualities of a soft milk bread into the mix. With the bread flour, the end result is a lovely, crowd-pleasing bun that’s great for snacking on its own or enjoying alongside a special holiday meal. If there’s one note to highlight: Ensure you are starting with a fresh pack of instant (or “rapid-rise”) yeast. This will make all the difference in the rise and the eventual yield.

(16 g) flour and ½ cup water in a small skillet, preferably nonstick, over medium heat with a heatproof rubber spatula to combine and cook, stirring constantly, until thickened to the texture of mayonnaise, about 2 minutes; remove from heat. Add 4 Tbsp. unsalted butter, cut into small pieces and stir until melted. Transfer tangzhong to the bowl of a stand mixer.

Add 2 large eggs, ½ cup whole milk, ¼ cup (50 g) sugar, 1 Tbsp. Diamond Crystal or 1¾ tsp. Morton kosher salt, and 1 ¼-oz. envelope instant yeast (about 2¼ tsp.) to tangzhong; stir vigorously by hand with rubber spatula until incorporated. Add remaining 4 cups (500 g) flour, attach bowl to stand mixer fitted with a dough hook or paddle, and mix on low speed until dough is smooth and soft (it may stick to bottom of bowl but should mostly clear sides), 20–25 minutes. If it tears, the gluten is not yet developed enough. Keep mixing until it can stretch; it may take up to 30 minutes.

#759. 5 Cookbooks That Go Deep on a Single Ingredient

If you’ve ever been starstruck by a perfect Cinderella pumpkin or a stately stalk of Brussels sprouts, you likely understand the appeal of a book focused on a single fruit or vegetable. While books like this aren’t always long, they typically go deep, with the fervor of an author who is fully obsessed.

One charming example, The Core of an Onion: Peeling the Rarest Common Food by Mark Kurlansky, melds authority with a dose of irreverence. First published in 2023, it’s a slim 200 pages, but the writer (who’s also written nonfiction books dedicated to oysters, salt, and cod) packs a lot in. “Onions have a way of taking on unexpected significance,” he posits. The first section covers the allium’s history and cultural impact. (Did you know that ancient Egyptians tied them to mummies’ feet as a preservative?) Kurlansky then offers loosely structured recipes for breads, sauces, and more, all centering the humble bulb.

Elevating a perhaps overlooked ingredient is a core tenet of single-­subject cookbookery. In The Compleat Squash: A Passionate Grower’s Guide to Pumpkins, Squash, and Gourds, a canonical 2004 ode to members of the Cucurbitaceae ­family, master ­gardener Amy Goldman Fowler’s passion is abundantly evident. “Sometimes I don’t know where pumpkins begin and I end,” she writes, before segueing into a series of squash and gourd portraits, each showing an individual specimen more striking than the last. Fowler shares recipes too, in addition to breed spotlights, resources for seed ­buying, and advice on growing your own.

Some such tomes aim to introduce the reader to the joys of less ubiquitous ingredients. Lane Selman and Margarett Waterbury’s Radicchio: A Celebration of the Beloved Italian Vegetable tells the “human and natural history” of the bitter chicory, along with instructions for how to grow it and cook with it. Selman is the founder of the Culinary Breeding Network, an initiative that rallies scientists, farmers, and chefs to geek out over new produce varieties. The book gets weird in the best way possible, with profiles of Italian growers and recipes including gnocchi with radicchio pesto, radicchio with prosciutto ­gratin, and chocolate–radicchio cake (why not?).

Radicchio reads almost like a community cookbook, and that’s intentional. Selman’s inspiration to pursue the topic was the sheer lack of writing on this oddball vegetable: “All of these people that we’ve met on our way down the rabbit hole needed to be included,” she says. “They’re the ones who have given us all this information. Sometimes it takes a village.”

The book’s retro aesthetic lends ­gravitas to an ingredient often relegated to garnish status.

Every autumn the siren song of baking calls bakers home until the volume becomes too much to bear. This recipe, which keeps things simple, relies on the winning combination of cinnamon and apples to perfume a vanilla cake with pockets of tender fruit. A blanket of cinnamon streusel crowns the cake, giving it a contrasting crunch and a coffee-cake-like character. Feel free to stash the cake on your counter, slicing it throughout the week, enjoying its plush interior as it magically gets better with time.

Lightly coat parchment with nonstick spray. Place 1¼ cups (156 g) all-purpose flour, 1 cup (packed; 200 g) light brown sugar, 1½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¾ tsp. Morton kosher salt, 1½ tsp. ground cinnamon, ½ tsp. baking powder, and ½ cup (1 stick) unsalted butter, melted, in a large bowl and mix with your hands, squeezing together as needed, until marble-size clumps form. Set streusel aside.

Toss 1 lb. Honeycrisp apples (about 2 large), peeled, cored, thinly sliced, slices halved crosswise, and 2 Tbsp. (16 g) all-purpose flour in a large bowl to coat.

Whisk 1 cup (200 g) granulated sugar, 1½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¾ tsp. No doubt new landscaping can be a costly home project, especially if it involves high-end materials and large specimen plants. The bottom line is that landscaping costs can fluctuate depending on the size of your yard, where you live, the materials you choose, and the amenities you want. But that said, your new garden doesn’t have to completely rock (and sink) your financial boat. What you need to do is approach your project honestly, smartly, and thoughtfully.

Here are some ways to save some money when designing your new garden:

This is, perhaps, the most important thing you can do to save money. Start by making your wish list (start big because you can always whittle it down), browse gardening books and magazines for design ideas, visit local nurseries and landscape supply stores to get an idea of what materials cost (and then remember that your landscape designer will probably mark that up). If you’re on a strict budget, be mindful and focus on what you actually need (a flat space for a grill) and not what you want (a built-in outdoor kitchen).

If I had to list the top things to splurge on, I would say: quality hardscaping like retaining walls, patios, and walkways as these are the bones of your garden and will look good all year. Also on my list: nutrient-dense planting soil or compost to get your plants off to a healthy start (never skip on proper soil prep) and an irrigation system with a smart controller to automatically supply the right amount of water to your plants. Where to save? Consider buying plants in smaller sizes (think 4-inch instead of one gallon, and 5 gallon not 15 gallon) and buying fewer plants at first and adding more slowly as your budget allows.

Asses what materials you might already have and what can be repurposed. Also scour yard sales and local neighborhood online sites and see if someone is offloading bricks, pots, or patio furniture. You may have to change your design a bit based on what you discover, but adopting this mindset can really help your bottom line.

If you’re trying to fill a bunch of pots or amend large areas, don’t even think about reaching for bagged soil. Get a truckload delivered, and if you have extra you can always store it and save it for the next time you need it. Also, if you can be patient, wait for sales at nurseries to buy some of your plants like annuals, herbs, and veggies for your beds and ground covers to fill in bare spaces.

By including a veggie/herb garden into your design, you will save money on store-bought produce and be able to harvest what you need. (How many wilted, half-used heads of cilantro or lettuce do you have in your fridge?) Be realistic, though, about how much time you have to tend to and harvest your crops; otherwise that is a money- and food-waster. Try also incorporating plants that do a job, like lemon balm to repel mosquitoes, rosemary to keep away munching invaders like deer, or flowers that provide seeds you can save to grow more plants. Last, consider adding perennials and bulbs good for cutting and making homemade bouquets.

A garden that is relatively easy to take care of either by you or a crew will save on maintenance costs. Plan for trees, hedges, and shrubs that don’t need routine shearing to look tidy; avoid fussy annuals and perennials that need constant deadheading; and abandon the large lawn idea that will need tons of water and fertilizer to look green. A water-conscious garden that incorporates natives, succulents, and drought-tolerant plants will also translate into a money saver.

You may be tempted to DIY it all in the interest of saving money, but before you assign yourself the role of head contractor or lead horticulturist, write yourself a reality check and acknowledge that you’ll likely have to let the professionals do at least some part of the project. You can certainly do a portion of the work yourself, but if done improperly it could actually cost you more in the long run. One thing to remember is that it’s okay to call in favors if your aunt is an architect or your brother works in a nursery. I believe in the it-takes-a-village philosophy. Ask for help. And remember that people usually love a homemade pie as a thank-you.

Melt 2 Tbsp. unsalted butter in a medium skillet with a lid over medium heat. #4249. Scientists restore a brain protein and reverse signs of aging in mice

📅 Sep 18, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Memory problems, thinning skin, and declining bone mass may seem like separate consequences of growing older. Yet experiments in mice suggest that changes in a protein deep inside the brain can influence all three. Restoring that protein, called Menin, improved several signs of aging, while a separate treatment with the amino acid D-serine improved cognition.

The findings came from a study published on March 16, 2023, in the open access journal PLOS Biology, led by Lige Leng of Xiamen University in Xiamen, China, and colleagues. The work identified a possible connection between brain inflammation, metabolism, and aging throughout the body. Research published since then has added to that picture, while also showing why the supplement findings require careful interpretation.

How a Brain Protein Could Influence Aging

The researchers focused on the hypothalamus, a small brain region that helps coordinate metabolism and other essential functions. It also appears to influence how the body ages. As inflammatory signaling increases in this region, it can contribute to changes both within the brain and in tissues elsewhere in the body.

Before the 2023 study, Leng and colleagues had found that Menin helps restrain inflammation in the hypothalamus. That raised an important question: Could losing some of this protection help set age-related decline in motion?

The team found that Menin levels fell with age in certain neurons within the ventromedial hypothalamus, an area involved in regulating metabolism. The same decline was not seen in astrocytes and microglia, two types of cells that support and protect the brain. This suggested that the change was specific to particular cells rather than a uniform loss across the region.

To investigate whether Menin loss could actually contribute to aging, rather than simply accompany it, the researchers created conditional knockout mice. These animals were genetically engineered so that Menin could be selectively removed. Reducing Menin in younger mice increased hypothalamic inflammation and brought on several aging-related traits, including lower bone mass, thinner skin, cognitive decline, and a modestly shorter lifespan.

Menin loss also disrupted a chemical pathway important for communication between brain cells. Mice with less Menin had lower levels of D-serine, an amino acid that helps activate receptors involved in learning and memory. These receptors help neurons adjust the strength of their connections, a process essential for storing information.

An enzyme involved in D-serine production (which was in turn regulated by Menin) became less active, reducing the amino acid's supply. The findings suggested that Menin could influence cognition not only through inflammation, but also by helping maintain the chemistry that supports brain signaling.

D-serine is sometimes used as a dietary supplement, but an important distinction can get lost in discussions of food sources. Serine occurs in foods such as soybeans, eggs, fish, and nuts, while the form incorporated into dietary proteins is L-serine. The body can convert L-serine into D-serine, but the two forms are not interchangeable, and eating these foods is not equivalent to receiving the experimental D-serine treatment.

The researchers next tested whether raising Menin levels could improve the condition of elderly (20-month-old) mice. They delivered the gene for Menin into the hypothalamus, allowing cells in that region to produce more of the protein.

Thirty days later, the treated mice had improvements in skin thickness and bone mass, along with better performance on tests of learning, cognition, and balance. The changes were accompanied by higher D-serine levels in the hippocampus, a brain region essential for learning and memory. The study also reported that restoring Menin extended lifespan in the treated mice.

A separate experiment tested a simpler approach: giving mice D-serine in their drinking water for three weeks. This improved cognitive performance, including in older animals. However, the supplement did not reproduce the broader improvements in physical aging traits seen after Menin restoration. The distinction matters: the study did not show that taking D-serine reversed aging throughout the body.

At the time, Leng described the potential significance of the findings:

"We speculate that the decline of Menin expression in the hypothalamus with age may be one of the driving factors of aging, and Menin may be the key protein connecting the genetic, inflammatory, and metabolic factors of aging. D-serine is a potentially promising therapeutic for cognitive decline."

Leng added, "Ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) Menin signaling diminished in aged mice, which contributes to systemic aging phenotypes and cognitive deficits. The effects of Menin on aging are mediated by neuroinflammatory changes and metabolic pathway signaling, accompanied by serine deficiency in VMH, while restoration of Menin in VMH reversed aging-related phenotypes."

Subsequent studies have explored related mechanisms, although they should not be treated as direct confirmation of the entire Menin aging pathway.

A study published in the Journal of Physiology and Biochemistry in March 2024 examined Menin in cultured mouse hippocampal cells exposed to the stress hormone corticosterone. A compound called itaconate increased Menin levels and reduced inflammation and a form of cell death. When the researchers silenced Menin, that protection disappeared. The result supported a protective role for Menin in another experimental setting, but it was a cell study, not a demonstration of slower aging in animals or people.

Other work has strengthened the case that communication between the hypothalamus and the rest of the body can influence aging. In a 2024 Cell Metabolism study, researchers at Washington University School of Medicine identified a different group of hypothalamic neurons that communicates with fat tissue. Interventions that maintained or stimulated this system increased physical activity and extended lifespan in mice. The study involved a different molecular pathway from Menin, but reinforced the broader idea that brain signals can affect aging beyond the brain.

A much larger view emerged in January 2025, when an Allen Institute team reported an analysis of roughly 1.2 million mouse brain cells in Nature. Some of the cell types most sensitive to aging were concentrated around the hypothalamus's third ventricle, a fluid-filled cavity. Many showed reduced activity in genes associated with neuronal function alongside increased activity in genes related to immune responses. That study mapped changes associated with aging rather than testing a treatment, but it highlighted the hypothalamus as an important area for further investigation.

Why More D-Serine Is Not Necessarily Better

Later research also complicates the idea that increasing D-serine should always benefit an aging brain.

In April 2025, a study in Cellular and Molecular Life Sciences examined mice engineered to develop features of Alzheimer's disease. In that model, an early rise in D-serine accompanied disruptions in brain signaling. Genetically removing the enzyme that produces D-serine prevented or reduced several later cognitive problems. This was a different biological setting from the Menin experiments, but it demonstrated that D-serine can have different effects depending on the underlying disease process.

Research published on September 16, 2026, in the Journal of Alzheimer's Disease pointed in another direction. In a different Alzheimer's mouse model, a diet enriched with L-serine increased blood levels of both L-serine and D-serine and partially restored measures of new neuron production in the hippocampus. It did not improve the buildup of amyloid, a protein associated with Alzheimer's disease. Importantly, this experiment tested L-serine and the production of new neurons, not D-serine supplementation as a treatment for human aging.

Taken together, these findings suggest that serine metabolism is a promising research target, but not a simple case of more being better. The form of serine, the condition being studied, and the outcome being measured all matter. Neither study directly confirms or overturns the original Menin results.

There is some human research on D-serine, although it does not establish an aging treatment. A small randomized study published in 2016, before the Menin work, tested a single dose in 50 healthy older adults. Participants improved on one measure of a computerized maze task, but the researchers found no significant benefit on the other cognitive tests or mood measures. The experiment did not establish lasting memory benefits, slower aging, or the safety of prolonged use in older adults.

The Menin findings leave several important questions unresolved. Researchers still need to determine what causes the protein to decline with age, how much of the resulting physical and cognitive deterioration can be prevented, and how long any benefits might last. They also need to understand whether altering Menin or supplementing D-serine could produce unintended effects.

The central possibility remains compelling: some changes associated with aging may be influenced by signals originating in a small part of the brain. Understanding those signals could reveal ways to protect function later in life. For now, however, the evidence points to an experimental pathway worth investigating, not a supplement proven to turn back human aging.

#4410. Ozempic may have revealed the brain’s hidden “craving center”

📅 Aug 12, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

For many people, the thought of a tasty burger or a cold pint of beer conjures up a vivid mental image and drives behavior.

This link between thinking and doing serves a clear function – it motivates us to get the necessities for life.

But for some, this process can malfunction. Preoccupation with these rewarding stimuli can lead to disorders of substance overuse, including overeating to the point of obesity and alcohol abuse.

Studies going back to the 1970s have linked vivid mental imagery with drug abuse.

Understanding this link between craving and consuming is central to understanding addiction. This has eluded neuroscience for decades, but the introduction of a new class of drugs for weight loss may have given us just the lever we need to understand it.

These new drugs – including Ozempic and Wegovy – mimic the GLP-1 hormone to stimulate insulin release, slow digestion, and increase feelings of fullness. They are known as GLP-1 agonists and were originally used to treat type 2 diabetes because they help control blood sugar.

As a side effect, people using these drugs also lost a lot of weight, in some cases almost as much as might be expected from bariatric surgery.

But there is another less well publicised effect. Human studies show that GLP-1 agonists reduce alcohol consumption. Preclinical animal studies suggest these drugs also reduce the use of cocaine, amphetamines, opiates and nicotine.

These drugs are changing how we think about the brain’s reward system. They may also open new treatment options for obesity, alcohol dependence and the consumption of other addictive substances.

We have a reasonable understanding of the brain’s “reward circuitry” associated with regions that produce the neurotransmitter dopamine.

These brain parts – the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and nucleus accumbens (NAc) – have been the subject of research on reward for decades. They are the obvious candidate regions to look for a mechanism for GLP-1 action in the brain. But they lack significant density of receptors for GLP-1 and are unlikely to be the direct mechanism.

We must, therefore, consider other brain regions to understand the anti-consumption effect of GLP-1 drugs.

One jump “upstream” from the dopamine-producing brain parts is a region called the lateral septum. This brain structure has been historically implicated in emotional regulation.

Back in 1953, pioneering US behavioural researchers Joseph Brady and Walle Nauta coined the term “septal rage” when animals with damage in the lateral septum showed increased aggression, while direct stimulation of this brain region reduced aggression.

Much more recent work has placed the lateral septum at the center of a neural connectivity network. This has reframed how we think about its function.

While a link between the lateral septum and another region called the hypothalamus is probably responsible for septal rage, the lateral septum links with many other regions with various functions.

The lateral septum inherits much of its primary input from a brain region called the hippocampus.

This region is well known as the place that lets us form long-term “episodic memories.” A famous case of hippocampal damage, Henry Molaison (patient HM), was unable to form new memories after his surgery for epilepsy. He effectively lived without a past, in permanent present tense.

The hippocampus also contains the remarkable “place cells” – neurons that fire corresponding to a person’s thoughts about their position in space and, as recent research has shown, time.

This “where and when am I” information gets forwarded to the lateral septum. Key research has recently shown the lateral septum also contains place cells, but these cells strongly respond to rewards. They effectively add “what is good in this place” to the “where and when am I” information from the hippocampus.

Critically, the lateral septum shares this information with the dopamine-producing regions we would normally associate with reward.

Neuroscientists now think of the lateral septum as the brain region that lets us “think about” rewards – our conscious perception of them – and communicates with the machinery in the brain’s reward system that produces dopamine to make us feel good about them.

There is one last reason to suspect the lateral septum as the mechanism behind the anti-consumption effect of GLP-1 agonists. It is absolutely loaded with GLP-1 receptors.

Emerging research points to this as the mechanism. GLP-1 activation directly in the lateral septum has recently been shown to reduce food consumption in mice. Earlier this year, another study showed the same for alcohol consumption.

My own lab has shown this year that GLP-1 drugs reduce a type of activity in the lateral septum that may prevent it communicating so effectively with other brain regions.

These findings are reshaping our understanding of how the brain processes rewards and have put the spotlight firmly on the lateral septum as the home of cravings.

#895. How to Make Better Pie Crust, According to a Pastry Chef

📅 Oct 20, 2020  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

There is a lot of subtlety that goes into making a good pie crust, the types of intuitive moves or small tips you pick up in a professional kitchen (or by baking with an experienced relative). But a few principles make all the difference: keeping the butter cold, avoiding overmixing, letting the dough rest, and baking the finished pie long enough.

Below, and in my book A Good Bake, I cover the little things that matter, from making and rolling out pie dough to crimping the crust and knowing when a pie is done. These are the techniques I use to make delicious, professional-looking pies, no matter what recipe I’m following.

A Good Bake by Melissa Weller with Carolyn Carreno

In a professional bakery, cooks prepare pie dough up to several days in advance. Set yourself up the same way at home. Making the dough ahead gives it time to rest and chill, so it’s ready to roll when you need it. You can also freeze pie dough up to one month in advance; wrap it well and defrost it overnight in the fridge.

The essential ingredients in pie dough are flour, water, a pinch of salt, maybe a very small amount of sugar, and some fat. Many traditional American pie dough recipes call for lard, rendered pork fat, which makes for an exceptionally flaky crust.

I love lard, but quality lard is not always easy to find, so I use good old-fashioned unsalted butter for my pie and tart crusts. With good technique, you can achieve plenty of flakiness and get that great buttery flavor.

Some big pieces, some smaller pieces, just right.

Many pie recipes call for cutting the butter into ¼" cubes. I prefer largish ½" chunks. After cutting the butter into pieces, I arrange them onto a plate and freeze them for 10 minutes so the butter gets as cold as can be without turning rock solid.

When mixed into the flour, some larger chunks remain intact. When you roll out the dough, those chunks form striations of butter. As the crust bakes, the water in the butter turns to steam, creating microscopic flaky layers—and a light, tender crust.

Looking for maximum flake? Check out our guide to laminating pie dough →

Lots of recipes call for adding ice water to the dough. I find that cold water from my refrigerator (like from a water filter or door dispenser) works fine. That said, if you want to use super-chilled water, it certainly won’t hurt.

Add water only after you mix the flour and butter. When the water joins the party, gluten starts to form. Too much gluten development toughens pie dough, making the finished crust heavy and dense rather than light and flaky. Mixing the flour with cold butter first coats the flour’s proteins, impeding the interaction between the water and the flour and reducing gluten development.

It’s okay if there are still some floury bits!

After bringing the dough together, I shape it into a round disk or brick, depending on the shape I’ll roll it into. Then I wrap it in plastic wrap, run a rolling pin over it to compact and flatten it, and put it in the refrigerator to rest.

A good rest lets the flour fully absorb any moisture so that the dough comes together completely. Resting the dough also relaxes the gluten, making it easier to roll out and less likely to shrink in the oven.

If your kitchen runs warm, work quickly and give the dough extra time in the refrigerator whenever it starts to feel soft or tacky. You can also chill your mixing bowl, bench scraper, and rolling pin before you start.

Take the dough out of the refrigerator, put it on the counter, and let it sit until it feels malleable but still firm (10 to 15 minutes) before rolling. The goal is for the dough to be just pliable enough to roll but not so soft that it starts to stick. Conversely, rolling our cold dough straight from the fridge can cause it to crack.

Once you start rolling, move quickly. Even the firmest dough block will soften quickly under the friction of the rolling pin and the heat of your hands. The whole process shouldn’t take more than a minute or two.

It may be unconventional, but I always butter a pie plate before arranging the dough. The butter helps hold the dough in place as I shape it and provides insurance for removing it from the pan after baking. I smear enough butter on the bottom and sloped sides of the plate to leave a visible layer. I usually use cold butter, but slightly softened also works.

There are two ways to “line” a pie plate—that is, to make sure the dough fits snugly inside. Either fold the dough in half, lift it from the surface you rolled it out on, and place it in the pan. Or roll the dough onto your rolling pin and carry it over on the pin. I fold the dough when lining a round pie plate, and I roll it onto a rolling pin when using a jelly roll pan, which requires a larger piece. Use whatever method you find easiest.

In either case, carry the dough until it hovers over the pie plate, tart pan, jelly roll pan, or other vessel, centering it as much as you can. Then gently lay it onto the dish and unfold or unroll it. Adjust the dough so that it’s centered on the pan.

Ease the pie dough into the bottom corners of the pan without stretching it.

Gently lift one edge of the dough with one hand and use the other to press it into the bottom of the pan, working your way around the perimeter. Use a delicate touch, and resist pulling the dough, which will cause it to spring back when baked and create thin spots in the crust.

Next, gently run your finger along the rim to press the dough onto it, just enough to ensure it adheres. This allows you to see how much dough is overhanging. Make sure it is even all the way around and sitting nicely on top of the rim.

Use kitchen shears to trim the overhanging dough so there is an even 1" hanging over the perimeter of the pie dish. (Discard the trimmings or smush them together into a disk and refrigerate or freeze the dough to make a pie another day.)

If you are making a double-crust or lattice-topped pie, trim the top sheet of dough or lattice so it is flush with the outside edge of the pan.

Keep it even: Place your thumb in the indent from your index finger just made.

In pie making, “crimping” means forming the decorative, wavy edge around the perimeter of a pie. To crimp, tightly roll the overhanging dough toward the center of the pie to form a thick lip that rests on the ridge of the plate. While many recipes call for folding the excess dough under, I prefer folding it up and over. When you’re finished, the dough shouldn't hang over the rim.

The process is the same whether you are making a single- or double-crust pie. For a double crust, roll the bottom crust over the top crust, sealing the filling inside.

As you form this ridge, use your fingers to shape the edge of the rolled crust so that it is rounded and even. Once the entire edge is rolled, place the index finger and thumb of one hand on the inside edge of the ridge and use the index finger of your other hand to push the outside edge toward the center of the pie, forming a V or U shape. Continue around the perimeter until you have crimped the entire crust.

There is a lot of chilling when making pie. First, you chill the butter. Then you chill the finished dough before rolling it out. You’ll also want to chill the pie dish after lining it, and again after crimping and filling the dough, before baking.

If you’ve ever wondered why pies in professional bakeries look so much prettier than your homemade ones, it’s because they chill them—or even freeze them—before baking. Chilling the uncooked pie helps it hold its shape better during baking.

In my kitchens, we often freeze a fully assembled fruit pie (overnight is best), then bake it directly from frozen. To bake a frozen pie, put it in the oven straight from the freezer and add 10 to 20 minutes to the total baking time, using the recipe’s visual indicators for doneness in the recipe.

Always put a baking sheet under pies before baking. This is especially important with fruit pies, to prevent the bubbling fruit juices from spilling over the edge of the pan onto your oven floor.

Also called pre- or par-baking, blind baking refers to baking the crust before adding the filling. For many custard pies, which cook faster than fruit pies, you’ll partially blind-bake the crust to keep it from getting soggy or even staying raw. For pies and tarts whose fillings are not baked at all, you’ll blind-bake the crust until it’s fully golden brown

To blind-bake a crust, line the shaped, crimped, unbaked pie shell with parchment paper (crinkle the paper first to fit the pie shell better), then fill the parchment-lined dough with pie weights to prevent the crust from puffing while it bakes. BA’s favorite pie weights are inexpensive steel ball bearings, but many cooks use dried beans or rice; label any food items you use as “pie weights” when the pie is done, since they will not be good for cooking after baking.

For the full technique, see BA’s guide to blind-baking pie crust.

Depending on how cold the pie is when you put it in the oven—and depending on your oven’s hot spots and other quirks—baking time can vary, so I always rely on the recipe’s visual cues for doneness.

The crust should be a rich shade of golden brown. For a fruit pie, the filling must be bubbling through the lattice in the center of the pie or the vent cut into its top. If the filling isn’t bubbling in the center (not just around the sides), the fruit hasn’t cooked down enough. Remove it too early, and you risk a filling that weeps when you cut a slice.

Custard pies must bake until they are set. A set custard will wobble slightly when you jiggle the pan, but it shouldn’t slosh around like liquid. Avoid baking a custard pie so long that it’s fully set, which can cause surface cracks as the pie cools.

Cooling time is essential to pie making. Custard pies need to set (and sometimes chill for several hours), and fruit pies need ample time to gel and firm. For beautiful slices that hold their shape, the wait is worthwhile.

Overmixing or overhandling the dough encourages gluten development, which can make the baked crust tough rather than tender. Mix only until the dough begins to come together; it should still look a little shaggy, with visible pieces of butter.

Pie dough can shrink if it hasn’t rested long enough or if you stretch it while fitting it into the pan. Let the dough rest in the refrigerator before rolling, gently ease it into the pie plate without pulling, and chill the shaped crust again before baking.

Slumped crusts can happen if you remove the pie weights too early or you don’t fill the crust enough. Add weights to fill the crust to the rim of the pie dish, and don’t remove them until the crust is set (it should look dry and the parchment will no longer stick).

The butter may have gotten too warm or worked too thoroughly into the flour. For distinct flaky layers, keep the butter cold and leave some larger pieces intact. If the dough becomes soft or tacky while you're working, return it to the refrigerator until firm.

Why does my pie dough crack when I roll it out?

Dough that’s too cold can crack when you roll it, while dough that hasn’t hydrated enough may crumble. Let chilled dough sit until it’s pliable but still firm before rolling. If it remains dry and won't hold together, the problem may be insufficient hydration.

But in our Making Perfect: Thanksgiving series, Rick Martinez and Chris Morocco gave the Thanksgiving staple a wake-up call via hot chiles and something perhaps even more unexpected: corn nuts.

But don’t worry about hard little nuggets in your dressing; we’re after the flavor, not the crunch. Pulverized into a fine powder and bloomed in butter with the aromatics, the corn nuts bring an earthy sweetness and seriously amp up the corn flavor.

You can use store-bought cornbread or a box mix, but we developed our Buttermilk Cornbread recipe specifically with stuffing in mind. Whichever you choose, drying the cubes in the oven helps them hold their shape as they soak up the stock. If the liquid pools when you first add it, let the cornbread absorb it for a few minutes before transferring everything to the baking dish.

What kind of cornbread is best for dressing?

A sturdy cornbread that holds its shape when soaked with stock is ideal. Molly Baz’s buttermilk cornbread recipe was developed specifically for making stuffing, but store-bought cornbread works too. Just don’t skip the oven-drying step, which helps keep the dressing from turning mushy.

Are there any substitutes for the corn nuts?

If you can’t find corn nuts, try finely ground Fritos, which have a similar earthy flavor, or another plain corn chip. Several readers have reported good results with this swap. You can also leave the corn nuts out altogether, though you’ll lose some of the concentrated corn flavor that makes this dressing distinctive.

Can I make this cornbread dressing without sausage?

Yep! But you’ll need to make a few other adjustments. If going for a totally vegetarian stuffing, you can easily swap in vegetable stock, but since the sausage provides a host of savory flavor, you’ll need to compensate. Add 1–2 Tbsp. soy sauce or a pinch of MSG in with the wine. You can also double up on the dried herbs—especially the sage—since breakfast sausage brings in those flavors as well.

Preheat oven to 325°. Divide 1 batch Buttermilk Cornbread or 3 lb. store-bought cornbread, cut into pieces (about 14–16 cups of ¾" cubes) evenly between 2 large rimmed baking sheets and bake, tossing occasionally, until dried out and lightly browned around the edges, 40–50 minutes. Let cool at least 10 minutes. Increase oven temperature to 350°.

Meanwhile, cook 1½ lb. breakfast sausage, casings removed if needed, in a large skillet over medium-high heat, stirring and breaking up with a spoon, until lightly browned and cooked through, 6–8 minutes. Transfer to a plate.

Reduce burner to medium heat and melt 1 cup unsalted butter (2 sticks) in same skillet. Add 2 medium onions, chopped, and 4 celery stalks, chopped; season with kosher salt. Cook, stirring occasionally, until softened but not browned, 8–10 minutes. Add 3 garlic cloves, finely chopped, and 2 Thai chiles or 1 jalapeño (with seeds), chopped. Cook, stirring once, until very fragrant, about 1 minute. Add ¾ cup corn nuts, finely ground in a food processor, blender, or mortar and pestle (about ½ cup) and cook, stirring often, until very fragrant and vegetables are starting to brown around the edges, about 3 minutes. Add ¾ cup dry white wine and cook, stirring occasionally, until almost completely evaporated, about 3 minutes. Add 1 Tbsp. finely chopped sage and 2 tsp. finely chopped thyme and toss to combine. Remove from heat.

Combine cornbread, sausage, and sautéed vegetable mixture in a large bowl. Whisk 3 large eggs and 3½ cups turkey stock or low-sodium chicken broth in a medium bowl until very well combined. Pour over cornbread mixture and let sit, gently stirring every minute or so, until cornbread has absorbed all or virtually all of the liquid. Season with kosher salt  (about 1 Tbsp. Diamond Crystal or 1½ tsp. Morton kosher salt) and freshly ground black pepper.

Lightly butter a 3-qt. or 13x9x2" casserole dish. Transfer stuffing to dish and tap dish lightly against counter to distribute and compact stuffing. Cover tightly with aluminum foil and bake until stuffing is very hot throughout and bubbles appear around sides, 40–45 minutes. Increase oven temperature to 425° and remove foil. Continue to bake stuffing until top is lightly browned, 15–20 minutes longer.

Do ahead: Stuffing can be baked at 350° 3 days ahead. Let cool, then chill. Reheat in a 350° oven before increasing temperature to 425° and removing foil.

#770. Alton Brown's Brined Turkey

📅 Nov 18, 2011  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

If you’re wondering how to fit an enormous brine-bathing bird into your fridge this holiday season, stop wondering. Alton Brown’s wet-brined turkey recipe uses an insulated cooler instead, freeing up precious refrigerator space while seasoning the bird throughout.

The brine—a simple mixture of kosher salt, sugar, and vegetable broth—helps the turkey retain moisture as it roasts. Once the bird has had its soak, pat it thoroughly dry and send it into a blazing-hot 500°F oven to get the skin hurtling toward golden and crisp. After 30–40 minutes, you’ll shield the breast with foil, lower the heat to 350°F, and let the turkey finish roasting.

That’s right: No basting. No hovering by the oven. Just a little advance planning and plenty of time to focus on sweet potatoes, apple pie, and the rest of your Thanksgiving feast.

If you'd rather skip the wet brine, try our dry-brined turkey. Short on time? Chris Morocco’s turkey in parts cooks in under two hours.

For a 14–18-lb. turkey, plan on 8–12 hours in the brine—enough time for the salt to work its way into the meat, seasoning it and helping it retain moisture. You may be tempted to leave the turkey soaking for 24 hours, but an extended brine can make it overly salty. Stick to the recommended window.

Yes, as long as you can keep the brine at or below 40°F the entire time. Use a thoroughly cleaned, food-safe insulated cooler and monitor the liquid with a probe thermometer. Add sealed ice packs as needed to maintain a safe temperature. The goal is to keep the turkey just as cold as it would be in the refrigerator.

Can you brine a turkey that’s already been brined?

It’s best not to. Many supermarket turkeys are sold prebrined or injected with a salt solution, sometimes labeled “self-basting,” “enhanced,” or “contains a solution.” Brining one again can make the meat too salty. Check the packaging before you begin and choose an unbrined turkey for this recipe.

How do you keep gravy made from brined turkey from getting too salty?

Brining seasons the turkey, but it can also make the pan drippings quite salty. Taste the gravy before adding any extra seasoning, and use low-sodium or unsalted broth for more control. If the finished gravy is too salty, add a few pats of unsalted butter or thin the gravy with salt-free broth.

Place wrapped one 14–18-lb. Frozen turkey in a 5-gal. cooler with a drain spout. Place cooler in a bathtub and pour in cold water to cover turkey. Let sit, draining and replacing water every 2–3 hours and making sure to keep water temperature below 40°, until turkey is thawed, 8–10 hours.

Remove turkey and clean cooler with soap and pat dry. Pour in 3 cups (1 lb.) Diamond Crystal or 1¾ cups Morton kosher salt, ⅔ cup sugar, and 2 qt. hot tap water. Stir to dissolve salt and sugar. Pour in 4 qt. low-sodium vegetable broth and 8 lb. ice (from 1 gallon water).

Unwrap turkey and remove giblets and any other parts (such as neck) that may be inside cavity; save for gravy if desired. Place turkey, breast side up, in brine. If turkey floats, fill a 1-gallon resealable plastic bag with ice water and place it on top of turkey. Close lid and set cooler in a cool place. Let sit, turning turkey over once if possible, 8–12 hours. Check brining liquid periodically with a probe-style thermometer ; if temperature is above 40°, add a few freezer packs enclosed in resealable plastic bags.

Place a rack in lower third of oven and remove remaining racks; preheat to 500°. Remove turkey from brine and pat dry inside and out with paper towels. Fill turkey cavity with 1 large onion, coarsely chopped, 2 large carrots, scrubbed, coarsely chopped, 2 celery stalks, coarsely chopped, and some thyme, rosemary, and/or sage sprigs; keep packing loose.

Place turkey, breast side up, on a V-shaped rack set inside a large roasting pan. Cut a piece of foil big enough to cover breast and mold over breast. Remove foil, then coat foil with nonstick vegetable oil spray and set aside.

Rub 2 Tbsp. unsalted butter between your hands until liberally coated (you may use disposable food-grade gloves if desired). Rub butter all over turkey, re-buttering your hands and repeating process until you have a nice, even coating.

Slide turkey into oven neck end first and roast until well browned, 30–40 minutes. Remove turkey from oven and place greased foil over breast. Insert probe-style thermometer through foil into thickest part of breast. Reduce oven temperature to 350° and return turkey to oven. Roast until probe inserted into breast registers 161°, and 181° for thigh, 2–2½ hours (do not open oven to baste turkey).

Transfer turkey to carving board and let rest 30 minutes. Reserve drippings for gravy.

Set roasting pan across 2 burners over medium heat. Add 1 cup low-sodium chicken broth and whisk to scrape up pan drippings. Whisk 1 Tbsp. cornstarch and 1 cup cold water in a small bowl to combine. Add cornstarch slurry to pan and cook, whisking, until gravy is glossy and thickened. Taste and season with kosher salt and freshly ground pepper if needed.
