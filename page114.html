#3872. Inside Varley Art Gallery: Branding by Underline Studio

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Explore the Varley Art Gallery of Markham branding by Underline Studio, built around a deconstructed VA monogram and bold print system.

Regional art museums often trap themselves in quiet, neutral grey templates. Named after Group of Seven painter F.H. Varley at 216 Main Street Unionville, the Varley Art Gallery of Markham houses a major collection of Canadian paintings while running packed studio classes and seasonal community workshops. Toronto-based Underline Studio took a far more direct route for the institution's branding. They looked straight at the deep indigos, cadmium reds, ochres, and forest greens sitting on the gallery's canvases, then paired that painter's palette with a geometric three-stroke monogram built from the initials V and A. It's a deceptively simple move. Two angled bars meet at a crisp bottom vertex to form the V, while a third parallel diagonal stroke sits right beside the right stem to suggest an open, crossbar-free A alongside the three-line geometric sans-serif lockup.

That three-bar mark doesn't just sit politely in a corner. Because the logo is constructed from three independent diagonal vectors in Illustrator, Underline Studio breaks those strokes apart and scatters them across exhibition print collateral as active graphic framing devices. Look at the isometric flat lay of booklets for the Winter 2017, Spring 2017, and Summer 2017 Exhibitions & Events guides, or the brochure for Inner Landscapes: The Portraits of James MacDougall curated by Tom Smart. Individual yellow, crimson, green, and violet bars slice right across historical oil portraits and contemporary illustrations. On the Childrens' Programs Spring 2017 cover, a single wide purple diagonal slash cuts from the bottom margin across a close-up photograph of a young student painting. Nothing feels static on the table.

For recurring seasonal guides, the system tightens up in InDesign. Across the uncoated covers for Summer Programs Winter 2018, Special Events Spring 2018, and Holiday Programs Winter 2018, the full VA monogram locks to the bottom edge at oversized scale â shifting from ochre, purple, and cool grey on a red cover to green, crimson, and yellow on deep violet. Small left-aligned title type in the upper corner keeps the layout clean and legible. Good institutional branding should invite the neighborhood inside, and this print system does the job with real confidence.

#4079. Psilocybin Replaces Lost Synapses in Depressed Brains

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A neuroimaging and genetic investigation led by researchers at the University of California, Santa Cruz, reveals precisely how psilocybin restructures neural connectivity in living brains. Publishing in Nature Neuroscience, the team demonstrated that psilocybin drives the rapid growth and maturation of dendritic spines, remodeling cortical circuits based on whether the brain is healthy or damaged by chronic stress. Crucially, the researchers established a double dissociation in Layer 5 cortical pyramidal neurons, proving that psilocybin’s structural neuroplasticity operates independently from its hallucinogenic effects.

Source: University of California, Santa Cruz

Psilocybin, the active psychoactive compound found in Psilocybe mushrooms, has emerged as a cornerstone of the modern psychedelic renaissance. Recognized as a potent “psychoplastogen”, a molecule capable of stimulating rapid structural and functional neural plasticity after an isolated dose, psilocybin has earned Breakthrough Therapy designation from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treatment-resistant depression and major depressive disorder.

Yet, despite clinical trials demonstrating rapid and durable antidepressant outcomes, the fundamental cellular mechanics governing how psilocybin repairs circuits in living mammalian brains have remained unresolved. Furthermore, neuroscientists and pharmacologists have long debated whether the acute psychedelic “trip” is an indispensable biological requirement for lasting clinical recovery.

Now, a study led by researchers at the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC), published in the journal Nature Neuroscience, maps the exact structural timeline of psilocybin-induced plasticity and demonstrates that structural healing can be genetically decoupled from hallucinogenic activity.

To observe structural remodeling at high resolution, the research team employed in vivo two-photon microscopy to track individual dendritic spines, the tiny receiving docks where neurons establish synaptic communication, along cortical neurons in living mice over several weeks.

Within 24 hours of psilocybin administration, researchers observed a pronounced surge in de novo dendritic spine growth. Beyond driving spine density, psilocybin accelerated the physical maturation of these new synaptic junctions.

While newly formed spines typically begin as short-lived, structurally unstable “stubby” protrusions, psilocybin prompted a high percentage to rapidly morph into large “mushroom” spines. These bulbous, structurally mature spines are packed with scaffolding proteins and AMPA neurotransmitter receptors, providing the physical substrate for resilient, enduring memory and affective stability.

“These newly formed connections can integrate into existing neural circuitry and persist over time, leaving a lasting structural trace of psilocybin’s effects on the brain,” said lead author Jacob Baker, a doctoral researcher in molecular, cell, and developmental biology at UC Santa Cruz.

A primary breakthrough of the study is that psilocybin does not simply flood the cortex with indiscriminate connections; rather, its action is state-dependent, calibrated to the brain’s baseline microenvironment.

In healthy control mice, the compound induced a temporary increase in spine formation coupled with the pruning of pre-existing connections over three weeks, maintaining homeostatic structural equilibrium.

However, in mice exposed to chronic unpredictable mild stress, a standardized model mirroring human depressive pathology and synaptic atrophy, psilocybin behaved differently:

“These findings challenge the prevailing view that psilocybin simply increases neural connectivity and suggest that its effects depend on the brain’s underlying condition,” said senior author Yi Zuo, PhD, professor of molecular, cell, and developmental biology at UC Santa Cruz. “This provides a new perspective on how psychedelics might help restore neural circuits disrupted by stress, with potentially important implications for understanding their therapeutic effects in depression and other psychiatric disorders.”

To test whether the intense perceptual alterations characteristic of psychedelics are biologically mandatory for structural plasticity, the team focused on serotonin 2A receptors (5-HT2ARs) within Layer 5 pyramidal neurons—the primary computational output cells of the cerebral cortex.

This confirms that the signaling pathways driving therapeutic structural neuroplasticity are anatomically and mechanistically distinct from those generating hallucinogenic subjective states.

The discovery arrives as regulatory bodies worldwide prepare for the integration of psychedelic medicine. Last month, the FDA convened a landmark public hearing evaluating Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) frameworks and clinical trial standardization for psychedelic therapies, where logistical constraints, such as requiring two trained clinicians for eight continuous hours per dosing session, represent a major scalability bottleneck.

By pinpointing the exact cortical subpopulation driving structural repair, the UCSC team’s findings provide a concrete molecular roadmap for designing non-hallucinogenic psychoplastogen analogs.
