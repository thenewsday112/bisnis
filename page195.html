This is part of a series with Perfect Earth Project, a nonprofit dedicated to ecological gardening, on how you can be more sustainable in your landscapes at home.

Firefly fans, luna moth lovers, woolly bear worshippers: Now is the time to take action. Leave the leaves! It’s crucial to biodiversity. Fallen leaves host these beloved insects and more who overwinter in them. They also enrich the soil when they decompose. And they reduce waste by staying out of landfills. The EPA estimates that the U.S. sends about 35 million tons of yard waste to the landfill annually, which adds to already high levels of methane, a potent greenhouse gas. According to the National Wildlife Federation, “solid-waste landfills are the largest U.S. source of man-made methane.”

While it’s best to leave the leaves where they land in your yard, we know it’s not always possible. Rake them under trees, shrubs, and in beds to use as free—and beneficial—mulch. Just don’t chop or mulch-mow them, which was once a recommended practice. (Mulch-mowing can kill all insect life, so leave the leaves whole.) Or store leaves in a leaf basket you create out of fallen branches. Perfect Earth Project founder Edwina von Gal has created chic “baskets” at her property to store fallen leaves and keep them out of the landfill. Here’s how you can do it at home.

Photograph courtesy of Perfect Earth Project.

We’re having more storms. Big storms—just ask anyone in the Northeast after last weekend’s Nor’easter. But don’t despair. Gather those fallen branches and put them to good use.

Using larger branches, map out the shape of your basket. The size is up to you. Create a cluster of smaller baskets or build one large one. Do you have a spot full of invasive plants or an area you want to prep for future planting? Place your leaf basket there. The leaves will smother whatever is growing beneath it and get it ready for new growth in the future.

Now start building. Lay down branches, overlapping and tucking them into each other to form a cohesive unit. Trim branches as you need to make them fit. Keep adding more branches, building layer after layer, until you reach your desired height. You can continue adding branches as you find them throughout the year to reinforce the structure. Just make sure to weave them into other branches so that they’ll stay in place.

Rake leaves off of paths and driveways and place them into your basket. The basket will keep them contained and the leaves and branches will decompose over time, enriching the soil beneath it.  “It might look like a lot of leaves and a full leaf basket this fall, but by next summer the mass of leaves will have drastically shrunk,” says Perfect Earth Project’s Tim Wheeler.

Naming is hard. The language we use shapes the way we think about things, but also the conversation we have about it. That’s why we have so much confusion about colors, icons, UI components, features — and that’s why we struggle to name design tokens, HTML classes, and variables.

Sometimes, the names we choose are too generic, so it’s difficult to understand what exactly is meant. And sometimes they are too specific, leaving little room for flexibility and reuse. How do we choose the right names? And which naming conventions work best? Let’s take a closer look.

If you’re looking for some inspiration for naming HTML classes, CSS properties, or JavaScript functions, Classnames is a wonderful resource jam-packed with ideas that get you thinking outside the box.

The site provides thematically grouped lists of words perfect for naming. You’ll find terms to describe different kinds of behavior, likeness between things, order, grouping, and association, but also themed collections of words that wouldn’t instantly come to one’s mind when it comes to code, among them words from nature, art, theater, music, architecture, fashion, and publishing.

How would you choose the right name for a given color? Fortunately, David Aerne maintains a tremendous repository of color names, currently with 30355 unique color names, sourced from various references and 1000s of user contributions.

Of course, the repository comes with a color picker and name search and plenty of other color pickers as well. Tremendous work, which is also a fantastic reference to keep nearby.

What makes a good name? Javier Cuello summarized a set of naming best practices that help you name your layers, groups, and components in a consistent and scalable way.

As Javier points out, a good name has a logical structure, is short, meaningful, known by everyone, and not related to visual properties. He shares do’s and don’ts to illustrate how to achieve that and also takes a closer look at all the fine little details you need to consider when naming sizes, colors, groups, layers, and components.

How to build a flexible design token taxonomy that works across different products? That was the challenge that the team at Intuit faced. The parent company of products such as Mailchimp, QuickBooks, TurboTax, and Mint developed a flexible token system that goes beyond the brand theme to serve as the foundational system for a wide array of products.

Nate Baldwin wrote a case study in which he shares valuable insights into the making of Intuit’s design token taxonomy. It dives deeper into the pain points of the old taxonomy system, the criteria they defined for the new system, and how it was created. Lots of takeaways for building your own robust and flexible token taxonomy are guaranteed.

Looking at what other people call similar things is a great way to start when you’re struggling with naming. And what better source could there be than other design systems? Before you end up in the design system rabbit hole, Iain Bean did the research for you and created the Component Gallery.

It’s a collection that includes plenty of examples for more than 50 UI components — from accordion to visually hidden — and also lists other names that the UI components go by. A fantastic resource, and not only with regard to naming.

Features often have low adoption due to poor discoverability. Feature adoption needs time — first the feature must be discovered, then understood, then tried, learned, and eventually built into existing workflow. Erin Gannon has written a helpful practical guide on how to make sure all of it happens.

Good names are driven by user needs and problems. They should signal the value or outcome of a feature (the job-to-be-done), and — most importantly! — tap into the user’s own language. So ask people to explain a feature in their own words and then use the words they use.

A wonderful example of naming guidelines for a complex multi-brand, multi-themed design system comes from the Vodafone UK Design System team. Their Variables Taxonomy Map breaks down the anatomy and categorization of a design token into a well-orchestrated system of collections.

The map illustrates four collections required to support the system and connections between tokens — from brand and primitives to semantics and pages. It builds on top of Nathan Curtis’ work on naming design tokens and enables everyone to gather insight about where a token is used and what it represents, just from its name.

If you want to explore more approaches to naming design tokens, I’ve put together a list of useful Figma kits and resources that are worth checking out.

Romina Kavcic created a helpful little tool for naming design tokens — from learning how they work and how to name them well to configuring your own naming structure with components, categories, states, and roles.

If you are looking for the right name for your product or service, Onym is a fantastic open-source repository that organizes tools and resources for finding names for brainstorming, vetting, guides, sprints, etymologies — all the way to cautionary tales and agencies that name things, as well as useful books to take a look at.

The right name is the one that’s well-understood and used by the team and users. So much time is wasted speaking about the very same thing but in different dialects — the designer’s dialect, the developer’s dialect, the product manager’s dialect, and the user’s dialect. That’s what often causes frustration and confusion.

So if you see low adoption, it might be merely because of the name. And pay attention to potential naming conflicts and put them in a backlog of things to address later. It’s one of those fine little details that can reduce frustration and confusion and help teams work together better.

#1510. AI Ambitions Are Running Into a Network Infrastructure Trust Problem

📅 Oct 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Enterprise ambitions around AI are running into a fundamental problem: Orgnanizations aren't always confident in the networks those projects depend on, according to a report from telecommunications provider Arelion.

The research found that concerns about network providers have caused organizations to rethink strategic initiatives over the past two years. Among respondents whose initiatives were affected, AI and data-driven projects were the most cited, at 49%. That put AI ahead of security and compliance programs at 44%, digital transformation at 42%, and cloud migration at 38%.

The findings come from Arelion's report, "The Trust Illusion: Why AI and Digital Transformation Need More Than a Reliable Network," based on a survey of 518 senior enterprise network decision-makers in the U.S., U.K., Germany, and France conducted by Savanta.

All respondents worked for organizations with more than 2,000 employees and influenced network strategy across data centers, cloud, and connectivity. Of those surveyed, 66% had final sign-off responsibility. The results suggest that as organizations pursue increasingly data-intensive AI projects, confidence in the network infrastructure supporting them could become another deployment obstacle.

On the surface, Arelion found little evidence of a trust crisis. A total of 93% of respondents said they largely or completely trust their current network provider, while 83% said that trust has strengthened over time.

The picture changes when respondents are asked about a serious network incident. Only 15% said they have complete confidence in their provider's ability to quickly detect and address such a problem. Another 42% said their provider seriously lets them down anywhere from a few times a year to monthly or more frequently.

Those concerns have consequences beyond the network itself. Responding organizations have delayed, scaled back, or stopped strategic initiatives because of provider trust concerns, while others have added resiliency or risk-mitigation measures.

AI and data projects were the most affected among respondents reporting an impact, as organizations try to move AI from experimentation into larger production deployments.

A serious provider failure could also have substantial consequences. Forty percent of respondents said such an event would cause major operational disruption, while 18% anticipated significant financial loss or severe financial or reputational damage.

Another tension runs through Arelion's findings. Enterprises need networks they can trust to support AI projects, but many of the same decision-makers are concerned about AI's growing role inside those networks. A total of 54% of respondents identified AI and automation in network management and security as the factors most likely to negatively affect network trust over the next three years. That ranked ahead of data sovereignty expectations and geopolitical instability, both at 39%, and regulatory requirements and quantum computing threats to current encryption standards, both at 38%.

At the same time, respondents aren't rejecting AI as a network technology. Proactive AI-driven fault prediction was among the capabilities they said would matter most to future trust, alongside stronger built-in security, greater accountability, and responsive support. That leaves enterprises in an unusual position: AI is increasing their dependence on digital infrastructure while also raising questions about how that infrastructure itself is managed.

Arelion has an interest in emphasizing the importance of network-provider trust, and the survey reflects the views specifically of network decision-makers at large enterprises. Still, the findings point to an infrastructure consideration that can get lost amid the rush to adopt new AI models and agents. AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. If organizations lack confidence in that foundation, access to better AI models alone won't remove the obstacle.

"Trust is influencing which strategic initiatives move forward, how organizations manage risk, and which providers they choose to work with," said Mattias Fridström, Vice President at Arelion. "As businesses become increasingly reliant on digital infrastructure, enterprises are looking for network providers that can demonstrate transparency, accountability, and operational excellence, not just availability."

Parking rules are as important as driving rules, and although some vary from city to city, most are the same everywhere.

Driving around the country is a favorite American pastime, and I’ve certainly done my share. When I arrive in a city in which I’ve never been before, I like to ditch the car for a while and walk around. But that can be risky if you don’t know the parking rules that apply there. And given the traffic flow modifications that many cities have recently adopted, the rules (some of them, anyway) seem to be changing faster than drivers can keep up.

Where can you park and where can’t you? If parking is legal, how long can you stay without getting towed, and how much will it cost if you get towed? But really, there’s no mystery, according to auto insurance specialist Melanie Musson.  “Knowing where you can and cannot park is pretty easy if you pay attention.”  All you have to do, she says, is follow the signs.

You can always park in a municipal parking lot or parking garage, as long as there’s room and you pay the fee. It’s usually also OK to park on the street, especially if other cars are there, but if the space you choose has a meter, it’s important to feed it. Fines in congested urban areas can be expensive. The cost of a meter violation rose by 40 percent in San Francisco since the pandemic and can set you back more than $100, according to the San Francisco Standard.

In increasingly congested cities, cars compete for curb space with bike lanes, commercial loading zones, pedestrian walkways, outdoor dining, bus stops and EV charging stations. To manage all this, some cities prohibit street parking in certain areas altogether.

#3734. As a Bike Shop Manager, I’ve Seen Pre-Ride Bike Checks Go Wrong—Here’s 7 Ways to Get It Right

📅 Oct 05, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A shop-tested checklist for tires, brakes, drivetrain, bearings, and the timing that prevents last-minute panic.

I’ve spent a lot of time working in bike shops—one of the biggest mistakes I’ve seen people make before a big ride or event is to do a pre-ride bike check the day before their ride. When they’re going through the bike maybe they over-torque and break a bolt or maybe they realize one of their wheels is out of true and needs some attention. Either way one day is not enough time for a lot of shops to get something done—especially during peak season.

As I prepped my bike for UnPaved Pennsylvania, I thought about the checklist and timing I use to prepare for a big ride. Instead of running through the bike the day before a ride as a pre-ride check, run through it about a week to a week and a half out from your ride. This gives enough time to do a pre-bike check, test ride, then even gives a week to get some small service done at a local shop.

This checklist is how I work through my bike to make sure I’m ready to pedal to the finish. There are still things to do the day before your ride, but those things consist of making sure electronics are charged, bottles are filled, and nutrition is together (and I always check tire pressure right before rolling out). Coming from someone who has fielded a lot of shop calls on a Friday from people that have a race Saturday and need something done—go through the bike several days before you roll out.

Follow this step-by-step checklist for you to have confidence rolling into your next big ride this fall.

Place your bike in a repair stand. If the bike is just a little dusty, just give it a good wipe down with a rag. If it is truly dirty, remove both wheels and wash it thoroughly. Keep your eyes out for any cracks or frame issues while cleaning the bike. I know this is worst case scenario, but I’ve seen it happen before.

If the drivetrain is grimy, spray the bike chain and derailleurs with degreaser and let sit for a few minutes. Fill the bucket with warm, soapy water. Wet a sponge, hold it on the chain, and turn the crank to draw the chain through the sponge until the links are clean. Clean the crankset and derailleurs as well. Then clean the frame and parts (including the wheels) with a fresh sponge. Rinse by dripping water from above. (Don’t spray high-pressure water directly at the bike because this can force water into the bearings.) Dry the bike and all parts with clean rags.

If you spot a piece of debris lodged in your tire, try carefully picking it out. Inspect your bike tires for cracks, cuts, blisters, and baldness. Replace tires as needed.

Also, check tire seating. There are lines on the base of the sidewalls that should sit just above the rim all the way around. If they dip below the rim edge or rise above it, the tire is not seated properly. If you find any of these problems, deflate the tire and reinflate it, making sure that it seats correctly. Reinstall the wheels on the bike, making sure that they are centered in the frame and the quick-releases are properly tightened.

If you run tubeless, this is a good time to check sealant as well. Before a big ride (especially off-road) I tend to top off sealant because I’d rather have a little more than too little. The best way to check sealant is to remove the tire, check sealant, and reinstall the tire. Admittedly, I don’t always go this route. My quick check is to remove the wheel and shake it to check if I can feel the sealant sloshing around in the tire—you will likely be able to hear it in there as well. If you can’t hear anything, it’s worth taking off the tire to inspect and see if there’s a lot of dried sealant. If there is this can impact how well the sealant does what it’s supposed to: seal.

You want to check and snug chainring bolts from time to time. Though major components should not come loose during normal use, it is still wise to check them periodically. Without forcing, tighten crank bolts, pedals, chainring bolts, stem binder, handlebar binder, seat binder, seat bolt, brake and derailleur attaching nuts/bolts, and bottle cage screws. (Everything is turned clockwise to tighten except the left pedal, which is turned counterclockwise.)

Also, make sure all your repair gear is in proper working order, including a pump or CO2 inflator. This is a good time to check if your extra AXS battery is charged and check if your electronic pump is charged, if using those pieces of gear. Another key is to make sure your extra tube (bring an extra tube on a long ride) is in good working order.

Keeping your chain and other bike parts properly lubricated is critical to a properly functioning—not to mention quiet—bike. If they are not internally routed, lube your bike’s shifter cables where they pass under the bottom bracket. Lube the chain, then shift through the gears repeatedly to test derailleur adjustments.

Because the rear derailleur’s cable is longer and gets more use, it’s more likely to go out of adjustment. Each click of the rear shift lever should cause the bike chain to immediately jump to the next cog. If not, the cable has probably stretched slightly, or you may have mistakenly adjusted it too tight. If the chain hesitates to go to a larger cog, the cable is slightly loose. If the chain is slow in moving to a smaller cog, the cable is too tight. Fix slow shifts to larger cogs by turning the adjustment barrel on the rear of the derailleur counterclockwise in half-turn increments. For slow shifts to smaller cogs, do the opposite.

For electronic shifting systems, do the same test, running through both front and rear shifting to make sure everything is operating properly. If you do discover any problems, consult the appropriate troubleshooting manual for your components to help diagnose and remedy the problem. Adjusting electronic systems is typically much easier than traditional mechanical shifting drivetrains. In fact, as long as your battery is adequately charged, it’s unlikely you’ll have to do much adjusting at all. Just make sure you’re fully charged before the big ride. Without a charged battery, your electronic system will not work, and there are few things worse than being stuck in one gear all day.

If things are out of alignment and you can’t figure out why, it could be the derailleur hanger. This is one more reason to get this checklist done at least a week out from your big ride. If your hanger is out of alignment sometimes it can be bent back, but other times it needs to be replaced. If you have a universal derailleur hanger (UDH), then it is pretty accessible at most shops. Otherwise the part may have to be ordered. A derailleur hanger is one part I always like to have an extra one or two of at home that are specific to the bikes I have.

Disc brakes are being found on more and more modern bikes, especially hydraulic disc brakes. If your hydraulic disc brakes feel soft or your brake levers come all the way to the bars, then it’s likely time to call the local shop for a brake bleed. With that being said, there are still things you can check on these brakes at home. While the wheels are in the bike, spin the wheel to eye up the rotor—it can help to have a flashlight here. While the wheel is spinning, check if you hear the rotor hitting the brake pad. If it’s hitting, you will hear a noticeable noise, and you can confirm by shining the flashlight between the rotors and pads. If it’s rubbing, the rotor may need to be realigned, or the calipers may need to get reset. Once you check the rotor, then drop the wheel out to check the pads with the flashlight; there should be at least 1.5 mm of meat on the pads.

For rim brake pads, a small turn of your brake’s barrel adjuster will move your pads in or out. Inspect all four of your bike’s brake pads. If the grooves are worn away, it’s time to replace the pads. Make sure that they strike the rim squarely. If not, use an Allen wrench to loosen the nut that secures the pad and reposition it. Squeeze the brake levers to feel the action. The pads should strike the rim well before the levers approach the handlebar. If not, tighten the brake by turning the barrels on the brake calipers. If it’s one piece, turn it counterclockwise until the pads are 1/8- to 1/4-inch away from the rim.

When I’m checking out my brake pads, I will also typically eye up my wheel as it comes through the frame to see if the wheel is out of true. Using the frame as a bit of a guide, it’s easier to see if the wheel has any wobble. If it needs to be realigned and truing a wheel is outside your scope, then see if your local shop has the time to true a wheel before your big ride. This is one more reason why it’s important to run through your bike at least a week before your big ride.

Make sure there is no play in your headset by properly tightening the stem cap: Stand in front of the bike, holding the fork in one hand and the down tube in the other. Push and pull on the fork to check for any movement in the headset. Rotate the fork slowly from side to side to feel for roughness. If it’s loose or tight, loosen the stem bolts, then remove play or tightness by adjusting the Allen screw atop the stem, and finish by securing the stem bolts.

Now check the bottom bracket bearings: Stand beside the frame, hold the crank arms, and push and pull, feeling for play. Most bottom brackets are sealed and reliable. If yours is loose, have a shop remove the crank arms and adjust it.

Once you’ve run through all these steps, take your bike for a short test ride. Shift and brake repeatedly, making adjustments as necessary. Very consciously listen for any rattles or other unwanted noises while you ride. I even like to wear my exact kit I’ll be wearing on the upcoming ride to make sure everything is tested together. Now you’re ready for your big ride—confident that as long as your body cooperates, there is nothing to prevent you from successfully getting to the finish line.

This also ensures your bike gets the love it needs after your big ride.

The Trump administration believes that the former standards set by the previous administration are too high and have forced automakers to produce vehicles that their customers do not want to buy.

“Eliminating the credit trading program restores fairness, puts all automakers on an even playing field, and ensures that manufacturers are spreading fuel-saving technologies throughout their fleets,” reads the White House’s official statement.

For now, the average American is not going to feel the impact of this new rule. Just don’t be surprised if the next time you go to shop for a car, you find yourself looking at some low fuel efficiency numbers and a lack of electric options.

When the Spokesman-Review announced plans to transfer Spokane’s 143-year-old, family-owned newspaper to a nonprofit founded by executive editor Rob Curley, it took over the front page.

“The People’s Paper,” read the April 16, 2025, headline, saying ownership would transfer “to you, the community.”

Key to the plan was Curley, the folksy self-proclaimed “journalism nerd” who burst into Spokane a decade ago, declaring a love for Gonzaga University basketball rivaled only by his love for Disney. The transition to Comma Community Journalism Lab was cast in media industry publications not only as a way to save Spokane’s daily newspaper, but as part of a growing movement to resurrect dying papers by making them less dependent on ads and corporate ownership and more on philanthropy and community partnerships. As proof of concept, the paper had already added several so-called “community-funded” journalists to its staff.

But in the Spokesman-Review newsroom this summer, that hopeful vision ran into persistent concerns about Curley’s leadership. More than a dozen current and former employees, along with contemporaneous records reviewed by InvestigateWest and RANGE, reveal how the newsroom had raised concerns about Curley’s management for years, culminating in his suspension from the paper on Aug. 7 and a vote of no confidence from the union, the Spokane Editorial Society. He is currently on leave as the paper conducts an internal investigation.

Employees said stories that might have angered powerful community figures or potential donors were killed or curtailed, and that they felt he retaliated against workers he viewed as disloyal. Staffers questioned his financial oversight of Comma, which hired his own son at a salary topping most reporters’ and recruited out-of-town executives to serve as top editors before Comma had met its fundraising goal. Current and former Spokesman-Review employees also described workplace complaints involving Curley and one of those editors, including allegations of inappropriate lewd and sexual comments around employees, including teenage interns.

Curley declined to comment for this article. Kirby hung up the call when a reporter pressed him on which claims specifically he believed were inaccurate.

Before joining the Spokesman-Review in 2016, Curley had been praised in some journalism circles as a leader in transitioning struggling newspapers away from print and into the digital world. In naming Curley as the Spokesman-Review’s new top editor, Cowles said Curley had “a proven record of delivering news that matters” and that his “innovative work” at previous newspapers helped them win top journalism awards “including a Pulitzer Prize,” the announcement said.

But reporters at his previous gigs viewed him differently: as a mercurial leader who hyped expensive, ineffective initiatives, sacrificing journalism ethics to keep up cozy relationships with powerful community institutions and politicians. The Orange County Register in Southern California was criticized for favorable coverage of Disney under Curley, who served as the paper’s top editor between 2014 and 2016 and proudly said he wore a Mickey Mouse garment every day. He also faced backlash when he revised an article two months after it had been published at the request of a politician, despite police reports and other records that didn’t support the change. Curley changed the story over the objections of the reporter and did not note the changes for the public with a correction or editor’s note — a decision journalism ethicists called an “unethical overreach.”

Journalists from the Las Vegas Sun in Nevada had their own problems with him. After he left the Sun in 2012, they noticed that Curley kept getting credit for the paper’s Pulitzer Prize in 2009 — credit that Cowles repeated in the 2016 hiring announcement. Sun employees clarified to the Inlander, a Spokane alt-weekly, that Curley, in fact, had nothing to do with the series that won the Pulitzer. Curley said he’d never told anyone he won a Pulitzer, blaming a series of misunderstandings for it showing up time and time again in his biography.

Curley stuck around Spokane, undaunted by the controversies from his past. And with layoffs and buyouts shrinking the newsroom in a struggling industry, Curley two years ago began testing the Comma pitch among staff by signaling that it would save their jobs. Reporters said he told them privately that they’d receive big raises once the transfer happened. If Comma didn’t move forward, he said the Cowles family would likely lay off staff or maybe sell the locally owned paper to one of the hedge fund–owned companies known for downsizing newsrooms, one current employee recalled.

Reporters felt Curley had compromised editorial independence at the Spokesman as well.

Staffers took issue with Curley altering an article in 2025 about a bond proposal from Spokane Public Schools. In the article, reporters wrote a passage explaining how the proposal could raise property taxes. The story had been cleared by the typical story editors, but late the night before the story was published, Curley had a copy editor change that passage “to be more in line with what the school district wanted us to be saying,” said Emry Dinman, one of the article’s two reporters. “My editors were all as outraged as I was.”

Staffers knew Comma was trying to partner with the school district as part of the ownership transition, though Dinman said he didn’t want to speculate on the intentions behind the late-night edit.

Current and former staffers also said Curley didn’t like articles that were critical of Gonzaga, a private university downtown most known for its basketball team that Curley frequently celebrated in his columns.

Rebecca Hansen-White, a former reporter at the Spokesman-Review from 2018 to 2020, said that during the COVID-19 pandemic she wrote a story about Gonzaga’s plans for an isolation facility, which included some light criticism from a local politician for the private university using government money to do so. But the story was never published. When Hansen-White asked what happened, she recalls an editor told her something to the effect of “that may have been one Rob didn’t want to run,” though she was never able to confirm if Curley spiked it himself.

“That was very frustrating. I worked hard on this and I’m going out to the community and I’m getting in these tough conversations with sources,” she said. “And then I come back to the newsroom and find that my work has been changed or removed without an explanation.”

Comma eventually set up shop on the Gonzaga campus in 2024.

The two editors who Curley hired for Comma in fall 2025 did little to ease reporter concerns about outside interference. Donna Wares and Ron Sylvester had both worked with Curley at the Orange County Register, and when Curley brought them into the Spokesman-Review newsroom, an email sent to the staff described their roles until the transfer as “consultants” — Sylvester for “newsroom metrics and trust initiatives,” and Wares for “enterprise story assignment, editing and Northwest Passages author recruitment.” Northwest Passages is the nonprofit’s book club and event series.

That distinction was crucial for Spokesman-Review staffers, who considered it a breach of journalistic integrity to have non-staffers weigh in on editorial decisions, even if it came from people who would one day be their editors.

“We were told through the union that Comma editors would not be able to touch our stories until the transition happened,” said Emily White, a former reporter who left the Spokesman at the end of August.

“They were tampering with our stories with no permission,” White said.

The union filed a grievance with the company, said Thomas Clouse, union president and longtime reporter. “Our understanding was that the publisher dealt with it,” he said.

As Comma took longer than initially expected to reach its $2 million fundraising goal, the transition kept being delayed. The presence of Comma staff in the newsroom, meanwhile, grew louder.

Comma had also hired one of Curley’s sons. Johnathan Curley made a salary of $62,400, according to a 2025 court document, topping the pay of almost every Spokesman-Review reporter. His title at Comma is “technical director,” and he handles video and audio equipment for Comma events. Previously, the 24-year-old worked as a part-time audio-visual technician for Cirque du Soleil in Las Vegas. Johnathan Curley did not respond to requests for comment.

Staffers questioned how much Sylvester and Wares were getting paid to be Comma editors before the transition, and they were concerned that Wares spent half her time in California, flying back and forth on Comma’s dime.

Sylvester lingered in the newsroom, with staff increasingly unclear of his role. Employees described Sylvester as a disorganized and unproductive editor who often shopped online for magician supplies while in the office. He was tasked with managing the summer internship program, they said, but his involvement was so marginal that other staff had to pick up his slack.

“If you ask what his job entailed, I wouldn’t be able to give you an answer,” one staffer said. Sylvester did not respond to requests for comment.

In early August, with the planned transition to Comma just weeks away, the newsroom scrambled to cover wildfires that had caught national attention. The staff worked day and night, eventually breaking the news that one of the blazes was set by an arsonist. Courts reporter Alexandra Duggan, as it turned out, had been one of the first people to see the alleged arsonist near the flames.

On Aug. 4, Sylvester told reporters they should change how they attribute documents in stories. Instead of citing “court documents,” he said they should cite “sworn affidavits.” Despite having no clear editorial role in the Spokesman-Review newsroom, Sylvester went into Duggan’s story that night and made a change reflecting his preference — except in that instance, the record was not in fact an affidavit. It introduced an error into the paper.

Clouse, the union president, said he called a meeting with management to try to understand why Comma editors were meddling with stories again. “That never got resolved,” he said.

Two days later, Duggan, who’d worked 13 straight days, was finishing up another story about the accused arsonist. Sylvester approached her and said, in a low voice, “you’re doing it again,” several employees who witnessed or learned of the altercation told InvestigateWest and RANGE. He’d been referring to her story saying “court documents.”

Duggan walked away. But Sylvester followed, and the two shouted back and forth at each other until other editors stepped in to cool things down.

“I wanted to make sure that our stories were truthful and honest and that they were not being edited without us knowing and without any communication,” Duggan said. “And I just wanted to keep the integrity of the paper.”

Curley sided with Sylvester, telling Spokesman-Review managing editor Lindsey Treffry that night to fire Duggan.

The next morning, during a series of meetings with Cowles, Treffry and other editors shared complaints they had about Curley. Cowles decided to put Curley on administrative leave, revoking his keycard access to the paper’s offices.

A few hours later, however, Curley got in the newsroom again by asking an unsuspecting intern for their keycard. He walked around asking where he could find Treffry, who, according to several staffers there that day, was hiding from Curley in the photo room with other editors. Duggan joined them, and it was there that Duggan learned Treffry saved her job. As they hid, Duggan texted White, who was not in the newsroom at the time. White shared the texts with InvestigateWest and RANGE that described how an HR employee escorted Curley out of the building.

“They chased Rob out of the newsroom,” Duggan texted. “I can’t see because I’m hiding.”

“I’m hysterical,” Duggan wrote, crumpled into a ball and crying.

Duggan said she’d “ride or die for this paper.” She said Curley’s attempt to fire her “felt like a slap in the face — it felt like betrayal.”

In an interview for this story, she said she’ll be grateful for the rest of her life that Treffry had her back.

Former reporter Rachel Alexander, who worked at the Spokesman-Review from October 2014 to July 2018, recalled a pattern of “cronyism and ethics violations.”

Curley excluded one of the employees from important meetings and avoided another employee and gave them “distasteful looks.”

Curley needed to make sure staff was “loyal” to him, employees said. One employee, who requested anonymity for fear of retaliation, said Curley and his allies tried to get them to divulge whether certain Spokesman-Review employees were loyal or not. Staffers said Curley also dangled future “executive” positions to two young women in exchange for their loyalty.

At least one former editorial assistant filed an HR complaint against Curley. He told Lauerman she was “special,” that she could be an “executive lady and make a lot of money,” and that he would be her mentor.

Lauerman felt like she was being manipulated, and she didn’t understand why Curley was flattering her so much to push her into a job that had never been discussed before.

Lauerman remembers the HR employee saying they’d “add it to my list” of complaints about Curley, and Lauerman requested that the complaint be kept quiet until she left the paper months later. She followed up with a letter detailing the experience in writing.

Lauerman said Curley avoided her in the months that followed. In March 2026, as her last day of work at the Spokesman approached, he asked her out to lunch, which Lauerman declined.

“The idea of Comma is a great idea, but I don’t trust Rob Curley to execute that idea,” he said. “I don’t think that he has the best interest of the journalists that he’s supposed to uplift. … I don’t trust him and I question his ethics.”

#1519. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

📅 Oct 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

For more information, go to the OpenAI site.

#3430. The Story of Earl Denman, Tenzing Norgay’s Forgotten Everest Partner

📅 Oct 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In the famous photograph of Tenzing Norgay standing on the summit of Everest on May 29, 1953, he holds his ice axe aloft, strung with flags. What you can’t see is the wool helmet under his hood. It had been left to him by a Canadian named Earl Denman, who six years earlier had convinced Tenzing to join him on a doomed, poorly equipped, illegal attempt on the mountain from Tibet.

Jennifer Peedom’s new film, Tenzing, recreates the triumphant 1953 conquest of Everest, with Genden Phuntsok in the title role, Tom Hiddleston as Edmund Hillary, and Willem Dafoe as expedition leader John Hunt. Denman’s name doesn’t appear in the cast list.

I first came across Earl Denman in 1997, in Jon Krakauer’s Into Thin Air, which describes him as a “poverty-stricken Canadian engineer” who “somehow” talked two Sherpas, one of them Tenzing, into accompanying him to Everest in 1947. Denman had written his own account of the attempt, Alone to Everest, published in 1954 and long out of print. The photograph facing the title page shows a lean man in a pale hooded smock on what looks like a ship’s deck, a coil of rope beside him, staring into the distance. With the hood pulled up and tied under his chin, he resembles a cross between Lawrence of Arabia and John Cleese.

Earl Denman was born on December 11, 1914, at Tod Inlet on Vancouver Island, Canada, where the Butchart family, founders of the famous Butchart Gardens outside Victoria, B.C., ran a cement works. His father worked there as an engineer until rheumatoid arthritis left him unable to walk. When Earl was about four, the family returned to England, as he later put it, “in the forlorn hope of gaining relief for a completely paralyzed father.” His father was bedridden for “seven painful years,” Denman wrote, nursed by Earl’s mother, and the family’s savings were “whittled away to nothing.”

According to Denman’s great-niece, Lisa Jones, the boys were sent to live with other people while their father was ill. Earl went to a couple named Topps at Heckington Fen in Lincolnshire. How long he stayed isn’t clear.

By his own account, Denman was “never really happy” in England. “How could I have been?” he wrote. Canada was another matter. “Childhood glimpses of the Canadian Rockies formed a lasting impression,” he wrote, “a first link in a chain of events leading from one mountain range to another.” Even as an adult, he still felt a nostalgia for his birthplace, “which distance and time cannot remove.” He grew up, in his own words, with “the far-away look of a dreamer.”

After five years as an apprentice electrical engineer, Denman departed for Africa and secured a job in the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. “I had dreamed of mountains, but had to be content with a barren desert,” he wrote.

Denman hamming it up in the ‘barren desert.’

On his first leave, he rode fish lorries south to the Ruwenzori, the Mountains of the Moon, in the Belgian Congo. Camped alone below the snow line, he “first came to think about Everest,” and decided he wanted to be the first to climb all eight of the Virunga Mountains, a chain of volcanoes straddling the borders of today’s Democratic Republic of Congo, Rwanda, and Uganda.

After serving “rather unspectacularly” in the Second World War, he set out from Bulawayo, in what was then Southern Rhodesia, for the Virungas, climbing them with local porters and guides and, often, no boots. Years later, he still claimed to be “the only one to have climbed all eight.”

Denman meant to climb Everest the same way, with a couple of local men and no army of porters. To him, the mountain was “essentially a mountaineering project pure and simple,” as he put it in his book, “wholly a matter of man pitting himself against the highest mountain in the world.”

Anything grander was out of reach, anyway. By his own count, he had “only £250 left over from my Virunga expedition” to cover his equipment, food, and the whole journey from Bulawayo to Darjeeling and back. His camera was “a cheap second-hand one, with punctured bellows,” and a watch was beyond his means. Nor did he have permission to enter Tibet.

Denman left Bulawayo in February 1947 and landed in Bombay on March 15. On the long train ride across India, the clickety-clack of the wheels “seemed to say, ‘You fool, turn back. You fool, turn back,'” he wrote.

In Darjeeling, a shop window full of mountain photographs caught his eye, and the name over the door, Paul, reminded him of someone from his reading on Everest: Karma Paul, the interpreter who had worked with the prewar British expeditions. A few questions inside the shop brought Karma Paul himself. He was no climber, but he listened to Denman’s mad plan. His response was one word: “Impossible!”

Karma Paul “took a lot of convincing,” Denman recalled, but told him to come back at 11 the next morning, when, Denman wrote, “he would introduce me to a capable Sherpa who might be willing to go with me.” In the meantime, Denman needed a permit to enter Sikkim, which meant signing a declaration that he would not go beyond Sikkim into Tibet. “I hated the duplicity that was involved,” he wrote, “but it was unavoidable.”

Two men were waiting in Karma Paul’s office the next morning. One was Ang Dawa. The other was Tenzing Norgay, a “Tiger” of the prewar Everest expeditions, who had already climbed to 27,000 feet on the mountain. Denman thought him wiry and vigorous. At first, he noted, Tenzing “remained unsmiling.”

Tenzing, Denman, and Ang Dawa. Photo: earldenman.com

Karma Paul, who called the plan “sheer suicide,” washed his hands of it, so Denman took the Sherpas back to his “bleak little apartment” and was frank about how little he could pay. Then he played his last card. With an official expedition, he told Tenzing, he would be just a porter, left in camp while the sahibs went for the top. With Denman, “If I succeed, you will succeed as well.” After a quiet word with Ang Dawa, Tenzing answered with a half-smile: “We will try.”

In Tiger of the Snows, the autobiography he told to author James Ramsey Ullman (Tenzing never learned to read or write), he admits he knew better. “Any man in his right mind would have said no,” he said. “But I couldn’t say no. For in my heart, I needed to go, and the pull of Everest was stronger for me than any force on earth.”

Because of that declaration, secrecy mattered. The three men left Darjeeling separately, met at an agreed spot outside town, and took the standard expedition route through Sikkim. At the Tibetan frontier, Tenzing steered them off the patrolled trade route and over a little-used pass.

On the trek to Everest. Photo: earldenman.com

By his account, hardly a day went by without trouble. Food ran short. At one point, the loaded yaks tumbled down a slope, very nearly taking the party’s supplies with them. When a patrol stopped them and ordered them back, they talked their way out of arrest, made a show of retreating, and then looped around. After that, they gave every town and village a wide berth until they reached the Rongbuk Monastery, at the foot of Everest’s North Side.

Tenzing hadn’t seen the mountain in nine years. “The old excitement returned, as strong as ever,” he said. “Here I was again where I was meant to be.”

From Rongbuk, they moved quickly, carrying everything themselves and pitching a new camp every day. Denman later calculated that the 1933 British expedition had needed 16 to 24 days to get as far as they got in four. Speed was their only advantage. Their clothes weren’t windproof, they had run out of tea, and their two tents, Tenzing recalled, “gave as much protection as a sheet of paper.”

Denman hardly slept. On his second night on the mountain, he wrote, sleep “would not come,” and he lay pummeling himself for warmth and wondering whether dawn would ever arrive. The Sherpas, meanwhile, hauled the loads without complaint. Reaching one camp after barely resting all the way up, they set them down, he wrote, “as though they were only slight encumbrances, like a load of children on a zoo elephant.”

That night, the weather turned. A “savage wind, like the breath from a Tibetan mastiff,” came up, and Tenzing shared his sleeping bag with him. Lying there, Denman wrote, “I realized properly, for the first time, how miserably poor we were and what little chance we stood.” By then, food had lost its taste, apart from cheese and slabs of milk chocolate studded with nuts and raisins. He kept the chocolate within reach at night, peeling off his gloves for every bite and sucking it slowly off the nuts to pass the hours. Outside, the ice pinnacles cracked “like pistol shots.”

At the foot of the North Col, they wrestled their single tent into the wind, piling rocks on the guy ropes where the pegs wouldn’t go into the frozen ground. The col itself looked less frightening than Denman had expected. Three men could climb it safely, he thought, but not in that wind. That night, he prayed, “Help me to do the right thing. Do not rob me of the chance to go forward, but give me strength to go back if I must.” He was frank about how little he cared what happened to him. “My whole life was Everest,” he wrote. “I did not want to live if we failed.”

The tent flapped all night, and two pegs tore loose. By morning, he admitted, his prayers had turned to “bitter oaths.” They drank hot cocoa at first light. Nobody discussed what to do next, but all three began taking down the tent, and they went up rather than down. “We would not give in so easily,” Denman wrote. On the slope below the col, the wind returned at full strength, and the sky turned to “an unrelieved dark mass, almost black.”

They turned around at about 23,500 feet by Denman’s reckoning. Jon Krakauer, in Into Thin Air, puts it closer to 22,000. Their hands were “on the verge of frostbite.” “We held our consultation, if it could be called a consultation,” Denman wrote. “We had played our hand.”

Denman, Tenzing said later, was “a determined, almost fanatic man with a fixed idea. But he was not crazy.” He agreed to turn back, and for that, Tenzing said, “I am as grateful as for anything that has happened in my life.” They came down almost at a run. Denman was in a hurry to put the mountain behind him, Tenzing remembered, “as if it were a thing he no longer loved but hated.”

His boots fell apart, and for a few days he walked barefoot. “My bitterness was like an open wound as we hastened down,” Denman wrote. He knew they had “attempted more than was humanly possible,” and that there was no disgrace in that. He felt humiliated all the same. The whole trip, from Darjeeling to Everest and back, took five weeks.

Denman returned in January 1948 with warmer boots and proper sleeping bags. What he didn’t have was a permit. Tenzing met him in Darjeeling, “all smiles,” assuming he had one, and when Denman said he didn’t, the smile faded. Tenzing told him it was too dangerous. Tibetan authorities were now looking for them, and neither Sherpa would risk another illegal crossing. So, a dejected Denman left all his gear with Tenzing, including a wool balaclava, in “the unwarranted hope” of a permit within a year or two. “It was the last time I was to see either of them,” he wrote.

Tenzing didn’t give up on his Canadian friend. That June, his wife wrote that Tenzing was in Lhasa, “trying his utmost to procure a pass for you.” Tenzing, who had his mail read to him and dictated his replies, kept writing. Of the gear Denman had left him, he wrote in a later letter, “Now and then I use them.” In January 1953 came another: “If you get permission, I rather prefer to accompany you than any other party.” Denman quoted it in an application to climb Everest in 1954 with a party of Sherpas led by Tenzing. Success, if it came, he wrote, “would be shared equally.”

Tenzing, right, and his wife. Photo: earldenman.com

It didn’t come to that. Tenzing joined John Hunt’s British expedition instead, his seventh trip to Everest, or “six times, including Denman,” by his own count. Looking back, he called Denman “a strange man” but “a brave one, a man with a dream,” and was sorry he had never come closer to realizing it. “In 1953, when I gained the top of Everest,” Tenzing said, “I was wearing a woolen balaclava helmet that he had left to me; so at least a little part of him has reached his goal.”

Denman, for his part, wrote that he was delighted a Sherpa had been given the first chance at the top, and especially this one, a man “for whom I have such a special affection and admiration.”

Denman did get to see the first film about Tenzing’s historic climb. At a private preview of Conquest of Everest, reported in the Johannesburg Sunday Express in January 1954, he said, in what the reporter called “his quiet, unassuming voice,” “I’d like to have another crack at her. Perhaps I will, too.” But with Tibet closed to him, he admitted the following year that he had “neither chance nor money” to go back.

Denman at work on a Volkswagen. Photo: earldenman.com

In South Africa, Denman wrote wildlife talks for SABC radio and newspaper columns against apartheid and, by 1961, worked at a Volkswagen assembly plant. That year, Tenzing got back in touch after a long silence. “Believe me, I often think about you and talk to my friends about our days in the mountains together,” he wrote. As for the factory job, “A man of the mountains cannot be happy with machines.”

Denman was eventually forced out of South Africa, which he blamed in part on those columns. His wife, Mary, stayed behind, and he later settled in New Zealand, where he received a letter from Tenzing in 1976, addressed to “My dear friend Denman.” Of their trip through Tibet, Tenzing wrote, “It was rather a tuf time, but I enjoyed it.” Denman kept writing and, in 1985, published The Fiercest Fight, an account of the struggle against apartheid.

Late in life, Denman was hit from behind by a car while cycling into town and never fully recovered. He died in December 1994, two days before his 80th birthday, from injuries his family chiefly blames on the accident.

Lisa Jones, a therapist in Bristol, England, learned about Earl Denman from her father, Graham, his nephew. She and her son, Frank, now look after his papers, which she keeps in a trunk in her living room. The family remembers a grumpy, solemn man, insular and quiet, with “no belly laughs.” Frank, who read more of the papers than anyone, called him privileged and arrogant. “I’m not sure he did it all through charm and good looks,” Jones says.

As a therapist, she sees a link to his childhood. For a boy of four sent away from his parents, she says, it would have felt like abandonment, which she believes left him wary of people and more at home in wild places. But she doesn’t think fame was the point. “It feels like he’s doing it for him.”

In 1984, Toronto filmmaker Vic Sarin wrote to Denman about making a documentary. His last question was the hardest: “Did you feel that you had failed or won in your attempt?” Denman had answered it, in a way, 30 years earlier in his book. Failure, he wrote, “though painful, is better than frustrated longing.”

Alone to Everest reads like something from the age of Burton and Livingstone, earnest and old-fashioned, and what stayed with me was its wistfulness. Every decade or so, around the anniversary of the climb, I pitched his story. Nobody seemed interested. Lisa Jones knows the feeling. The rest of the family, she says, showed little interest in the papers. “There’s a little pilot light here,” she says, “and I’m going to ignite it again.” Earl Denman may not be in Tenzing. But he is in that famous photograph of his old friend, if you know what you’re looking at: the wool helmet on Tenzing’s head, at 29,000 feet, where its owner never got to go.
