A hands-on guide with Gemma 4, Ollama, and the OpenAI Agents SDK

An agent can decide which tools to call and how to use their results.

This flexibility is certainly useful, but it also creates a need for boundaries during execution.

Probably the first thing you will think of is adding instructions to describe the behavior you want. But instructions are only a soft form of enforcement; there is no guarantee that the agent will always observe them.

Guardrails, on the other hand, provide stronger control. Concretely, they provide a runtime checkpoint where we can validate an agent’s input or output, accept/reject/modify it before the run continues.

In this post, we'll show how we can add a runtime guardrail using the OpenAI Agents SDK.

We'll use Gemma 4 E4B running locally through Ollama to show that these guardrails also apply to agents powered by locally hosted models, not necessarily only the OpenAI models.

Learn this step by step with the interactive AI Agents roadmap.

A typical agent run moves through several stages. Starting from receiving a request, the agent needs to decide what to do, then it will call some tools, process their results, and eventually produce a response.

The OpenAI Agents SDK allows us to place guardrails at different points along the agentic problem-solving process:

Input guardrails: check the request before the agent starts.

Tool-input guardrails: check a tool call before the tool receives its arguments.

Tool-output guardrails: check a tool’s result before it returns to the agent.

Output guardrails: check the agent’s final response before it is shown to the user.

This categorization lets us enforce a boundary at the point where it matters. Depending on the position and implementation, a guardrail can allow the agent to continue, or reject a value, or replace it with a modified version.

#752. Could lowering high blood pressure be as simple as stimulating your ear?

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A small device that delivers gentle electrical pulses through the ear could offer a new approach to treating stubborn high blood pressure. In a clinical trial led by researchers at Queen Mary University of London, the technology proved safe, well tolerated, and practical for patients to use at home.

Although the treatment did not significantly lower blood pressure compared with a placebo device across all participants, researchers observed encouraging results in patients with more severe hypertension. The findings suggest that stimulating nerves in the ear could eventually help certain people whose blood pressure remains elevated despite taking medication.

The results come from the SCRATCH-HTN trial, published in Hypertension, a journal of the American Heart Association. Researchers emphasize that larger studies are needed to determine whether the approach can provide meaningful benefits and which patients might respond best.

How Ear Nerve Stimulation Could Lower Blood Pressure

The experimental treatment uses a technique called transcutaneous autonomic neuromodulation (tAN), which aims to influence the autonomic nervous system. This network of nerves automatically controls essential bodily functions, including heart rate, digestion, and blood pressure.

By delivering mild electrical signals to nerves in the ear, researchers hope to influence the body's natural mechanisms for regulating blood pressure without requiring an invasive procedure.

The handheld device, developed by Afferent Medical Solutions, uses two small clips positioned on the tragus, the small flap of cartilage located just in front of the ear canal. These clips deliver gentle electrical stimulation, allowing patients to administer the treatment themselves without visiting a clinic.

Clinical Trial Tests the Device in 63 Patients

The clinical trial, funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR), enrolled 63 people with uncontrolled hypertension. All participants were already taking at least one medication to lower their blood pressure, but their readings remained above recommended levels.

Researchers randomly divided the participants into two groups. One received active nerve stimulation, while the other used a sham device designed to serve as a comparison. This approach helped investigators assess whether the electrical stimulation produced benefits beyond those associated with using the device itself.

Participants used their assigned devices for 30 minutes daily during the first two weeks. For the following 10 weeks, they reduced their sessions to once per week.

The results showed that the treatment was safe and generally comfortable for participants. Most also followed the prescribed schedule, suggesting that the technology could be practical for regular use at home.

However, when researchers compared blood pressure changes across the entire study population, they found no statistically significant difference between patients receiving active stimulation and those using the sham device.

Promising Results in Patients With Severe Hypertension

Despite the lack of a significant overall benefit, the researchers identified a potentially important finding among participants who entered the trial with more severe hypertension.

A prespecified subgroup analysis, meaning the researchers had planned this comparison before examining the results, revealed a greater reduction in blood pressure among these patients when they received active nerve stimulation rather than the sham treatment.

This finding raises the possibility that the technique may work better for people with particularly difficult-to-control blood pressure. However, the trial was relatively small, and the subgroup results require confirmation in larger studies before any conclusions can be drawn about its effectiveness.

Dr. Ajay Gupta, Chief Investigator, Clinical Reader at Queen Mary University of London and Consultant Physician at Barts Health NHS Trust, said:

"This is an exciting, highly patient-acceptable technology that could ultimately provide a cheap, scalable solution for millions. Although the trial did not achieve significance across the broad unselected group, those with more severe hypertension exhibited a clear blood pressure reduction in the active group compared to sham.

"As the first and most thorough study of its kind, this points towards important new research directions that could help us understand whether non-invasive neuromodulation can benefit particular groups of patients with uncontrolled hypertension."

A Potential New Option for Hard-to-Control Blood Pressure

High blood pressure, also known as hypertension, is a major contributor to cardiovascular disease and increases the risk of heart attacks and strokes. It places additional strain on blood vessels and the heart, potentially causing serious damage over time.

Although medications help many patients manage their condition, a substantial number continue to have blood pressure readings above recommended targets even while receiving treatment.

This ongoing challenge has encouraged scientists to explore additional approaches that could complement existing therapies.

Ear nerve stimulation is one such possibility. Because the device is small, does not require surgery, and can be used at home, it could offer a convenient option if future research establishes that it reliably lowers blood pressure in certain patients.

For now, the findings represent an early step rather than proof of an effective new treatment. The researchers say larger clinical trials will be essential to confirm the encouraging results in patients with severe hypertension and identify which groups, if any, could benefit most from this approach.

#723. A hidden bias in human language challenges 70 years of science

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers at the University of Vermont have discovered a surprising pattern in human language that challenges a scientific theory widely accepted for more than 70 years. Their findings suggest that the meaning of words is shaped by something more fundamental than emotion: our understanding of power, danger, and order.

The study, published in Science Advances, introduces a new approach called "ousiometrics," which uses mathematical methods to measure the essential meaning of language. Its most striking finding is that the words people use show a consistent and widespread preference for safety over danger.

This hidden safety bias could transform how researchers study language, understand human behavior, and develop artificial intelligence systems that interpret what people say and write.

Scientists Discover a Hidden Safety Bias in Human Language

Since the 1950s, much of the scientific understanding of word meaning has relied on a framework developed through pioneering research by psychologist Charles Osgood and others. Known as the VAD model, it describes meaning through three emotional dimensions: valence (positive vs. negative), arousal (excited vs. calm), and dominance (controlling vs. submissive).

This approach became influential in psychology, linguistics, and artificial intelligence, particularly in systems designed to identify the emotional tone of written language.

However, the University of Vermont researchers found evidence that this long-established model overlooks important aspects of how meaning is organized.

Their analysis examined billions of individual uses of more than 20,000 words across a wide range of real-world texts. Using advanced computational techniques, the team identified a different underlying structure. They found that the three dimensions in the traditional VAD model are not independent of one another, as previously assumed.

Instead, their results indicate that word meaning can be more effectively described through three separate dimensions: power (weak vs. powerful), danger (safe vs. dangerous), and structure (ordered vs. chaotic).

The research, supported by the US National Science Foundation, Google, MassMutual, and other funders, found that this new framework accounted for more than 90% of the variation in measured word meaning. The traditional VAD approach explained approximately 72%.

That improvement could be especially important as large language models, automated content moderation, and other AI technologies increasingly depend on understanding human communication.

Why Human Language Favors Safety Over Danger

One of the study's most unexpected discoveries emerged when the researchers examined how words actually appear in everyday communication.

Across books, news reporting, social media, and spoken language, words associated with safety appeared much more frequently than those associated with danger.

This finding offers a new explanation for a familiar phenomenon called the Pollyanna principle, which describes the tendency for human language to lean toward positive rather than negative words.

For decades, researchers generally interpreted this pattern as evidence that people favor positive emotional expressions. The new analysis suggests that something more fundamental may be responsible.

According to the researchers, the apparent preference for positivity largely reflects an underlying preference for safety.

"The Pollyanna principle's positivity bias," the study concludes. "is, in fact, a one-dimensional projection of an underlying safety bias."

"This is a big observation that comes out of this work," said Peter Dodds, director of the UVM's Complex Systems Institute and senior author of the study. "Expressions of safety are crucial to all language."

Could Human Language Be Shaped by Survival?

The discovery raises an intriguing possibility: the structure of human language may reflect evolutionary pressures associated with staying alive.

From this perspective, words do more than communicate feelings. They also help people recognize threats, evaluate uncertainty, establish trust, and coordinate actions with others.

Being able to distinguish between safety and danger is essential to survival. The researchers suggest that this basic human priority may also influence how meaning is organized in language.

Throughout daily life, people constantly exchange information about whether individuals, places, situations, and behaviors are threatening or safe. The new findings suggest that these distinctions may be more central to communication than previously recognized.

They also offer a different interpretation of why positive language is so common. Rather than simply expressing happiness or approval, many positive words may convey a sense of security, stability, or predictability.

Julia Zimmerman, a postdoctoral researcher in UVM's Computational Story Lab and a co-author of the study, said the three newly identified dimensions reflect experiences that are fundamental to human life.

"Power, danger, and structure," she said, "are relevant to every person that's ever lived."

Researchers have also long recognized that people tend to favor language associated with goodness and low aggression. The team argues that these familiar tendencies may arise from the same deeper pattern.

"We now understand," the team writes, that these are "shadows of an underlying linguistic safety bias."

Although the results are consistent with a possible connection between language and survival, they do not establish that evolutionary pressures directly caused the safety bias.

A Discovery That Could Change How AI Understands Language

The findings have implications that extend well beyond linguistics.

Many artificial intelligence systems use sentiment analysis to determine whether language expresses positive, negative, or neutral feelings. Some approaches also draw on the traditional VAD framework to measure emotional meaning.

If that framework does not fully capture the underlying structure of language, systems built around it could overlook important distinctions in what people communicate.

The researchers suggest that incorporating power, danger, and structure could eventually help AI models interpret language more accurately and explain their assessments more clearly. This may be particularly useful when analyzing trust, risk, and decision-making.

In linguistics, the findings offer a different way to understand the foundations of meaning. Instead of treating emotional tone as the primary organizing principle, the new framework emphasizes distinctions related to strength, safety, and predictability.

Psychology could also be affected. Decades of studies have used the VAD model to investigate emotions, perception, and behavior. A different set of fundamental dimensions could encourage researchers to reconsider how some of those findings are interpreted.

The discovery may also interest neuroscientists. The human brain is highly sensitive to threats and signals of safety, and the newly identified linguistic bias suggests that these biological priorities may be reflected in language. This potential connection could help researchers explore relationships between brain activity and symbolic communication, although further research would be needed to establish them.

A New Tool Measures the Hidden Meaning of Words

To investigate these patterns, the researchers created analytical methods designed to measure meaning across enormous collections of written and spoken language.

One of their central innovations is the "ousiometer," a tool that rapidly evaluates the essential meaning of large bodies of text and produces an average meaning score.

(The word "ouisa" comes from Ancient Greek and is a root for the English word "essence.")

The ousiometer builds on an earlier tool developed by the research team called the "hedonometer" (a happiness meter). While that instrument measures the overall emotional tone of language, the new approach examines broader patterns of meaning.

The researchers demonstrated that the ousiometer can analyze material from remarkably different sources, including Jane Austen's novels, Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stories, the New York Times, Wikipedia, Twitter, and transcripts of talk radio programs.

The tool can also track how meaning shifts as a story unfolds.

For example, the researchers analyzed an English translation of Victor Hugo's Les Misérables and mapped its changing meaning throughout the novel.

They called this changing pattern an "ousiometric trajectory." The visualization resembles the twisting shape of a multicolored protein, with the narrative moving across a grid based on four contrasting pairs: dangerous/safe, weak/powerful, gentle/aggressive, and bad/good.

By following this path, researchers can observe how the underlying meaning of the text changes from one section of the story to another.

Why Counting Word Frequency Changes the Results

Another important innovation involved distinguishing between words themselves and how often people actually use them.

In linguistics, researchers describe individual words as categories called "types," while each appearance of a word in text is known as a "token."

(For example, as a category "apple" is a type and every time the word "apple" is used in a sentence is a token.)

This distinction matters because a word that appears millions of times contributes much more to everyday communication than one that is rarely used.

Earlier research often gave each word equal importance, regardless of its frequency. The Vermont team instead incorporated how often words appear in real communication, allowing patterns to emerge that might otherwise remain hidden.

This approach was particularly important for identifying the widespread preference for safety-related language.

The ten-member research team was led by Peter Dodds and Chris Danforth, professors in UVM's College of Engineering and Mathematical Sciences. Their collaborators included researchers from the Santa Fe Institute, the Complexity Science Hub in Austria, Howard Hughes Medical Center, the University of California, Berkeley, the University of Adelaide, and MassMutual Data Science.

Rethinking What Human Language Reveals About Us

Understanding this hidden safety bias could have consequences for how researchers study communication across society.

If words consistently reflect a preference for safety, that tendency may influence how stories are told, how information spreads, and how people interpret events and experiences.

The findings could eventually inform research into political communication, mental health messaging, and AI systems designed to interact with people.

More broadly, the study challenges the idea that human language can be understood primarily through emotions such as happiness, fear, and excitement.

Instead, the researchers propose that meaning has a deeper structure connected to power, danger, and order. Their findings suggest that language may reflect the challenges humans face when navigating uncertain environments, maintaining relationships, and identifying threats.

By revealing this previously overlooked structure, the team offers a new way to understand what words communicate and why certain kinds of meaning dominate everyday conversation. Human language, their research suggests, may be more closely connected to our fundamental need for safety than scientists have traditionally recognized.

#577. You Could Unlock Deeper Parts of Reality by Linking Your Mind With This Futuristic Device, Scientists Believe

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This could finally unravel one of the hardest problems in science.

Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

Some researchers believe consciousness is a byproduct of the constantly firing web of neurons hiding inside our craniums. However, others believe something more theoretical must be involved to explain our complex perception—quantum physics. Hartmut Neven, PhD, head of Google’s Quantum Artificial Intelligence Lab, is one of those quantum consciousness believers, and he says there’s a way we can test the theory: by entangling a brain with a quantum computer.

If scientists were able to achieve the feat, not only would they be able to prove where consciousness arises from, but also expand the mind to a whole different level. In a 2024 paper published in the peer-reviewed journal Entropy, Neven proposes his own experiment to test the idea of quantum consciousness.

Neven’s team assumes our brains are made of tiny pieces of information called quantum bits, or qubits, which are the basic units of quantum computing (think: qubits are to quantum computing what 0s and 1s are to binary code). Theoretically, there would be a certain number of qubits in a participant’s brain. If researchers managed to entangle—or intrinsically link—the brain with a quantum computer, they could create an “expanded quantum superposition” involving the total qubits in the brain plus the qubits in the computer, which should result in the participant reporting a richer experience, proving that consciousness is quantum.

Expanding your consciousness wouldn’t literally look like making your brain bigger; instead, you would gain a richer experience of the world around you, similarly to how perception is changed by psychedelics, Neven explained in an interview with New Scientist. If scientists managed to successfully entangle a brain with a quantum computer, it could lead to higher levels of thinking, creativity, and understanding.

Neven’s quantum theory of consciousness builds upon a framework first proposed by English mathematician Roger Penrose, PhD. In his 1989 book, The Emperor’s New Mind, Penrose introduced a controversial but highly impactful theory of consciousness called Orchestrated Objective Reduction, or Orch OR. In it, Penrose argued consciousness isn’t computational, that you can’t force a brain to perform consciousness in the same way you would make a machine complete a task. At the same time, he suggested that classical physics could not entirely explain consciousness.

Instead, Penrose turned to quantum physics for a plausible explanation. His Orch OR theory supposes that consciousness involves a principle called quantum superposition, in which an object momentarily exists in multiple states at once. The most famous example of this paradox is Schrödinger’s cat. In the thought experiment, there is a cat in an opaque box. As a viewer, you have no way of knowing the state of the cat inside, so it simultaneously exists as both dead and alive. In terms of binary code, a superposition suggests something could momentarily exist as both 0 and 1. Penrose argues that consciousness forms when a superposition collapses during measurement, or when you realize the state of an object. In the case of Schrödinger’s cat experiment, this would be when you open the box and observe the animal inside.

While Penrose’s theory is valuable, Neven argues that it’s more productive to flip the framework on its head. According to Neven, consciousness occurs when a superposition forms rather than when it collapses.

“When [Penrose] associated conscious moments with the collapse of superpositions, this opened the possibility of faster-than-light communication, which goes against fundamental rules of physics,” Neven tells New Scientist. “I don’t like this—I’m the more orthodox physicist on this point. But if, instead, we say that a conscious moment is experienced when a superposition forms, not when a superposition collapses, then this challenge with faster-than-light communication goes away.”

Vlatko Vedral, PhD—an Oxford quantum physicist and frequent Popular Mechanics contributor—suggests a similar idea. “What if our biological makeup limits how creative we can be?” he writes in an article. “I envision quantum chips designed to suppress the ‘noise’ that induces introspection, allowing a longer interference period for our intuitive thoughts to develop.”

Vedral goes on to explain that, once we learn how the brain stores and manipulates quantum effects, we could theoretically create a device that interferes with them. Much like Neven explained, Vedral hypothesizes that this could help enhance creative thinking. He wonders if humans could become more adept at problem-solving or even “see further domains of reality that would otherwise remain hidden.”

Brain-entanglement experiments seem like they could finally shed some light on the nature of consciousness—but, like most research pertaining to consciousness, it’s much easier said than done. Neven himself even says actually conducting his experiment “is technically still very challenging.” Vedral also concedes that, at least for now, his hypothesis might just be wishful thinking. Another sweeping critique of the idea that our brain hosts quantum processes is that it may be too warm and wet to do so.

Should scientists eventually figure out how to entangle a human brain with a quantum computer and prove consciousness occurs at the quantum level, the possibilities could be endless—your favorite ice cream flavor may end up tasting a whole lot sweeter or you might unlock a higher level of being.

#741. NASA’s asteroid Bennu samples reveal a 4.5-billion-year-old secret

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Samples collected from asteroid Bennu by NASA are revealing surprising new details about how our Solar System formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago. A new chemical analysis challenges earlier theories about the asteroid's birthplace and suggests that Jupiter played an important role in shaping the materials from which Bennu and other ancient objects formed.

Bennu completes one orbit around the Sun every 1.2 years and makes a relatively close approach to Earth about every six years, coming within roughly 300,000 kilometers (186,000 miles). NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission traveled to the asteroid, collected material from its surface, and successfully delivered the samples to Earth in 2023.

On September 23, 2023, a capsule carrying approximately 120 grams of asteroid material landed in the Utah desert. Scientists around the world subsequently received small portions of the precious cargo for detailed study.

Among them was Maria Schönbächler, Professor of Isotope Geochemistry at ETH Zurich, whose laboratory received half a gram of Bennu material. Her team immediately began examining the samples for clues about the asteroid's history.

The research, now published in Science Advances, has uncovered a distinctive chemical signature that not only helps identify Bennu's origins but also provides new evidence about the processes that shaped the early Solar System.

Bennu's Chemical Fingerprint Reveals Its Cosmic Relatives

To investigate Bennu's origins, the ETH Zurich researchers examined isotopes of three elements: iron, titanium, and chromium.

Isotopes are different forms of the same chemical element that contain varying numbers of neutrons, giving them slightly different masses. By measuring the relative amounts of these isotopes, scientists can identify a material's chemical fingerprint. These patterns provide clues about where an asteroid's ingredients originated and, in some cases, when they formed.

The analysis revealed that iron and titanium are distributed remarkably evenly throughout Bennu's material. It also uncovered a close chemical relationship between Bennu, the asteroid Ryugu, and a rare group of primitive meteorites known as CI meteorites.

CI meteorites are carbon-rich remnants of the early Solar System that have undergone relatively little alteration over billions of years. They are exceptionally uncommon among meteorites recovered on Earth.

Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites share strikingly similar isotopic fingerprints, suggesting that their original materials came from the same reservoir of ancient cosmic dust. Their chemical signatures also differ substantially from those of other known asteroids, meteorite groups, and planets.

These similarities offer an important clue about where these objects formed and what conditions existed during the Solar System's earliest stages.

Scientists Discover Bennu's Unexpected Birthplace

For years, researchers generally believed that primitive asteroids like Bennu originated in the outer Solar System, perhaps in the same distant regions where comets formed. They also suspected that Bennu's original material came together relatively late in the Solar System's development.

The new findings challenge both assumptions.

Instead, the researchers propose that the material that became Bennu, Ryugu, and CI meteorites accumulated near a boundary known as the water-ice line.

This boundary marks the region in the disk surrounding the young Sun where temperatures were low enough for water vapor to freeze into ice. Around 4.5 billion years ago, it may have been a particularly important meeting place for material traveling from different parts of the developing Solar System.

Dust originating in both the inner and outer regions mixed near this boundary. Water ice helped the smallest particles stick together, allowing them to gradually accumulate into larger bodies.

"Bennu is a hybrid: the material does not clearly match either the inner or the outer Solar System," says Schönbächler.

Its unusual chemical composition suggests that Bennu formed from a combination of materials originating in both regions rather than from a single, isolated source.

Jupiter May Have Played a Crucial Role in Bennu's Formation

The researchers also developed a scenario explaining how this mixing could have occurred, with Jupiter emerging as a key player.

Jupiter began forming extraordinarily early in Solar System history, within approximately one million years of the Sun's birth. At that time, the young Sun was surrounded by a rotating disk of gas, dust, and ice.

Within this disk, countless small particles gradually collided and stuck together, building larger objects through a process called accretion. Over time, some of these growing bodies became asteroids and planets.

As Jupiter rapidly increased in mass, its immense gravitational influence began reshaping the distribution of material throughout the disk.

The researchers suggest that the growing gas giant acted somewhat like a structural barrier, preventing most larger particles from crossing its orbital region while still allowing finer dust to move around it.

This fine material, originating from different parts of the disk, could then mix extensively near the water-ice line.

According to the proposed scenario, the parent bodies of Bennu and Ryugu, along with the objects that produced CI meteorites, formed from this thoroughly mixed material.

Jupiter may therefore have helped determine not only where these ancient asteroids formed but also which ingredients became incorporated into them.

Ancient Dust and Ice Reveal the Ingredients That Built Earth

The proposed formation scenario could also explain two of Bennu's most intriguing characteristics: its abundance of water-bearing material and its chemical resemblance to the Sun.

Near the water-ice boundary, ice could evaporate as temperatures changed, releasing water vapor that later condensed again in cooler regions. This process would have helped concentrate water in the material from which Bennu formed.

Meanwhile, Jupiter's influence may have limited the movement of larger particles, leaving the asteroid's parent material dominated by fine dust.

Because these tiny particles could circulate and mix efficiently throughout the disk, they preserved a chemical composition that closely resembles the overall elemental mixture found in the Sun.

The process is somewhat similar to the way fine household dust spreads and settles across many different surfaces rather than remaining in one location.

This extensive mixing could explain why Bennu's material retains such a representative sample of the ingredients present during the Solar System's earliest history.

"Bennu may offer our best glimpse of the original mix of chemical elements from which the terrestrial planets were ultimately built," as Schönbächler states.

Bennu is considered an exceptionally primitive asteroid because its material formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago and has experienced relatively little chemical alteration since then.

That makes its samples particularly valuable for reconstructing the conditions that existed before Earth and the other rocky planets finished forming.

By studying the asteroid's chemistry, scientists can better understand how the Solar System developed and which processes determined the composition of its planets.

Bennu's water-bearing minerals and organic compounds also make it important for investigating how early Earth obtained some of the chemical ingredients necessary for life.

Although these findings do not establish exactly how those materials reached our planet, they provide valuable evidence about where such ingredients originated and how they were distributed throughout the young Solar System.

A New Mission to Mars' Moon Could Reveal More

Despite the new discoveries, several questions remain unanswered.

"We are now wondering whether other asteroids have the same isotopic signature as Bennu and Ryugu," Schönbächler goes on to state.

Researchers also want to determine more precisely how Jupiter influenced the movement of dust and whether its gravitational effects were responsible for favoring the accumulation of particularly fine particles.

Additional asteroid samples could help scientists test the proposed formation scenario and establish whether Bennu's chemical signature is shared by other ancient bodies.

With the Bennu analysis now complete, Schönbächler is looking ahead to another opportunity to examine material from the early Solar System.

Japan's space agency, JAXA, is preparing a sample-return mission to Phobos, one of Mars' two moons. The mission is scheduled to launch at the end of October 2026, with the goal of collecting material from the Martian moon and bringing it back to Earth.

"It would be exciting to also obtain this material to analyse it in my laboratory," she says.

Schönbächler plans to apply to JAXA for access to the samples. However, the scientific community will need to be patient. The capsule carrying material from Phobos is not expected to return to Earth until 2031.

Those future samples could provide another important piece of the puzzle, helping researchers determine how different regions of the early Solar System exchanged material and ultimately produced the planets, moons, and asteroids we observe today.

#782. Astronomers uncover a mysterious magnetic web above the Sun

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Scientists have uncovered a surprisingly complex network of magnetic structures hidden above regions of the Sun that appear relatively calm. Using the Sunrise-III solar observatory, which traveled high into Earth's stratosphere aboard a balloon in 2024, an international research team led by the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan (NAOJ) discovered intricate, thread-like magnetic features that had previously been difficult to observe.

The findings reveal that even the Sun's seemingly quiet regions contain elaborate magnetic structures. They also offer new clues to how energy travels upward through the solar atmosphere and contributes to its heating.

A Hidden Web of Magnetic Threads Above the Sun

Sunrise-III detected numerous thin, elongated magnetic structures woven into a larger network of magnetic fields above a quiet region of the Sun. This network, known as the magnetic canopy, extends through the chromosphere, a layer of the solar atmosphere located above the Sun's visible surface.

The canopy develops as magnetic fields concentrated at the solar surface (photosphere) spread outward and arch upward into the chromosphere (chromosphere). Rather than forming a relatively smooth, uniformly expanding structure, however, the new observations reveal that the canopy contains many narrow, thread-like features.

To investigate how these structures form, researchers used computer simulations that successfully reproduced the patterns recorded by Sunrise-III. The results suggest that movements on the solar surface twist magnetic field lines, creating the intricate structures observed higher in the atmosphere.

The observatory also examined another quiet region near the solar limb (visible edge of the Sun). There, it measured magnetic fields within spicules, narrow jets of solar material that extend upward from the surface. These observations revealed how the distribution of magnetic fields changes with increasing height above the Sun's visible edge.

Why the Sun's Quiet Regions Are Difficult to Study

Although the Sun is constantly active, much of its surface lacks prominent features such as sunspots. Scientists describe these comparatively inactive areas as "quiet." They cover most of the Sun, making them especially important for understanding how our star behaves.

However, quiet does not mean magnetically inactive. These regions contain magnetic fields that are generally weaker than those found in more active areas of the Sun. Their subtle structures are difficult to measure, especially with telescopes on the ground, where turbulence in Earth's atmosphere can blur fine details.

Sunrise-III was designed to overcome this obstacle by carrying a powerful solar telescope far above most of the atmosphere.

A Balloon-Borne Telescope Reveals New Solar Details

Sunrise-III is an international research project built around a 1-meter telescope carried by a high-altitude balloon. During its 2024 mission, the observatory floated approximately 35 kilometers (about 22 miles) above Earth in the stratosphere, where atmospheric interference is greatly reduced.

This elevated vantage point allowed the telescope to capture exceptionally detailed measurements of the Sun's magnetic fields.

The balloon's gondola carried three scientific instruments developed through contributions from different countries. One of these was SCIP (Sunrise Chromospheric Infrared spectroPolarimeter), a near-infrared instrument developed under NAOJ's leadership.

SCIP is designed to measure magnetic fields continuously across different layers of the solar atmosphere, from the photosphere to the chromosphere. This capability allowed researchers to examine how magnetic structures extend and change with altitude rather than studying only a single layer.

New Clues to How the Sun's Atmosphere Is Heated

By observing from the stratosphere, Sunrise-III provided researchers with an unusually detailed picture of magnetic fields above the Sun's quiet regions. Its measurements revealed that the chromosphere contains far more intricate magnetic structures than a simple expanding canopy would suggest.

Understanding these structures could help scientists investigate how energy generated by motions near the solar surface is transferred into higher layers of the atmosphere.

The discovery offers an important step toward explaining how magnetic activity contributes to heating the Sun's atmosphere, including in regions that appear calm when viewed from afar.

#788. These tiny snakes roll four times faster than they slither

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Tiny snakes found in Southeast Asia have developed an extraordinary way to escape danger. Instead of relying on the familiar side-to-side motion of slithering, dwarf reedsnakes can rapidly roll across the ground, reaching speeds about four times faster than their usual movement.

Scientists have now uncovered how these small reptiles accomplish such an unusual feat. By studying video recordings captured from multiple angles, researchers discovered that the snakes coordinate their movements with remarkable precision. While the rear portions of their bodies rotate rapidly, their heads remain relatively steady, facing forward and staying at a nearly constant height.

[See link to video after the end of the article.]

The findings reveal how an animal can achieve an unexpected form of movement through specialized behavior and body control, even without the physical structures normally needed for rolling.

Rolling might seem like an effective way to travel quickly, but it presents a major challenge for living creatures.

A wheel can spin continuously around a fixed axle, allowing it to rotate without disturbing whatever it supports. Animals, however, have no comparable mechanism separating their rotating bodies from their heads.

If an animal's entire body spins together, its head and sensory organs must also rotate. This can make it difficult to maintain balance, adjust body position, and steer in the intended direction. As rolling speed increases, these problems become even harder to manage.

Yet dwarf reedsnakes belonging to the genus Pseudorabdion have found a way around these limitations.

These small Southeast Asian snakes are among the few animals known to deliberately roll as a means of escaping potential predators. Their unusual behavior has appeared in field guides and was previously documented during a field observation, but scientists had not fully understood the movements that made it possible.

Tiny Snakes Roll Four Times Faster Than They Slither

To investigate this remarkable escape strategy, researchers filmed dwarf reedsnakes from several angles and carefully examined how different parts of their bodies moved during rolling.

The results revealed impressive speeds. While rolling, the snakes reached peak forward velocities ranging from 1.72 to 3.69 meters per second, approximately four times their maximum speed when moving through lateral undulation, the familiar side-to-side motion used by most snakes.

Despite traveling so quickly, the snakes were able to follow a narrow racetrack measuring just 20 centimeters across, demonstrating that their rolling movements are not simply uncontrolled tumbling.

Closer examination of the recordings revealed a surprisingly intricate sequence of movements.

Rather than spinning their entire bodies together, the snakes alternate between rolling on one side and swinging a curved section of their bodies downward beside their heads. The researchers called this downward sweeping movement a "stroke."

During each successive cycle, the side touching the ground switches, and the stroke changes direction from left to right or right to left. By repeating this carefully timed pattern, the snakes generate continuous forward rolling motion.

The Remarkable Secret Behind Their High-Speed Escape

One of the most surprising discoveries involved what happens to the snakes' heads during these rapid movements.

Although the rear portions of their bodies undergo substantial rotation, the snakes keep their heads pointed in the direction they are traveling. Rather than spinning along with the rest of the body, the head remains comparatively stable.

The researchers also found that the snakes maintain a relatively consistent head height above the ground. This stabilization becomes especially noticeable at higher speeds, when the snakes tend to hold their heads elevated and minimize upward and downward movements.

Keeping the head steady while the rest of the body rotates may help the animals maintain control and navigate effectively during their rapid escapes.

This ability is particularly remarkable because the snakes accomplish it without a mechanical separation between the rotating and stabilized portions of their bodies.

What Rolling Snakes Reveal About Evolution

The discovery demonstrates how sophisticated coordination can allow animals to overcome physical limitations that might otherwise make certain types of movement impractical.

Although dwarf reedsnakes lack anything resembling a wheel's axle, they effectively divide their bodies into regions with different functions. Much of the body performs the powerful rotational movements needed for propulsion, while the head remains stabilized to support controlled travel.

This unusual strategy allows the snakes to combine speed, directional control, and rapid body rotation in a way that would be difficult to predict from their anatomy alone.

More broadly, the findings highlight an important principle of animal evolution: physical structure does not necessarily determine the full range of movements an animal can perform. Innovative behaviors and precise muscular coordination can open up entirely new possibilities, allowing creatures to accomplish feats that their body shapes might initially suggest are impossible.

While strength training prevents injuries, makes you more durable, and can even boost your running economy, says Des Clarke, an RRCA-certified run coach based in Phoenix, I often spent evenings in the gym lifting weights instead of allowing my body to recover from 40-mile weeks.

As I started training for a half marathon for the second time, I decided to put more of an emphasis on mileage. I didn’t want to nix strength from my schedule entirely, but I had to figure out how to dial back my time with the weights and increase my running time in a safe, balanced way.

Doing so helped me run a 1:46:50 half marathon on a majorly hilly course. Here’s what I did to get there so you can strike a better balance, too.

For anyone running half marathons and marathons (especially someone who’s newer to longer distances like I am), higher mileage weeks give you the base you need to run longer and faster.

“If you want to get better at running, it is true that to a point, you need to run more,” says Clarke. “You’re building the aerobic capacity, strength, and durability that you need to run those distances without getting injured.” That looks like building volume incrementally over time, she says.

Before I started half marathon training, I was running at least two times each week for about eight months. I was pretty consistent, but knew I could afford to add more miles. So before starting my plan, I added another day of running to my schedule, and then when I kicked off the first week, I tackled four days a week of running.

I also added some more miles to my midweek runs. Last training cycle, I usually opted for four-milers, but this time, I bumped it up to five or six miles.

Clarke typically recommends repeating the same weekly mileage for two to three weeks in a row to make sure an athlete has adapted to that particular load before increasing again. I did this during several weeks of my plan.

In that same vein, it’s useful to take a deload week every three weeks or so to better absorb training gains, Clarke says. This involves scaling back your mileage by around 10 to 20 percent before returning to your typical volume. My training plan also included these, allowing me recovery time while pushing mileage.

To balance this increase in mileage, I dropped to two lifting days per week instead of three, cutting down on volume (and consequently my overall time spent in the gym), which Clarke recommends. Last year during marathon training, I spent three days a week lifting weights for about an hour or more. This training cycle, I’ve spent 30 to 45 minutes lifting weights one to two times a week. I split up my usual hour-long routine into two separate days and alternated between them.

I remember how exhausting it felt to spend more than four hours a week strength training on top of 35- to 40-mile weeks during marathon training. Cutting my time in the weight room in half each week (from three hour-long lifts to two 45-minute lifts) not only allowed me to fit in a few extra miles but take my recovery more seriously, too.

When it came time to run a Wednesday track workout, for example, I felt fresh and ready to tackle the intervals with more energy. My two-mile warmups always felt doable and I didn’t dread the speed or feel like I was running on tired legs.

My mood and energy levels changed a lot outside of my runs, too. I noticed that during and after my recovery days, I went to work feeling a lot more focused, happy, and engaged in social activities.

As a result, I felt more refreshed and excited to return to each run—a feeling I didn’t have in my previous training cycle, even when running fewer miles per week.

Before I started training, I planned on aiming for a sub-two-hour half marathon. But after talking to a run coach who believed I was capable of running faster, I decided to aim for something around the 1:45 to 1:50 range instead, about 10 minutes faster than what my goal would’ve been initially.

I ended up finishing a mere few seconds away from my half marathon PR (done on a much flatter course) and felt smooth through every mile.

I also tackled some of the best pacing I’ve ever accomplished: I started out with the 1:50 pacer and naturally moved up after the first three or four miles, averaging an 8:10 pace overall, even with multiple hills throughout the race.
