#275. Russia in Africa: What is Putin hoping to achieve?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Following its invasion of Ukraine and the resulting rift with the West, Russia has declared that it intends to make a "historic return" to Africa. What have we seen of this so far?

The conflict with the West sparked by Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine  prompted Russian President Vladimir Putin to seek support from other parts of the world. Africa has become a focus, with foreign minister Sergei Lavrov visiting the continent increasingly often.

Russian foreign ministry spokesperson Maria Zakharova has described it as a "historic return," and declared no one and nothing could be compared to what Russia did for Africa in the 20th century.

A Russia-Africa summit has been held in Russia at roughly three-year intervals since 2019. The third takes place in Moscow on October 28 and 29. The program focusses on economic cooperation. Other topics range from politics and security to science and culture. But Russia is actively working with only a few African countries, mainly in the north of the continent, and this is reflected in the trade figures.

"South Africa is still Africa's largest national economy, but its volume of trade with Russia is small, far less than with Egypt and Algeria," says Ivan Klyszcz from the International Centre for Defence and Security (ICDS) in Tallinn, Estonia.

"Egypt and Algeria in particular are key partners for Russia. Egypt plays an important role in this regard. Russian diplomats and business representatives are trying to extend relationships with other countries, especially Tanzania and Ethiopia, but with no appreciable success so far."

Egypt is the second-largest economy in Africa, and the bilateral volume of trade with Russia has more than doubled since 2021; in 2025, according to Russia's Federal Customs Service, it was $10.5 billion (€9.4 billion). Consignments of Russian grain are the main drivers, followed by products of the metal industry. Egypt's exports to Russia are primarily fruit and vegetables.

There are investment projects, too. Russia is building a big nuclear power plant in the Egyptian region of El-Dabaa on the Mediterranean coast. Construction is being financed by a Russian loan of 25 billion US dollars, which covers around 85% of the costs.

Egypt is one of the few African countries with which Russia enjoys stable business relationships, as well as having extensive experience of cooperation, says Nikita Smagin, an expert on Russian policy in the Middle East. "In the long term, Egypt could become Moscow's gateway to Africa and to other markets," he says. "But Cairo is pursuing its own interests. It would be too optimistic to hope that Egypt is lending its support purely out of political solidarity."

According to the Russian customs service, around 39% of Russian–African trade in 2025 was with Egypt, and total sales between Russia and all 54 countries of the African continent came to around 27 billion US dollars. Africa's share of Russia's foreign trade is therefore very small (3.9% in 2025), and not at all commensurate with the attention Moscow is devoting to its relations with African countries.

Russia is not a significant trading partner for Africa, either. The most important partner for African countries is the European Union. In 2025, the volume of trade with the EU came to around 385 billion US dollars. African countries' statistics also show that other partners were well ahead of Russia. The volume of trade with China was in excess of 348 billion US dollars in 2025; with the United States it was almost 105 billion, and 103 billion with India.

Fidel Owusu, a Ghanaian expert with Geopolitical Intelligence Services (GIS) in Liechtenstein, comments that Russia's economic presence in Africa has not been as significant as that of China, the United States, India, or other traditional powers like France and Britain. "If you look at the volume of trade and the kind of investment," he says, "in recent times, some middle powers such as Turkey and the UAW are doing far better than Russia."

Beverly Ochieng, a senior analyst with Control Risks, a global risk and strategic intelligence consultancy in Senegal, observes: "Western countries continue to dominate in strategically important areas such as raw material extraction, finance, and the energy sector, even if their political presence in the region is declining." According to her, "Russia will compete more intensively for access to critical mineral resources and energy projects. We see this with lithium projects in Mali as well as with nuclear power initiatives across the continent."

Russia's presence in Africa is more obvious in the military sector than in the fields of trade and investment. Starting with the arrival of the Wagner Group, Russian mercenaries have been supporting African regimes, particularly those of the unstable countries in the Sahel region. In exchange, their associated subsidiaries are granted access to raw materials. Following the death of the Wagner Group leader, Yevgeny Prigozhin, most of these operations were taken over by the Africa Corps, a paramilitary group set up and controlled by Russia's defense ministry.

According to the Congressional Research Service, the think tank of the US Congress, the largest Russian military contingents, each comprising several thousand men, are in the Central African Republic, Mali, and Libya. The Africa Corps is also keen to extend its presence in Burkina Faso and Niger. Russian mercenaries have also been spotted in Equatorial Guinea.

Irina Filatova from the University of Cape Town stresses that there are several reasons why it's important for Russia to maintain a military presence in Africa. "The countries have sizeable deposits of minerals such as gold and uranium. Through them, it's possible to exert influence over Europe's southern borders, including in matters of migration and regional security. With regard to Libya, the country is an important logistical hub for connections with the Sahel and central Africa. Goods can easily be supplied via Libya to countries without access to the sea," Filatova says.

Ultimately, Russia is still one of the biggest suppliers of arms to African countries, even though Russian exports have fallen over the course of the war in Ukraine. The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) estimates Moscow's share of the African market for the years 2021–2025 at 15%, which puts it in third place behind the United States (19%) and China (17%). Between 2020 and 2024, its share was even higher: It was in first place with 21%, followed by China with 18%, and the US with 16%.

Ivan Klyszcz from the ICDS says that while Russia is not a significant economic partner for Africa, the fact that it supplies food and fertilizer and has a military presence in some countries means that Moscow's influence in Africa is far greater than is suggested by the volume of trade and investment alone.

"I believe Russia sees the diplomatic weight of Africa," says Fidel Owusu. "When I talk of diplomatic weight, I mean numbers – the continent has the largest number of countries. Russia wants that kind of support in the UN General Assembly whenever votes are cast. In recent times it has done better than in earlier votes."

In recent years, Russia has been positioning itself on the international stage as representing the interests of the Global South.

"We want to work with Africa, we are working with Africa and we will continue to do so. But our aim is not, as it was for the colonial powers, to make money or steal something from Africa, but to develop together," Russian president Vladimir Putin said recently at a conference of the Moscow-based Valdai Discussion Club.

References to the colonial past appeal to some countries but not others, says Irina Filatova. "Africa is not a country, it's a continent. There are differences in the perception of Russia, as well as in the way Russia positions itself. Also, the views of the political elites are often substantially different from those of ordinary citizens. In South Africa, for instance, the Russian narrative is received very positively. An anti-colonial agenda is also popular in other countries, but it isn't necessarily associated with Russia."

#266. EU's 'Made in Europe' push unnerves global auto suppliers

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In shielding Europe's carmakers from Chinese rivals, the EU risks hurting close partners from Turkey and Morocco to Japan and South Korea. Billions in trade and hundreds of thousands of European jobs are at stake.

It is meant to save Europe's auto industry from China's unfair trade practices. The European Union's Industrial Accelerator Act (IAA), proposed in March, seeks to upgrade European car factories and only subsidize vehicles produced in Europe.

Yet, the EU's "Made in Europe" plan is causing deep concern among other allied carmaking nations — such as Turkey, Morocco, Japan and South Korea — all of them deeply embedded in European supply chains.

After months of deadlock over the IAA's potential impact on their own vehicle production, Germany and France earlier this month reached a key compromise that would allow suppliers from non-EU members like the United Kingdom and Norway to qualify for "European origin" status.

Other key players in the automotive sector, however, will be excluded — including Turkey, which has enjoyed seamless access to the EU market since the mid-1990s.

While the proposals still face major legislative hurdles in Brussels before becoming law, Ayhan Zeytinoglu, chairman of the Economic Development Foundation (IKV), thinks the Franco-German framework could be devastating for Turkey's auto sector.

"73% of Turkey’s total automotive exports go to the EU," Zeytinoglu told DW. "If we encounter a 'Made in Europe' barrier in the EU market, this will cause great damage to our trade with the EU and to our exports."

Excluding Turkey would also harm European automaking rather than strengthen competitiveness against China, he added.

Turkey's auto exporters association OIB calculated that the country's automotive exports to the EU totaled $30.1 billion (€26.8 billion) last year.

The IAA requires that 70% of the total value of a vehicle's components — excluding the battery — should be made in Europe.

However, the initial proposal only applies to battery-electric vehicles (EVs), plug-in hybrids and fuel-cell models, which last year made up just 27% of all new EU car registrations.

Tommaso Pardi, director of the international automotive research network GERPISA and a senior researcher at the French National Center for Scientific Research (CNRS), notes that the 70% target is remarkably generous.

"If you take an average European-made electric car and take away the battery, you would find that around 83% [of parts] come from Europe," Pardi told DW.

Under the revised German-French plan, some self-charging hybrids and petrol and diesel vehicles could still be sold after 2035 and would also be affected by the "Made in Europe" rules.

These cars contain a much lower proportion of European parts. Most of Turkey's nearly 800,000 vehicle exports to the EU last year would fall under this much larger umbrella.

Another key reason for excluding Turkey is that Chinese automakers are expanding their presence there, hoping to gain back-door access to the EU market, but face major roadblocks.

They include SWM Motors, whose factory in the northwestern city of Eskisehir closed within a year because the cars produced didn't meet EU safety standards.

BYD's plans to open a $1 billion plant in Manisa — a large industrial hub near the Aegean coast — have been put on hold.

Morocco, meanwhile, has received Chinese investments worth more than €10 billion ($11 billion) since the COVID-19 pandemic, according to consultancy AlixPartners, much of it in batteries and auto parts.

For France, the North African nation plays a similar role to China for Germany's auto sector — a low-cost production hub that helps French carmakers stay competitive. Budget-friendly French models would likely become more expensive if Morocco were excluded.

Japanese carmakers, which build several of their models in European factories, could also lose out. Cars imported from Japan would not qualify for EU subsidies, while models built in France, Portugal and Eastern Europe would benefit only if enough of their parts come from Europe.

South Korean automakers, meanwhile, could also be hurt. Hyundai and Kia build cars in the Czech Republic and Slovakia, but also ship models from Asia, which would not qualify. Korean battery plants in Poland and Hungary, however, would count as European.

Pardi notes how European car giants are actively lobbying to water down the IAA to protect their high profit margins.

"They want to limit the IAA to retain the biggest scope to still source from abroad, particularly from China," he said.

EU-based automakers warn that higher parts costs would put them at an even bigger disadvantage against Chinese rivals, while European battery supply is unlikely to meet demand anytime soon.

Ankara is also lobbying Brussels to be included in the "Made in Europe" program, while automakers are seeking a compromise that would allow Turkey to join at a later stage.

Pardi said this would be "only for European companies that are based there [Turkey and Morocco] ... while excluding all recent investments involving Chinese capital [in those countries]."

European auto-parts suppliers are, however, on the opposite side of the argument, calling for further strengthening of IAA. They warn that up to 650,000 auto jobs in the region could be lost by 2035 without better protection for their members.

"A lost industrial job is very hard to win back, and the damage reaches well beyond large suppliers," Benjamin Krieger, Secretary General of the European Association of Automotive Suppliers (CLEPA), told DW.

Krieger thinks the IAA helps European industry to level the playing field against unfair competition from China, adding: "We must stop the bleeding."

With broad public support for measures that ensure that EU public funding "benefits value creation in Europe," CLEPA's Krieger thinks allies like Turkey, Morocco, South Korea and Japan may not lose out if they meet the EU's criteria.

"The EU should offer access to the IAA through an opt-in mechanism with objective criteria: regulatory alignment, social and environmental standards, no market distortion, respect for the rule of law and reciprocity, backed by anti-circumvention measures. Partners that meet them are welcome," he said.

#256. Can we regrow damaged seagrass meadows?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Seagrass protects coasts, shelters thousands of species and stores CO2. But some 30% of this valuable ecosystem has disappeared since the late 19th century. Can we bring it back?

Half a dozen divers are navigating the cold waters of the Baltic Sea off the coast of Dahme, a small beach town in northern Germany. Keeping the roots intact, conservationists carefully lift shoots from a donor meadow of seagrass and place them in a bag.

They only take a few shoots from each spot, which will be planted where the conservationists hope to regrow another meadow. They've already picked out a prime area for the young plants, which need sandy sediment, shelter from waves and currents and sufficient light and oxygen to thrive.

The scientists see the work as critical. Myriam Spicka, who heads the NGO Seagrass Conservation, says she has "seen the changes" in the more than 30 years she's been diving. And she is not the only one who has witnessed the destruction.

Scientists estimate that around 60% of the Baltic coast's seagrass meadows have disappeared.

"That's the bad news," said marine ecologist Thorsten Reusch from the GEOMAR Helmholtz Center for Ocean Research Kiel . "The good news is that the decline has stopped."

Underwater seagrass meadows feed and shelter fish, crabs, shellfish and many other species. They improve water quality and slow waves, helping protect shores from erosion. And they store carbon in the seabed about 30 to 50 times faster than forests.

Around 70 species of seagrass can be found along the coasts of more than 150 countries. Some grow just below the surface, others at depths of 60 meters or almost 200 feet.

"Some countries have far more seagrass, particularly in the Indo-Pacific," said Reusch. "There, it can play a major role in their carbon balance."

But the value of seagrass goes beyond carbon storage. Its meadows in the Baltic are among the sea's most valuable habitats. They shelter wildlife, stabilize the seabed and act as natural filters by trapping particles and nutrients.

Planting individual seagrass shoots is painstaking work so people like Reinhard Helbig are trying to make it easier.

Through ZosteraTec, the start-up he co-founded, he helps restore marine habitats, including seagrass meadows damaged by offshore activity, such as wind farm construction. His work begins on the beach.

"Stormy weather helps us. Waves and currents wash seagrass out of the seabed. A day or two later, it washes up here, still fresh," he said.

The collected shoots are grown and multiplied in ZosteraTec's aquaculture. Several months later, the marine saplings are laid on a plant support. A specially developed machine binds the planted strands with biodegradable thread. The resulting lengths can be laid on the seabed with less diving work.

"We plant here on land to cut down on underwater work. That means fewer dives, and we can do the painstaking work on shore," Helbig said.

Researchers at the Geomar Helmholtz Center in Kiel are also testing new ways to restore seagrass across larger areas, including using seeds.

Marine ecologist Esther Thomsen reaches into an aquarium tank and pulls out a flowerpot containing seagrass.

"Ten percent of the plants in our meadows produce seeds. You can clearly see the seeds sitting inside the seed pod," she said.

"The big advantage of restoring meadows from seed is greater genetic diversity. And in the future, we should be able to sow seeds over much larger areas," explained Thomsen.

Reusch from Geomar said they currently "think the most promising way to grow meadows that can withstand climate change is to plant individual shoots as a foundation, then sow seeds between them to increase genetic diversity."

Whether a planting mission is successful is revealed months or even years later.

Thomsen and her colleagues are visiting the first seagrass meadow planted in Germany's Baltic waters in 2021. The divers count shoots, check how densely they grow and record how the meadow has developed.

They then compare it with the donor meadow that supplied the plants. Only that comparison reveals whether the new crop has merely survived or is beginning to function more like a natural one.

"We've just checked the seagrass meadow, and it's really dense. We counted lots of shoots per square," Thomsen said. "It's looking really good."

#244. Gen Z protests in Germany: Is France-style violence looming?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Young people in Germany are preparing to protest over education and climate change. Politicians fear violence like in France, but an expert sees differences.

As politicians in Germany look ahead to the coming weekend, many fear that cities across the country could witness the kind of violent unrest that recently shook France. An initiative known as "Gen Z regelt" ("Gen Z sorts it") has launched a social media campaign calling for demonstrations in 23 cities nationwide.

"Gen Z" broadly refers to the generation born between 1995 and 2010. Many feel that their parents' generation has done too little to combat climate change and has allowed the gap between rich and poor to widen ever further. At the same time, they are concerned about their educational prospects and future opportunities.

In France, what began as largely peaceful protests recently spiraled into serious unrest. Cars were set alight in several towns, while in Limoges the town hall was torched. In Lille, Lyon, and Toulouse and elsewhere, police deployed tear gas, leading to numerous arrests.

Many politicians, particularly within Chancellor Friedrich Merz's center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU), are warning of similar scenes this coming weekend.

Alexander Throm, the CDU parliamentary group's domestic spokesperson, told the daily Handelsblatt newspaper: "Scenes like those in France must be prevented at all costs."

In Throm's view, far-left groups and criminals from immigrant communities could "hijack" the demonstrations. "An ominous alliance is emerging, particularly in Berlin," he warned.

Marc Henrichmann, also of the CDU, chair of the Bundestag's parliamentary oversight panel for the intelligence services, also expressed concern: "The far-left extremist scene is internationally connected and coordinated." He said that violent far-left activists now even cross national borders to engage in looting and acts of violence elsewhere.

But Sebastian Koos, sociologist at the University of Konstanz whose research focuses on youth and protest movements, told DW that he does not expect Germany to experience unrest on a scale comparable to that seen in France, even though events in the neighboring country helped spark the protests now being planned across Germany.

Koos sees key differences between young people in the two countries. "Unlike in France, these protests are not driven by the same level of anger," he said. "They have been planned, officially registered and carefully organized. The website, in particular, places strong emphasis on peaceful protest and repeatedly stresses a non-violent approach."

At the same time, the sociologist does not rule out the possibility that extremist groups could exploit the authorized demonstrations to stir up unrest: "It is certainly very important for the police and public authorities to be well prepared."

Quentin Gärtner, 19, studies biotechnology in Heidelberg and served until November of last year as secretary-general of the Federal Student Conference, which represents around 7.5 million school students in Germany.

"What unites us is that we have run out of patience with the education system. Many young people are deeply frustrated," he told DW. "The performance of German school students continues to decline, teaching positions are constantly being cut, and in many cases school buildings have seen little or no meaningful investment for years."

For Gärtner, the discontent reflects a broader problem with parliamentary democracy in Germany and many other Western countries.

"Politicians want to win elections. They focus on the groups that matter most to them electorally rather than on the future and the long-term sustainability of their policies," he said.

"And in our aging societies, older people and the baby boomer generation are the groups that carry the most political weight."

Opposition parties in Germany have also voiced understanding for the concerns of younger people. Speaking to Germany's public broadcaster ZDF, Green Party co-leader Felix Banaszak said: "The intergenerational contract is no longer working. Older generations have driven the climate into a dead end."

He argued that the social welfare system was also reaching its limits and that rents had become unaffordable. According to Banaszak, the only thing the state seems to offer young people are the letters sent to 18-year-olds asking them to consider joining the military. Germany is currently desperate to expand the Bundeswehr and is considering reintroducing national service.

By Tuesday, around 134,000 people had registered on the "Gen Z regelt" website to take part in the protests planned for the coming Saturday. With several days still to go before the demonstrations, that number could rise further.

#234. Evacuation from Oleshky: How are preparations going?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A lawyer with the Save Ukraine Foundation, Myroslava Kharchenko, told DW about what has been happening in Russian-occupied Oleshky since a temporary ceasefire brokered by Melania Trump took effect.

Since a ceasefire took effect in the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky in Ukraine's Kherson region on October 27, preparations have been underway to evacuate residents trapped there.

Following Russia's occupation of the town in 2022 and the flooding caused by the destruction of the nearby Kakhovka dam in 2023, residents were cut off from the outside world. All access routes to Oleshky, which lies on the Dnipro River, had been mined. Former Oleshky residents who managed to escape and talked to DW described hunger, no access to medicine, and no electricity or water. About 2,000 people are still living in the town.

According to the Save Ukraine Foundation, it has rescued at least 300 civilians from Oleshky over the past six months. DW spoke with lawyer Myroslava Kharchenko, who works for the organization, about preparations for the upcoming evacuation.

DW: A ceasefire is already in effect in Oleshky. Do you know whether any evacuation efforts have begun?

Myroslava Kharchenko: No, we do not have that information yet. What we do know is that the ceasefire came into effect on October 27 at 3 a.m. Kyiv time, which is 4 a.m. in Russia. Mine-clearing operations on the road began at around 8 a.m. What is happening inside Oleshky itself, however, is entirely within the military's remit. Everyone involved in this operation has been asked to maintain strict confidentiality. Any careless remark or action could jeopardize the entire evacuation effort.

What I can see is that information is being actively collected through closed internal local chat groups. Registration lists and application forms are being shared, and people are filling them out themselves. Organizers are contacting those who previously requested evacuation to verify details such as their date of birth and current place of residence. They are also asking people to provide contact information for neighbors or relatives who may be nearby or in the same building, because communications are extremely unreliable.

The organizers are also collecting information about the health conditions of those planning to leave. Among them are people who are ill, have limited mobility, or are recovering from recent injuries, including blast and mine-related wounds. We therefore expect those organizing the evacuation to ensure that medical assistance is available for those who need it.

How exactly is the evacuation expected to take place? Will people be evacuated through Russian-controlled territory or via the Dnipro River? Have any routes already been agreed upon?

As far as we know, a river route would be extremely dangerous. We have not heard any confirmation that such an option has been approved. It therefore appears likely that the evacuation route will pass through the temporarily occupied Ukrainian territory and possibly through Russia before people are able to reach Ukraine. For security reasons, however, none of these details are being made public.

What do you know about the number of people expected to be evacuated?

We are actually very pleased that the evacuation is not expected to take place in a single day. That is important because we hope that those who manage to leave safely during the first stage will serve as an example for others.

People still trapped in Oleshky are extremely afraid. There have already been numerous provocations, and this is not the first attempt to establish such a route or humanitarian corridor.

As a result, there is a great deal of suspicion. Those concerns are based largely on uncertainty, and some people may choose not to leave during the initial phase of the evacuation. Overall, however, we estimate that around 500 people may eventually be evacuated.

How would you assess the role of Melania Trump in making this evacuation possible?

We are immensely grateful to Melania Trump for drawing attention to the humanitarian catastrophe currently unfolding in Oleshky. What is taking place there is in fact genocide committed by the Russian Federation.

We are thankful for her efforts and hope they will help make this evacuation possible. We will be even more grateful once most people are safely removed from the combat zone and arrive in Ukrainian government-controlled territory.

When could the physical evacuation of civilians begin?

I can't say how long it will take. What seems clear, however, is that time will be needed to clear mines from the roads leading into the city. We also understand that many streets inside Oleshky are mined, so additional work will be required to make routes within the city safe.

There is a hospital from which people need to be evacuated. Nearby is a morgue where bodies also need to be recovered.

I do not know whether that will be included as part of this humanitarian corridor, but it is undoubtedly important. The bodies need to be identified and the causes of death established. That process will also take time, regardless of how quickly the military is able to work.

It is difficult to make any predictions because, as you can see, everything is being conducted under strict secrecy. Statements from both the Russian and Ukrainian sides have been extremely cautious, and that is clearly justified under the circumstances.

#219. Bayern Munich head coach Vincent Kompany's health scare highlights the need for better support for elite coaches

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Vincent Kompany’s health concern is a reminder that elite football's pressures affect coaches too. Better support, clearer structures and mental health safeguards are increasingly vital.

Vincent Kompany has just returned home from hospital. The Bayern Munich head coach admitted that he hadn't listened to his body, "and then at some point, my body just said, 'Enough is enough!'"

Bayern Munich's sporting director Max Eberl, who left Gladbach in 2022 due to his own health concerns, told German public broadcaster ARD that the 40-year-old Belgian had been suffering from meningeal irritation, which if untreated can become meningitis.

"They found out what it is. It is a meningeal irritation that brought on the incident last Friday," said Eberl, referring to when Kompany collapsed during training and required an emergency doctor.

"Honestly, he is doing as well as can be expected. He has been discharged from hospital today, which is the biggest and best news for all of us."

The news has been met by great concern from the German football community. Bayern fans held a banner showing their support, many German media outlets wrote opinion articles softly reminding that coaches are humans too and they too need support. Even the wider coaching community has started to ask questions.

"It’s something you take to heart and think about," Freiburg head coach Julian Schuster said after his team lost to Bayern recently.

"We coaches aren’t always very good at taking care of ourselves in those situations."

Eberl, though, shut down the suggestion that this was related to pressure or stress.

"It's an illness. Any one of us can get it. It has nothing to do with the pressure we face in the Bundesliga," Eberl said.

Nevertheless, with Kompany admitting he hadn't listened to his body and his assistant coach Aaron Danks saying that "getting Vince to rest is a challenge", it is clear that the Belgian had been working very hard of late.

Kompany, whose father became the first Black mayor in Belgium after moving there from the DR Congo, has faced an enormous amount of adversity in his life. At just 14, his parents divorced and he told The Guardian in a 2019 interview: "I need to be fed stuff constantly, otherwise I don’t know how to function. I can't remember a lazy Sunday."

The Belgian's own history, both in his childhood and as former professional player, also plays a role in understanding the challenges coaches face in elite sport.

"I think we (coaches) have a really poor measure of ourselves within this example of like, wasn't listening to my body. I think a lot of coaches are really poor at that,"  coaching mentor Cody Royle, who has worked with teams in the NBA and the Premier League, told DW.

"And so part of my role is to enhance their attunement to that, to ask questions to that effect, to try to perhaps even impart some things that I've learned along the way or heard from others or other coaches that are going through similar things so that you can actually start to put words to what that feeling might have been or where you maybe touched burnout but it didn't quite get there and you're able to rescue something and start to really develop skill within it," he said.

"I think with Vincent's example we also need to account for the fact that this is a young man still finding his way in coaching and so he's still understanding what those triggers are within his body and what they're really telling him Maybe he had physical things that he could read when he was playing. These are more mental triggers, and he's still learning that and that's part of what he needs to do. But there should be people around that help."

It begs the question, in 2026, at a time when awareness around mental and physical health is greater than ever, particularly in elite sport, how is this still possible?

"I suspect that many head coaches aren't able to let go or delegate," Frank Wormuth, an educator for the German Football Association (DFB), told DW.

"They want to control everything because they don't feel secure when the team loses," continued Wormuth, adding that that's why coaches often bring close friends into their staff.

"You'd think professional coaches wouldn't have to worry, since they've earned enough money, but that’s not a good coach's priority—success is far more important."

A former coach, Wormuth admits that he had moments in his career when he had lost sight of how much of a role football was playing in his life.

"I never felt like I was doing too much until my kids pointed out to me—when I was at home—that I wasn't the coach anymore, but should be fulfilling the role of a father," Wormuth said.

"That's when I realized that I was truly operating 24/7 as a coach in my head, even when I was at home."

Royle believes coaches need to learn more about how to manage themselves.
