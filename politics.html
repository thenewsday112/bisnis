#974. Nobel Prize in literature: How the winner is secretly chosen

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Can Xue is considered one of this year's favorites; Margaret Atwood and Salman Rushdie are other strong contenders. But what goes on behind the scenes in the selection of the Nobel Prize in literature?

On Thursday, October 8, at around 1:00 p.m. CEST, all eyes in the literary world will be set on Stockholm.

That's when the new permanent secretary of the Swedish Academy, Ingrid Carlberg, will hold a press conference to announce the winner of the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature, marking the end of a lengthy selection process that officially began a year earlier.

The Swedish Academy is the body responsible for awarding the prize. Its 18 members consist of writers, professors and intellectuals.

In September of the previous year, the Academy's Nobel Committee sends forms to hundreds of individuals and organizations eligible to submit nominations for the Nobel Prize in literature.

According to the Nobel Prize website, these include professors of literature and linguistics at universities and colleges, previous Nobel laureates in literature and the heads of writers' associations that represent the literary community in their respective countries. Anyone from one of these groups who has not received a form may also submit a nomination.

"The list can reach 200 names per year of nominated authors," explains Gisele Sapiro, research director at France's National Centre for Scientific Research. She studies how the Nobel Prize, as a global institution, influences the international circulation of literature, power dynamics in the literary world and translation markets.

"The committee can also add names itself, such as authors who were nominated in previous years," she says.

In the spring, the Nobel Committee then selects five names through a multistage process and presents them to the Academy. Over the summer, Academy members read the nominees' works and discuss them. The Academy always announces the decision in early October.

As for the criteria the Academy uses to make its decision, it prefers to keep them to itself.

The public doesn't learn which nominees missed out until 50 years later — that's how long the Academy waits before releasing the list.

Sometimes decades pass between an author's first nomination and actually receiving the prize. Danish writer Johannes V. Jensen, who won the Nobel Prize in literature in 1944, waited 40 years. Doris Lessing received the award in 2007 after 35 years, while Thomas Mann, who won in 1929, had to wait 27 years.

In his will, Alfred Nobel, the prize's founder, stipulated that it should be awarded to the person who had produced "the most outstanding work in an idealistic direction." That leaves plenty of room for interpretation.

According to Gisele Sapiro, the Academy aims to honor ambitious, influential literature that has, for example, given rise to a new literary movement. "At the same time, the work has to be universal, being able to transcend its own culture," she says.

Translations can be one indication of this. "I noted in my research, however, that all the laureates since 1990 had been translated into English, French and German — except for Abdulrazak Gurnah [the Tanzanian-British 2021 Nobel laureate], who had not been translated into German before winning — meaning that I think these three languages are the most important," Sapiro explains.

Sapiro says it is also a major advantage if nominees have already won other major literary awards, such as the International Man Booker Prize, the Cervantes Prize or the Büchner Prize.

Politics can also influence the Swedish Academy's decisions.

On several occasions, the prize has gone to writers who openly opposed the regimes in their home countries, such as Belarusian writer Svetlana Alexievich in 2015. She is an outspoken critic of authoritarian President Alexander Lukashenko.

Genre, on the other hand, does not matter. In the past, laureates have been honored not only for novels but also for poetry and plays. In 2016, singer-songwriter Bob Dylan received the prize for his song lyrics.

So far, only 33 of the 122 laureates have come from outside Europe. There is also a clear gender imbalance: only in the past eight years have men and women alternated as winners, significantly increasing women's share of the total number of laureates to 14.9%.

#871. Nord Stream: Germany puts suspected Ukrainian pipeline saboteur on trial

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

A Ukrainian citizen faces court in Hamburg, accused of attacking the Nord Stream gas pipelines in 2022. It raises questions about Kyiv's possible role in the explosions. German support for Ukraine is also under scrutiny.

The aerial photos made headlines worldwide: On September 26, 2022, massive quantities of methane gas bubbled on the surface of the Baltic Sea near the island of Bornholm. Unknown people had blown up three of the four sections of the Nord Stream 1 and 2 gas pipelines from Russia to Germany.

The attack, which occurred seven months after Moscow launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine, meant the end of a mega energy project between Germany and Russia in which both sides had invested billions, and which secured affordable Russian gas for Germany. However, the flow of gas via Nord Stream 1 had been restricted or stopped several times by Russia since the Ukraine war began; Nord Stream 2 was not yet operational.

From October 14, Ukrainian citizen Serhii K. is facing trial before a court in the northern German city of Hamburg. He was arrested in Italy in August 2025 on a European arrest warrant and later extradited to Germany. This week he must answer for the attacks before the Hanseatic Higher Regional Court. He is accused of being an accomplice to a war crime by attacking civil energy infrastructure, disruption of public services, causing an explosion and destroying structures.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has repeatedly said that Ukraine was not involved in the 2022 attack on the Nord Stream underwater gas pipelines.

"Ukraine has never officially taken part in any operation related to the blowing up of Nord Stream," Zelenskyy told reporters again in August in response to a question about the ongoing investigation.

The fact that the accused is Ukrainian is especially politically sensitive. Germany continues to support the country after it was attacked by Russia: militarily, financially, with humanitarian aid and by taking in about 1.3 million refugees.

Critics of Germany's support for Ukraine, especially the right-wing extremist Alternative for Germany (AfD) and the left-wing populist Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) want to improve relations with Moscow, resume energy deliveries from Russia – including via a repaired Nord Stream pipeline — and stop supporting Ukraine. Some of them have practically delivered their verdict on Serhii K., although the trial has not yet begun, and assume that Ukraine's political or military leadership was behind the attack on the pipeline.

Sahra Wagenknecht, former leader of the BSW, told news agency Reuters in 2025 following Serhii K.'s arrest: "It is totally absurd that Germany spends many billions on aid for Ukraine but never demands an explanation from [President Volodymyr] Zelenskyy. Questions should also be raised about possible compensation."

Alice Weidel, co-leader of the AfD, has shared similar sentiments. In early 2026, she asserted that the German government must seek financial repayment from Ukraine. If the AfD came into power, she would demand Kyiv compensate Germany for blowing up the pipeline and pay for its repair. According to her, a country that does such things is "no friend of Germany." She has, however, failed to provide proof that Ukraine was behind the attack.

In an interview with public broadcaster Deutschlandfunk in 2023, German naval commander Göran Swistek pointed out that, given the technical, financial, and organizational effort involved, “there is likely a state-run structure behind it.” In his opinion, a group of amateurs could not have carried out the act.

However, he considered it unlikely that this state could be Ukraine, since Ukraine, after all, would not want to jeopardize German support. At the time, Swistek thought a false lead pointing to Russia was possible.

The German-Russian Nord Stream project had many opponents from the beginning, including Poland, the US, the Baltic countries and — especially since Russia's full-scale invasion — Ukraine. That is why investigators always ask: Who benefits from the explosion?

Henning Hoff from the German Council on Foreign Relations (DGAP) thinks it is important to shed light on the background context.

"By halting supplies or using the threat to do so as leverage, Russia exerted immense pressure on Germany at the time, and gas markets went haywire," he told DW. "The explosion thwarted Russian attempts to extort Germany, prices for natural gas sank. I think one could say: The explosion was in German interests."

But what will happen if the Ukrainian Serhii K. is found guilty and a connection to the military or political leadership of Ukraine is proven? Could the German government still continue its current course regarding Ukraine?

The German government faces a dilemma: On one side, judicial independence must be preserved and consequences for the sabotaging German infrastructure must be pursued. On the other side, the German government wants to prevent Russia from instrumentalizing the case. However, pressure on Germany would increase enormously, also from within the country.

Roderich Kiesewetter, a lawmaker with the ruling center-right Christian Democrats (CDU), said there is no reason to deviate from the current course of support, even in the case of involvement by Kyiv: "Irrespective of the outcome, no consequences at all are appropriate, because our support for Ukraine is carried out in the interest of our own national security and stems from a sense of responsibility for a free Europe."

The debate around support for Ukraine is escalating in Germany, regardless of the court process. Public backing for Ukraine is decreasing, especially for deliveries of weapons.

The AfD gained significant support in three state parliament elections held in eastern Germany during September. The party could potentially supply the state premier in Saxony-Anhalt. The AfD has also led nationwide polls for months, far ahead of the conservative CDU of Chancellor Friedrich Merz.

In addition, the AfD and BSW have drawn a connection between planned government spending cuts on social services and aid to Ukraine.

Wagenknech told a campaign event before the state parliament elections that the federal government was "Sinking billions upon billions into the black hole of Ukraine, while in Germany, in our schools, the children are no longer properly learning reading, writing and arithmetic." Tino Chrupalla, co-leader of the AfD, raised the question of whether citizens were prepared to spend so much money on a non-EU and non-NATO country.

Given very expensive energy costs, it is not only the AfD and BSW who suggest repairing Nord Stream and receiving new deliveries of gas from Russia. Some CDU politicians favor this too, such as Saxony state premier Michael Kretschmer. He views a repaired gas pipeline as a possible bargaining chip for talks with Russia. Kretschmer's position, however, contradicts the federal CDU party line and European Union sanctions policies.

His party colleague Kiesewetter considers it dangerous: "Russia has always used energy as an instrument of geo-economic extortion. It would be extremely dumb if we were to again learn nothing from history and cling to a special path with Russia. That would fuel mistrust among our partners in Europe. I can only advise everyone, including people from the CDU, to keep their distance from such calls, because they are against German interests."

Hoff from the DGAP also rejected positions like Kretschmer's. "That is an illusion. There is no returning to the time before Russia's war. The supposedly cheap Russian gas, which has cost Germany dearly, will never flow again, if only because Putin has no interest in it doing so. It serves only as an instrument of propaganda for polarizing German politics and weakening support for Ukraine."

In any case, the outcome of the trial in Hamburg will undoubtedly influence the public debate about further support for Ukraine.

#728. How big is Russia's threat against Germany?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Experts believe that, so far, Germany has been ill-prepared — both militarily and mentally — for Russian provocations. They also warn against the idea that ending support for Ukraine would make Germany safer.

Warnings keep mounting about Russia's threat to Germany. On October 12, Sinan Selen, president of Germany's domestic intelligence agency, the BfV, said that Russia's covert attacks and destabilization efforts against Germany and other European countries are "not a temporary development but a long-term hybrid attack."

Even if the war in Ukraine were to end, said Selen, the Russian threat would not disappear. "For Russia, this is about completely redrawing the map of Europe and redefining its own role within Europe and in global politics."

He added that Russia's aggression would continue to be felt not only in Ukraine, but also in the Baltic states, in Poland and in Germany.

German Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt has described the threat situation as one in which "our country is not at war, but we are the daily target of hybrid warfare." Russia's goal, he said, is not to defeat Germany militarily "but to subdue us politically and socially."

Security experts, however, are not so sure that Russia is only interested in exercising political and social influence. Last year, political scientist Carlo Masala told the political talk show Maischberger that "Russia is preparing for a major war." And he didn't mean just a cyberwar. Inspector General Carsten Breuer, the Bundeswehr's highest-ranking officer, agreed.

For now, though, Russia remains tied up in its war against Ukraine. According to NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, there is no immediate military threat to NATO territory. But according to military experts, European intelligence agencies, and the German Defense Ministry, Russia will have the personnel and equipment to wage a conventional war against NATO by 2029. The Russian defense industry is running at full speed, and Moscow plans to expand its armed forces to 1.5 million soldiers.

At the moment, Russia has other objectives, believes Stefan Meister, an expert on Eastern Europe at the German Council on Foreign Relations (DGAP): "I don't think Russia has any interest in a direct military conflict with NATO right now. It wants to test NATO and also display its capabilities," he tells DW.

An example of Russia's hybrid war might be the failed attack with an explosive-laden drone against a Ukrainian transport aircraft at Leipzig-Halle Airport in August. Soon after, the German government stated that it was certain Russia was behind the attack.

Security expert Nico Lange told DW at the time that Germany was very lucky the explosives did not detonate: "Honestly, it was pure coincidence and not the result of German defensive measures."

As for why Germany was targeted specifically, Lange said: "I believe that Germany has always been a primary target of Russian influence and hybrid warfare. Russia believes that Germany is the key to European decision-making. Germany is now leading the way in supporting Ukraine."

Moscow swiftly responded to the German government's accusation with an angry denial. Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov said it was "the beginning of a real war," though there was no immediate further escalation.

Nevertheless, there is always the risk that things could spiral out of control following a Russian provocation and subsequent response, according to Stefan Meister: "Accidentally shooting down an aircraft that they were trying to intimidate would trigger a counter-reaction. But I don't believe that Russia is currently aiming for a direct war but rather for provocations, polarization, and sowing uncertainty among the population."

Not everyone shares the assessment that Russia poses a threat to Germany — some believe that the threat can be averted.

Despite the war in Ukraine, politicians from the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) and the populist left-wing Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) want to improve relations with Russia, resume Russian energy supplies and end support for Ukraine.

AfD co-leader Tino Chrupalla said in early September, "People are fed up with this escalation against Russia, with the billions being squandered there on a system that is actually funded by German taxpayers' money."

The message appears to have resonated among the population, especially in eastern Germany. The AfD saw strong gains in all three state elections in September. In Saxony-Anhalt, it became the strongest party by far and may even run the government there soon, which would be the first time that a far-right party has held power in Germany since the end of WWII.

The CDU's security policy expert Roderich Kiesewetter disputed the claim made by the AfD and BSW that ending aid to Ukraine would mean greater security and more money for German schools and infrastructure: "Spending on education, social policy, and infrastructure is only possible if we live in freedom and democracy — and, above all, in peace and security. Anyone who believes that we'll have more money for social programs if we stop supporting Ukraine and let Russia have its way is either gravely mistaken or is making themselves a tool of Russian propaganda."

The perceived security threat has led the German military, the Bundeswehr, to fast-track significant modernization through substantial investment. But it is struggling to find enough recruits for its planned expansion. Many young Germans have taken to the streets to protest against plans to reintroduce conscription. Defense Minister Boris Pistorius faced criticism from inside his own party, the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD), for saying that Germany must become "capable of waging war." Many found the statement to be too militaristic.

Is Germany prepared for a military threat? "Not at all. Germany is not well prepared militarily or in the civilian sector, and the population isn't mentally prepared for it either," said Meister. "Just look at the demonstrations against conscription and the military that continue to take place, even though Germany needs a draft. For decades, we've grown up in this culture of pacifism, in a comfort zone. I believe there is very limited societal resilience, but above all, we are not at all mentally prepared for war."

Chancellor Friedrich Merz and his conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) are under increasing political pressure — especially from the far right — to scale back support for Ukraine. But Merz never tires of emphasizing that Ukraine will continue to receive support under his chancellorship, calling it a matter of Germany's security. He recently visited Kyiv once again and, together with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, surveyed the destruction caused by Russia.

But this also means that the threat from Russia will continue and even intensify, says Stefan Meister: "I believe we will see a massive increase in attacks such as the one in Leipzig, of provocations involving drones. We will see attacks on infrastructures and possibly also on specific individuals within the military-industrial complex. I believe this is the real, concrete danger facing Germany in the coming months."

#675. Brutal, revolutionary empire builder: Genghis Khan's legacy

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Berlin is host to a major exhibition on the founder of the Mongol Empire and his successors. Here's how Genghis Khan disrupted the course of history.

Genghis Khan is one of history's most radical figures. A blood-thirsty invader whose conquests caused the deaths of an estimated 40 million people, he was also a visionary political thinker who established the largest contiguous land empire in history.

The founder of the Mongolian Empire died in 1227. Ahead of the 800th anniversary of his death, Berlin's James-Simon-Galerie explores his legacy and the "Mongol Century" (1250–1350) in the exhibition "Genghis Khan and the World of the Mongols."

The ruler remains "incredibly relevant" to this day, says Matthias Wemhoff, the director of the exhibition project.

"What the era of Genghis Khan — the 'Mongol century,' if you will — vividly demonstrates is how quickly established structures can collapse, how rapidly vast empires can vanish from the map and how swiftly the entire landscape can be utterly transformed," the archaeologist tells DW.

The charismatic leader's unprecedented impact on the world also evokes present-day disruptive individuals who are triggering sudden, unpredictable societal shifts, says Wemhoff: "There's always been this question: Are world politics shaped by individuals or rather by groups and sociological processes?"

In this case, the answer is clear, says the museum director, who also co-wrote a 2026 book on the nomadic leader.

"If Genghis Khan hadn't existed, the course of world history would truly have been entirely different — so much of it is inextricably linked to his person," Wemhoff says.

Genghis Khan was born as Temujin around the year 1162. His father, a Mongol chieftain called Yesugei, died when he was still a child. His family was abandoned by its tribe. To secure his familial position, Temujin killed his older half-brother, Bekter, when he was still a young teenager.

Thanks to his charismatic personality, he built alliances and found followers who helped him become the sole ruler on the Mongolian steppe. He formally adopted the title of Genghis Khan in 1206.

At its peak, the Mongol Empire covered approximately 24 million square kilometers (about 9.3 million square miles), stretching from China and Korea to parts of Eastern Europe and the Middle East, and accounting for over 16% of the Earth's total land area.

Wemhoff lists various innovations under his rule: For one, Genghis Khan transformed warfare. Commanders were promoted for skill, discipline and loyalty rather than based on aristocratic rank or tribal loyalties. Soldiers were organized into decimal units of 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000.

Despite being illiterate by modern standards, he also pushed for the introduction of the first written script for the Mongolian language, which allowed to build a state bureaucracy, track population and tax registers, record decrees, issue legal decisions and handle diplomacy.

A set of laws were established that replaced fragmented tribal customs with a unified legal system.

Trade was simplified through his adoption of a paper currency.

A policy of religious freedom was also established across the Mongol Empire, allowing all individuals to practice their chosen faith without state coercion.

Before the Mongol Empire, the Silk Road was fragmented by local conflicts and heavy taxes. Mongol rule placed the entire route under a single authority, making long-distance travel vastly safer.

The Mongol Empire is known as a period of relative political stability, economic growth and cultural exchange, an era historians call the "Pax Mongolica."

But "it all came with incredible levels of cruelty. The millions of people who fell victim to the Mongols — the mass executions, the abuse of women — this happened on a scale previously unheard of," notes Wemhoff.

The exhibition opens with a raid that happened after Genghis Khan's death, but took place just 260 kilometers (around 160 miles) away from Berlin: the 1241 Battle of Legnica, in Silesia, Poland.

It embodies the brutality and intensity of the Mongols' methods.

There came the "highly mobile, agile Mongols on small horses — lightly armored in lamellar gear, wearing helmets that left the face exposed, and wielding bows capable of firing 20 or more arrows in a single minute," points out Wemhoff.

European knights, heavily armored and mounted on large, massive warhorses, didn't stand a chance against their enemies.

"When a hail of arrows rained down on them, the horses eventually went down, and the knights ended up lying on the ground, stuck on their back, much like a June bug," he adds. "The two styles of combat were simply mismatched."

The Mongols' western campaigns shifted historic power centers in a way that still impacts world politics today.

For example, after the Mongol invasion of Kievan Rus', which was the first East Slavic state, its thriving capital Kyiv was reduced to a depopulated ruin. Moscow became a key regional power, partly because it chose to cooperate with the Mongols rather than pursue resistance.

"In our Western European collective memory, the Mongol invasion plays hardly any role. It's something of an anecdote," says Wemhoff, noting that the era of Genghis Khan and his immediate successors is barely mentioned in standard Western European school curricula.

Germans are mainly familiar with the name of the empire's founder through the 1979 Euro-disco pop band Dschingis Khan, which represented West Germany at the Eurovision Song Contest with a campy song of the same name that reduced complex history to extreme tropes.

But in eastern Europe, the Mongol invasions "changed everything fundamentally," says Wemhoff. "And so fundamentally that we still feel the consequences of it today."

The exhibition "Genghis Khan and the World of the Mongols" runs from October 21, 2026, to April 18, 2027.

#452. Serbia: Hope, tension and apprehension as voters prepare to elect new parliament

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

After two years of protests, Serbia faces a pivotal election on Sunday, with a student-led challenge to Aleksandar Vucic's lengthy rule. Allegations of electoral irregularities and fears of a post-election crisis abound.

A sense of hope swept through Belgrade on Thursday evening as thousands gathered for the final rally of the Student List, the group of candidates put forward by the student movement that has been protesting against the government for almost exactly two years.

The Student List is calling for an end to the current regime in Serbia via the ballot box in Sunday's parliamentary election.

Many of those who attended the rally had spent days walking to the Serbian capital from all four corners of the country, turning the campaign finale into a display of solidarity and peaceful determination.

"We have walked across Serbia from one end to the other, visited villages and neighborhoods, looked you in the eye and realized that we can count on you," biology student Arsa Stojanovic told the crowd.

"People, it smells like freedom," added Aleksandar Cupic, professor of transport and traffic engineering, and a candidate on the Student List.

After 24 months of protests sparked by the collapse of a concrete canopy at Novi Sad railway station, which killed 16 people in November 2024, Serbia is heading into an election unlike any other in the 14 years since the Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) first came to power.

For the first time in years, the ruling party faces a serious political challenge, which this time comes from a student movement that has channeled widespread public discontent into an electoral campaign.

"These are the first elections since [Aleksandar] Vucic came to power in 2012 that are competitive and whose outcome is uncertain," political scientist Dusan Spasojevic told DW. "The student movement has generated hope among citizens and substantial support. Polls suggest that they could even win more than 40% of the vote."

The Student List has framed the election as a chance to confront corruption and demand accountability for a disaster that became a symbol of institutional failure.

In doing so, the students have sought to put aside the ideological, social and foreign-policy divisions that have long shaped Serbian politics.

Just a few kilometers away, also in Belgrade, the ruling SNS held its own final rally.

The message here was familiar: A vote for the SNS means stability, continuity and further prosperity, while a change of government would risk plunging the country into chaos.

The party framed the election as a battle for Serbia's future and sovereignty, accusing its opponents of serving foreign interests.

"They tried to destroy our country on foreign orders, and that is why this is the hardest battle," former President Aleksandar Vucic told his supporters. "They attacked us with billions of euros, and we defended ourselves with integrity, our program, love for our homeland and Serbia's achievements."

The campaign has increasingly been overshadowed by violence, reflecting a society in which political opponents are often portrayed not simply as rivals, but as enemies.

By the final week of the campaign, CRTA, an independent election-monitoring organization, had recorded 116 incidents involving physical violence or verbal threats. The incidents included physical confrontations, attacks on campaigners and attempts to seize campaign banners.

CRTA also reported that opposition activists, particularly those linked to the Student List, were also subjected to surveillance  and police questioning. In some cases, the organization said, police were slow to protect opposition candidates and supporters.

In the final runup to the election, there have also been a series of investigative reports examining alleged links between people connected to the ruling SNS and criminal networks.

Published by independent media in the days before the election, the reports have brought renewed attention to questions of corruption, political influence and accountability — issues at the heart of the student movement's challenge to the government.

But Spasojevic is skeptical that the revelations will make a significant difference to the election outcome.

He argues that those who trust investigative media are likely to have already made up their minds, while voters who rely on pro-government outlets may never even see these reports.

There are also serious concerns about the fairness of the election process.

CRTA told DW that the election campaign has not been either free or fair from day one, citing "a narrowing of freedoms and deepening inequality" among candidates and voters.

Its report found a pronounced imbalance in media coverage: Almost three-quarters of airtime devoted to political players on national television has featured government representatives, while more than 80% of coverage on the Student List has been negative.

Ahead of the vote, the Balkan Investigative Reporting Network (BIRN) published the testimony of a former member of the SNS, who described how public-sector employees were pressured to deliver votes for the party.

DW has also learned from sources who want to remain anonymous that voters have been offered between €30 and €150 to vote for the SNS, with holiday packages reportedly offered to entire families if they pledge all their votes to the ruling party.

Many fear a period of turbulence, uncertainty and political crisis after the vote, with election night itself a particular source of anxiety.

For weeks, the authorities have warned that students, allegedly with the help of foreign intelligence services, are preparing violence in the streets. In the final days of the campaign, several people were arrested on what the prosecution has said was the suspicion of plotting disturbances on election night.

The best-case scenario, says Dusan Spasojevic, would be a decisive victory for one of the two opposing camps, leaving little room for the other to challenge the result.

"But polls suggest that the margin will be narrow, and we will probably enter some kind of post-election crisis," he said, adding that a narrow victory for the ruling party would almost certainly be contested, given longstanding concerns about the fairness of the electoral process and allegations that some votes may have been obtained illegally.

Whatever happens, the European Union's response could be crucial.

"I think the international response will be crucial in preventing major incidents and a further escalation of the crisis. It is the only thing that can persuade the authorities to accept defeat and refrain from an overly heavy-handed response to any protests or incidents," he told DW.

An EU Commission spokesperson acknowledged to DW that key recommendations on improving Serbia's electoral framework remain unimplemented and also stressed that democracy and the rule of law are key to the accession process. Serbia has been an EU candidate country since 2012.

Back in Belgrade, the students ended their rally with a kolo, a traditional Serbian circle dance, projecting confidence in their chances at the ballot box.

But the path to victory could prove difficult. The movement has vowed to defend every vote — setting the stage for a potentially tense election night if the results are disputed.

#383. Prague honors Roma resistance fighters against Hitler

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

More than 1,400 Roma fought against the German Nazi occupation in Czechoslovakia. A new exhibition in Prague showcases the stories of partisans and soldiers whose fates barely received any attention for decades.

For more than half a million Roma in Czechia and Slovakia, October 15, 2026, marks an important milestone.

That day, an exhibition on a largely forgotten chapter of Czech history opened at the Museum of the Romani National Revival in Prague. The exhibit explores the role of Czech and Slovak Romani resistance fighters during the Nazi German occupation and the dismantling of Czechoslovakia from 1938 to 1945.

Until now, Czech and Slovak Roma appeared in the history of World War II almost exclusively as victims of the Holocaust against Roma and Sinti. In the so-called Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia, Nazi Germany murdered approximately 90% of the Czech Roma during World War II.

However, what is less well known is that a large number of Czech and Slovak Roma fought against the German occupiers as partisans in resistance groups and as soldiers in the Czechoslovak Underground Army, or Defense of the Nation, ON.

The exhibition at the Museum of the Roma National Movement therefore marks a turning point in how the Roma are viewed during World War II. At least that is the hope of museum director Emil Scuka, a lawyer, former prosecutor and one of the best-known Czech Roma activists.

"We were not only victims; we were also heroes," Scuka said in his speech to several hundred attendees at the exhibition opening, most of whom were Roma from Czechia and Slovakia. "Many people, not even among the Roma themselves, are aware that we Roma fought against Nazism," he noted. In his view, "organizing and opening the exhibition was something my generation owed to these Roma heroes and today we are repaying that debt."

Scuka called on those present: "Bring your children and grandchildren here so they can see for themselves and realize that the Roma have heroes too!"

The smell of rotten food can make us recoil. Certain sexual practices can provoke a similar reaction. And in today's polarized political climate, even political opponents can become objects of disgust.

Psychologists have long investigated whether sensitivity to disgust helps explain political beliefs. A review of 24 studies found that people who reported being more easily disgusted tended to hold more socially conservative views.

"We think that it evolved in part to reduce exposure to things that could make you sick," psychologist Yoel Inbar, of the University of British Columbia in Canada, told DW.

Inbar explains that avoiding potential sources of infection can also mean missing out on new experiences.

"Some people are more on the cautious conservative side, and some people are more on the exploring risk-taking side when it comes to pathogen exposure," he said.

Indeed, the language of contamination also appears in political rhetoric. During his 2024 presidential campaign, Donald Trump repeatedly described immigrants as "poisoning the blood of our country." Trump has also described himself as a "germaphobe."

Inbar acknowledges that the evidence linking sensitivity to disgust with conservative political beliefs remains uncertain.

"We have hypotheses. I don't know that we have as strong of evidence as I would like," Inbar said.

Disgust is not exclusively conservative. And what people find revolting can depend on their values.

A 2020 study titled "Is Disgust a "Conservative" Emotion?" found that liberals and conservatives were disgusted by different things.

Conservatives, generally aligned with the Republicans in US politics, reported greater disgust at examples involving sexual behaviors and drug use, while liberals, generally aligned with the Democrats, reported greater disgust at animal abuse and environmental pollution.

However, when asked how easily disgusted they were in general, there was no clear difference across the political spectrum.

Some early studies suggested conservatives showed stronger physical reactions to frightening or disgusting images, measured partly through changes in sweating.

But subsequent research, including a 2020 study by political psychologist Bert Bakker at the University of Amsterdam, struggled to reproduce those findings.

Studies based on questionnaires, however, have generally found that people with more conservative attitudes report being more easily disgusted.

"If bodily reactions don't differ across political groups, why do feelings?" Manos Tsakiris, of Royal Holloway and the Centre for the Politics of Feelings at the University of London, told DW.

Tsakiris and colleague Mariana von Mohr wondered whether the answer might lie in how people perceive and interpret signals from their bodies.

Scientists call the perception of internal sensations, such as hunger, breathing or a racing heart, "interoception." It's sometimes called our eighth sense, alongside the familiar five, plus our senses of balance and body position.

"A bodily signal doesn't arrive with a label attached," Tsakiris told DW. "We have to notice it and make sense of it."

The researchers surveyed 632 US adults, asking how much they noticed and trusted signals from their bodies — a measure known as "interoceptive sensibility."

They found that people who were more easily disgusted by sexual behavior tended to be more conservative, regardless of how aware they felt of their bodies.

But for moral disgust, the link to conservatism was found only among people who reported lower bodily awareness.

What particularly surprised Tsakiris was that perceived bodily awareness alone had no link to political views.

"People who are highly attuned to their bodies were neither more liberal nor more conservative," he said.

Inbar questions whether all forms of disgust are equally useful for studying political attitudes.

He and Joshua Tybur, one of the creators of the Three-Domain Disgust Scale, generally prefer measuring pathogen disgust — reactions to potential sources of disease — rather than sexual disgust.

Questions about sexual disgust may already reflect conservative beliefs about sex.

"It's almost asking people the same thing twice," Inbar said.

The new study found no significant link between pathogen disgust and conservative views.

Inbar wants the findings on bodily awareness and disgust tested again, with predictions set out in advance. For now, he considers them "suggestive at best."

Tsakiris suggests that people who struggle to understand their bodily feelings may be more vulnerable to political messages telling them what those feelings mean.

"I know what you're feeling. You're feeling angry or you're feeling disgusted about this thing," he said, illustrating how someone might put an emotional label on another person's unease.

Such labels could influence political behavior, he argues, although the study did not directly test this.

"This is how disgust can do political work: it can turn moral judgments about individuals into judgments about whole groups," he said.

The research cannot establish whether disgust shapes political beliefs or existing beliefs shape what people find disgusting. Bakker notes that political beliefs can influence how people describe their own personalities, suggesting the relationship could work both ways.

"Voting behavior is very complex and citizens make up their mind about politics in a complex way," he told DW.

Inbar also cautions against exaggerating disgust's importance. "The thing to emphasize in your story is that the relationship, while it's consistent, is quite small," he said.

"It's one of the many influences on people's politics."

Tsakiris advocates what he calls "critical feeling": questioning our emotional responses to provocative material, including social media posts, just as we question the information itself.

Look at this political moment a certain way, and you can see the establishment coming back to life. The most anticipated case is next week’s US midterms, whose polling looks severe enough for the Republicans to have commentators musing the end of the Trump era. Presidents almost always get a whacking in the midterms, of course, but Donald Trump seems on course for a historic one. Historic enough to return not just the House to the Democrats, but the Senate, which was frankly meant to be unthinkable. That’s on the cards because suddenly Democrats are in potential winning positions in places as red as Texas and Kansas.

But if the establishment is finding comfort anywhere, it will be in the Democrats’ numbers. That party, you will recall, is facing its own insurgency in the form of avowedly left-wing candidates – occasionally self-identified democratic socialists – who dispatched the Democrat establishment in some high-profile primaries. Now we have a spread of Democrats the political coverage variously designates as either “progressives” or “moderates”. And right now, the polling is showing that the moderates are significantly outperforming the progressives. Put another way, the progressive candidates currently seem a drag on the Democrat vote. If they win, it will be because the Democrats had enough margin for error. The closer you are to being the establishment candidate, the better your chances seem.

Now look to Britain, where gravity seems to have taken hold of the insurgents. A suite of polls this week has Reform sinking to third place, with the major parties eclipsing them after two years of running behind. The Greens, meanwhile, have lost half their vote since their peak in February. The result has Labour riding its “Burnham bounce” into a clear first place. The Conservatives are now second. To be clear, that merely has the Tories returning to somewhere near their disastrous 2024 election result. But the direction of travel has them chuffed about that.

If all this is as it seems, we’re looking at something significant. You couldn’t say the underlying conditions that have been driving the populist surge have drastically changed. The cost of living is still an enormous stress everywhere, and public attitudes to immigration remain sceptical. And yet somehow the insurgencies seem fractionally out of puff. Whatever we’re seeing in these societies, it’s not a sudden contentedness with life. It’s more likely the discontentedness remains, but is slowly decoupling from the politicians who once profited from it.

Hereabouts, exercise caution. You probably don’t need me to tell you how slippery US polling has been in the past decade. Sometimes they underestimate the Republican vote – especially Trump’s personal vote in presidential elections – and sometimes they undersell the Democrats. The Economist carried a fascinating analysis last week that found that heavily red states are consistently redder than the polls suggest, and heavily blue states bluer. Normally that doesn’t matter because the result is foregone. But if it holds true this time, we’ll see Democrat hopes dashed in places like Iowa or South Carolina. And Democrat hope restored in places like Maine and New Hampshire.

Similarly, British politics is experiencing some strange conditions. Partly, that’s about Reform’s season of scandal: suspect donations, and elected representatives calling Asians “parasites” and black children “animals” – all of which led to high-profile sackings and suspensions. There’s good reason to believe this pushed once-disillusioned Tories back home. But Reform might be suffering in another way too. One theory is that its recent influx of Tory conversions has completely undermined its outsider status. Reform never pitched itself as a kind of Tory party-in-exile. Once it becomes that, there is less reason for its existence.

Meanwhile, Labour has a prime minister who seems newer than Nigel Farage. Perhaps even more of an outsider. Andy Burnham wasn’t even in the parliament until June. He was parachuted in via a byelection for the express purpose of unseating Keir Starmer and becoming prime minister. It would be too much of a stretch to call Burnham an insurgent. But he’s not continuity, either. He gives the disillusioned a way of listening again, and hearing things afresh. You see the hints of this in the polling of people who didn’t vote in 2024. Until recently, Reform owned these people, having support from 63 per cent of them. Now they have only 26 per cent of them, while Labour now claims almost as many, on 22 per cent.

None of this is quite the empire striking back. It is more the desire for change assuming a different shape. In America, the anti-incumbent mood is clear. Trump, as the chief incumbent, is now a lightning rod for dissent. The looming swings are starkest in places he won comfortably, and are dampened in seats the Democrats are meant to win. In Britain, the title of incumbent has become far more ambiguous. But Labour’s fortunes seem to have risen the less incumbency it exuded, and Reform’s case is the reverse.

That’s less a picture of stability returning than one of restlessness. Clearly, insurgencies have a half-life, determined by how proximate they become to power. This has been one of the clearest trends of Trump’s political career: he builds momentum in opposition, storms to sensational victories, then promptly repels voters when in office. But when he or his fellow travellers are ejected, the disillusionment that put them there hasn’t suddenly been extinguished. It merely reconfigures itself.

Sometimes, under certain conditions, that reconfiguring happens around the establishment. But it doesn’t seem to happen for establishment reasons. And that lays down a particularly wicked challenge: incumbents must exude a sense of constant renewal, a kind of perpetual motion, but one that doesn’t look like chaos or incompetence. Perhaps here Burnham will provide a useful case study. He could hardly begin with a cleaner slate. Now we’ll see how long it takes for the smog of high office to sully it.

It’s exhausting, I admit. But it’s worth observing what it isn’t. For instance, it isn’t politics driving itself off a cliff. It’s not things moving in a linear direction towards authoritarianism, as many fear. It seems more a series of convulsions to no particular end. That’s not to advise complacency, of course. It’s more a case of: strap in. Because in our age, things don’t so much return to normal as pass through normality on the way to somewhere else.

Rail and trucking through neighboring countries offer still less capacity. They face high costs, limited infrastructure, border procedures and sanctions risks.

If crude cannot be exported, Iran’s problem moves from the sea back to its oil fields.

Tehran can send more crude to domestic refineries, use available storage or cut production. Kpler estimated earlier in the blockade that Iran had already begun reducing output as inventories rose and usable storage space tightened.

Usable storage is not the same as total tank capacity: some tanks hold condensate, and not all can simply be switched to crude.

Further production cuts would mean less revenue and could make restarting output more difficult. But continuing to pump oil with nowhere to send it risks filling remaining storage and eventually forcing deeper cuts.

The financial impact will also emerge gradually because payments for cargoes already delivered can arrive weeks later.

The United States says the blockade has denied Iran at least $25 billion in oil revenue, a CENTCOM estimate cited by the Journal rather than an independently verified measure of Tehran’s net losses.

Iran has not forgotten how to evade sanctions or lost the network it built to sell oil despite them. But before Tehran can disguise a cargo, transfer it between ships or sell it at a discount, it first has to get the oil out of Iran.
