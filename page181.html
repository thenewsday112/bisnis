#2832. Courtney Dauwalter Has Me Rethinking Failure

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

This is a special edition of Managing Editor Abby Levene’s newsletter from October 9, 2026. Every week, she shares her most unfiltered thoughts on the sport and beyond. But it doesn’t usually make it onto the website. It’s a newsletter, after all. To have it delivered straight to your inbox, sign up for her newsletter here.

Aloha from the Ironman World Championship in Kona! This morning I was running on Ali’i, the main drag along the water here on Hawai’i’s Big Island, with … Courtney Dauwalter. (Yes, the Baggy Shorts Boss is in Kona, more on that in a second.) We’re both training for the California International Marathon in December so we were doing a little interval workout.

This is my fourth time covering this race in Kona with our sister publication, Triathlete, so I’ve spent a lot of time running back and forth on this infernal road waving to the throngs of racers running and biking along it. By this point in the week three days out from the race, everyone is pretty locked in. Those spandex-clad triathletes on Ali’i are nervous; they’re looking inward, not smiling or waving back much.

Courtney and I were in the middle of our workout when I saw my colleague Chris running towards us. I smiled and waved, trying to get his attention with my eyes. (We were working hard, after all.) He stared blankly at me. Then he saw Courtney behind me, and it clicked.

“I didn’t recognize you; you looked so serious!” he said to me afterward. “But Courtney had a huge smile on her face and gave me a big wave.”

WTF man, I was smiling and waving too! Well, Chris told me, Courtney has an aura.

And he’s right. Throughout the run, legions of triathletes smiled and waved to us (well, to Courtney). I felt transported into a different, brighter, bubblier world. A positive feedback loop of joy. But it wasn’t just Courtney’s dazzling smile that was rubbing off on me and everyone else on Ali’i this morning. That infectious enthusiasm stems from a place much deeper inside her, I think, as evidenced by a conversation we had about failure earlier in the week.

Courtney is here because in a shocking plot twist, Salomon—the beloved mountain brand best known for its snow and trail running equipment—is taking over the Ironman World Championship title sponsor. More on that and what it means for the future of endurance sport next week.

In an exclusive interview, I had the privilege of being the first journalist to ask Courtney about UTMB. (The three-time champ DNFed at mile 40.) What she had to say illuminated a lot about how she thinks about athletics, and it’s a perspective I’ll be trying to adopt forever:

Courtney’s main focus this year was the Cocodona 250 in May followed by the Hardrock 100 in July, she said. (Narrator: In case you forgot, those both went quite well, including a course record at Hardrock.) UTMB was the cherry on top.

“After Hardrock the body was all in one piece,” she said. “The brain was feeling decent. I took some recovery, tried to recharge the batteries, and then decided let’s just push the marble forward and see if we could make UTMB happen, because in our minds we were like, why not try? Why not pin on the bib and just see?”

She wasn’t sure if her body and mind were in fact recharged. And well—

“When the race started, it was pretty clear that the answer to those questions was a definitive ‘no, there’s nothing there for you,’” she said. “Everything felt pretty horrible from the start.”

She kept going, thinking maybe things would turn around, but they didn’t. But that’s not actually why I’m telling you this; it’s because of what she said next:

“It’s a bummer that trying can’t just be the cool part by itself. I would like if trying hard things could just be the win alone. I understand, of course, it’s a race, and with races are results. But I think athletes would maybe be more inclined to jump into more random ideas or challenges if a DNF wasn’t such a dark cloud that some people associate it with.”

I think it’s easy to discredit how brave Courtney is because she’s so freaking good at running. She tries new things, and she most often wins. But as evidenced by her willingness to try events ranging from the Western States 100 to Big’s Backyard Ultra to road marathons, she seems genuinely driven by curiosity. (“Joy is in the driver’s seat,” she told me in another interview this spring.) That curiosity is a positive force that overshadows fear of failure—a remarkable feat for someone so in the public eye that everyone recognizes her at a race in a totally different sport.

Speaking of that totally different sport, after that discussion I had to ask the obvious question: Does being here make any part of her maybe want to race an Ironman?

“I didn’t know that I was Ironman-curious or triathlon-curious, but I also am definitely crazy-idea-curious, always,” she said. “And so I would say there’s probably a 100% chance I’ll try.”

Hearing the excitement in her voice filled me with newfound courage. I’ve been super nervous to try a road marathon after so many years of focusing on the trails, worried it might not go as well as I want. Reframing that fear of failure to curiosity to try is immensely empowering to me. That’s not to say the result doesn’t matter to Courtney, or that our own results shouldn’t matter to us. It’s that even for someone like Courtney, who has won basically everything, the trying part is where the magic happens. And trying is something we can all do, too.

Before she races an Ironman, Courtney needs help with the swim, she said. She’s never biked over 100 miles. But really, there’s just one real problem she needs to solve:

“Do you think that you can wear baggy shorts?” she asked. “I’m concerned about my outfit.”

#2355. If You See a Traffic Light With a Yellow Border, Here’s What It Means

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Some traffic lights have yellow borders while others don't. Here's why reflective frames matter and why they aren't on every signal.

Traffic lights are something drivers encounter almost every day. And even though they’ve been looked at countless times, most drivers barely even notice some of the subtle details about them.

One detail that stands out at some intersections is a bright yellow border around the traffic lights. It’s a small change to a familiar piece of road equipment, and it can make a difference at busy intersections. Learn what the borders do and why they’re not on every traffic light.

The yellow borders around traffic lights are strips of reflective material attached to a black panel called a backplate. Much like reflective road signs, they catch vehicle headlights and help drivers spot traffic signals at night. The black background also makes the colored lights easier to see against bright skies and nearby buildings.

“This treatment is recognized as a human factors enhancement of traffic signal visibility, conspicuity, and orientation for both older and color vision deficient drivers,” according to the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA).

The borders also help drivers recognize intersections during power outages.

“This countermeasure is also advantageous during periods of power outages when the signals would otherwise be dark, providing a visible cue for motorists to stop at the intersection ahead,” according to the FHWA.

Research suggests the borders can improve road safety. A 2005 study found that adding yellow reflective borders reduced crashes at the intersections researchers examined. The FHWA now recognizes them as a proven safety measure. However, the borders don’t change how traffic lights operate or how long each light stays on. Drivers still need to follow normal traffic rules, including when a signal stops working.

Federal traffic safety standards allow yellow reflective borders, but don’t require them at every intersection. State and local transportation agencies generally decide where to install them. Some intersections may need better visibility because of glare, nearby buildings, or poor lighting. Transportation agencies may also add reflective borders when installing new signals or upgrading existing equipment.

Cost can influence those decisions, too. Adding reflective strips to an existing backplate is relatively simple, but installing a new backplate may require additional support. That’s why two intersections along the same road might have different traffic lights. The lack of a yellow border doesn’t necessarily mean a signal is unsafe.

#2362. Previous Homeowners May Still Have Access to Your Smart Home — Here’s How To Check

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Smart cameras, locks, and thermostats may still be linked to former owners. Experts explain how to check access and secure each device.

Moving into a new property can be a scary and intimidating process. There are so many new things to learn, and the last thing a new homeowner wants to worry about is home security. And while you should absolutely change the locks after moving in, proper safety measures don’t end there.

It’s possible for previous homeowners to still have access to smart home tech they left behind. Security cameras, thermostats, and other smart devices can remain linked to the previous owner’s account without the new homeowner realizing it.

Learn which devices need resetting, and what new homeowners should do to keep their homes secure.

Unfortunately, there’s no easy way to tell just by looking at a device.

“It is much safer to just assume the old owner can still access them,” says Kurtis Baron, an offensive security specialist at the cybersecurity company Echo Secure.

Nathan Connell, founder and editor of Future Technology, recommends trying to register each device through its manufacturer’s app. If the app says someone else already owns it, the previous account may still be linked. For example, Ring has an ownership transfer process for previously registered cameras and doorbells. Connell also recommends checking the app’s shared users list for unfamiliar accounts.

“The previous owner, or anyone they shared access with, can still unlock doors, open the garage or watch camera feeds remotely,” Connell says.

Baron recommends resetting all inherited smart devices, including cameras, alarms, locks, thermostats, and connected appliances. Some might be harder to identify than others. According to the Federal Trade Commission, “light bulbs or security systems may be difficult to detect as ‘smart’ devices.”

Homeowners should follow the manufacturer’s instructions to remove the previous account, factory reset each device, and register it under their own account. Google’s moving guide explains how to transfer Nest devices, some of which require the previous owner to remove them first.

“Old PIN codes on keypad locks and alarm panels often stay active too,” Connell says.

“The key to privacy in the Internet-of-Things (IoT) world is your router,” according to the FTC, since connected devices typically access the internet through it.

Baron recommends getting a new router rather than reusing the previous owner’s equipment. Homeowners should also set up their own Wi-Fi password and check for unfamiliar connected devices.

Some older smart devices may need replacing, especially if the manufacturer no longer provides security updates. Baron recommends replacing anything that can’t be fully factory reset or has become outdated.

A device linked to a previous owner’s account doesn’t always need replacing, though. Some manufacturers offer ownership transfers or can help new homeowners regain control. Until then, homeowners shouldn’t rely on an inherited smart lock or security camera for protection.

Connell recommends asking sellers for a list of every connected device included in the sale, along with manuals, hub information, and account transfer instructions.

The FTC also recommends leaving behind manuals that explain smart features, particularly those that might be difficult for new homeowners to identify. Sellers should confirm they’ve removed their accounts and revoked access for anyone previously authorized to use the devices.

Baron suggests including a requirement in the closing paperwork confirming that the seller has removed access to all smart devices included in the sale. That gives new homeowners a chance to set up their own accounts and passwords without leaving access in someone else’s hands.

#3499. The Three Most Common and Costly Things People Miss When Buying a Used Bike

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Before buying a secondhand bike, inspect these potentially costly trouble spots first.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

The customer came in right before a race because there was an issue with their shifting on the training ride. Yes, there was an issue with cable tension, but that wasn’t the biggest problem. The missing chip of carbon where the top tube met the seat tube overshadowed anything going on with the rear derailleur. When I asked the customer if he knew the chip was there, he said, “I bought the bike used and the seller said it was just a chip in the paint.”

Unfortunately, I’ve been on that end of the conversation with a lot of customers who brought in used bikes over the years. I get why someone would buy a used bike. Even when I ran shops, I would suggest used bikes as a way people could save money on a new (to them) bike. But there were too many times someone brought a used bike in for us to run through and we would find things like frame damage or wheels that needed to be replaced. Prices of new bikes have gone up dramatically over the last few years. Used bikes have their place for so many riders, but be aware of what you’re buying. Sometimes between the price of the used bike and then the parts and labor needed to make it ride-ready, you’ll be toeing the price of a new bike. Many of those new bikes also come with things like one free service or warranties from the manufacturer.

If you’re in the market for your next bike, it does make sense to consider used options. There are plenty of deals to be had on the used bike market. Here, I’ll give you the three most common mistakes I see people make when going the used route and give you my best practices when considering that used bike.

The biggest mistake I’ve seen people make is missing damage on the frame. I’ve had customers bring in used bikes where the frame had chips or cracks in the carbon or irreparable damage to aluminum frames. While there are shops that offer carbon repair, there are not a lot of reliable options for frame repair.

Let’s compare some pricing—an entry-level road bike is around $1,200 depending on the brand. Many times this will include some type of warranty, at least one free service or check on the bike, and at a minimum a free floor fit for you on the bike.

If the used bike’s frame needs to be replaced, then step one will be finding a frame compatible with as many existing components as possible. Many times parts like cables and housing, bottom brackets, and handlebar tape will need to be replaced when swapping to a new frame. Once a new frame is found, the parts listed above are added, and service gets put on the ticket, you are at or above the price of what that new bike would have cost.

This can be avoided by doing a thorough inspection of the frame when looking at a used bike. Do a thorough check of the frame—even the hard to get to parts like where the tires hide and under the frame. If I were buying a used bike I would bring a shop rag to wipe any dirty spots to do my due diligence. I wouldn’t recommend buying a damaged frame, unless you like it for wall art.

Wheels can be the second biggest money pit on a used road, gravel, or hybrid bike. When I was a service manager, we had to replace many wheels on newly purchased used bikes because the existing wheels were either damaged beyond repair or because the parts and labor to repair them outweighed the price of a new wheel.

The pricing was always surprising to people. Most shops I’ve worked in priced wheel builds around $100. If we were going to pull the hub (center of the wheel) and other components from the old wheel to install on a new wheel, there was a small, but additional charge. For people getting a used entry-level road, mountain, or hybrid bike, the labor of a wheel build, not including parts, could outprice the cost of a new wheel.

Reasons we would need to rebuild or replace a wheel were things like cracks in the rim at the spoke nipples, severe knocking in the hub (where the wheel develops too much lateral movement), or when the wheel was significantly out of true. Wheels can sometimes be straightened out (truing a wheel), but there are points where it can’t be brought back.

When looking at a used bike, there is a relatively easy way to check all of these wheel issues. First, look at the base of the spokes where they meet the rim. If you see cracks on the rim at any of these points, the wheel will need to get replaced. Once you check the base of the spokes, then hold the frame in one hand and the top of the wheel or tire in the other. Test for lateral movement in the wheel by holding the frame still and trying to move the wheel side-to-side. If it doesn’t move laterally, that’s another box checked.

Finally, spin the wheel while it is on the bike. Sometimes it can be hard to tell if the wheel is straight if the used bike has a flat tire. Try using your thumb as a guide. Hold your thumb a set distance from the rim surface (at least an inch). This technique will allow you to see if there is a severe wobble in the rim. Remember to keep your fingers far enough away from the wheel to not get them stuck in the spokes of the wheel as it spins. A small wobble can usually be fixed for under $50 at most shops.

This can be the hardest thing to see on a used bike because it can be really hard to tell how worn a drivetrain (chain, cassette or gears, and chainrings or crank) is with the naked eye. When someone is committed to buying a bike, a chain checker is one of the tools that can save you hundreds of dollars in the long run. A chain checker is a small, relatively inexpensive tool that checks how worn a chain is. By checking how worn a chain is, you can tell what other parts will need to be replaced. Many shops will recommend replacing an 11, 12, or 13 speed chain when a chain checker reads over 50 percent worn, replacing a chain and cassette or freewheel (gears on the back wheel) over 75 percent worn, and replacing chain, cassette or freewheel, and chainrings or crank (gears in the front near the pedals) over 100 percent worn.

If you don’t have the tool there are ways to check visually too. Sometimes you can see wear on the gears on the rear wheel (called the cassette or freewheel). When the teeth on the gears on the front or back start to become uneven and resemble shark teeth that is another quick visual check.

One fairly reliable trick to see if the chain is stretched is to try to pull the chain straight up and away from the gears by the pedals (called the chainrings). If it easily lifts up and away, and especially if there is a significant gap between the chain and chainring, there is likely a significant level of wear in the system. Having the chain checker is an easy way to tell just how much.

Pricing to get this fixed can easily exceed a few hundred dollars when all three components of the drivetrain (chain, cassette, and chainrings) need to be replaced. If the bike needs any additional labor that price can climb quicker than people expect.

When I ran a shop, I would always suggest customers get at a minimum a safety check or base-level tune-up on a used bike. This allows an experienced bicycle technician to inspect the bike, torque all the bolts and parts on the bike, and adjust things like shifting, brakes, and tire pressure. Even if the bike is a new bike that the seller said they just never rode, you never know who built it initially. I’ve had bikes in the stand that were in this category and the handlebar spun around with the slightest bit of pressure.

If you don’t want to take it to a shop, then consider options like local bike clubs, local bicycle nonprofits (our local example is the Coalition for Appropriate Transportation), or even an experienced cycling friend. Anyone who can run through the bike with an experienced set of eyes can help get you on the right path.

I used to suggest having the seller meet the potential buyer at my shop. This would allow both parties to be there when a service technician or adviser inspects the bike for an impartial assessment of the condition of the bike. Before using your local shop as the meet-up, give them a call to see if this is something they can offer.

This list includes the most common mistakes I would see specifically in the road, gravel, and hybrid categories of bikes. It does not touch on mountain bikes because that is an entire list in and of itself. Parts like suspension forks, rear shocks, dropper posts and pivot bearings are all things to be cautious about with used mountain bikes. If you have any other tips for buying a used bike let me know in the comments below.

Our new repo star history API now provides trends without returning individual identities or requiring unbounded database work. These are the engineering tradeoffs we made.

Maintainers look at star history to answer a practical question: did a launch, release, or event change a project’s trajectory? To reconstruct that curve from the GitHub API, tools had to request pages of stargazer records (each person who currently stars the repository and when they starred it), keep the timestamps, and throw the identities away.

Over time, that design created problems at both ends. Identities were available for bulk collection, so bad actors started scraping public stargazer lists for spam and phishing campaigns. For highly starred repositories, the endpoint could also make the database process many thousands of entries to return a single page.

The fix seemed clear: return counts instead of people. But returning less data didn’t actually make the query any cheaper.

Now maintainers have a direct way to get the curve: we built an endpoint that returns up to 30 weeks of daily star counts in a single request, with no identities attached. Keeping that cheap to serve meant reducing how much data the database processed, not just how much it returned, and the endpoint does that by filtering stars by time before it counts anything.

We first addressed the immediate risks to developers and to our database by restricting stargazer and watcher lists to repository administrators and collaborators. After the access change, callers without the required permission could receive an empty response or a 403 Forbidden response.

That change also cut off tools that had used the old response to calculate star history, often without displaying or otherwise using the usernames it returned. So we talked with maintainers and tool builders to understand what they still needed. They used star history as an approximate signal of interest: to compare activity before and after a release, or to see whether an announcement reached new developers. What they cared about was the curve.

The old API response had made identity look like part of that contract because every timestamp arrived attached to a user. In practice, tools such as Star History needed counts and dates, not usernames or other user data. Once identity was no longer part of the requirement, we could design an aggregate endpoint for the actual job: retrieving changes in star counts over time at a useful resolution.

When the API change disrupted Star History, users rallied around the project. GitHub’s Community team listened and connected us with product engineering, who dug into our use case and worked alongside us to design a purpose-built endpoint. A difficult situation became a constructive collaboration between GitHub, Star History, and the broader community—ultimately leading to a better solution.

Once we knew what maintainers needed, the next step was understanding why the original design couldn’t provide it cheaply. The original GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/stargazers endpoint was built to answer a simple question: who starred this repository? It returned a paginated list of people, and if the request specified a custom media type, each entry also included when the star was created.

The star model was built for the same job. It records the current set of people who have starred a repository, which keeps showing a star count or a list of stargazers simple. It was not designed as a historical event log: when someone removes a star, that record is removed. When tools began using the list as a time series, reconstructing a growth curve meant retrieving individual identities and processing every current star. Because each page held at most 100 people, the work grew with the repository’s popularity: building the full curve for react/react, with about 251,000 stars, took about 2,511 requests, so tools often ran into rate limits or sampled pages instead of reading them all.

Serving a page required the database to query the repository’s current stars. Due to a bug in the pagination implementation, every request also counted all of those stars to work out how many pages existed, even though the caller received only one page of up to 100 people. On a highly starred repository, that meant counting many thousands of entries on every request. In the week before we restricted access, nearly 37,000 of these count queries ran slowly enough to be logged, averaging nearly 3 seconds each, and database safeguards killed more than 1,000 of them, which contributed to request timeouts.

The chart below shows what all that counting cost. After we restricted access, the database served about 60% fewer stargazer requests, and the total time it spent on the count query fell by about 80%.

Removing identities handled the privacy requirement. The harder part was keeping each request from scanning the repository’s entire current star set.

Our first spike kept one record per star and returned the starred_at value without the stargazer’s identity for callers who lacked collaborator access. At first, this looked like the quickest path forward, because it directly served the request we had heard from tool builders. It also closed the identity-harvesting path with a relatively small change to the existing serialization and authorization flow.

This approach addressed identity exposure and would have allowed existing tools to keep working. But API consumers would still have to paginate through and process potentially many thousands of individual records to build an aggregate history. It also tied the new use case to an endpoint and pagination model designed for listing people rather than retrieving trends.

Aggregation seemed like the obvious next step. Instead of returning one row per star, the query could group stars into daily or weekly buckets, sort those buckets, and paginate the much smaller result.

The problem was where pagination happened. In a straightforward SQL query, the database first finds all stars for the repository, groups them into buckets, and sorts those buckets. Only then does it apply LIMIT, which controls how many buckets are returned, and OFFSET, which determines where the page begins:

For a popular repository, an anonymous request could still scan and aggregate many thousands of index entries to return a handful of buckets. Returning fewer buckets would shrink the JSON response, but it would not reduce the expensive grouping and sorting the database had already performed. For react/react, reading the full history that way would have meant examining roughly 6 million rows, because every one of the 24 requests would have reprocessed all of its stars. To reduce the query cost, we had to narrow the rows before aggregation began.

The design we shipped paginates star history by calendar week. For each request, it translates page and per_page into an indexed created_at range, filters the stars to those weeks, and then groups the matching records into daily counts. A response includes up to 30 weeks, and increasing page moves backward through the repository’s history.

With the date range applied first, each request processes the weeks being returned instead of every current star in the repository. For tools, that means one request now covers up to 30 weeks of history, no matter how many stars the repository has. For react/react, that’s about 24 requests instead of about 2,511, and the database examines each star about once (roughly 251,000 rows) across the full history. This fits the primary use case because maintainers usually inspect growth across weeks or around a known release, launch, or event. Week-based ranges also align with other repository activity APIs: the response follows the same shape as the weekly commit activity endpoint, GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/stats/commit_activity.

The time-range design limits each request to a defined period, but it does not limit the number of stars within that range. A week with an unusually large number of stars can therefore still produce a slow or expensive query. The normal request path no longer queries every current star, but exceptionally dense weeks remain a scaling limitation.

Comparing the performance of the old and new API paths also surfaced other places where GitHub was counting stars on demand. We found the same pattern on the repository stargazers page on GitHub.com and updated it to use the repository’s cached star count. Slow queries from that page, which had numbered in the hundreds each week, dropped to zero. We recently shipped a similar fix to other stargazer API endpoints.

#3566. Kona Mechanics’ View of the Ironman World Championship

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

On the Thursday before the 2026 Ironman World Championship, I was cruising down Aliʻi Drive around Kaliua-Kona, Hawaii, after a delicious lunch at Clover and Mug Coffee when it struck me that there weren’t many triathletes out biking and running. Over the next six miles, I saw a total of only 15 athletes (two of whom may have just been locals on an afternoon run).

Then I turned onto Palani Rd. and was greeted by the sight of around 30 athletes with pink, turquoise, and a few neon yellow wristbands wheeling their bikes up and down the hill. As I pulled into the parking lot, I quickly learned where all the Ironman athletes were – at Bike Works Kona, our town’s one and only dedicated triathlon shop.

After finding a parking spot, I tried to fight my way through the stream of jittery athletes and into the shop. I settled in to chat with Taylor Bushong, a traveling race mechanic who had a pop-up work stand on the shop’s lanai, about the crazy things he’d seen during the week of the 2026 Ironman World Championship.

It begins with the absurdly catastrophic: “Someone brought in a bike for us to build and it had two front wheels. How do you leave home with two front wheels? I blame it on taper brain,” said Bushong, speaking from personal experience as an Ironman Wisconsin 2025 finisher.

“Someone else brought in a bike with two rear wheels,” added Ivey Langley, a globe-trotting race mechanic. Could the two athletes combine their taper half brains to form one complete brain and one complete bike? Not quite, but fortunately, there happened to be a few wheels available for rent from the shop.

“Then there was the guy who needed us to fix his front derailleur. He insisted it would only take five minutes,” Bushong said. Like many of the repairs that the traveling mechanics and employees of Bike Works Kona tackle during race week, fixing the derailleur was not as simple as turning a screw or two. “I had to drill his frame and rivet something back into it. That’s really the hardest part of Ironman week – managing athletes’ timeline expectations. Managing the unrealistic ‘can you just’ requests. The understanding of the level of the request seems to be outside the scope of recognition.”

When time is of the essence, changing tires is a reasonable request but new chains are tricky, especially at 3 p.m. on Friday, three hours before the bike check-in cut-off. That’s when one athlete chose to seek help with a chain that was skipping after they replaced the old one.

“They failed to realize that if the old cassette is heavily worn, you’ll need a new cassette and a new chain because the new chain won’t sit properly in the worn grooves of the old cassette. When you pedal hard, the chain will skip over the teeth,” Langley said.

Replacing a chain can be tricky when there’s a tight deadline, but changing out wheels is a nightmare. And it often starts with a seemingly bright idea: Give your older, 11-speed bike an upgrade by borrowing Zipp wheels from a friend. If the friend rides a brand new, 12-speed Cervélo, however, you could run into trouble.

“One person came in on Friday [the day before race start] because their gears weren’t working after they changed out their race wheels,” Langley said. “ I had to explain that they had a 12-speed cassette on an 11-speed drivetrain and their gears were jumping because the spacing between cogs, the chain width, and the shifter indexing on an 11-speed drivetrain don’t match a 12-speed setup.”

The consensus among everyone I spoke to at Bike Works Kona was that Friday was the most chaotic day of Ironman week, demanding that the staff act as both professional mechanics and temporary therapists.

“There was one athlete who was on their way to check in his bike when the back tire just went flat. The tube he was putting on was not working so he came back in here. He was so worried that he wouldn’t make it to bike check-in that he started crying. I had to calm him down,” Bike Works’ Marc Grande told me once I finally made it through the shop’s door to the sales counter. “Sometimes they need new tubes or valve extenders. Other times, they just need someone to look at their bike and tell them that their bike is OK and there’s nothing to worry about.”

“But sometimes they need new bike computers,” added my husband, Patrick Stover, who helped at least three triathletes whose computers had popped off while they were on training rides during race week. “Often, it isn’t enough to click it into the mount. If you don’t want to buy a new Garmin the day before Ironman, use a tether or find another way to secure your bike computer.”

The most common issue that arose during race week was with tubeless tires. Triathlon has largely been moving toward tubeless tires and the appeal feels like a no-brainer: Tubeless tires with sealant are far less prone to flats than tubular tires — and you can run them at lower pressures for lower rolling resistance and increased comfort. But you have to know how to properly put sealant in those tires.

Or you can head to a bike shop in a panic before your race, like hundreds (literally, hundreds) of athletes did this year during Ironman World Championship week. There were tubeless tires that were bone dry. Somehow there were both tires lazily hanging off their rims, while others obstinately clung to their rims. It wasn’t pretty. And it wouldn’t happen if more of us learned how to add sealant to our tires and test out our tubeless repair kits earlier in the season.

Another common yet preventable problem? Gear that was lost or damaged because of poor packing practices. If you’re packing your bike yourself, don’t forget to take the CO2 cartridges out. If you do forget, the TSA is going to go through your bike case and “help” you repack. Spoiler alert: It rarely goes well.

“We’ve heard firsthand from athletes whose cases were opened by TSA. One lost a shoe. The other lost a wheel. Save yourself the headache and buy your CO2 canisters once you get to Kona,” said high-performance technical director at Breakthrough Sports and lead mechanic for neutral support for pro athletes at the T100 Series Anthony Foy, who was on hand to help the Bike Works staff and traveling mechanics. “We’ve also seen shifters broken on the front end because people forget to remove the handlebar extensions when they pack their bikes.”

A handful of athletes visited the shop for help replacing their gear after their luggage was lost, including their race kit. You can’t control these things, but it helps to pack your goggles, race kit, bike and run shoes, and helmet in your carry-on. You really don’t want to be rushing around trying to replace those familiar touchpoints once you get to Kona for ostensibly the biggest race of your year (or life).
