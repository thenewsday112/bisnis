Coneflowers are one of my favorite flowers to grow for their cheerful summer blooms and their ability to attract pollinators. I love that they tend to just get on with everything, with or without my help. That being said, though...

Well, even when their petals have faded and the garden is beginning to wind down for winter, though, I wouldn't advise you to forget about your coneflowers just yet. In fact, October is a useful time to give these perennials a little attention, helping them prepare for the colder months while making the most of what they still have to offer.

The good news is that coneflowers (Echinacea spp.) don't need a complicated fall care routine. With that in mind, then, what do these pretty blooms want from us all when the weather starts to turn?

I feel it's my duty to point out that one of the most helpful things you can do for coneflowers resist the temptation to cut everything back, as their dried flower heads can provide food for hungry birds, while leaving healthy stems standing can add structure to the garden through winter.

There are, however, a few things worth checking before the weather turns colder. From deciding which seedheads to leave in place to spotting diseased foliage, here are three October tasks I'd prioritize for healthier coneflowers and a more wildlife-friendly garden.

If there's one thing I'd encourage gardeners to do with their coneflowers in October, it's to leave the healthy seedheads standing. Once the colorful petals have dropped, the central cones dry out and contain seeds that birds can forage on through fall and winter.

American goldfinches are particularly fond of coneflower seeds, and watching these bright-yellow birds perch on the sturdy stems and pick out the seeds is a lovely reminder that a garden can keep giving long after its flowers have faded. Other seed-eating birds (including your very own birth month bird) may also visit, depending on your region and the species growing in your garden.

I'd resist the urge to deadhead every faded flower simply to make the border look tidy. Instead, leave plenty of healthy seedheads in place and let them provide a natural food source. They'll also add interesting shapes and texture to the winter garden, particularly when dusted with frost.

One thing to keep in mind is that not every coneflower cultivar produces the same amount of viable seed. Some highly bred double-flowered varieties may offer less to birds than simpler, single-flowered forms. If supporting wildlife is a priority, consider growing native purple coneflower (Echinacea purpurea) and other varieties known to produce seed.

Although I'd leave healthy coneflower stems standing, I wouldn't ignore signs of disease. October is a good time to look over the foliage and remove material that's badly affected by fungal problems, particularly powdery mildew.

Powdery mildew often appears as a pale, powdery coating on leaves and stems. It can be more noticeable late in the growing season, especially when plants have experienced humid conditions or poor air circulation. A little cosmetic damage isn't necessarily a reason to remove an otherwise healthy plant, but heavily affected foliage shouldn't simply be left to sit in the garden.

I'd stick to the Three D's rule and cut out badly diseased material using clean pruning tools and dispose of it appropriately rather than adding it to a home compost pile. This helps reduce the amount of infected debris left around the plant.

It's important to be selective here. I wouldn't cut every coneflower down just because some leaves are looking tired. Healthy stems and seedheads can remain standing for birds and winter interest, while diseased material can be removed as needed.

My final October task would be to prepare coneflowers for winter without disturbing them unnecessarily. In most gardens, established coneflowers are hardy perennials that don't require extensive protection, but a little attention to the soil can help.

I'd check that the soil around the plants isn't compacted and make sure water can drain away rather than pooling around the crowns. Coneflowers generally prefer well-drained soil, and persistently wet conditions can cause problems during colder weather. If the ground is dry, I may water the plants before the soil freezes, particularly if conditions have been unusually dry. And a light layer of mulch can help moderate soil temperatures and protect roots, although I'd avoid piling it directly over the crown.

Most importantly, I'd leave the main cleanup until spring. Once the weather warms and new growth begins to emerge, I can cut back the old stems before they get in the way. Waiting also means I don't remove useful seedheads too early or disturb wildlife that may be using the garden through winter.

Basically, coneflowers don't need much fuss in October, and that's part of their appeal. By leaving healthy seedheads for goldfinches, removing diseased foliage when necessary, and protecting the root zone without cutting everything down, I can help these reliable perennials get through winter while keeping the garden useful to wildlife.

Rather than treating fall as a time to strip the garden bare, I'd see it as an opportunity to be more selective. A few standing stems can provide food, shelter, and winter interest – and when spring arrives, the coneflowers will be ready to grow again. And you can't say better than that, right?

For some cancer patients, eating sweets worsens antibiotics’ disruption of gut bacteria

Sugary diets and antibiotics impact the gut microbiome. Now, researchers are exploring these two effects combined.

While a spoonful of sugar might help us gulp down a bitter-tasting antibiotic, it also may increase the damage that these drugs cause to the gut microbes.

In patients being treated for blood cancers, eating sugary items during antibiotic treatment was linked to a greater reduction in gut bacteria diversity than taking the antibiotics alone. The combination was also tied to the growth of gut bacteria associated with hard-to-treat infections, researchers report September 30 in Nature.

Cancer patients have lots of variation in their diets and take medications that can damage their microbiome. So, these patients provide a unique opportunity to study dramatic gut changes “that we believe are also relevant in the healthy context,” says Jonas Schluter, a microbiologist at NYU Langone Health.

Previous evidence separately suggested that antibiotics and sugars can disrupt the gut microbiome. But their combined effects, particularly in people at greater risk of infection, was unclear.

Researchers closely tracked more than 9,000 meals eaten by 173 people hospitalized in a treatment center to undergo blood-forming stem cell transplants. “Diet records are one of the biggest black boxes in a lot of diet microbiome studies,” since meals are often self-reported, says Tiffany Weir, a microbiologist at Colorado State University in Fort Collins, who was not involved in the research.

Standardized hospital meals allowed researchers to measure multiple days’ worth of food intake down to the gram, then compare the amounts of various nutrients eaten with bacterial diversity data obtained from stool samples.

For every additional 100 grams of sugar (the equivalent of 2.5 cans of Coke) that patients on antibiotics ate in the two days before sampling, the team found a 24 percent decrease in bacterial diversity. Consuming sweets and sugary beverages during antibiotic treatment was also associated with blooms of Enterococcus bacteria, which can be drug-resistant and are linked to difficult-to-treat infections associated with longer hospital stays and higher mortality.

“Some bacteria, sometimes, survive antibiotics, or perhaps more dangerously, they are resistant to [them],” Schluter says. By giving these bacteria the “universal resource for life, which is simple sugars, we are exacerbating their dominance. It’s almost like burning down the rainforest with a lot of fire, and then trying to extinguish the flames by putting in more petrol.”

The researchers also exposed mice on standard or sucrose-rich diets to a single dose of antibiotics. They found that the sugar exacerbated the enterococcal abundance after antibiotic exposure.

“Reducing sugar intake could be another way for patients to support their gut microbiome during antibiotic treatment,” says Sarah Berry, a nutrition scientist at King’s College London who was not involved in the study. Still, more research is needed to determine whether the findings apply to anyone on these drugs.

Schluter hopes that further studies and clinical trials can help uncover more about the exact relationship between sugar, antibiotics and the gut microbiome.

“You need to take antibiotics to fight infections, but you should also be aware that they have side effects to the microbiome,” Schluter says. “Now, we need to find strategies to mitigate this damage.”

#835. One of Earth’s most powerful supervolcanoes is refilling with fresh magma

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

One of Earth's most powerful supervolcanoes is slowly refilling its enormous underground magma reservoir, thousands of years after producing one of the largest volcanic eruptions in recent geological history. Scientists at Kobe University have discovered evidence that fresh magma is accumulating beneath Japan's Kikai caldera, which unleashed a catastrophic eruption about 7,300 years ago.

The discovery offers a rare look at how giant volcanic systems rebuild their magma supplies after massive eruptions. It could also provide important clues about the behavior of other supervolcanoes, including Yellowstone in the United States and Toba in Indonesia, potentially helping scientists improve their ability to recognize warning signs of future eruptions.

How Supervolcanoes Store Enormous Amounts of Magma

Some volcanic eruptions are so immense that they dramatically reshape the landscape. The amount of magma released can be staggering, enough to bury all of New York City's Central Park beneath a layer roughly 12 kilometers (7.5 miles) thick.

When such enormous volumes of magma escape from underground, the rock above the emptied reservoir can collapse, leaving behind a broad, relatively shallow depression known as a caldera. These features are associated with some of the most powerful eruptions in Earth's history.

Famous examples include Yellowstone, Toba, and Japan's Kikai caldera, most of which lies beneath the ocean. Kikai last experienced a giant eruption approximately 7,300 years ago, producing the largest known volcanic eruption of the Holocene, the geological epoch that began roughly 11,700 years ago and continues today.

Although scientists know that giant caldera volcanoes can erupt more than once, they still have an incomplete understanding of how the enormous quantities of magma required for these events accumulate underground. This makes it extremely difficult to anticipate when another major eruption might occur.

"We must understand how such large quantities of magma can accumulate to understand how giant caldera eruptions occur," says Kobe University geophysicist Nobukazu Seama.

Scientists Discover a Massive Magma Reservoir Beneath Japan

Kikai's underwater location might seem like an obstacle to studying the volcano, but it actually gives researchers an important advantage. The surrounding seafloor allows scientists to carry out extensive geological surveys across the volcanic structure.

"The underwater location allows us to implement systematic, large-scale surveys," Seama explains.

To investigate what lies beneath Kikai, researchers from Kobe University collaborated with the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC). They used powerful airgun arrays to generate seismic pulses that traveled through the crust beneath the ocean floor.

Instruments called ocean bottom seismometers recorded how these waves moved through the underground rock. Because seismic waves travel differently through solid rock and regions containing molten material, scientists can analyze their behavior to investigate structures that cannot be observed directly.

The findings, published in Communications Earth & Environment, revealed a substantial region containing a high proportion of magma directly beneath the site of Kikai's massive eruption 7,300 years ago.

By examining the seismic measurements, the team was able to determine the approximate size and shape of the underground reservoir. Its position and extent suggest that it occupies the same magma storage system involved in the ancient eruption.

"Due to its extent and location it is clear that this is in fact the same magma reservoir as in the previous eruption," Seama says.

Fresh Magma Is Recharging the Ancient Supervolcano

Perhaps the most intriguing finding is that the magma currently beneath Kikai does not appear to be simply leftover material from its catastrophic eruption thousands of years ago.

Previous investigations revealed that a new lava dome has been developing near the center of the caldera over approximately the past 3,900 years. A lava dome forms when relatively thick, viscous lava accumulates around a volcanic vent rather than flowing freely across the surrounding landscape.

Chemical analyses also showed that volcanic material from this dome and other more recent activity differs in composition from the material released during Kikai's giant eruption.

That difference provides an important clue. It suggests that the magma now stored beneath the volcano was supplied by new injections of molten rock from deeper within Earth's crust or mantle, rather than remaining unchanged since the previous eruption.

"This means that the magma that is now present in the magma reservoir under the lava dome is likely newly injected magma," summarizes Seama.

Together, the seismic observations and chemical evidence help researchers develop a clearer picture of how a giant volcanic system can gradually rebuild its underground magma supply following a massive eruption.

This replenishment process does not mean that another catastrophic eruption is imminent. Instead, it reveals how a magma reservoir can be recharged over thousands of years, an important process that scientists need to understand before they can reliably assess the long-term behavior of supervolcanoes.

What the Discovery Could Reveal About Yellowstone

The findings could have implications far beyond Japan.

Researchers believe the process identified beneath Kikai may help explain why other enormous caldera systems, including Yellowstone and Toba, also contain large magma reservoirs relatively close to Earth's surface.

"This magma re-injection model is consistent with the existence of large shallow magma reservoirs beneath other giant calderas like Yellowstone and Toba," says Seama.

The proposed model suggests that, following a giant eruption, new magma can gradually enter and replenish an existing underground reservoir. Understanding how this happens could reveal important patterns in the long-term cycles of magma accumulation, volcanic activity, and major eruptions.

Researchers now hope to improve the seismic techniques used at Kikai so they can investigate the movement and storage of magma in greater detail.

"We want to refine the methods that have proved to be so useful in this study to more deeply understand the re-injection processes. Our ultimate goal is to become better able to monitor the crucial indicators of future giant eruptions."

By revealing how fresh magma accumulates beneath a volcano responsible for one of Earth's most extraordinary eruptions, the research brings scientists closer to understanding the processes that eventually make such colossal events possible.

What do frozen pipes, skidding tires, and high heating bills have in common? They’re all winter-weather nuisances that can be avoided with adequate preparation. If you use these expert-backed strategies to winterize your home, garden, car, and personal care routine, no amount of snow or ice will spoil your enjoyment of the season.

Winter storms can deposit heaps of tree branches onto your roof. These can then snap under the weight of snow or ice and go on to pierce holes in your roof, loosen shingles, or get swept onto a neighboring roof where they can cause damage. To avoid surprise leaks in your roof, periodically prune branches that hang over the house, and use a roof rake to clear away any branches that fall onto the roof. (Call in an arborist if the job’s too big for you.)

Even after you turn off an outdoor spigot, water that lingers in an attached garden hose can freeze and cause pipes behind the spigot to burst, spouting water into your house. Always disconnect and drain garden hoses after using the spigot in cold weather, and consider shutting off the valve inside the house to protect your pipes.

When your gutters become blocked with leaves or twigs, snow and ice that would ordinarily get directed away from the house will collect (and eventually melt) on the roof or run off onto the ground, leading to rotting fascia boards below the roofline, cracks in the foundation or walkways, or leaks in the basement. To avoid costly structural damage, inspect and, if needed, clean your gutters before the first freeze. Use a trowel or scoop for large debris, and rinse away smaller debris with a garden hose.

Many unsuspecting homeowners have gone out of town during a particularly cold stretch only to return to a flood caused by an exposed pipe that froze and burst. Wrap insulation sleeves around pipes that run through unheated areas, such as attics and crawl spaces, to protect pipes from freezing and heat loss. To minimize the risk of frozen pipes, Jonathan Faccone, interior designer and founder of Halo Homebuyers, advises, “Make sure to keep the heat on, even when you’re away from home.”

Applying caulk or weatherstripping to drafty windows and doors can keep the cold out and the heat in, maximizing indoor comfort and saving you anywhere from 5 to 10 percent in energy costs, according to the U.S. Department of Energy. Sealing up gaps can also prevent melted ice or snow from rotting exterior features. So, take a day before the temperatures dip to caulk joints between walls and window frames and door frames, and place weatherstripping around window sashes.

An underinsulated home is subject to cold spots and higher heating bills, while a properly insulated home enjoys evenly distributed warmth, increased indoor comfort, and energy savings. The most effective places to insulate are exterior walls, attics, and unheated garages, but it’s important to find and repair any leaks in those areas before adding batt, spray-foam, loose-fill, or blown-in insulation.

Have an older hot water heater? If its R-value is less than 24 (or if it’s warm to the touch), consider wrapping it with a water heater insulation blanket. Made of materials that range from foil to fiberglass, these blankets act like a jacket for your water heater—and they can reduce heat loss by 25 to 45 percent. With the 7 to 16 percent you’ll save in energy costs, you can recoup the purchase price of the blanket in less than a year.

To ensure your fireplace is ready for the winter months, have it inspected once a year, and stock up on supplies like firewood and fire starters. You’ll also want to have an ash bucket nearby for safe and efficient ash removal. Always keep flammable materials and objects, such as blankets and baskets, at least 3 feet away from the fireplace to avoid any hazardous situations. When the fireplace isn’t in use, close the damper above the firebox to prevent warm indoor air from escaping and cold drafts from entering the house through the chimney.

If your fan blades are still set to turn counterclockwise, the warm air you crave will float to the ceiling, while cold air will get pushed down toward the floor level where you are. To avoid wintertime chills, switch the direction of your ceiling fan so that it rotates clockwise and the blades circulate warm air down to you.

Before the first frost, it’s important to aerate the lawn to allow the soil to breathe after all the natural compaction that has occurred over the past year. This can be done with an aerator, a tool with spiked tines that pierce the soil, or, for larger lawns, with an electric- or gas-powered aerator or one that can be towed behind a lawn mower. But once your turf has gone dormant for the winter, keep kids, pets, and equipment off it as much as possible; heavy traffic can damage the turfgrass crowns that grow at soil level, resulting in bare patches of grass in spring.

A car breakdown stinks at any time of year, but it’s the worst when you have to open the hood and diagnose the problem in freezing temperatures. “Depending on where you live, extreme cold, snow, ice, and road salt can take a big toll over time if your car isn’t protected,” cautions Jason Farrell, certified master technician at Mechanic’s Diary. “Moisture gets into places it wouldn’t normally, and moving parts like brakes and suspension have to work harder, so prioritizing maintenance is key.”

To avoid getting stranded in the cold, check your car’s manual to see if you’re due for a service, and be sure to make that appointment before the first freeze. If you can’t afford to make all recommended repairs, focus on the battery, brakes, cables, spark plugs, and tires.

You can’t wait for snow to thaw before you hit the road, so be sure to keep ice melt, such as a bag of rock salt, in the back seat of your car. Having ice melt close at hand means you’re always ready to take care of ice on walkways and driveways to help prevent nasty slips, falls, or car accidents. Carry a bag of sand, too: You can throw it over the salt to provide traction when you’re trying to get your car out of an icy spot.

If you regularly drive in heavy snow, consider swapping your traditional tires for a set of snow tires, which have treads with large gaps that help improve traction.

“Having the right [tires] for the conditions can make a huge difference in safety and control,” Farrell explains. To preserve the tread on snow tires, wait until daytime temperatures are consistently 50 degrees Fahrenheit or below to install them. “I always recommend getting snow tires installed in sets of four if you live somewhere with frequent snowfall,” Farrell says. “The deeper tread helps grip the road better on slick surfaces. And they don’t just help you brake and turn—snow tires can also help you accelerate from a full stop more easily too. It’s worth the investment to feel more secure getting around in bad weather.”

While an ice scraper and a spray bottle of homemade de-icer are two effective ways of clearing ice off your windshield, both require that you get out of your vehicle and face the brutal cold. Installing winter windshield wiper blades, which have flexible rubber and anti-clogging arms, will help fend off snow and ice as you sit comfortably in your warm car.

Seventy percent of winter-weather injuries happen in cars, according to the National Severe Storms Laboratory. Your friends and family may not always be able to rush to your aid during a roadside emergency, so it’s a good idea to enroll in an automotive club or a roadside assistance program offered by your car insurance company. If you opt in to one of these services, keep the customer support number in your phone contacts so you have it when you need it.

If you like toasty indoor temperatures but can’t afford high heating bills, acclimate yourself to a lower, more energy-efficient thermostat setting by starting at 72 degrees Fahrenheit and then gradually reducing it to 68 degrees as you adapt to the change. A 1-degree reduction in a programmable thermostat setting can reduce your heating bills by 1 percent if maintained for 8 hours, according to Direct Energy. With a few layers of clothing on, you may never even notice the difference in temperature.

If you’re away from home by day or can make do with less heat as you sleep, you can boost the cost savings by bumping the thermostat down to 62 to 66 degrees Fahrenheit. “Smart thermostats are a great tool for regulating the temperature in your home and saving energy costs,” Faccone says. Even with the thermostat set to moderate temperatures, central heating can quickly evaporate moisture from your skin, leaving it dry and flaky. Invest in a humidifier, a device that emits water vapor to increase the moisture of surrounding air, to help you maintain supple skin that glows all winter long. Regularly clean the humidifier and change the filter according to the manufacturer’s instructions to prevent the growth of bacteria and mold.

A long, piping-hot shower may be tempting on a cold day, but the water’s high temperature can dry out your skin and aggravate skin conditions like eczema or psoriasis, and the extra soak time can spike your water bill. Keep your skin hydrated and your costs low by limiting showers to 5 minutes and using warm (not hot) water.

To keep the driveway clear and safe during the winter, shovel or plow regularly. Removing snow and ice from the driveway not only prevents hazardous conditions but also ensures easy access to and from your home, which can be crucial during emergencies. Shoveling snow can put a huge strain on the heart, however, and can increase the risk of heart attack for some people. If you don’t feel up to shoveling, hire someone to help you.

When temperatures drop, rodents seek out warm places to spend the winter. Prevent your home from becoming a rodent refuge by sealing up cracks and holes that a mouse can fit through. Replace loose weatherstripping surrounding doors and windows, which may provide another entry point for rodents. Because mice often take up residence in woodpiles, store firewood at least 20 feet away from your residence.

Many of the strategies used to keep rodents out of the home also apply to insects, but there are some additional things you can do to avoid bug infestations. Generally speaking, insects are drawn to moisture, so make your home less hospitable by keeping interior spaces clean and dry. Because insects can find their way indoors through holes and cracks in exterior piping, insulate all pipes to stop pests in their tracks.

According to American Home Shield, conditions like diabetes, heart disease, and arthritis can all be exacerbated in the winter, so it’s prudent to schedule a vet checkup as winter approaches. If your pets don’t already have microchips, now is a good time to get them since snowy weather can cause cats and dogs to become disoriented. Though dog clothing may look silly, short-haired, small, very thin, or elderly dogs can benefit from warm winter jackets to keep them comfortable during cold-weather walks.

Winter storms seem to be getting worse every year, so don’t be caught off guard when the next one hits: Invest in a generator if power outages are a common occurrence in your area. Also, always keep flashlights in an easy-to-access location and make sure they have fresh batteries, with spares easily accessible. When a storm is on the horizon, make sure mobile phones and portable power stations are juiced up so that you will have a means of communication if the power goes out.

Stocking up on essentials is smart during winter storm season. You’ll want to keep bottled water (Direct Energy recommends a minimum of 1 gallon per person per day) and plenty of nonperishable food items on hand in case the power goes out. Consider stocking up on staples like canned fish, beans, and vegetables, and packaged nuts and cereal, all of which are fairly nutritious and easy to prepare. It wouldn’t hurt to have paper products like toilet paper, paper towels, and paper plates in your stockpile too.

If you live in a region where temperatures typically drop below freezing, winterize your irrigation system to prevent frozen or burst pipes. The process generally involves turning off the water supply to the system, turning off the timer, and opening the valves to remove water from the pipes and sprinkler heads. When the system is drained, close the valves. Some systems are equipped with an automatic drain function, while others are best cleared by blowing out with compressed air. Always check the manufacturer’s instructions for your sprinkler system, and call in a pro if you’re not comfortable doing it yourself.

One of the best ways to prevent pipes from freezing is to leave under-sink cabinets open, especially at night when temperatures drop. Simply prop open closed cabinet doors to keep warm air circulating around the pipes.

As winter temperatures settle in, the cold weather hits your garden first. Protect your plants from frigid temperatures and insulate the roots against the cold by laying a thin layer of mulch to your garden bed (no more than 2 to 3 inches). #864. Dental implants can hijack your immune system, scientists discover

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For tens of millions of people, dental implants have provided a way to replace missing teeth with a full set of fixed, functional teeth rather than removable dentures. However, an estimated 10% to 20% of implant patients eventually develop peri-implantitis, a serious infection that damages the surrounding jawbone.

Antibiotics frequently fail to control this condition, and scientists have struggled to understand why. Now, researchers believe they have uncovered the mechanism responsible.

A new study published in PNAS Nexus by scientists at the Rutgers School of Dental Medicine reveals that bacteria can gradually corrode dental implants, releasing microscopic titanium particles into nearby tissues. These particles interfere with the immune cells responsible for fighting infection, trapping them in a state of excessive inflammation that progressively destroys jawbone.

The researchers examined human tissue samples, studied human immune cells in the laboratory, and conducted experiments using genetically engineered mice. Their investigation identified a calcium channel in macrophages, the immune cells that engulf bacteria, that becomes activated by titanium particles. When this channel was disabled in mice, the disease was prevented.

This discovery provides the first credible drug target for a condition that affects as many as one in five dental implant recipients and costs healthcare systems worldwide more than $1 billion annually.

"For the first time, we show why all the antibiotic treatments that work around teeth do not work around implants," said Georgios Kotsakis, senior author of the study and assistant dean for clinical research at Rutgers School of Dental Medicine. "Now that we know the cause, we can start developing therapeutics."

Why Dental Implant Infections Resist Antibiotics

One of the biggest mysteries surrounding peri-implantitis has been its similarity to periodontitis, the gum disease that develops around natural teeth. Both conditions begin with the same types of bacteria found in the mouth, yet they respond very differently to treatment.

For natural teeth, antibiotics combined with routine professional cleaning can resolve the infection. Around dental implants, however, the same medications are successful in fewer than half of cases. Even when treatment is attempted, the underlying jawbone may continue to deteriorate.

For the past two decades, scientists have largely concentrated on the bacteria responsible for these infections. Researchers in Kotsakis' laboratory decided to investigate another possible contributor: the implant material itself.

Their work showed that bacteria accumulating on implant surfaces create acidic biofilms that slowly damage the titanium. Over time, this corrosion can release billions of microscopic metal particles, each smaller than a red blood cell.

Titanium particles can also be dislodged during routine dental cleaning. This is particularly concerning when instruments designed for natural teeth are used on implants, because they can scrape and damage the metal surface.

How Titanium Particles Hijack the Immune System

Once released into the gums, the tiny titanium fragments attract a bacterial toxin known as lipopolysaccharide, which coats their surfaces. This combination creates a deceptive target for the immune system, making the particles resemble unusually large bacteria that cannot be broken down.

Macrophages, the white blood cells responsible for engulfing and destroying harmful microorganisms, attempt to consume these particles. But because the cells cannot digest titanium, they become stuck in a prolonged state of excessive inflammation.

As a result, the macrophages produce large quantities of inflammatory signaling molecules, including interleukin-1 beta. This protein is also associated with inflammatory processes involved in rheumatoid arthritis and Alzheimer's disease.

The resulting immune response gradually breaks down the bone supporting the dental implant. At the same time, the macrophages become less effective at fighting the bacteria that triggered the infection in the first place.

Laboratory experiments revealed just how severely their defenses were disrupted. Macrophages exposed to titanium particles engulfed fewer than half as many bacteria as immune cells that had not encountered the particles.

"These particles are little magnets that attract the bacterial toxin, and they hijack the immune system, preventing it from clearing bacteria," said Kotsakis. "You have a perfect storm that defies antibiotics."

Scientists Identify a Potential New Treatment Target

After uncovering how titanium particles disrupt immune defenses, the researchers investigated the molecular pathway responsible for the damaging reaction.

They traced the process to TRPC1, a calcium channel (a specialized, pore-forming protein structure within cell membranes) found in macrophages. This channel plays a central role in the inflammatory response triggered by titanium particles and bacterial toxins.

To test its importance, the team used genetically engineered mice that lacked TRPC1. When these animals were exposed to the same combination of titanium particles and bacteria, their immune systems responded much more effectively.

The mice developed dramatically smaller abscesses, produced fewer inflammatory signaling molecules, and regained the ability to clear bacteria. Disabling the channel prevented the destructive disease process observed when TRPC1 was present.

The results point toward a potentially new approach to treating peri-implantitis by targeting the immune system response rather than relying exclusively on antibiotics.

With funding from the National Institutes of Health, Kotsakis and his colleagues are now evaluating potential drugs that act on the same molecular pathway in human cells.

Why Proper Dental Implant Cleaning Matters

Although the research opens the door to future therapies, it also reinforces an important preventive measure for people who already have dental implants.

Regular professional cleaning remains the strongest known protection against peri-implantitis. However, the instruments and techniques used during those appointments can make a significant difference.

Until approximately a decade ago, many dentists routinely cleaned implants with the same metal scalers used to remove deposits from natural teeth. Research by the Rutgers team and other scientists has since demonstrated that these tools can damage and corrode titanium surfaces, potentially releasing additional particles and accelerating the disease.

Today, nonabrasive cleaning methods have become standard practice, helping protect implant surfaces while reducing the risk of complications.

By revealing how corroded titanium can interfere with immune defenses, the findings offer a new explanation for why some dental implant infections are so difficult to treat and identify a promising direction for developing more effective therapies.

Two recent papers measured this on language models. Kim et al. compared more than 350 models and found that, on one leaderboard dataset, two models agree 60% of the time when both are wrong, and that larger, more accurate models have highly correlated errors even across developers [4]. Kohli measured panels of LLM judges with a Kish effective sample size and found that nine frontier models from seven families carried about two independent votes' worth of information on natural-language inference [5]. A recent post repeated that measurement with five judges on 300 sentence pairs and landed inside Kohli's range [6].

I wanted a version anyone can rerun and get the same numbers, up to floating-point noise, so I used open models and read their answers as probabilities instead of sampling them. I also added a control for one bias that can fake agreement: every model gets its own random order of the answer options.

Figure 4 lays out the whole experiment before the details.

The test is MMLU, 57 multiple-choice subjects that range from elementary mathematics to law [7]. I drew 1,500 questions spread evenly over the subjects and set aside the questions whose options refer to each other ('all of the above'), because shuffling the options would break them.

The panel is six open models of 3 to 4 billion parameters from five developers (Table 1). I also planned to include Llama 3.2 3B, but its gated download failed, so it is not in the panel. Open weights let me read exact probabilities instead of sampling answers.

Each model gets the standard five-shot MMLU prompt (three or four examples for about 3.5% of questions where five would not fit in 2,048 tokens), and I read the probability it assigns to the next token being A, B, C or D. Nothing in the main experiment is sampled, so a rerun should give the same answers, up to floating-point noise on near-ties. Exact ties between two options are rare and go to the earlier one.

One detail matters more than it looks. Earlier work found that LLMs prefer particular option letters, mostly because the model assigns more probability to certain letter tokens a priori [8]. Two models that both lean toward A would agree on wrong answers for reasons unrelated to what they know. To take that out, every model sees each question with its own random ordering of the options, and I map its answer back to the original option before comparing anything. The weakest model I tried shows why this matters: a 1-billion-parameter model from AI2 put 74% of its answers on A or B, even though the right answer was equally likely to land on any letter. As a control, I also ran everyone on the original order.

The function does four things. It marks every wrong answer, correlates those marks for every pair of models, averages the pair correlations, and puts that average into the formula. Everything else I ran (the prompts, the option shuffling, the bootstrap intervals) is plumbing around it.

The average pairwise error correlation, which I will write as φˉ\bar{\varphi}φˉ​, is 0.41 (95% bootstrap interval 0.39 to 0.44), where 0 would mean the mistakes are unrelated. No pair is anywhere near 0: all 15 fall between 0.33 and 0.48. The one pair from the same developer, Qwen2.5 and Qwen3, sits at 0.45, and the 14 pairs from different developers average 0.41. When two models are both wrong, they choose the same wrong answer 59% of the time, close to the 60% that Kim et al. report for one leaderboard dataset [4]. Each added model contributes less than the one before: going from one model to two adds 0.4 effective votes, and going from five to six adds 0.07.

Votes only matter if they change the answer, so I also ran the vote. For every possible panel of each size I took the most common answer and compared its accuracy with what independent voters of the same individual accuracy would score. I computed that baseline exactly, assuming a voter who is wrong picks uniformly among the three wrong options, and I used odd panel sizes so that ties do not blur the picture.

With five models the independent baseline is 87.1% and the real vote scored 71.2% (95% interval 69.2 to 73.2), a gap of about 16 points. All six models together scored 71.8%, against 90.3% for independent voters.

The best single model, Qwen3-4B, scored 72.7%, and none of the 57 possible panels of two to six models beat it when each model casts one vote. The best panel, three models, tied it at 1,091 of 1,500 questions.

Counting only each model's top choice throws information away, so I also tried averaging the models' probabilities. That does better: all six together reach 73.8%, a gain of 1.1 points over Qwen3-4B with a 95% interval of -0.8 to 3.0 points, and the best of the 57 panels, picked after the fact, reaches 74.1%. Neither is distinguishable from the best model alone, and both fall well short of the 90% that independent voters would reach by plain voting.

All six models agreed on 35.8% of the questions, and when they did the shared answer was wrong 5.8% of the time (31 of 537 questions). Independent voters would agree on only 7.8% of the questions, and would be wrong 0.008% of the time when they did. All six models were wrong on 112 questions (7.5%), which independent voters with the same accuracies would manage on about two.

Some of this correlation is not surprising. A hard subject is hard for every model, and that alone produces correlated errors. To see how much, I shuffled each model's results across questions within each subject, separately for every model. That keeps every model's exact accuracy in every subject and destroys any agreement on individual questions. With only subject difficulty left, the average error correlation falls from 0.41 to 0.09, so subject difficulty accounts for about a fifth of it and most of the rest sits at the level of individual questions.

Figure 8 puts that next to the letter-bias control. With a shared option order the average correlation is 0.45, against 0.41 when each model sees its own random order. The gap between those two bars is what sharing the option order adds, about 0.04, and a paired bootstrap puts that difference between 0.02 and 0.06. Letter preferences were mild inside the panel: no model put more than 30% of its answers on any one letter, where an even split is 25%.

What this cannot do is separate a shared blind spot from a question that is simply hard for everyone, because both look like several models failing on the same item. The benchmark adds its own noise: an estimated 6.49% of MMLU questions contain errors [9], and a wrong answer key marks every model that knows better as wrong together.

Self-consistency samples several answers from one model and keeps the most common one [10]. A cheap stand-in is to draw the answer letter from each model's own probabilities, several times, and vote. Figure 9 compares that with voting across different models.

Six samples from one model are worth 1.4 effective votes on average (range 1.3 to 1.7), and eight samples are worth 1.5, against 2.0 for six different models. The accuracy gain tells the same story. A vote of five samples from one model gains 2.2 points over a single sample's expected accuracy on average (range 1.3 to 3.6), while five different models gain 5.7 points over the average single model. The right panel of Figure 9 shows gains rather than accuracy levels, because a sampled answer is less accurate than a model's top choice and the two lines would otherwise start in different places.

A letter drawn from a probability is not a sampled chain of thought, and real self-consistency samples reasoning paths. This comparison says little about how well real self-consistency works. It only compares two sources of votes under the same plain readout.

Vals tested teams that split a job into parts [1]. I tested voters who answer the same question, on multiple-choice exams, with small open models. None of this is a verdict on agent teams. What carries over is the arithmetic: a vote is worth the independent information behind it, and the number that measures that can be computed on any labeled set where each model's mistakes are recorded.

If you run a panel of judges, or a team in which agents check each other's work, compute φˉ\bar{\varphi}φˉ​ on a few hundred items with known answers before adding a member. A new model from a different developer is not automatically a new opinion. In this panel, switching developers lowered the error correlation only a little (0.45 for the one same-developer pair, 0.41 on average across developers), and no pair of models fell below 0.33. One same-developer pair is too few to say how far that generalizes. The ceiling 1/φˉ1/\bar{\varphi}1/φˉ​ also says where to stop adding voters and start adding something else: a tool call, a retrieval step, a test that can actually be run, a person.

Frontier models behind APIs, chain-of-thought answers in place of letter probabilities, and the manually re-checked subset of MMLU [9] would each change these numbers in ways I can't predict from this run.

Counting models is easy. Counting how many of them can be wrong in different ways takes one correlation matrix, and it is worth computing before the next model joins the panel.

#2896. “I Was Sure I Was Going to Be Mauled.” The Season the Grizzlies Went Crazy

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 23, 2026 11:00 AM EDT

It was a bad season for grizzlies. Maybe the warm, wet summer had something to do with it, but for whatever reason, the bears were uncharacteristically aggressive. I’d been coming to these Alaskan mountains for five to eight weeks each season for nearly a decade to hunt, fish and live in the wilderness. I had seen hundreds of grizzlies, but with few exceptions, they always had been timid or curious, seldom dangerous. I usually carried a bear tag, partly because if I was forced to kill one, I wanted to keep the hide (in my home state of Alaska, a bear killed in self-defense becomes the property of the state if you haven’t got a bear tag or if it isn’t taken in season). I also wanted a seven-foot bear. Grizzlies in the northern interior and Arctic survive largely on vegetation and don’t get as big as the salmon-fed coastal bears. A seven-footer was as good as you could expect to see.

The first part of my annual expeditions was reserved for sheep hunting with Cheri Flory, a friend who has taken several trophy-quality caribou and moose. After the tiny Cessna 185 had clattered to a white-knuckled stop on an almost too short, rocky river bar, we floated downriver for two days. From there we backpacked several more days up an unnamed river, swollen and angry from recent rains, into ram country.

When we arrived at my campsite, on a bench above the river, a large, dark grizzly was grazing placidly on blueberries exactly where I have pitched my tent for the last several years. We shouted and I fired the 7mm-08 into the air. As the shot echoed between sheer, limestone walls, the bear ran straight up a steep, scree slope and over a ridge 2,000 feet above. No surprise. That’s how they usually behaved.

In the next few days, we spotted several more bears near camp, and even though they all behaved as expected—they moved away once they sensed us—I was vaguely uneasy. That unease was almost a foreboding, and it lasted until the end of the expedition.

Early one morning, Cheri and I started from camp to find a band of rams I’d seen from a distance the day before. A quarter-mile downwind of the tent, she spotted a bear laying on his belly on a talus slope just above her. The bear lay with his chin on folded paws, apparently just waiting for us to go by (grizzlies have better eyesight than most people give them credit for, and this one could clearly identify us 300 yards away). I wasn’t particularly worried, and there was no reason to think this bear would be any different from the rest we’d seen. I hollered, cursing the bear and his relatives. It had no effect. I shouted again, and this time fired a shot over his head. He sat up and cocked his head at us, but didn’t show the slightest inclination to run. We shouted ourselves hoarse, and I fired several more shots, but it didn’t faze the bear. In fact, he seemed almost amused by it.

Possibly he’d had the same treatment in earlier years, and because he hadn’t suffered, was no longer afraid. Probably, too, the smell of our camp was just too intriguing to ignore. No matter how careful you might be, in a bear’s incredibly keen nose, there will always be the smell of food around even the cleanest camps.

We had to protect our gear and food, so we turned back to camp. A ruined camp would have been a serious matter—we had 300 miles of wilderness backpacking and river travel to civilization, and the journey would take us six weeks. The loss of camp would be just as serious, perhaps, as a mauling. After all, we had antibiotics, pain pills and stitching gear in case of a mauling, but no spare tent or sleeping bags.

The moment we turned for camp, the bear padded after us. I was startled by the maneuver. For some reason it was the last thing I expected, so I opened the bolt to be sure that I had a full magazine and one up the throat. As I jammed the bolt shut, I reflected grimly that though the 7mm-08 was a good sheep gun, it was hardly the stuff for a charging grizzly.

As the distance closed, the bear slowed, then stopped and began browsing on soapberry bushes. I was pretty sure that it was a ruse. He didn’t want a confrontation just yet and was testing us to see just what he had to deal with. We walked slowly toward camp, taking pains not to hurry, which might cause the bear to chase us. I watched over my shoulder and prodded Cheri along (she was more fascinated than worried by the bear’s antics, and at the most irritating times she insisted on pausing and watching). The bear stripped the soapberry branches, leaves and all, as if that was all he was interested in, but watched us out of the corner of his eye and edged closer as he fed. He was still testing us, still trying to find out just how far he could go.

A moment after we walked into camp, the grizzly trotted over the crest of the bench, following our trail like a bloodhound with his nose to the ground. He stopped, looked at us and the tent, then swaggered slowly forward in an exaggerated, side-to-side gait. I hoped it was all bluff, but grabbed an aluminum pot and pan and while banging away, went to meet the bear. If there was going to be trouble, I didn’t want it in camp, where Cheri could get hurt or the tent destroyed. He stopped. I could see the surprise in his expression, and he let me come. At 30 yards the sweat began to trickle down my forehead, despite a cool breeze, and my skin suddenly felt too tight. I thumbed off the safety as my heart pounded in my ears, and advanced again. Finally, to my immense relief, the bear gave, hesitantly at first, then turned and walked slowly off the bench, glaring back over his shoulder almost as if to say, “I’m not quite through. Yet.”

We watched the bear browse on soapberry bushes for hours. Several times I fired the 7mm-08 at rocks only feet from his nose in hopes of driving him off, but all he did was stare stupidly at the rock powder hanging in the air. He didn’t even flinch. He obviously wasn’t impressed, and he slowly began moving toward camp again.

Eventually, he edged to within 40 yards of the tent, and twice made short, bluff charges, probably hoping we’d abandon camp and leave him to his explorations. He made clear our choice, though: Either move camp or he’d force a showdown. I didn’t want to shoot him—bear season wouldn’t open for several weeks and there’d be a great deal of red tape with the fish and game department if I did. Besides that, I’d strangely developed an affection for the wayward bear. After all, we were the intruders, and it was his country.

We had tried every diplomatic means to diffuse a confrontation, and nothing worked, so we began breaking camp. Cheri was stuffing the tent into my backpack when, without warning, he charged. The bear came at a gallop, growling fiercely but still not as intensely as a full charge might be. But I knew if he pressed home there’d be no telling what might happen. I couldn’t take the chance, so I aimed low near his feet, hoping to change his mind. If that didn’t work, I’d have time enough for one more quick shot.

He rolled over at the muzzle blast, bawling and swatting at the air as I worked the bolt. He regained his feet and then stumbled off the bench toward the creek. He sat down 100 yards away and licked his paw and glared at us on the slope just above.

We quickly stuffed our few final items into the packs and started up-canyon. I brought up the rear and kept a close watch behind. We hadn’t gone a quarter of a mile when the bear was nosing around our just vacated campsite. He didn’t limp in the least—apparently, I’d stung his paw with ricocheted debris.

Two hours later, I glanced down the canyon, and the bear was following our trail a half-mile below, again with nose to the ground exactly like a bloodhound. I’m used to being the hunter, so it was more than a little unsettling to suddenly find myself being hunted by something so much larger and more powerful. Only if the 7mm-08 was used properly (it was seeming smaller and smaller all the time), could it equalize things. For a moment, I felt a bit like David, and the bear was Goliath—finesse vs. brute strength. Eventually, he closed the distance to about 200 yards and kept it there. Then, finally, as we crossed a high shale ridge and dropped into the next drainage, he quit following. I breathed a huge sigh of relief when I was relatively sure that he’d quit dogging us, but it was several nights before I slept soundly again, and even then I kept one ear open and a loaded rifle beside me.

The sheep hunt was successful. We saw a half-dozen more grizzlies and all behaved themselves, and two weeks later we trekked back down to the river where we had left our cache stored in steel drums. We had 250 miles of wilderness river to float as we hunted for moose and caribou on our way to our bush-village destination. In time, we collected a good moose and a huge caribou, spotted several more passive grizzlies, and on the lower part of the river toward the end of the expedition, came into flat country where spawned-out salmon littered beaches and gravel bars. Bear tracks were everywhere, and in two days we spotted six grizzlies as we floated. Each night I unloaded and heaved the 14-foot raft up onto the bank next to the tent and tied aluminum pans to a line so I’d hear if a bear came into camp (if anything happened to the inflatable raft, we’d be in dire trouble).

One evening, we pulled up at a likely looking campsite. I climbed the bluff to check it out, without a gun, as I usually do, and forced my way through a screen of willows into the shadowy spruce timber to look for a place to pitch the tent. I walked around for a moment, then heard a commotion behind me.

I was sure that it was just a surprised moose crashing off, but when I turned I was face to face with a large grizzly charging down on me through the dusk and thick trees. Spruce branches were flying like straw, and the ivory of his teeth were almost glowing in the gloom. Any bear chasing you is apt to seem huge, but this one was honestly a big bear.

I was unarmed and as helpless as I’ve ever felt. My chest tightened as I waited for the impact and the teeth tearing into me. I was as sure as I’ve ever been of anything that I was going to be mauled, as sure as a falling rock climber is sure he’ll hit the rocks below. I was so positive of a mauling that I’d accepted it and was already thinking beyond to Cheri waiting in the raft. She was an experienced wilderness hand (this was her third trip), and I felt sure that she could get out the four days or so left downriver to the native village. But what of the grizzly? Would Cheri get away?

“Cheri, get the gun!” I said in a loud voice that wasn’t quite shouting and that sounded like someone else, hoping she’d be able to protect herself when the bear was through with me. Immediately she shouted something back and the bear veered at 15 feet, almost disappearing momentarily behind a deadfall, thoroughly confused by the second voice from a different place. It gave me a chance, and I rushed through the willows and leaped off the bluff onto the raft, at the same time hissing, “Row!” This time I kept my voice low, feeling that if I said it any louder it would bring the grizzly down on us. Cheri leaned urgently into the oars, and a moment later we were out into the current. I was elated, flying on an adrenaline high and almost giddy with the sudden release of tension and unexpected reprieve. I was still alive, still in one piece, and incredibly surprised about it. A moment later, the bear was on the bank looking for us, and I was trying to free the rifle from the duffel. Bear season had opened by then, and I could have cheerfully shot this one because he’d frightened me badly enough to sprinkle my beard with a handful of my first gray hairs (at least I blame it on that, because they showed up several weeks later). But before I could free the gun, he was gone.

We floated downriver until well after dark, trying to get as far away from the bear as possible before pitching the tent. Neither of us slept much that night, and I awoke frequently, quickly breathing and sweating from visions of bristling, bloody-jawed grizzlies standing over me.

Late the next afternoon, we tied off the raft in an eddy and climbed a low hill to pick some blueberries for dessert that night. This time I took the rifle and kept a careful watch as Cheri picked. We talked, and I felt sure any bear in the area would hear us and would have plenty of time to move off. We worked slowly over the hill, and suddenly, just below, was another bear.

“Oh God, not again!” I thought, feeling the blood thicken behind my eyes. This bear was very blond, and he saw me at the same time I saw him. He stood abruptly on his hind legs—seeming to tower over us like those Hollywood bears in old “B” westerns—nose raised into the wind and head rocking quickly from side to side as he tried urgently to scent us. I worked the bolt and brought the rifle up. Cheri heard the hard, metal sound of the bolt and froze, knowing what it was without seeing it. There was that same brassy taste in my mouth, and I knew before the bear started that he’d charge. We were just too close; given the adult male grizzly’s disposition and ego, he had no choice. He dropped quickly to all fours with an explosive snorting-grunt and charged, the glossy blond fur on his shoulders and neck filling the riflescope as he rushed silently, intently, bounding across the spongy tundra and closing the distance faster than I would have believed. At the shot, he seemed to hang there and stare a moment, almost in surprise, before sagging into the blood-colored dwarf birch. Then it was silent, nothing moved or made a sound; I had the feel of a mouthful of flannel as I tried to wet my lips.

Only after I lowered the rifle and breathed again did Cheri stand up from where she’d been picking berries on hands and knees. Later, I realized it had taken a great deal of self-possession to freeze that way with a grizzly charging you and with your survival in someone else’s hands. The big grizzly lay motionless less than 20 feet away; the bullet had shattered the vertebrae just behind the skull. He squared just seven feet, a very large bear for the country, and his hide was spectacular. But I killed him because I had to, even though I now very much appreciate the trophy.

He smelled wine-sweet, the odor of the fermented blueberries around us, as I stood next to him letting the tension uncoil. Cheri and I were both strangely subdued that afternoon, standing on the blazing crimson, gold and green tundra.

Why the grizzlies had been short-tempered that season is anyone’s guess. I’m fairly certain that I could wander up there for the rest of my life without another serious bear encounter, let alone three charges within a few weeks. But despite that, I’ll now carry a gun whenever I’m in grizzly country, something I never did before unless I was actually hunting. And without trying too hard, I can see again the big, dark grizzly charging down on me in the dusky gloom of the forest as I stand unarmed and as helpless as I’ve ever been. As I relive it, my palms become clammy and my scalp prickles as I wait for the impact and the teeth.

That season, I learned that I wasn’t the most important, the most powerful, maybe not even the most deadly creature in that wilderness. I also learned humility.

I didn’t shake that foreboding, gloomy feel of doom until days after the chartered plane landed at my cabin on the Yukon River, when I was sleeping in sheets again, behind a heavy log wall.

Creatine has been around the gym long enough to have survived several generations of invention. And reinvention. And re-re-invention… Let’s just say it’s been a supplement industry staple for decades; it’s cheap, well studied, and, when combined with resistance training, actually useful. Naturally, that wasn’t sexy enough. So, now the Internet has served up a study suggesting you can take creatine, skip the gym, and build muscle anyway. The next sound in my brain after I read it was Ronnie Coleman’s voice in my head sayin’ his famous line: “Everybody wants to be a bodybuilder, but nobody wants to lift no heavy-ass weights.”

But the study is real! Researchers at Texas A&M published it in August in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition, and the results have been making the rounds again this fall. I’m very familiar with the ISSN, I’ve presented at one of its conventions. I know several of the members. The organization is legit. And Texas A&M is no joke either.

Researchers recruited 73 sedentary adults between the ages of 45 and 65 for the 12-week study. Of those, 64 completed it. Some chose to follow an exercise-and-diet program. Others didn’t. Within those groups, participants were randomly assigned either ten grams of creatine monohydrate a day or a placebo.

Then this happened. The non-exercisers taking creatine gained about 2.4 pounds of lean tissue on their body-composition scans. The non-exercisers taking a placebo didn’t show the same gain. The creatine group also improved on some strength tests. So, yes, something interesting happened. But is it what we’re afraid it is?

Notice I said “lean tissue,” not two and a half pounds of brand-new muscle. The researchers used DXA scans to measure body composition. Those scans are useful, but they don’t distinguish freshly built muscle protein from all the water sitting inside lean tissue. Creatine increases water stored in muscle, especially when people first start taking it. The study didn’t directly measure changes in body water, so nobody can tell you how much of that apparent gain was actual new muscle.

There’s another detail that tends to disappear somewhere between the scientific journal and the breathless headline. The no-exercise comparison involved just thirteen creatine users and thirteen placebo users. That’s not worthless. It’s also not the population of a medium-sized city. And the researchers randomized the supplement, not the decision to exercise. Participants chose whether to join the training program, so comparisons between exercisers and non-exercisers aren’t the same as a fully randomized test of exercise itself.

The people who did train combined resistance and aerobic exercise three times a week with a calorie-restricted diet. Creatine users in that group gained nearly three pounds of lean tissue and showed greater improvements in several performance measures. That’s interesting, too, although the study can’t tell us that creatine replaces lifting. If anything, the bigger picture still favors the traditional arrangement: take the supplement if you want, but keep showing up and doing the work.

The investigators also reported some promising changes in memory tests, blood lipids, and blood-sugar markers. I’m not dismissing those findings. But they tested a lot of outcomes in a relatively small study, and the authors themselves say the individual cognitive and metabolic results are exploratory. That’s scientist speak for “hmm… now do it again with more people.”

For completeness, the study was funded by a foundation gift. A creatine manufacturer supplied the supplements and paid for one laboratory assay, and the senior researcher disclosed a position on that company’s scientific advisory board. That doesn’t make the results false. It does make disclosure worth reading, which is something every person reporting supplement research ought to do before writing a headline.

None of this changes my opinion of creatine. It’s one of the few products on the shelf with a substantial body of evidence behind it. Ten grams daily was the dose in this experiment; it doesn’t suddenly replace the more familiar 3- to 5-gram maintenance approach used by many lifters. And if you have kidney disease or other medical issues, a conversation with your clinician would be appropriate.

So can creatine help somebody who doesn’t lift? Possibly. This trial suggests it might, and that’s worth investigating. Has anybody proved that a scoop of powder builds meaningful muscle while you sit on your ass? Not from these data. The supplement is interesting precisely because it may do more than we once thought. And what we once thought was pretty damn good. We don’t have to turn it into magic dust to appreciate that.

#893. MIT scientists build a tiny robot that swims using living muscle cells

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A swimming robot does not necessarily need a bulky motor or powerful mechanical parts to move through water. MIT researchers have demonstrated that a single, carefully engineered layer of living muscle cells can generate enough force to propel a tiny robot forward.

The engineers developed an extremely thin swimming robot that uses living muscle tissue to move its fins. Described in a new study published in Advanced Functional Materials, the device has a flexible gel framework roughly the length and width of a stick of gum. The gel forms two fins, each coated with a layer of muscle cells far thinner than a human hair. These cells have been genetically modified to contract when exposed to light.

By directing light onto either fin, the researchers can trigger muscle contractions that make the fin flap and push the robot through water. Adjusting which fin receives light and how frequently it is illuminated allows the team to control both its speed and direction.

In experiments, the remarkably thin robot successfully swam and turned through a simple underwater maze. At maximum speed, it covered approximately four times its body length per minute. Although that is much slower than an Olympic swimmer, who can travel up to 65 body lengths per minute, it is comparable to the swimming pace of a cow shark moving leisurely through the ocean.

"It takes a lot of force to move through water versus air," says study author Ritu Raman, associate professor of mechanical engineering at MIT. "The robot's quite strong, given its size. "

A New Generation of Robots Powered by Living Muscle

The device represents the first demonstration of a very thin, two-dimensional robot that can move from place to place using living muscle tissue.

Most existing biohybrid robots rely on relatively large, three-dimensional structures made from laboratory-grown skeletal muscle. Producing these structures typically requires millions of cells. Raman explains that thinner designs could reduce manufacturing costs while potentially improving movement efficiency.

"Currently, biohybrid robots from our group and others' are built from bulky, 3D chunks of lab-grown skeletal muscle that require millions of cells to fabricate," says Raman, who notes that thinner, less bulky designs such as the team's new bot could be cheaper to build and could move more efficiently. "We believe that biohybrid robots powered by living muscle could one day perform delicate jobs like exploring environments too fragile or unpredictable for conventional hardware, because living tissue is soft, responsive to its surroundings, and can heal itself."

The MIT research team also included first author Maheera Bawa, along with Arielle Berman, Laura Schwendeman, Ferdows Afghah, and Seanbiron Johnson.

From Artificial Muscle to a Swimming Robot

The new swimming robot builds on an earlier achievement by Raman's laboratory. Last year, the researchers created an artificial muscle disk inspired by the human iris, demonstrating how a thin layer of living cells could produce precisely controlled movements.

For that project, the engineers patterned a gel disk with grooves extending outward from its center and forming concentric circles. They then placed living muscle cells on the surface, where the cells grew into a thin layer following the patterned channels.

When illuminated, the muscle tissue contracted in different directions, causing the disk to stretch and compress. Its movements resembled the way the iris of the human eye changes shape as the pupil expands or narrows.

The experiment established that researchers could grow extremely thin muscle layers in intricate arrangements and use light to control their movements along multiple directions.

"People hadn't seen this muscle architecture engineered from scratch before," Raman says. "And the cells were moving in multiple directions. But they only moved about 100 microns. From a robotics perspective, their movements were tiny."

For the latest project, the researchers wanted to dramatically increase the amount of movement and force generated by the muscle cells. Their goal was to produce contractions powerful enough to propel a robot through water.

They determined that achieving this would require a better foundation for the living muscle tissue.

The earlier artificial iris had used fibrin, an exceptionally soft gel, as its underlying structure. However, the team found that fibrin could quickly shrink under the forces exerted by contracting muscle cells, limiting the effectiveness of the resulting movement.

To overcome this problem, the engineers investigated three characteristics of the gel foundation: its chemical composition, its stiffness, and the dimensions and geometry of the grooves used to organize muscle growth.

"For engineering any type of tissue, it's known that these are knobs you can tune," Raman says. "And we wanted to optimize all these parameters to support live muscle cells."

Engineering a Stronger Muscle-Powered Robot

Finding the right supporting material required testing gels with different formulations, stiffness levels, and surface patterns.

The researchers compared several groove shapes, including narrow channels with square profiles and longer channels with curved bottoms. They discovered that muscle cells aligned more effectively inside square-shaped grooves.

This alignment was important because muscle cells that grow in the same direction are more likely to merge into fibers. Those fibers can then form stronger muscle tissue that contracts in a coordinated way. The square grooves therefore provided a more effective structure for producing force.

The team also replaced fibrin with gelatin methacrylate (GelMA), a material commonly used to grow and engineer biological tissue.

By adjusting the formulation of GelMA, the researchers produced supporting gels with varying degrees of stiffness. They then examined how muscle cells developed on each surface and measured their ability to generate force.

Stiffer gels produced the best results. On these firmer surfaces, muscle cells grew in better alignment and generated stronger contractions.

The engineers next investigated how thick the gel should be. They determined that a film measuring approximately half a millimeter offered an effective balance. It was sturdy enough to support a single layer of muscle cells while remaining sufficiently lightweight to prevent the contracting cells from detaching from the surface.

The team further improved muscle performance through a form of exercise. Repeated pulses of light stimulated the cells to contract, strengthening the muscle tissue before it was incorporated into the swimming robot.

With the gel structure optimized and the muscle cells strengthened, the researchers assembled their swimming device.

The finished robot consisted of a thin gel framework divided into two fins. Both sides of the gel were patterned with square-bottomed grooves and covered with living muscle cells. As the cells developed, they fused into organized muscle fibers capable of contracting together.

Each fin could be activated independently using light, giving the researchers a simple way to control the robot's movements.

"You can think of the robot as having two independent muscles," Raman says. "If we shine a light on just one, only that muscle moves. If shining on both, they both flap."

To test the device, the researchers placed it inside a large petri dish filled with water. They then moved a light source by hand, illuminating the fins to guide the robot through a maze positioned inside the dish.

As the muscles contracted, the fins flapped and propelled the robot through the water. By changing the light stimulation, the engineers successfully controlled its movement and helped it navigate the obstacles.

Although the robot demonstrated the ability to swim and steer, its current shape remains relatively simple. The primary objective of the experiment was to establish that an exceptionally thin layer of engineered muscle tissue could generate enough force for swimming.

The researchers now plan to refine the robot's body to improve its speed. Beyond demonstrating a new approach to biohybrid robotics, the technology could eventually be useful for monitoring aquatic environments or operating in locations where conventional rigid machines might cause damage.

"Our next goal is to optimize the body design to enable faster swimming," Raman says. "But even at slow swim speeds, one could imagine a muscle-powered swimmer being used for purposes like environmental monitoring in aquatic environments."
