#314. India slashes cost of cancer drugs after court rules mark-ups are ‘extortion’

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Case filed in 2022 by patient unable to afford medicine leads to cap on drug prices for millions who face ruin if diagnosed

The Indian government has moved to reduce the price of cancer drugs after a retired bank clerk took her case to the supreme court.

From next month, 137 cancer drugs in India will be cheaper as the government tries to tackle the tragedy of poorer patients either forgoing treatment, selling land or falling into debt to meet the high cost of some medicines, which are making large profits for hospitals and pharmacists.

Sarojam Radhakrishnan, a former bank employee in Kerala, was diagnosed with breast cancer in 2021. Doctors prescribed life-saving targeted treatments but the monthly cost, of more than 63,000 rupees (£500), was far beyond her family’s means.

Realising that thousands of families were being ruined by the cost of such drugs, Radhakrishnan filed a petition to the Kerala high court in 2022 demanding government intervention to lower the costs.

When the case reached the supreme court, the judges looked at hospitals’ and retailers’ mark-ups on cancer drugs and called the situation “carnage” and “extortion”.

During a hearing on 29 September, justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta noted how a life-saving cancer drug supplied to hospitals at 2,700 rupees carried a vastly marked-up price of 27,000 rupees.

The judges found that hospitals, distributors and chemists enjoyed large margins on medicines. Analysis by the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority revealed a trade mark-up on some cancer drugs averaging 170%, with some extreme examples stretching to more than 700%.

A 30% trade margin cap on the price of 42 drugs has been in place for some years but many others remained off this list. Now, prodded by the supreme court’s findings, the government has imposed the cap on 137 other drugs.

“The measure is expected to reduce medicine prices by up to 70% and help cancer patients save 2,500 crore rupees [£199m] annually, significantly reducing the amount they pay from their own pockets,” a statement from the Department of Pharmaceuticals said.

People on low incomes in India dread illness, as the cost of treatment can mean financial ruin, particularly for cancer patients.

Whenever the Indian Cancer Society holds a free diagnosis camp in Gurgaon where he lives, Krishan Kalra urges local drivers, maids, cooks and tailors to turn up because tests they cannot afford are offered free.

As a former member of the council of the Rajiv Gandhi Cancer Institute and Research Centre, Kalra knows the importance of early diagnosis, but he said very few people attended. “When I ask why, they say they’re too scared – if something serious like cancer is detected, they haven’t got the money to pay for the treatment so they prefer to remain in the dark,” he said.

With the cost of cancer drugs ranging in price from 38,000 to 380,000 rupees, many families quietly walk out of the doctor’s consulting room upon hearing the estimated cost of treatment.

Studies by the Public Health Foundation of India show that spending on healthcare pushes about 55 million Indians a year below the poverty line.

Few Indians can afford the premiums for comprehensive health insurance. The government’s nationwide insurance scheme, Ayushman Bharat, covers treatment for some cancers but it provides only 500,000 rupees’ worth of annual cover for a family – the cost of just one dose of Kadcyla (ado-trastuzumab emtansine), a drug used to treat breast cancer. Patients may need up to 14 doses.

Dr Ravi Kannan is a surgical oncologist and director of Cachar cancer hospital and research centre in Silchar, Assam. He has worked in hospitals elsewhere in India and is familiar with doctors’ trauma of seeing patients walk out to go home and die.

“Some families start the treatment, hoping against hope to manage the cost. They start but they don’t come back for chemotherapy or radiation. They can’t sustain the cost,” he said. “The decision is a welcome step if the outcome is that more patients can access the drugs they need.”

India records about 1.56m new cancer cases annually – up from 1.39m cases in 2020 – making it a significant public health challenge.

As the supreme court delves deeper into the subject, what was clear, said Jyotsna Singh, co-convener of the Working Group on Access to Medicines and Treatments, a civil society network, was that a “total overhaul” of the healthcare system was needed.

“Right from the cost of manufacturing a drug to the point of sale, we need to make the system transparent and regulate the entire supply chain. Only then will these drugs become available to those who need them,” she said.

Singh said companies and hospitals were likely to try to find legal loopholes to wriggle out of the 30% cap, suggesting that hospitals may offset the lower price charged for a drug by raising charges on other items.

The supreme court judges have also asked the government’s lawyers why the cap should apply only to cancer drugs and not the entire range of non-patented drugs. The court has given the government until 27 October to respond.

Unfortunately, the court’s ruling came too late for Radhakrishnan, who died in September 2022 while her case was still pending in Kerala.

#325. Aung San Suu Kyi’s son speaks to detained former Myanmar leader for first time in five years

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Kim Aris says ‘hearing her voice again means more than words can express’ amid loosening of detention restrictions

The son of Myanmar’s Aung San Suu Kyi has been allowed to speak to the detained former democratic leader for the first time in more than five years.

“Today, I was finally able to speak with my mother, Daw Aung San Suu Kyi, after more than five years. Hearing her voice again means more than words can express,” Kim Aris said in a post on Facebook on Thursday.

Aung San Suu Kyi is under house arrest in the capital, Naypyidaw, according to Myanmar authorities, where she has been sequestered since being unseated in a 2021 military coup.

Images accompanying his Facebook post appeared to show Aris, who lives in the UK, standing outside the Myanmar embassy in London.

“I am deeply grateful to everyone who has stood by my mother and me. I believe your voices helped make this moment possible,” he said in the post. “But my mother is 81 and remains unjustly detained. One conversation is not regular family access.”

Myanmar’s military has held the Nobel peace prize laureate in almost total seclusion since the February 2021 coup, which ended a tentative decade-long experiment with democracy. But in recent months authorities have increasingly advertised the loosening of her detention and published photographs of her taking private meetings, as supporters have demanded “proof of life”.

On Monday, authorities said she received a package from Britain’s King Charles, including two books about soil and a “sealed diplomatic communication” from the monarch. Buckingham Palace did not comment on the contents of the communication.

Myanmar authorities said Aung San Suu Kyi was moved from a prison in Naypyidaw to house arrest in April, and in August they published images of her meeting the nation’s Red Cross representative.

After ruling Myanmar as military chief since the coup, Min Aung Hlaing was installed as civilian president this year.

Analysts described the election that put him in office as a charade, and said he could be loosening restrictions on Aung San Suu Kyi in an attempt to court international legitimacy.

#333. Indian court orders release of two leaders of Cockroach movement

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka spent a week in jail after defying restrictions on protests in Delhi amid government crackdown

An Indian court has ordered the release of two leaders of India’s Cockroach movement who were jailed last week amid a mounting state crackdown on youth-led protests.

The two leaders of the Cockroach Janta party (CJP) movement, Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka, had spent a week in Delhi’s Tihar jail after being arrested by Delhi police last week.

On Wednesday, a special magistrate ordered that Das and Ranka be released, despite efforts by police to keep them in prison. They were made to sign a bond pledging no further breach of peace.

Their arrest came after a draconian police crackdown on a CJP march held in the capital two weeks ago to protest alleged manipulation of the country’s electoral roll, which led to the barricading of central Delhi and thousands of young people being detained.

Das and Ranka were taken into custody for a second time days later after they jumped police barricades and held up an Indian flag at a site in central Delhi that police had cordoned off.

The pair were charged with trespass and violating the peace and sent to jail for five days, which was then extended by two further days. Their detention had provoked outrage among leaders and supporters of the youth movement, who accused the government of “cowardice” for jailing CJP leaders for holding a peaceful protest.

After the magistrate’s ruling on Wednesday evening, Das said that “truth has triumphed”.

The release of the two leaders is likely to come as a blow to the ruling Narendra Modi government, which has implemented an unforgiving crackdown on recent protests that have erupted across India.

The CJP, alongside the opposition alliance, student groups and civil society, have been calling for the resignation of the chief election commissioner, Gyanesh Kumar, who is accused of overseeing a revision of the electoral roll that benefits Modi’s Bharatiya Janata party (BJP) and has led to more than 130 million names being deleted from the voter register. The BJP has denied any interference.

The CJP began as an online satire earlier this year, but has since grown into a fully fledged political movement, challenging the powerful Modi government. Over the summer, it was Cockroach-led protests that forced the resignation of Modi’s education minister after sustained demonstrations.

Abhijeet Dipke, the founder of the CJP movement, vowed over the weekend that the Modi regime and threat of jail would not intimidate the Cockroaches. Dipke was also detained on Friday after he called for a peaceful demonstration in central Delhi where CJP supporters would gather and wave the Indian flag in solidarity with Das and Ranka.

He had said that, once his fellow Cockroach leaders were released, they would “gather, re-strategise, and determine how to move forward”.

“The illegal arrests and detentions we have witnessed over the past few days indicate an attempt to intimidate us,” said Dipke. “But we will not be cowed down or retreat. This is a long battle and we are prepared for it.”

#554. Kast and Paz open the way in La Paz to normalising Chile-Bolivia relations

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Montevideo, October 30th 2026 - 00:16 UTC

Chilean President José Antonio Kast and his Bolivian counterpart Rodrigo Paz pledged on Friday in La Paz to move towards normalising diplomatic relations “at the highest level”, broken off since 1978, during a visit the Chilean government described as historic as the first official bilateral visit by a Chilean president to Bolivia in more than seven decades. The two governments signed economic integration and border security agreements, although they did not announce the restoration of embassies.

“President Paz, today we stop turning our backs on each other. We did not come just for a gesture, we came to begin making history,” Kast said after the bilateral meetings. Paz replied that the two countries have “historical differences” and that the claim to sea access is enshrined in Bolivia’s Constitution, “but Bolivia understands that this history cannot hold back its development. Problems of the past must be resolved.” Kast invited Paz on a state visit to Chile in 2027.

Ministers from both countries signed agreements on mining integration, electricity interconnection, bi-oceanic corridors, border security and the migration status of Bolivians living in Chile. They also discussed reviving the Arica-La Paz railway, provided for in the 1904 treaty between Bolivia and Chile, 24-hour operation at the Tambo Quemado-Chungará border crossing and the fight against smuggling. According to the Oruro customs post, more than 76% of Bolivia’s foreign trade passes through the Chilean ports of Arica and Iquique.

Bolivia’s claim to sovereign access to the sea, which it lost at the International Court of Justice in 2018, was not on the agenda. “We are not going to renounce our historical claim, but it is also clear that we have to address a fundamental agenda,” said Bolivia’s Minister of the Presidency, Fernando Aramayo.

The rapprochement comes at a time of alignment between the two governments, which this year joined the Shield of the Americas, the US-backed regional security alliance. The Bolivian opposition, however, criticised the visit over the destruction in Chile of part of a seized shipment of Bolivian timber over alleged traces of drugs. “The situation is tense, we cannot be hypocrites,” said deputy Elena Pachacute of the Unidad alliance.
