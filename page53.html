#2938. Trail Community Pushes Back on Plans To Demolish 2 Popular Appalachian Trail Shelters

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

More than one year after the National Park Service (NPS) announced plans to demolish two historic Appalachian Trail shelters in Pennsylvania, a local trail club is leading a campaign to save the structures, citing their practical and historical value to hikers.

The shelters on the chopping block are 501 Shelter at northbound mile 1197 and Eckville Shelter at mile 1236. While most of the AT’s 250-plus shelters are primitive three-sided lean-tos, both Eckville and 501 are fully enclosed structures with running water and electricity. Both have long had volunteer caretakers living on-site to maintain the properties and assist hikers. This is still the case at Eckville, which remains operational; 501 Shelter was permanently closed last year in anticipation of the demolition.

The NPS reportedly intends to carry out the demolition in late 2026 or early 2027 but has not confirmed an exact timeline. According to reporting by Outside, the agency has said that the shelters are slated for removal because they do not meet safety standards and are located too close to roads, and as part of a larger campaign to rewild the AT.

The NPS did not immediately respond to a request for comment for this story.

“NPS has planned removal of these sites for years,” said Luke Weir of the Appalachian Trail Conservancy (ATC) in an email to The Trek, adding that the shelters do not comply with modern NPS housing, volunteer, and visitor use policies.

“In the past, other nonconforming structures, from sheds to houses and even a rundown old motel, have been similarly removed from the AT corridor,” Weir said. “The action is consistent with ATC policy and Overnight Site Planning Guidance, revised in 2023, which recommends locating ‘overnight sites at least two miles from motorized access points’ to promote a remote backcountry experience where possible.”

Yet proponents of the two shelters say an exception should be made because of their unique cultural value and usefulness for hikers. The Blue Mountain Eagle Climbing Club (BMECC), which maintains a 65-mile stretch of the AT encompassing both shelters, has launched a campaign to fight the demolition called SOS: Save Our Shelters.

The interior of the 501 Shelter photographed in 2025. The shelter has since closed permanently.

“We are not ready to throw away our valued shelters and the infrastructure that the AT offers for people to get out in nature,” said campaign co-chair Daree Sicher in an email to The Trek.

The campaign argues that the two buildings provide safe haven for hikers in adverse conditions, as well as valuable access to reliable clean water.

On this topic, the ATC’s Weir pointed to the trail’s Comprehensive Plan. “Shelters are a tradition on the AT, but use of the Trail should not depend on them. No attempt is made to provide such amenities for every potential user, so each person must be prepared to do without them. Shelter density and design should be consistent with a sense of the natural,” the plan reads.

Removing 501 Shelter leaves a 19-mile stretch of the AT without a shelter between William Penn and Eagle’s Nest shelters. If Eckville is also demolished, hikers would face another shelter-free stretch of roughly 16 miles between Windsor Furnace and Allentown Hiking Club shelters.

The shelters themselves remain popular with hikers, many of whom see them as a cherished part of AT culture.

The Eckville Shelter’s current caretaker, Mick Charowsky, is a former thru-hiker who has stewarded the property for 40 years. 501 Shelter, meanwhile, was perhaps most famous among AT hikers as the only backcountry shelter on the AT where you could order a pizza for delivery before its closure.

Save Our Shelters has started a petition asking the NPS to preserve the shelters and is urging members of the trail community to contact the NPS and their elected officials to oppose the demolition.

“It’s a damn shame because these resources are invaluable to hikers and even offer a reason for us to spend money in these small towns,” wrote AT thru-hiker and Trek blogger Sara in a 2025 blog post about the planned demolition of the two buildings.

Hospital appointments and clinics return to normal today, although nonessential UNM Health Sciences Center staff are being asked to remain at home.

"The University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center (HSC), which includes the UNM Health clinical delivery system, experienced a cybersecurity event Monday, Oct. 26, 2026," the university said in a statement on Monday evening.

"Leadership activated an Emergency Operations Center to coordinate the response and provide guidance to HSC employees, students, and UNM Health system frontline providers."

The university announced the attack at 1430 local time, immediately cancelling all remaining clinics and classes, dismissing everyone other than critical and clinical staff, and urging patients not to attend HSC facilities.

It has not commented on the nature of the cyberattack, although that hasn’t stopped researchers from speculating about ransomware’s involvement. Rumors were fuelled by the newly assembled BYOD ransomware group claiming responsibility for the attack on Monday afternoon.

It's important to note that just because a purported ransomware group took credit for the attack, it does not mean they actually were responsible, nor does it mean they deployed a ransomware/locker payload as part of the attack.

However, the group's take on the digital raid is that it compromised 36,000 machines, "both [Active Directory] forests," two Oracle databases, the personal data belonging to every student and employee, as well as internal emails and data from other systems.

#4071. The Myths We Play: Jung and the Inner Life of Gamers

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Growing up in the '90s, I can assure you that video games had a reputation problem as they moved into more living rooms across North America. In my small Ontario town, my parents raised a few eyebrows for letting their three boys play the Nintendo 64. Despite the collective worry, video games did not turn me into a menace. I grew up to become a law-abiding taxpayer, a recycler of (mostly) clean containers, and a therapist who deeply values imagination, creativity, and compassion.

To this day, some headlines warn against video games, and people worry about gamers. Many therapists have also quietly assumed that a client who games a lot is a client who is pathologically avoiding something. However, a 2024 article in the peer-reviewed journal Psychological Perspectives offers a different view.

Jungian analysts Pamela Power and Sheryl Corchnoy argue that games are not only entertainment; they are also a place where the psyche shows itself.1 Corchnoy recalls a case consultation where a colleague mentioned, with concern, that his client played World of Warcraft. The room apparently nodded gravely, whereas Corchnoy felt the opposite: curiosity. She wanted to know who the client played in this game, and why.

That curiosity is worth sharing with anyone who games, or who loves someone who does.

Jung believed the psyche speaks in images.2 Powerful psychological symbols show up in dreams, myths, fairy tales, and art across human cultures since time immemorial. These images also tend to find their way into whatever stories move us. In the modern era, one of the places people spend the most time experiencing stories and mythology is a video game.

Power and Corchnoy point out that many modern games are built from archetypal mythic material: gods, quests, monsters, facing death, and heroic returns. If the psyche goes where we go, then for billions of players around the world, it has gone into these worlds, too. Here are a few of the reflections their article offers.

Corchnoy noticed that within her first hours of playing, she was already calling her character "I." Players describe leaning in their chairs to peek around a corner on screen. This is projection in action: We pour something of ourselves into the figure we play.

That is why the character you choose can be meaningful. Avatars, especially the ones we generate ourselves, may carry an aspect of psyche that wants more room to live in your inner life.

For me, it’s often the arcane mage: a scholar of forbidden knowledge who is likely hunched over ancient tomes in a tower somewhere. Jung wrote extensively about the psyche’s religious function—our innate pull toward mystery and meaning. In game, mine seemingly expresses itself by setting things on fire with secret words. Calcinatio!

One of the article's most striking stories involves a player who wandered into a temple in World of Warcraft and found a figure based on the Norse god Thor. The game asked nothing of her here; there was no reward and no one watching. Nonetheless, she typed a command and had her character kneel. Asked why, she said kneeling simply felt like the only fitting response to a god. This reflects archetypal emergence; the game supplied the god image, but the player supplied the reverence.

Those unprompted choices are often the most revealing. Do you stop to watch sunsets or watch lightning storms? Collect things? Help strangers? Pick fights? Complete all the achievements? These small acts may reveal what you long for, or what is quietly asking to be integrated into your life.

Power draws on the Jungian philosopher Wolfgang Giegerich to separate two kinds of stories. Fairy tales move from a problem to a solution, whereas myths circle around one deep truth without tidying it up. She reads Stray, where a lost cat finds its way home as a fairy tale, and The Last of Us, a story of a world breaking down as a myth.

Are you drawn to enchanting fairy tales or messy epic myths right now? Sometimes we need tales where the light returns, whereas other times we need myths that stay with what hurts in an expansive way. Our instinctual cravings can hint at what our inner life is working on, and where we are headed next.

Online games are not only private experiences; they are also deeply communal. In 2010, one World of Warcraft player organized a charity run for breast cancer awareness, asking others to create pink-haired gnome characters and race across the game world together. The run grew into an official in-game holiday, and Blizzard Entertainment (the game’s maker) went on to raise millions for disaster relief and other humanitarian causes.

Psychological researchers have also pointed to potential benefits of gaming, including problem-solving, emotional regulation, creative thinking, greater curiosity, and social connection.3 Like any powerful medium, games can be used to heal or hide. When people tell me they are gamers, I often ask, "What does your gaming mean to you?" as the answers are rarely trivial. A question like this tends to open conversations around authenticity, courage, play, loneliness, grief or trauma, stress, and belonging, which is the same rich clinical material I often find in night-time dreams.

Video games may be one of our newest mythologies and offer archetypal stories we directly live. The psyche rarely stays where we expect it, and sometimes, it follows us into other worlds.

2. Jung, C. G. (1963). Memories, Dreams, Reflections. Pantheon.

3. Granic, I., Lobel, A., & Engels, R. C. M. E. (2014). The benefits of playing video games. American Psychologist, 69(1), 66–78.

#2894. Mont Blanc Has Grown Almost Two Meters — But Not in the Way You Think

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

These days, topographic surveys typically show that a mountain’s altitude is decreasing due to shrinking snowcaps. But strangely, Mont Blanc, the highest peak in the Alps, has grown significantly since its latest measurement in 2023.

Mont Blanc is now 1.88 meters taller than it was three years ago, according to a recent report by France’s IGN (National Geographic Institute). The new official altitude is now 4,807.47 meters (15,772.5 feet) above sea level.

However, it may be wise to wait before changing the figure in climbing guides. Measurements are carried out every two years, and each time the registered altitude varies:

Graphic showing the results of the last few measurements on Mont Blanc, by LaChangingMeteo

Mont Blanc’s “summit is constantly shifting,” Jean des Garets, chief geometer in the Haute-Savoie department, told Le Monde.  “Each individual measurement is not significant in itself.”

So, what’s with the yearly differences? Obviously, it’s the variation in the thick but ever-changing snowcap that covers the summit.

The mountain’s rocky summit measures around 4,792m above sea level. The snow cover varies with the seasons. The difference from the previous measurement shows that 2023 was much drier at summit altitude.

The survey team on itsir way to the summit of Mont Blanc.. Photo: ING

A team from the Order of Chartered Surveyors (Ordre des géomètres-experts) acclimatized first by climbing Bishorn (4,153m). They then spent some days measuring several points around the mountain. Finally, they climbed Mont Blanc via the normal route on September 15-16.

Once on top, they pinpointed the highest point using a GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite System) pole, which measures altitude in real time.

The recent survey featured an interesting novelty. In addition to altitude, the survey also measured Mont Blanc’s gravity by installing a gravimeter on the summit for the first time ever. This measured the force with which the Earth attracts the device. In so doing, they wanted to determine whether the summit has sunk, regardless of ice cover.

The gravimeter on the summit of Mont Blanc, September 16. Photo: ING France

“When the height of Mont Blanc changes, we can’t know for sure whether the ground or the rock has moved or if the ice has melted,” explains Olivier Jamet.

Gravity provides the missing information: mass. If the ice has melted, there is less material under the instrument, but the rock has moved closer to it. Distinguishing these two effects requires a model of the ice cap and the massif, and above all, repeated measurements at the same location. And the effort is worthwhile, especially now that the effects of climate change on Alpine glaciers are becoming increasingly apparent.

The gravimeter on the top of Mont Blanc measured the gravity at 9.7944689 m/s².

“For comparison, this value is 9.81 m/s² in Marseille (located at sea level) and 9.8043046 m/s² in Saint-Gervais-Le Fayet (580m above sea level),” ING reports. “This difference in value, even if it seems small, allows for the detection of changes in mass, particularly in the mass of the ice. Repeated over time, these gravity measurements will thus contribute to improving the determination of the altitude of the highest peak in Western Europe.”

#2744. Tanner Wanish and Michael Vaill Are Ready for Rock Climbing’s Limelight

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Wanish (left) and Vaill during the Goliath Traverse (Photo: Joshua Noll)

My favorite scene from the new adventure film Nobody & Goliath involves cigarettes.

Having completed most of the gargantuan Goliath Traverse in California’s Sierra Nevada—a 32-mile ridgeline that passes over 60 summits—rock climbers Tanner Wanish and Michael Vaill are dizzy with exhaustion. They’ve spent the past eight days climbing in the no-fall zone. They’ve gone long stretches of the trip without water or food.

To celebrate almost being done, Wanish and Vaill spark up a couple of lung darts and puff away at 12,000 feet, giggling and backslapping like a couple of high schoolers.

“We were just daydreaming about all of the food and comforts we wanted once we finished, and Mike randomly said he could go for a smoke,” Wanish told me recently. Both he and Vaill sometimes chew nicotine pouches for an extra burst of energy on the crag, he added, but never puff cigarettes.

“I’m not a smoker, but that one was quite enjoyable,” he added.

Look, I’m no smoker either—tobacco products of all forms make my stomach turn. But I still love the scene. It hammers home the film’s primary thrust, which is to introduce mainstream audiences to Wanish and Vaill, alongside an intoxicating message: Climbing’s newest superstars are a different breed.

And no, it’s not just because they enjoy a cig every now and then.

I called Wanish and Vaill recently to talk about the film and their newfound stardom in rock climbing.

“We do still feel like outsiders,” Wanish told me. “I do have this really significant sense of imposter syndrome when I’m with these professional climbers. But it’s starting to change.”

Don’t feel bad if you’re unfamiliar with their names. The duo has built a following within rock climbing circles for their speedy ascents of the biggest walls in Yosemite National Park. Nobody & Goliath, which is touring theaters across the U.S. in 2026, is their first big film project.

Outside has written about them once before. In October 2024, they became Yosemite’s new power couple with a speedy finish on the so-called Yosemite Triple: El Capitan, Half Dome, and Mount Watkins. They did it faster (17 hours, 55 minutes) than any team in history, surpassing times set by some of the valley’s greatest climbing duos: Alex Honnold and Tommy Caldwell, Dean Potter and Timmy O’Neil, Brad Gobright and Jim Reynolds, among others.

A week after the triple, Vaill and Wanish did the route again, and then tacked on a fourth climb, the south face of the Washington Column. They called this pioneering test of endurance the “Yosemite Quad.”

Lost in many of the news stories about their fast climbs was an eye-popping datapoint. Neither Vaill nor Wanish had been climbing for very long. At the time, Wanish had been in the sport for less than five years. Vaill, similarly, had started climbing in his mid-twenties.

“Christmas Day, 2016, that was the first day I ever touched rock,” Vaill, who is 34, told me. “I’d seen pictures of Yosemite, but had no idea I’d end up doing those types of climbs.”

Prior to becoming full-time climbers, Wanish served in the Navy SEALs, while Vaill was a scientific researcher with a PhD in biomedical sciences. They grew up in the southeastern U.S.—Wanish in South Carolina and Vaill in Georgia. Both guys told me their early twenties revolved around booze and school and attending college football games—not pushing their bodies on vertical faces of rock.

“I was partying way too hard,” Wanish said. “Bars every weekend, drinking with the guys. That lifestyle.”

Plenty of late bloomers go on to achieve lofty goals in climbing, but few become the focus of the feature-length films. It’s no secret that climbing’s marquee documentaries tend to focus on athletes with long and storied histories in the sport: think Girl Climber‘s Emily Harrington, or Dawn Wall and Caldwell, Honnold and his many films, and even more recent movies about wunderkind Connor Herson.

Even more core climbing filmmakers train their lens on the sport’s child prodigies, like Adam Ondra or Chris Sharma.

Wanish and Vaill recognize their outsider status amongst climbing’s biggest athletes. Ondra and Herson tackle routes rated 5.15. Vaill and Wanish, meanwhile, hit their limit on routes in the 5.11 or 5.12 range.

“We haven’t spent the last 20 years training on a campus board,” Vaill said. “One in a million people has the genetic potential to climb a route rated 5.15, and we’re not those guys. Our story is about simple things that every single person out there has. Our story is about how we tapped into it.”

The secret sauce behind Wanish and Vaill is their climbing partnership. Neither guy, on his own, is anywhere near being crowned the world’s best rock climber. But when teamed together, the duo push each other to climb harder and faster and longer than even Honnold and Caldwell.

“I don’t think we would be able to do this stuff without each other,” Wanish said. “Both of us have this ability to really commit to something—like a big climbing objective or to the micro moves that get you up a single pitch.”

Nobody & Goliath, which was made by filmmaker Joshua Noll, shows what this actually looks like. Footage captures the two egging each other on as they zip up big walls in Yosemite at 2 A.M. They coach each other as they grab at tiny holds on the side of El Capitan.

They move fast on the rock wall and rarely stop to rest. When one guy is exhausted and the other is still going strong, they use this imbalance to push on until they reach the top.

“There are times when you’re climbing and you get tired, and you think, ‘This kind of sucks. Should we just go down?’ And if your partner agrees with you, then you’re probably going to bail,” Vaill said. “With Tanner, I know he’s going to want to keep going, which means I am, too. And after talking it over, I know we’re going to be 100 percent ready to rage.”

The first half of Nobody & Goliath showcases how their partnership propelled them from anonymous dreamers to the pinnacle of Yosemite big wall climbing.

The two met on a Mountain Project message board after Wanish, who had just become obsessed with the sport, was looking for a climbing partner with equal levels of enthusiasm. At the time, both guys were at that very special period in one’s relationship with outdoor recreation, when all you want to do is practice your specific sport, all day, day every day.

When they found each other, their mutual desire to push one another led them to progress from local crags near San Diego to El Capitan in under a year.

It then propelled them to take on the valley’s speed climbs. As Wanish said, their ability to push each other “pulls every second of slack out of these walls.”

“If you look at the way a normal person climbs a pitch, they look up, look down, think about what they’re going to do, and then maybe make a move,” he said. “We are able to commit to these moves without thinking about them. We’re not hesitating or pausing because we’re not scared. And when you do that, you’re able to take these walls that take people three or four days and do them in five hours.”

Vaill called his partnership with Wanish a “cheat code.”

“I feel like Tanner and I have been on this rocket-boosted trajectory through rock climbing because we tapped into the power of partnership in climbing,” he said.

Stories come and go about famed duos in the world of adventure, and the tales usually include platitudes like the ones Wanish and Vaill have expressed about teamwork and commitment. To see a world-class climbing partnership actually take shape on film gives Nobody & Goliath mainstream appeal that other climbing movies don’t always capture.

I’ve watched most climbing documentaries that have attracted casual audiences, and many have a similar structure. A prodigious climber becomes obsessed with a mountain or route, and commits all of their energy to completing the goal. The climber can’t move on with his or her life until they reach the summit.

Nobody & Goliath is different. After their Yosemite exploits, the two take on the Goliath Traverse, a brutal trek across the Sierra Nevada’s highest peaks, hoping to become the second group to complete it behind climber Vitaliy Musiyenko, who first did it in 2021.

But at no point during the arduous trip do Wanish and Vaill seem singularly obsessed with the accomplishment. Sure, they want to complete it. But the trip isn’t a life-altering mission, à la Harrington free climbing the Golden Gate route up El Capitan in Girl Climber.

Instead, Wanish and Vaill commit to each other. Cameras capture them as their strength and stamina ebb and flow over eight days of punishing climbs. At times, Vaill is totally depleted—the result of a recent climbing injury. Wanish must navigate the strength imbalance, and eventually finds ways to pick him up.

In other scenes, the guys find humor in the sucky repetition of climbing loose and dangerous pinnacles and then rappelling back down, only to face another tricky ascent. Wanish and Vaill come across as guys you’d want to grab a beer with—a quality that lifelong elite climbers don’t always possess.

The camaraderie and friendship is what ultimately help them finish the route.

In some ways, this story is more relatable to the average outdoor athlete than that of uber-talented pro climber taking on a lifelong dream that mere mortals will never approach. Wanish told me the film’s name originates with this idea.

“That’s why they call it Nobody & Goliath, because we are the nobodies,” Wanish said. “We’re just two guys who are willing to try hard and commit. And in a way, it shows that most people are capable of doing something like this. You just need to be willing to go all-in, and you’ll be shocked what you’re capable of.”
