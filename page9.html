#347. Manosphere and online misogyny should be treated as public health issues, say UK experts

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Report calls for an effort to tackle harms in similar way to obesity and smoking, and stresses need to ‘offer boys something better’

The manosphere and online misogyny should be treated by the government as a public health issue, in the same way as obesity or smoking, experts have said.

Writing in the Lancet, the authors of a new report argue that a coordinated response is needed, involving governments, schools, health services and community organisations, similar to the public-health response to tobacco use and ultra-processed food.

“Men’s problems are framed [in the manosphere] as the result of feminism rather than structural conditions, and masculine identity is commodified through products and paid content that normalise and reinforce health-harming and performance-oriented masculinities,” the authors write.

The report defines the manosphere as “a diffuse commercial ecosystem and loose collection of online communities united by shared beliefs about masculinity, misogyny and male victimisation”.

It goes on to outline how the manosphere has evolved into a highly lucrative online industry that makes money by promoting “the most extreme expressions of misogyny and aggrieved masculinity”.

Prof Kent Buse, a co-author of the paper and co-founder of the social justice thinktank Global 50/50, said: “This is a business model, not a fringe. Outrage and grievance are good for business, so the system rewards the most extreme content. Expect the playbook that served tobacco so well: blame the individual, and call regulation an attack on freedom. Public health has a tested toolkit for commercial harms and we should use it.”

He added: “Boys and men are not the problem. The problem is the conditions that leave them isolated and without a secure future, and the companies that profit from their anger and insecurity.”

The authors also point to growing disengagement from education among boys around the world, as well as loneliness, money worries and social exclusion, which can leave young men searching for a sense of belonging. These problems, they say, can make them more vulnerable to online communities that offer simple explanations for their difficulties and often encourage them to blame women and feminism.

Prof Sarah Hawkes, the report’s other author, said: “The manosphere offers boys and young men belonging, identity and an explanation for why life feels hard.

“Those needs are real. A response that only removes the manosphere, without offering something credible in its place, will fail. We need to offer boys something better, not just tell them what to avoid.”

Mark Brooks, the policy and communications director at the Centre for Policy Research on Men and Boys, welcomed the report’s position that it was a public health issue and that men, boys, women and girls were all victims of this content.

“Far too often, the manosphere discussion has positioned all men and boys as a problem and not taken into account the broader economic, educational and cultural challenges that drive their engagement,” he said.

“Not all men and boys engage with this content equally. We are currently working on research to identify who is most at risk.”

#89. Manosphere must be treated as a public health problem like smoking, academics warn

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Boys and young men have their insecurities cultivated in the manosphere and subsequently monetised, the study warns

Academics have called for the so-called manosphere to be treated as a public health problem, with the harms it causes online confronted in the same way as those created by the tobacco industry.

The manosphere, according to the authors of a new report, is “a diffuse commercial ecosystem and loose collection of online communities united by shared beliefs about masculinity, misogyny and male victimisation”.

The movement centres on a so-called crisis of masculinity and a shared conviction that feminism has systematically disadvantaged men, they said.

It has contributed to many health harms, the authors argued. These include violence against women and girls and mental health issues, as well as “delegitimising” the idea of boys and men seeking help.

In this online environment, the authors said, outrage and grievance are “good for business”.

Boys and young men are especially having their insecurities cultivated in the manosphere and subsequently monetised, they said.

The report, published in The Lancet, stated: “Similar to the tobacco and ultra-processed food industries, content creators and hosting platforms deploy strategies that individualise responsibility.

“Men’s problems are framed as the result of feminism rather than structural conditions, and masculine identity is commodified through products and paid content that normalise and reinforce health-harming and performance-oriented masculinities.

“Efforts to regulate or moderate content are frequently framed as threats to men’s autonomy, restricting political space for intervention.”

Professor Sarah Hawkes, report co-author and co-founder of Global 50/50 social justice think tank, said: “The manosphere offers boys and young men belonging, identity and an explanation for why life feels hard.

“Those needs are real. A response that only removes the manosphere, without offering something credible in its place, will fail. We need to offer boys something better, not just tell them what to avoid.”

Fellow co-author Professor Kent Buse, affiliated with the Department of Global Population Health at Monash University Malaysia, argued that boys and men are not the problem, but rather the business model set up to target them.

He said: “This is a business model, not a fringe. Outrage and grievance are good for business, so the system rewards the most extreme content.

“Expect the playbook that served tobacco so well: blame the individual, and call regulation an attack on freedom. Public health has a tested toolkit for commercial harms and we should use it.

“Boys and men are not the problem. The problem is the conditions that leave them isolated and without a secure future, and the companies that profit from their anger and insecurity.”

The authors said factors such as educational marginalisation, social exclusion and isolation in boys and young men must be addressed in various ways.

These include boosting employment, skills to improve access to jobs, re-engaging boys in education and ensuring “equitable access to mental health and other frontline health services” designed to reach this group.

Tackling the manosphere, they argued, should involve a co-ordinated response by governments, schools, health services and community organisations, as for any other public health risk.

They suggested communications strategies should avoid “framing masculinity as inherently toxic or boys and men as inherently problematic”.

Regulation, they said, should focus on the online platforms rather than the users, similar to bans on tobacco advertisements directed at children, rather than criminalising smokers.

The authors argued the manosphere “is best understood and addressed as a public health problem” and that regulation should include “strengthening existing content moderation to restrict harmful environments and harnessing the power of algorithms to promote positive messaging and norms alongside regulation of the structural features that sustain harm”.

The report concluded: “The harms to boys and men, to women and girls, and to wider society, provide a clear basis for action.

“Reducing these harms requires addressing the societal and economic conditions, commercial structures, and social norms that sustain the appeal of the manosphere.

“This is a political, legal, economic, and social challenge demanding co-ordinated, sustained action on multiple fronts — a public health framing can unlock such an approach.”

Mark Brooks, from the Centre for Policy Research on Men and Boys charity, said: “We’re extremely pleased to see this report classifying this as a public health issue and making the really important point that men and boys are targets for online harms as well as women and girls.

“All too often, discussion around the manosphere represents men and boys as being the problem without taking into account the broader economic, education and cultural challenges that drive their engagement.”

Separate research, also published in The Lancet, considered how patriarchal expectations around masculinity are harming men’s health.

It argued that online narratives around masculinity and male grievance are filling a void left by an absence of support.

The author, Professor Jim Catto, Professor of Urology at the University of Sheffield, said: “These findings highlight why we need to look beyond individual behaviour and consider the wider environments that shape men’s attitudes and actions.

“Tackling the online influence of the manosphere should be part of a broader public health conversation about how we support men and address the social factors affecting their health.”

#626. Patterson's failed appeal leaves few legal avenues for the mushroom killer

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Erin Patterson's murder convictions were upheld by Victoria's Court of Appeal. (AAP Image: James Ross)

Victoria's Court of Appeal has rejected Erin Patterson's appeal against her convictions, leaving the 52-year-old with few remaining legal options in her bid for freedom.

Patterson was convicted last year of three counts of murder and one count of attempted murder after serving a deadly beef Wellington lunch laced with death cap mushrooms to her relatives in 2023.

A Victorian Supreme Court jury found her guilty of murdering in-laws Don and Gail Patterson, along with Gail's sister, Heather Wilkinson. Mrs Wilkinson's husband, Ian Wilkinson, was gravely ill following the lunch, but survived.

Erin Patterson appeal live updates: For the latest on the court's decision to impose a harsher sentence, read our blog.

The Leongatha mother has always maintained her innocence and claimed she did not know the mushrooms in the beef Wellington were poisonous.

In August, Patterson appealed against the three life sentences handed down over the crime, but her lawyers were unsuccessful in having her conviction quashed.

Today, the Court of Appeal not only dismissed Patterson's appeal, but increased her sentence to life in prison without parole.

Former criminal barrister and legal researcher Christopher Corns said the Court of Appeal's decision meant there were very few options for Patterson to plead her case moving forward.

"The general principle in Victoria is that an appellant can only have one appeal, at least under the section that Patterson has used to appeal the conviction," Dr Corns said.

"However in 2019, reforms were introduced to permit a second appeal against conviction in very rare circumstances."

He said a second appeal could only be launched in the case of "fresh and compelling" evidence coming to light that would exonerate a convicted person.

A court sketch of convicted murderer Erin Patterson during her appeal hearing in August. (Supplied: Anita Lester)

Dr Corns said examples could be seen in the case of Nicola Gobbo, where several of her former clients were able to go to the Court of Appeal for a second time.

However, Dr Corns said in this particular case, it was unlikely to be an option.

"It seems to me that it is most unlikely that in Erin Patterson's case that she would be able to utilise that avenue, because if there was any significant evidence it would have been turned up by now by her," he said.

With that legal avenue unlikely, Dr Corns said Patterson's "only hope" was the High Court of Australia, which is able to overrule Victoria's Court of Appeal.

But Dr Corns warned the High Court of Australia was not simply another court where those convicted could "have a second go" at appealing.

"There are very strict criteria that the High Court applies to determine which cases they will hear or not," he said.

One high-profile example is Cardinal George Pell, who in 2020 had his convictions quashed by the High Court of Australia after his appeal was first rejected by the Victorian Court of Appeal.

Cardinal George Pell fought a high-profile legal battle in the High Court of Australia. (Reuters: Mark Dadswell)

Criminal law barrister Nick Papas KC said while the case of Cardinal Pell was a strong example of such an appeal, in reality it was "generally very difficult" for those appeals to be successful.

He said there needed to be a "fundamental failure" with the way Court of Appeal judges had interpreted the law for a person to have their appeal heard.

"You can't just think there was a mistake," Mr Papas said.

"It's got to be a clear-cut error that justifies an intervention over the decision of the Court of Appeal."

This season marks the second time the championship has been decided in a best-of-seven format, with last year's victory by the Las Vegas Aces over the Phoenix Mercury coming via a 4-0 sweep.

#242. Netanyahu accused of smearing Arab voters to hold on to power

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Prime minister claims Arab Israeli parties used foreign backing to steal elections, in which his coalition lost its majority

Benjamin Netanyahu has been accused of using smear tactics to cling to power, after he claimed that Arab Israeli parties had used foreign backing to “steal” Tuesday’s election, in which the prime minister’s coalition lost its parliamentary majority.

Netanyahu and his allies called on the internal security service, Shin Bet, to investigate alleged vote buying and other irregularities in Palestinian communities in Israel, supposedly funded by foreign money.

Gadi Eisenkot, the leader of the main opposition bloc, accused the prime minister and his Likud party of trying to derail the election results.

“It’s already clear that Likud and Netanyahu have embarked on a coordinated, organised campaign seeking to delegitimise the results of the election,” Eisenkot said in a video message.

The prime minister’s allegations were quickly disowned by a range of government officials, including police officers and members of the election committee. Netanyahu was also rebuked by the president for “tarnishing an entire community”.

On Wednesday night, the prime minister posted a statement by Likud online, which described the claimed violations as “an attack on democracy whose purpose is to lead to a foreign takeover of the Knesset, the Islamisation of Israel, and the overthrow of rightwing rule”.

Tuesday’s elections led to Netanyahu’s far-right coalition losing its majority after four years in power. The main Jewish opposition bloc also fell short, however, leaving two Arab Israeli parties, the Joint List and Ra’am, holding the balance of power.

Final results released early on Friday local time showed the pro-Netanyahu bloc winning 53 seats in the 120-member Knesset and Eisenkot’s bloc taking 48 seats. Joint List and Ra’am won the remaining 19 seats, a historic high for the Arab Israeli community, which makes up about a fifth of Israel’s population.

The prime minister’s attack appeared aimed at stopping the Jewish and Arab opposition from voting together in the new Knesset to oust the incumbent speaker, Amir Ohana, a Likud politician and former Shin Bet official, and advancing their own legislation.

The Likud statement posted by Netanyahu on X said: “It is clear that no Zionist party should cooperate with Ra’am and the Joint List, replace the Knesset speaker through votes bought for cash, and form a government that is dangerous to Israel.”

Opposition leaders lambasted the claims of Netanyahu, 77, who has run Israel for 19 of the past 30 years.

Yousef Jabareen, the Joint List leader, accused the prime minister of incitement. “Netanyahu was defeated at the ballot box,” Jabareen said, adding that he “refuses to come to terms with the fact that the Arab public voted and decided”.

Eisenkot said any investigation should be conducted “without fear or favour,” and with “the utmost caution”, but insisted it could not be used as a reason to delay the convening of the newly elected Knesset, due to be sworn in within 14 days of the election.

The acting director general of the central elections committee, Dean Livneh, said there appeared to be no basis for the claims. Police officials quoted across the Israeli press said the allegations were groundless, adding that voting had been relatively trouble-free.

The president, Isaac Herzog, called for political leaders to “avoid tarnishing an entire community that sought to exercise its right to vote and take an active part in democracy”.

It was not specified in Likud statements, but press reports said Qatar was the foreign power alleged to have funded vote-buying and voter mobilisation. Majed al-Ansari, an adviser to Qatar’s prime minister, said its government “categorically” denied any involvement in the elections.

The record turnout among Arab Israelis (Palestinians with Israeli citizenship) appeared to be driven by outrage at routine discrimination and fear that it could become far worse if extremist and openly racist members of the government, such as the national security minister, Itamar Ben-Gvir, and the finance minister, Bezalel Smotrich, secured another term in office.

On Thursday, Ben-Gvir called for the arrest of Arab party leaders, and Smotrich demanded they be subjected to Shin Bet interrogation.

Voting in Nazareth, the biggest majority-Palestinian town in Israel, was brisk on Tuesday, with few signs of irregularities, other than apparent ploys by the government and coalition parties to suppress the Arab vote.

Some packets of ballots sent to Nazareth polling stations were in Hebrew alone, rather than in Hebrew and Arabic. Election workers said that monitors sent by far-right parties had attempted procedural ploys to slow down voting but had been largely unsuccessful.

The leaders of the Joint List and the Zionist “Change” bloc have ruled out a formal coalition, but officials from both have discussed the possibility of tactical voting in the Knesset to unseat Ohana, the speaker, and to pass some consensus legislation.

One proposed bill would establish a commission of inquiry into the security failures that helped lead to the Hamas attack on Israel on 7 October 2023, something Netanyahu has blocked over the past two years.

Ahmad Tibi, a party leader on the Joint List, said Netanyahu was trying to subvert the election results by trying to sabotage a common front between the Eisenkot bloc and the Arab Israeli parties.

“This entire campaign is directed against Eisenkot, to prevent him, intimidate and scare him off forming a government that relies on external support from the Joint List or Ra’am,” Tibi told the Reuters news agency.

Naftali Bennett, a former prime minister who is a partner in the “Change” bloc, pointed out the irony of Netanyahu denouncing Qatar for election meddling while the Israeli leader’s own political advisers are being accused of taking Qatari money for promoting the Gulf monarchy’s image.

“This morning, suddenly, when it serves his interests, Netanyahu remembered Qatar and is using it to sow chaos and undermine public confidence in the democratic process,” Bennett wrote on X.

Justice McLeish described Patterson's crimes as "extraordinarily cold and calculating."

"The applicant's intent to murder all four victims was sustained for nearly three weeks from when she invited them to lunch, through to preparing and serving the poisoned meal and then throughout the protracted suffering and ultimate deaths that resulted," he said.

The decision to reject Patterson's appeal leaves her with few legal options, with an appeal to the High Court of Australia her only avenue.

The second related to the use of expert evidence derived from Patterson's mobile phone data used by the prosecution to show she had visited areas identified in internet posts as death cap mushroom sites.

The proposed introduction of mining and fracking to a South Australian food bowl has young people considering what it could mean for their future.

The Limestone Coast's economy has been dominated by agriculture since early European settlers dug drains to create some of the most productive farmland in SA.

But mining and fracking are positioning to become a part of the region's economic future.

Gomoroi woman and climate and youth advocate Jazmin Bingham, 19, said it represented a change in what the future could hold for local jobs, communities and the environment.

Jazmin Bingham is a member of Seed, an Indigenous youth-led climate advocacy group.  (ABC South East SA: Sam Bradbrook)

"Young people are generally not at the table when decisions are being made and that's just the way it is, and things just need a change," she said.

"Our lands are so sacred and so rich. That's why the agriculture and primary industries are so strong down south.

Fracking, or hydraulic fracture stimulation, has proved controversial on the Limestone Coast over its potential effects on the region's groundwater system.

Conventional gas extraction has occurred in the South East for more than a century.

But in May, the SA government announced legislation to repeal a 10-year moratorium on fracking in the region, introduced in 2018.

The bill will fail without support from state parliament's crossbench, but Premier Peter Malinauskas has made it clear the moratorium will lapse in 2028.

Zoe Moore says young people should be engaged in proposed change for the region.  (ABC South East SA: Sam Bradbrook)

"If we always have the same older generations making decisions for us, it will continue to impact us longer than it will impact them," she said.

An alternative Greens bill to ban fracking permanently in the region has passed the Upper House, but will be defeated in the Lower House by Labor's sizeable majority.

Mining and energy minister Tom Koutsantonis said the state needed cheap gas to meet its energy needs and grow the economy.

Tom Koutsantonis has backed gas as a way to grow the economy.  (ABC News: Ashlin Blieschke)

"If we want to make things here, we need the cheap inputs of energy and one of those cheap inputs of energy is gas," he said.

"The idea of locking it up and importing it from somewhere else, given that we've got it here, is the antithesis of what young people need in this state.

"Long-term jobs require cheap energy and cheap energy comes from an abundance of gas."

Naracoorte farmer Joe Schultz's land falls inside the exploration area for a proposed rare earth mine in the Wrattonbully region.

Joe Schultz is the third generation of his family to farm in the region.  (ABC South East SA: Sam Bradbrook)

The 28-year-old is a member of the Limestone Coast Sustainable Futures Group, set up in opposition to the proposal.

"I feel like a young farmer here trying to carve my way out and carve my life into a beautiful farming career, and I've got so many other things that I never thought I would even consider," he said.

Joe Schultz is thinking twice about investing in his farming future. (ABC South East SA: Sam Bradbrook)

Travis Beinke says the proposed Koppamurra project could create stable future jobs and complement farming.  (ABC News: Lincoln Rothall)

The project has an initial expected operating time of 12 years, and the company planned to create about 100 jobs.

"Mining provides an alternate economic contributor to the region in times where farming can go through tougher times depending on seasons," Mr Beinke said.

#109. Scientists for the first time injected an AI-created virus into a human

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

The announcement has not been verified in a peer-reviewed study

Scientists have given a human patient a virus designed by artificial intelligence for the first time, in an effort to fight infections that no longer respond to antibiotics.

California-based AI startup Radical Numerics said a patient with a serious bacterial infection received an experimental treatment containing viruses created by its AI model, Omnii-1.

The model designs bacteriophages, or phages, which are viruses that infect and kill bacteria without targeting human cells. Doctors already use phage therapy as an experimental treatment for serious bacterial infections, particularly when patients have few other options.

Rather than searching for a virus that could kill the patient's bacteria, Omnii-1 redesigned part of a virus so it could better recognize and attack the specific bacterial strain causing the infection. The company says the AI-driven process could eventually take just days.

The treatment was administered through a special program that allows doctors to try experimental therapies when conventional options are limited.

Radical Numerics said this was the first time AI-designed phages had been used to treat a human patient. The announcement was published on the company's website but has not been confirmed in a peer-reviewed study, and the company has not said whether the treatment helped the patient.

The approach could offer another option for treating hard-to-treat infections. Doctors typically have to find a naturally occurring virus that can target the specific bacteria causing a patient's illness.

Radical Numerics hopes its AI model can speed up that process by designing viruses to target a patient's particular infection, potentially helping doctors find a treatment faster.

The company said it tested its AI-designed viruses against bacteria taken from the patient before administering the treatment. The virus mixture eliminated the patient's antibiotic-resistant bacterial sample in laboratory testing, according to the company.

However, it has not released details about the patient's diagnosis, the treatment's outcome, or whether the infection was ultimately cleared.

The development comes amid growing interest in using AI to accelerate medical research, alongside concerns about how the technology could be misused.

In August, researchers at Stanford University and the Arc Institute announced that they had used AI to design previously unseen viruses capable of killing E. coli bacteria in laboratory experiments. Of nearly 300 AI-designed viral genomes synthesized and tested, 16 produced functional viruses. Those experiments were conducted in bacteria, not humans.

Researchers have warned that AI systems capable of designing new viruses raise biosafety concerns, particularly if similar methods are adapted to create viruses that infect humans.

The Stanford team deliberately focused on viruses that attack bacteria rather than humans or animals, but scientists have called for stronger safeguards as AI-driven biological research advances.
