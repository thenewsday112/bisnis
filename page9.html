#1204. Spain grows fruit and vegetables for Europe, but it's running out of water

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Spain's southern coast produces up to 25% of Europe's fruit and vegetables. But amid intensive farming, climate change and illegal wells, the water it takes to grow them is drying up.

Donana National Park in southwestern Spain — one of Europe's most important wetlands, home to millions of migratory birds and threatened species like the Iberian lynx — is running dry. But rising temperatures and drought caused by climate change are only part of the problem.

The UNESCO-protected region on the Atlantic coast relies on an underground aquifer that's been tapped by hundreds of illegal wells. The reason: Europe's fruit and vegetable supply. Spain provides one-quarter of Europe's fresh produce, supplying colder parts of the continent with fruit and vegetables year-round.

The scale of water extraction in Spain is staggering: In 2016, WWF Spain estimated that the Donana wetland was home to over 1,000 illegal wells and more than 1,700 ponds illegally irrigating an area as big as 4,200 football fields.

"It's gotten hotter every year. And the water dries up so quickly," one local resident in the town of El Rocio, just north of the wetlands, told DW.

"For me, day to day, the main issue is my animals," said another, hitching his horse up to a tourist carriage. "I have to draw water for them, but the well doesn't have enough water to provide it all at once."

The Donana area used to be royal hunting grounds, consisting of marshes and ponds. In the early 20th century, parts began to be used for dryland farming, agriculture in arid regions that relies heavily on rainwater.

But in 1971, Spain — still under the dictatorship of General Francisco Franco — declared the region's irrigation to be of national interest. The Almonte-Marismas Plan, named after the aquifer it tapped into, was part of a massive, state-sponsored effort to increase food production.

Authorities decided to bet on strawberries, which they called "red gold." According to calculations based on FAO data, strawberries can yield up to €150,000 (about $170,000) in gross revenue per hectare per year, compared to roughly €20,000 for potatoes or €15,000 for grapes.

But growing strawberries at scale also meant using a lot more water. Authorities handed out generous quotas and allotments, seemingly without considering how much extraction the aquifer could handle. Even those legal quotas weren't enough for the ever-expanding fields.

Farmers turned to illegal wells and reservoirs to make up the difference. They built them to stockpile water, draining supplies from local residents and the UNESCO World Heritage Site next door.

"There was extreme drought and lack of attention from the administration," said Pedro Banez, of the Condado Water Users' Association. "And we found ourselves with a number of farms — or parts of farms — that fell outside the land classified as irrigable agricultural land."

The turning point came in 2019 when, following numerous reports of unlawful wells, a 2-year-old fell into deep, reportedly illegal borehole on farmland near Malaga and died. In the aftermath, Spain's Civil Guard police force created dedicated units focused on water theft.

Two years after that, in 2021, the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that Spain had failed to protect the Donana aquifer, finding the country in violation of EU law.

Today, police and NGOs like WWF Spain track illegal extraction — near Donana, and other overtaxed agricultural regions across the country — using a mix of old and new tools. They start with satellite images of the region, comparing them against land rights maps to flag areas that appear greener than their legal status allows. Drones follow, giving investigators a closer look at where the water is actually coming from.

Some wells are completely illegal. Others are legal but extract more than they're allowed, which requires closer, on-site inspection to prove.

Investigators check the flow meters that measure how much water a well is pulling. Tampering is common, often using a powerful magnet which disrupts the internal mechanism of the flow meter, allowing water to pass through unrecorded.

Farmers caught extracting illegally can be charged with crimes against the environment and fraud. Most face fines, but sentences can run up to five years in prison.

In a bid to undo decades of overuse, local and national authorities have negotiated a €1.4 billion joint plan. The government has continued shutting down illegal wells, sealing almost 300 in the Donana region alone.

Officials have also started buying up farmland, rewilding it and turning it into protected areas, while setting up funds to create jobs in other sectors like tourism and renewable energy.

Farmers, meanwhile, have had to change how they water their crops.

Many have switched to drip irrigation systems, where each plant gets its own individual dripper, paired with moisture sensors that report exactly how much water a plant needs. That cuts down on the overuse of both water and fertilizer.

Farmers have also begun planting less water-intensive crops and shifting their growing season to colder months.

Spain isn't alone in confronting this problem. As economies mature, fruit and vegetable production starts being outsourced to places with cheaper labor, fewer restrictions and more sun.

Mexico, Brazil and Colombia grow food for parts of the US, while countries in southeast Asia export a lot of produce to China or Japan. Some countries in northern Africa, including Morocco and Egypt, also supply fresh fruits and vegetables to their European neighbors to the north. But, as in Spain, intensive agriculture has strained local water supplies.

Morocco has tried to turn the tide by using satellite comparisons to monitor irrigation and offering subsidies for farmers who switch to drip irrigation. In India, the government is trying to move farmers toward less water-intensive crops.

And in California, the US state has paid farmers to halt certain types of irrigation and introduced legislation to monitor and allocate water more sustainably.

Securing water, no longer seen as an unlimited resource, will increasingly be a challenge as the effects of climate change intensify. Addressing overextraction will take a coordinated effort from governments, farmers and the industries that depend on their water.

#1192. Global trade weathers Iran, Ukraine and Trump's tariffs

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Over the past six years, COVID, two wars and Donald Trump's tariffs have shaken world trade. With globalization already stalling, is this a temporary shock or the start of a great reversal?

From COVID to Donald Trump's tariffs — with wars in Ukraine and the Middle East in between — world trade policy is enduring the most unpredictable period of the postwar era.

The pandemic shut factories worldwide and accelerated a shift away from China, the world’s largest manufacturer. The two conflicts then disrupted global supplies of oil and gas, food and fertilizer.

US President Donald Trump’s tariffs, meanwhile, pushed US duties to their highest level in decades, forcing firms to find new suppliers and markets.

Robert Staiger, chief economist at the World Trade Organization (WTO), calls the turmoil "the most serious and sustained disruptions since the [world trading] system was created 80 years ago."

Despite these shocks, global trade has remained surprisingly resilient.

According to the WTO's Global Trade Outlook published Thursday, the growth in goods trade is now projected to reach 3.9% this year, a major upwards revision from the March guidance of 1.9%.

The WTO estimated that growth could even reach 4.1% in 2027, lifted by strong demand for the advanced technology behind the artificial intelligence (AI) build-out.

The report said "global supply chains adapted to disruptions in energy and fertilizer markets [from the Iran war], while strong investment in AI-related infrastructure boosted trade in AI-enabling goods.”

In the first six months, AI-related tech accounted for nearly half (47%) of goods trade growth globally.

Since returning to office last year, Trump has continued to pivot from WTO rules on global trade.

The US president has set country-specific duties, breaking the WTO’s equal-treatment rule. He has since negotiated exemptions and "reciprocal" tariffs with individual countries.

Despite this, globally, about 72% of goods are still traded on the Geneva-based agency's core terms, down from roughly 80% in 2022.

On a longer horizon, though, globalization appears to have stalled. WTO data shows that since about 2015, world trade has grown only as fast as the global economy. Despite the recent geopolitical upheavals, globalization has yet to shift into reverse.

Policymakers and economists say it is too soon to know whether the wars, alongside a pivot toward protectionism in the US, China, the EU and post-Brexit Britain, mark the end of an ever-interconnected world.

"The world is certainly not becoming more globalized," Thomas Sampson, an associate professor at the London School of Economics, told DW. "But it's still an open question whether we're actually at deglobalization or just shifting around who we trade with."

Since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the EU has sharply cut its reliance on Russian pipeline gas from 37% in late 2021 to around 10%, according to Eurostat, the bloc's statistics agency. EU oil and coal imports from Russia are now negligible.

These moves spurred new energy deals with the US, Norway, Australia and Kazakhstan, with liquefied natural gas (LNG) replacing Russian pipeline gas.

Moscow did find new energy buyers — notably China and India — which took Russian oil at a large discount to global benchmark prices.

Camille Reverdy, an affiliate fellow at the Brussels-based think tank Bruegel, doubts whether the EU will rekindle its energy trade with Moscow when the war ends.

"If the alternatives become reliable sources, I don't see it coming back," Reverdy told DW.

When the Iran war began in late February, Tehran effectively shut the Strait of Hormuz, one of the world's most critical energy transit chokepoints. Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates sent some of the missing oil by pipeline to alternative ports.

According to the International Energy Agency, the world also replaced the missing millions of barrels with increased production from the US, Kazakhstan, Brazil and Venezuela. Several nations have also tapped about 300 million barrels from their strategic oil reserves, Reuters reported in August.

Almost seven months into the conflict, energy prices remain elevated, with Brent crude trading above $100 for much of the war. Several analysts are now warning of dangerously tight supply in the fall and winter.

The squeeze from Trump's tariffs is also weighing on trade.

US imports from China, for instance, fell by around 28% last year, according to the Washington-based Peterson Institute for International Economics. At the same time, its imports from the rest of the world climbed by nearly a tenth — much of it from Vietnam, Taiwan and Mexico, as part of rerouting efforts to cut reliance on Chinese suppliers.

Trump’s attacks on the WTO have raised a sharper question. If the Geneva-based body were shelved, what would replace it? Experts have floated several alternatives.

These include continuing Trump's country-by-country tariffs or a web of free trade deals between economic blocs, including BRICS nations of the Global South.

The third, mooted by think tanks like Chatham House, would see the EU, Japan, Canada, Australia and Mexico keep trading by WTO-style rules at a scale the US and China could not ignore.

Bruegel's Reverdy thinks the EU would prefer a reformed WTO to the alternatives.

"The EU still wants to be seen as the leader of free and multilateral trade," she told DW, noting how Brussels has added new layers of trade policy, including possibly making Canada an associate member.

LSE's Sampson warned that continuing with Trump's "law of the jungle" would disadvantage smaller countries, who he said have a "bigger voice" under the current WTO system.

Noting how "trust in the US as a trading partner has collapsed," Sampson told DW it could take many years for confidence to return, even with a Democratic president.

WTO economists, meanwhile, have modeled different futures, noting how further trade fragmentation along geopolitical lines could cut global GDP by about 5% by 2050.

In a world in which the WTO vanished and only a patchwork of free-trade deals remained, GDP could fall closer to 7%, with the smallest and poorest economies hit hardest.

Reinforcing multilateral rules, however, could raise global GDP by roughly 3%, the agency said.

#1167. El Nino is a 'postcard from the future' — and could cause 451,000 excess heat deaths

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The current El Nino could cause 451,000 excess heat deaths. Researchers warn it offers a glimpse of temperatures that may become normal within 20 years.

Peruvian fishermen gave El Nino its name — "the little boy" or Christ Child — after noticing unusually warm Pacific waters around Christmas that caused the fish they depended on to suddenly become scarce.

But what those fishermen observed locally can have consequences thousands of kilometers away. El Nino can shift temperature and rainfall patterns around the world, bringing extreme heat and drought to some regions and heavy rain and flooding to others.

Researchers now estimate that the unusually high temperatures linked to the current El Nino could cause around 451,000 excess deaths worldwide from June 2026 to February 2027.

The event is a "postcard from the future," Tamma Carleton, head of research at the University of Chicago's Climate Impact Lab, told DW. Land temperatures over the coming months are forecast to run about 1.2 degrees C (2.1 F) above normal.

"For the average person, that's roughly the warming climate change is projected to bring about 20 years from now, arriving all at once," Carleton said.

Normally, winds blowing west across the Pacific push warm surface water towards Asia and Australia, allowing colder water to rise near South America. During El Nino, which occurs erratically every two to seven years, those winds weaken. Less cold water then rises, and much more of the Pacific stays unusually warm.

The warmer water heats the air above it and fuels rainfall over the central and eastern Pacific. The rising air spreads high in the atmosphere, sending waves that disrupt weather worldwide.

"It is like throwing a stone into a lake," climate researcher Fred Kucharski of the Abdus Salam International Centre for Theoretical Physics in Trieste, Italy, told DW. "Initially the surface perturbation is local, but then the energy spreads all over the lake through wave propagation."

Kucharski said the effects can cross the tropics within one week and reach higher latitudes in about two. Where the waves travel determines whether a region becomes hotter, cooler, wetter or drier.

The current El Nino developed in June and has strengthened rapidly since. It is now on course to become an exceptionally strong — sometimes called "super" — El Nino.

The Climate Impact Lab says its estimate covers only the first nine months of El Nino, as far as the current seasonal forecasts allow. The event is expected to continue into Northern Hemisphere spring, and its effect on global temperatures could last longer.

"We expect the final number to be meaningfully higher than what we are currently reporting here," Carleton said.

The estimate of 451,000 excess deaths covers deaths linked to the direct health effects of unusually high temperatures associated with El Nino.

Extreme heat strains the heart and kidneys, makes it harder for the body to maintain a safe temperature and can worsen cardiovascular, respiratory and other illnesses. As a result, the underlying condition is often recorded as the cause of death rather than the heat that aggravated it.

"Most heat deaths never show up in official statistics as heat deaths," Carleton said.

El Nino can also alter rainfall, contributing to drought in some regions and heavy rain and flooding in others.

Floods can kill, contaminate drinking water and spread disease. Drought can damage harvests and cause water and food shortages.

Deaths from these other indirect effects are not included in the 451,000 projection.

Donald Trump thinks the name artificial intelligence sounds "fake" and has ordered US agencies to use "Super Intelligence" instead. Yet, tech firms and researchers have little reason to play along.

What's with Donald Trump and renaming things? First, the Gulf of Mexico became the Gulf of America — in US federal documents, if not globally. Then Lake Ontario became Lake America. Now he wants to rebrand the defining technology of our time: artificial intelligence (AI).

Earlier this month, the US president claimed on his Truth Social platform that the term artificial intelligence is "inaccurate" and "ineloquent." He said "superior," "extreme," or "supreme" intelligence were better alternatives.

The tech industry has yet to adopt the phrase, however, while academics warn that superintelligence is a future term for when technology can beat humans across the board. They say the current AI models are years away from that milestone.

"Most AI is not superintelligence — it's just different intelligence," Pedro Domingos, a computer science professor from the University of Washington, told DW. "AI is doing different things from what humans do, so calling it superintelligence is misleading."

Computer scientists and mathematicians coined the name "artificial intelligence" in 1956 at a summer workshop at Dartmouth College in New Hampshire. "Artificial" simply meant made by humans, as opposed to natural human intelligence.

Not all experts agreed with the name. In the book "Machines Who Think," historian Pamela McCorduck cited AI pioneer Arthur Samuel, who said the term "makes you think there's something kind of phony about this."

Despite this, the name artificial intelligence has stuck for 70 years. Critics say changing it now will not quieten the many fears around AI, including job losses, human obsolescence or machines slipping out of our control.

"The name change is fostering these kinds of fears: 'Oh, this is superintelligence and it's going to make humans obsolete, turn on us and take our jobs'," warned Domingos. "If you want to maximize the backlash against AI, rename it Superior Intelligence."

Those public fears are growing fast. In a Public First poll for Politico earlier this month, about half of respondents in six countries — the United States, Canada, France, Spain, Germany and the United Kingdom — backed a pause in further AI development to reduce the risk that the new tech could destroy humanity.

A separate CNN/SSRS poll found nearly nine in 10 Americans are more concerned than excited about AI surpassing human intelligence.

Those concerns have already reached the US Congress. Last week, Vermont's independent Senator Bernie Sanders co-introduced a bill to "permanently ban the development of artificial superintelligence" that humans cannot control.

The legislation also calls for a pause on advanced AI development until federal testing and oversight are in place.

Trump's new term could, however, suit the major US AI players, who are keen to play up their advanced models. Those systems already outperform humans on many specialized tasks and until recently were far ahead of China.

Yet both Anthropic, the firm behind the Claude model, and OpenAI, which created ChatGPT, have pushed back plans to go public. Some market watchers blamed the delays on recent scares where AI models hacked into computer systems or ran tasks they were not meant to.

With Chinese models closing much of the quality gap with much less computing power, US labs may still appreciate the branding that helps confirm they are still leading the AI race. Global spending on AI is expected to surpass $1 trillion (€880 billion), according to Goldman Sachs research.

"The [US] labs have every interest in calling it super, hyper, superior and whatnot intelligence, because it's playing up their capabilities. It's like just marketing," said Domingos, the author of the 2015 book "The Master Algorithm."

On that journey, Elon Musk has predicted that AI learning could be automated, with limited human input, by the end of this year or 2027. OpenAI thinks it could take a year longer. But even then, AI systems won't be fully recursive.

Richard Dawkins set out how genes drive evolution in his popular 1976 book. Credit: Greg White/Fairfax Media/Getty

I first read The Selfish Gene in the 1980s, as a graduate student in neurobiology and animal behaviour. I admired the clarity with which biologist Richard Dawkins distilled mid-twentieth-century work in evolutionary biology.

The idea at the core of the book, published in 1976, was thrillingly simple: that a living organism is just a gene’s way of making more organisms that can carry the gene down the generations.

Dawkins’s paradox: dissecting the body’s battle to keep selfish genes in check

Dawkins’s paradox: dissecting the body’s battle to keep selfish genes in check

Back then, The Selfish Gene seemed pretty harmless. Dawkins’s ideas tracked with mainstream sociobiology. It was a popular book that covered exactly the sort of ideas that my lecturers were already teaching.

Re-reading it now, with decades of scholarship in the history of biology under my belt, it seems less benign. The determinism baked into the “gene’s-eye view” in the book troubles me, as does the passivity of the organism in Dawkins’s telling — a “vehicle”, a mere “replicator”. The implication: that traits such as aggression are hard-wired into people. Fortunately, over the past couple of decades, new tools, fresh models and scholars in many disciplines have begun to embrace the complex reality of biology. Readers in search of an understanding of life on Earth today would do better to reach for books that take a broader view.

The Selfish Gene was the apotheosis of the ‘modern evolutionary synthesis’. That theory uses mathematics to quantify species evolution at the population level, through changes in the frequency of different versions of genes, known as alleles, across time and space.

Dawkins wrote in The Selfish Gene that the gene’s-eye view was “implicit” in the 1930s writings of modern-synthesis pioneer (and eugenicist) Ronald Fisher and his contemporaries, but was made “explicit” by evolutionary biologists George Williams and William Hamilton, in the 1960s. “For me,” he wrote, “their insight had a visionary quality. But I found their expressions of it too laconic, not full-throated enough.” The Selfish Gene, then, takes the ideas to their extreme.

Richard Dawkins book of the dead is haunted by ghosts of past works

Richard Dawkins book of the dead is haunted by ghosts of past works

The modern synthesis blends two branches of evolutionary biology and genetics: neo-Darwinian evolution and Mendelian genetics. Neo-Darwinian evolution was established in the 1890s by biologist August Weismann in his book Die Keimplasma (The Germ Plasm). The book builds off On the Origin of Species, in which Charles Darwin outlines a central tenet — organisms evolve over many generations through an internal process called natural selection. This is the Darwinism of the first edition of On the Origin of Species, published in 1859. Yet over five successive editions, published between then and 1872, Darwin had given increasing credence to the ‘effects of use and disuse’ — the idea, associated with naturalist Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, that characteristics acquired during an organism’s lifetime can be inherited. Darwin wrote, for example, about blind cave fish (Astyanax mexicanus). Their ancestors had eyes when they first nosed into a lightless cave. But after going unused for generations, the eyes degenerated, and the change gradually became heritable.

Darwin saw no reason that his theories couldn’t accommodate such phenomena. He didn’t speculate on mechanisms. Today, an evolutionary biologist might argue that any animal that put less energy than its neighbours into making a complex but useless structure, such as an unused eye, would have surplus energy to devote to, say, bigger gonads or defending its territory, and thus would gain a survival advantage. To Weismann, however, incorporating use and disuse into Darwinism was a dangerous move towards teleology, and risked ceding biological change to bishops and mystics. He proposed that only the germ plasm (the material carried in sperm and egg cells) was passed from parents to offspring. Nothing outside could affect what was transmitted.

Trays containing the entire human genome, split into more than 20,000 fragments of DNA.Credit: James King-Holmes/SPL

The second field that makes up the modern synthesis, Mendelian genetics, was invented not by Gregor Mendel, the nineteenth-century monk after whom the field is named, but by naturalist William Bateson, in his strange and brilliant 1902 book, Mendel’s Principles of Heredity: A Defence. Mendel ran experiments crossing peas, and in 1866 published a paper describing rules that governed how traits were passed down — how many offspring of a green and yellow pea cross would be green and how many yellow, for instance. We now know that only some traits, controlled by single genes, follow the simplistic patterns he observed.

The conservative Mendel claimed only to have found a law “valid for Pisum” (the pea genus); Bateson claimed that all heredity followed Mendel’s laws. Bateson’s book was ‘a defence’ against the theories of his rival and erstwhile best friend, Walter Weldon, who argued that such neat, simple laws do not describe how nature works. Weldon pointed out that traits such as height vary continuously, in a bell-shaped, normal distribution. He argued that such traits result from many factors, both hereditary and environmental.

In the end, as historian Gregory Radick has argued, Mendelism became the universally accepted form of heredity more for practical reasons than because it captured an essential truth. It’s easy to teach and methodologically simple.

Both components of the modern synthesis focus on the gene as the sole determinant of heredity. Both halves reached essentially universal acceptance well before Dawkins came along — the spit and swagger of the field’s proponents keeping challenges and alternatives at bay.

To a historian, this universal acceptance sounds close to ideology. Fortunately, since The Selfish Gene was published, scientists’ understanding of the basic mechanisms that drive evolution has changed drastically.

Amphibians were able to live in the sea 237 million years ago, according to research published today in Nature Ecology & Evolution1.

Two fossils of a type of primitive amphibian from a group called temnospondyls were found in what is now Guizhou province in China — which would have been a salty sea during the Late Triassic period. The researchers have provisionally named the species Gerrothorax maritimus. The 74-centimetre-long creatures had flattened bodies and were almost completely covered in body armour.

There are more than 8,000 modern amphibian species, but none of them lives exclusively in a marine environment. “This is potentially a revolutionary discovery,” says Steve Brusatte, a palaeontologist and evolutionary biologist at the University of Edinburgh, UK. “If this Triassic amphibian, Gerrothorax, really was fully marine, then it is a game-changer.”

Schematic of one of the amphibian fossils, colour-coded on the basis of skeletal parts, and with bones labelled.Credit: Diao, L. et al./Nature Ecol. Evol.

Amphibians have varying levels of salt tolerance, says Ryosuke Motani, a palaeobiologist at the University of California, Davis, and a co-author of the study. But even salt-tolerant animals need to recover from excess salt in one way or another, he explains. “In most cases, they do it by drinking fresh water.”

The researchers argue that this was not an option for G. maritimus: the excellent preservation of the skeletons, so far from any land mass that existed at the time, suggests that they were not swept there by water. The team also measured isotopes of the element strontium, which can help to date minerals and water in marine samples. The levels in the ancient animals’ bones matched those in the seawater at the time, which suggests that the creatures “grew up drinking the seawater”, the researchers write in the paper.

Only, there is no guarantee. An investment fund is not a savings account and any loss in value affects the investor. Gundogdu expects that money market funds will largely be redeemed. With hedge funds and equity funds holding "junk stocks" that are virtually untradeable, however, the situation could prove more difficult.

It is important to distinguish between market losses and damages resulting from potential irregularities.

Economist and columnist Guldem Atabay emphasized that a distinction must be made between price losses and manipulation: Investors bear the market risk, but they do not have to accept false information, artificially inflated prices, or a lack of oversight.

If funds are closed or liquidated, the shares and other securities in the portfolio can be sold. The question is how much they are worth and how easily they can be sold.

Financial expert Gundogdu believes that the fund's assets will not cover investors' claims. Furthermore, she says there have been structural failures. "The crisis is far greater than a simple liquidity crisis. There are flaws in management and oversight."

She therefore considers it possible that the assets of the fund founders and managers could also be tapped to make up for the shortfall.

Economist Atabay calls for those responsible to be identified and for the damages to be recovered from them. Following the liquidation of the funds, it must be clarified who is responsible for any legal violations. "Liquidation is no substitute for accountability," Atabay said.

The crisis has already led to the resignation of a high-ranking female politician in the ruling AKP party. Fatma Betul Sayan Kaya, deputy party chair and former minister of family affairs, asked Turkish President and AKP leader Recep Tayyip Erdogan to "relieve her of all her posts." This has been a phrase frequently used within the AKP for years when someone is forced to resign.

She was facing allegations regarding stock transactions by her and her husband, Ilyas Kaya, in connection with the fund crisis. Political responsibility, she wrote on social media, goes beyond legal responsibility. She said her position should not hinder the investigation.

The opposition now intends to bring the issue before Parliament. Selcuk Ozdag, deputy leader of the Yeni Yol parliamentary group, announced that as soon as Parliament reconvenes, he will submit a motion for a parliamentary investigation. The investigation is intended to determine whether politicians are involved in the manipulation scandal.

#1075. Manchester City Premier League charges: What do they mean?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Since being bought by the UAE in 2008, Manchester City has become one of the world's most successful football clubs. A raft of serious financial charges threatens to undermine this and bring chaos to the Premier League.

Manchester City has won the English Premier League eight times over the last 14 years. Whether those titles will remain is one of a plethora of issues at stake after England's top division charged one of its most high-profile clubs with more than 100 breaches of its financial regulations.

On Tuesday, the Premier League announced that an independent commission had found City had drawn up "sham contracts" as well as "relying on sham agreements to artificially inflate the club's revenues and reduce its costs." These contracts, dubious sponsorships and a number of other methods were, according to the commission's report, used to inflate the club's revenue to the tune of about £900 million (€1.05 billion) over the period between the 2009-10 and 2017-18 seasons.

The case has been ongoing for eight years after initial revelations from Der Spiegel, a German magazine, and Rui Pinto of the 'Football Leaks' website. City continues to deny any wrongdoing.

"The Club is innocent of the accusations made by the Premier League and a comprehensive body of irrefutable evidence exists in support of all of its positions, relating to this case," read a club statement. "Manchester City FC will now pursue the appeal avenues open to it, on the basis that the opinion contains clear material errors, of law, principle and fact, and is unsafe."

On Friday, the club issued a further statement saying it had lodged a "comprehensive Appeal."

The 750-page report was damning, accusing City of inflating contracts and sponsorship deals, running secondary contracts off the books, making "concerted efforts to stop and frustrate the Premier League investigation" and signing off inflated image rights deals to artificially boost their earnings. The report says all this enabled them to pass both Premier League and UEFA financial spending limits.

The Premier League's chief ­executive, Richard Masters, described the case "as the most significant in Premier League history," adding that the club "systematically broke Premier League Rules for nearly a decade."

Although the Premier League, the world's most commercially successful football league, has drawn some praise for its actions, others point out that it took Pinto leaking emails and media reports to take action. The report shows that Manchester City, which had won the league once in 71 years before being taken over by the United Arab Emirates (UAE) in 2008, were consistently posting revenues way higher than any of their more established and successful rivals.

In this context, its the owners. The Abu Dhabi United Group, essentially an investment vehicle for the UAE royal family, bought the club in 2008. Mansour bin Zayed Al Nahyan, know simply as Sheikh Mansour, fronted the takeover. He is a member of the ruling Al Nahyan family and the UAE's vice president and deputy prime minister. Khaldoon Al Mubarak, who also holds several government positions in the UAE, was brought in as chairman.

Nation-state ownership of this type is controversial in European football. Manchester City's owners have long been criticized for the country's human rights record and using the club for sportswashing. Similar criticisms have been made of European champions Paris Saint-Germain, which Qatar owned and City's Premier League rivals Newcastle United, which is owned 85% by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund.

City's CEO, Ferran Soriano, joined in 2012 as City expanded their ownership into foreign leagues, buying up clubs in the US, Japan and Australia. He replaced former head coach Roberto Mancini with fellow Spaniard Manuel Pellegrini before former Bayern Munich and Barcelona coach Pep Guardiola took over in 2016, ushering in the club's most successful period.

The club immediately started splashing out on big-money transfers after the UAE investment arrived. At the time, neither UEFA nor the Premier League had the tight financial rules they now have. UEFA introduced its Financial Fair Play (FFP) rules for the 2011-12 season, while the Premier League followed with its Profit and Sustainability Rules (PSR) in 2013-14.

City's free spending and relatively low commercial income meant they were likely to fall foul of such rules. The club was handed a conditional €60 million UEFA fine for losses in the 2011-12 and 2012-13 seasons. They would later be banned from the Champions League on similar grounds in 2020 before being reinstated on appeal.

In an attempt to try and comply with these financial rules, City set up a system they called "Project Longbow" in 2012. The commission said much of this was genuine, but one arm of it was an agreement with a third party called Fordham that was, according to the ruling, "little more than a front." Essentially, it reads, the club's UAE owners "would be used (and were used) to enable Fordham to acquire from the club (at a sizeable, artificially inflated price) the club's entitlement to benefit financially from its players' image rights."

In other words, City's owners were pumping money into their own club through vastly inflated sponsorship and image rights deals so their income looked higher and they could spend more, particularly on player transfers and wages. The report also claims that City paid three individuals, whose names are redacted, through a third party to keep them off the books.

That's the question on everyone's lips. So far, no sanctions have been imposed on City, and the appeal will likely drag proceedings out even longer. Meanwhile, the club, which is top of the Premier League, faces Liverpool on October 11, after the international break.

While there is no precedent for such a tranche of charges, clubs have previously fallen foul of the Premier League's PSR regulations. Everton were handed a 10-point penalty for a £19.5 million breach over a period ending with the 2021-22 season. This was based on a Premier League tariff of six points for the initial breach, plus an additional point for every £5 million the club overspent. Given that City's overspending is close to a billion pounds, applying that would certainly relegate them, though the Premier League does not operate England's second-tier league, the Championship.

For now, it looks like City and the league will play on under a cloud. But if the verdict remains after appeal, clubs and individuals whose careers and honors stand to be impacted will likely come forward and litigate. City themselves could be relegated, docked points over successive seasons, face transfer bans or any combination of those things. The titles won in the relevant period could also be stripped.
