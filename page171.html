#3416. Is Cycling Good Cardio? Here's How It Compares to Running and Swimming

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Learn how cycling compares to other cardio activities like walking, running, and swimming.

Riding a bike is as good a workout as it is a ton of fun. Hop on a bike—any kind—and your heart rate will pick up, your breathing rate will increase, and you’ll break a sweat. Whether you choose to fly over asphalt on a road bike, cruise back roads on a gravel setup, or push pedals in an indoor studio, you’re doing your mind and body big favors when you hop in the saddle.

One of the biggest payoffs? Cardio benefits. Read on for how cycling boosts your cardio fitness and how it stacks up to other forms of cardiovascular exercise, like running and using an elliptical.

Cardio exercise is any exercise that increases your heart rate and respiration (breathing rate). Cardio exercise can be aerobic or anaerobic. Broadly, what makes cardio aerobic or anaerobic is the intensity level of the exercise (and physiologically, the source of energy for that exercise).

Aerobic exercise, defined by the American College of Sports Medicine, is a rhythmic activity that uses large muscle groups and can be maintained continuously. This is also called steady-state exercise, and it can include running, walking, cycling, rowing, using the elliptical or stair-stepper, swimming, and hiking.

Anaerobic cardio exercise is high-intensity, and true anaerobic activity can only be performed for short periods of time; sprinting is a good example of anaerobic cardio exercise. Activities like jumping rope and mountain biking—and any of the aforementioned activities performed in high-intensity spurts—can also fit into this cardio category.

In any case, “cardio exercise often consists of extended periods of time with an elevated heart rate and an increase in oxygen demand,” says Alexander Rothstein, ACSM-certified exercise physiologist, strength and conditioning coach, and coordinator for the exercise science program at New York Institute of Technology.

“Performing cardio exercise will stress the cardiovascular system, which will signal the need for adaptation and cause improvements in the body’s ability to supply and utilize oxygen,” he explains.

The goal of cardio exercise is to increase the efficiency of your heart, lungs, and circulatory system, thereby improving your overall fitness and endurance.

“Cardiorespiratory fitness reflects the ability of the cardiovascular and respiratory systems to supply oxygen-rich blood to skeletal muscles during sustained physical activity,” says Joe Scarrella, triathlon coach and certified personal trainer at Life Time Chanhassen in Minnesota.

This type of fitness “is vitally important to health and wellness, especially to the ability of engaging in normal activities of daily living without excessive fatigue,” he says. “Because of its heart-strengthening and overall health benefits, cardio training should always be near the top of priorities when allocating time to a fitness program.”

Yes, cycling is cardio exercise—an excellent form of it, in fact. Cycling requires continuous movement that increases your heart rate. When you pedal, your heart works harder to pump blood to your muscles. This steady effort challenges your cardiovascular system, helping it become more efficient over time.

While cycling is a fantastic form of cardio, it’s not your only option. Here’s a look at how it measures up to other popular forms of cardio exercise.

Cycling burns more calories per hour than walking, according to the Compendium of Physical Activity. Cycling at a leisurely pace (less than 10 mph) can burn about 330 calories per hour for a 180-pound person. Walking at a leisurely pace (2.5 mph) burns about 246 calories per hour for a person of the same weight.

Both cycling and walking are low-impact exercises, making them ideal for those with joint pain or arthritis. Cycling allows more room for increasing speed or resistance without changing joint impact, which offers a more intense cardio workout for individuals with joint concerns.

On the flip side, walking is a weight-bearing form of exercise, while cycling is not, so walking could be the better choice for individuals with bone density concerns.

Walking is more accessible because it requires no equipment beyond a good pair of shoes. Cycling, on the other hand, requires much more gear: a bike and helmet, at minimum, plus safe space to ride outdoors. An indoor stationary bike or bike trainer is required for cycling inside, and while there are budget options, it’s still an investment.

If you're looking for a simple, accessible form of low-intensity cardio, walking might be the best option. However, for a more intense workout that’s still easy on the joints, cycling is the way to go.

Running tends to burn more calories than cycling, because it’s more difficult for many individuals to achieve the increase in heart rate that they may get from running, Rothstein says. It may take longer to burn as many calories as one would while running.

According to data from the Compendium of Physical Activity, cycling at a “self-selected moderate pace” burns about 573 calories in one hour for a 180-pound person. That same person may burn about 615 calories “jogging at a self-selected pace,” according to the Compendium.

Running is a high-impact exercise, which can place stress on your joints, especially your knees and ankles. Cycling, in contrast, is low-impact, making it a better option for people with joint issues or those recovering from injuries.

Both running and cycling primarily work your lower body, including your quadriceps, hamstrings, glutes, and calves. During cycling, your core and back muscles may be recruited more to maintain posture on the bike, depending on the style of your bike. When running, your core, back, and arms all work to maintain posture and stability.

If joint health is a concern or you prefer a lower-impact workout, cycling is the better choice. Running is a viable option for people who can handle the joint impact and/or don’t have the means to invest in a bike or stationary bike.

Swimming and cycling burn similar amounts of calories, depending on intensity. According to the Compendium of Physical Activity, swimming freestyle at a vigorous pace for one hour burns about 800 calories for a 180-pound person. The same person would burn about 818 calories cycling at 14 to 16 mph, which may be a moderate or vigorous effort depending on your fitness level.

Swimming has virtually no impact on joints because the water supports your body, making it an ideal option for those with joint pain, injuries, or mobility issues.

Cycling is also low-impact but not quite as easy on the body as swimming. Depending on the type of bike, some individuals may struggle with mobility when cycling. (For example, on a road bike with drop bars, it can strain the neck and back to reach them. In such a case, a bike with an upright frame might be a better choice.)

Swimming is a full-body workout, engaging both your upper and lower body, as well as your core. You’ll recruit more muscles in the arms and shoulders while swimming, compared to cycling, which primarily targets the lower body.

If you want a no-impact, full-body workout, swimming might be your best option. Keep in mind that sticking to a swimming plan requires having access to a lap pool, which isn’t feasible for everyone. Even though a bike can be a big investment, it may be the more accessible option in this case.

Cycling burns more calories than elliptical training at the same effort level. For a 180-pound person, cycling at a moderate pace (12 to 14 mph) burns around 654 calories in 60 minutes, while a moderate 60-minute session on the elliptical burns about 409 calories.

Both are low-impact exercises, making them suitable for people with joint concerns. The elliptical may provide an advantage for people who struggle with mobility, because you remain in an upright position and don’t have to significantly flex the spine like on the bike.

Elliptical machines engage both the upper and lower body, while cycling primarily focuses on your lower body, though the upper body and core are still engaged to some extent while riding a bike.

If you prefer a workout that engages your whole body, the elliptical may be the better choice. However, if you like the idea of getting outside and/or exercising with a community, cycling offers a more dynamic experience in that sense.

Overall, “all forms of cardio are similar in their cardiovascular health and weight loss benefits,” says Rothstein. “The increased oxygen demand and overall cardiovascular stress will result in improved health and well-being. The ability to burn calories is also similar even if the rate might be different between types of cardio.”

Adaptations in stamina are where the most significant differences lie, says Rothstein. “We adapt based on the specific training stimulus, so in cyclists, stamina improvements will mostly be shown in cycling performance. For example, if you train cycling, you will not improve your running unless you also train as a runner. This is because even though both have cardiovascular adaptation benefits, the muscular adaptations will be specific to the type of training.”

In other words: Train what you want to be good at doing.

#3957. Why some people escape Alzheimer’s despite carrying a high-risk gene

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Some people carry one of the strongest genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's disease yet never develop it, remaining mentally sharp well into their 80s. For years, scientists have wondered what protects these individuals despite their increased genetic vulnerability.

Now, researchers at Columbia University have uncovered a biological mechanism that could help explain this remarkable resistance. Their findings point to a rare genetic mutation that protects the brain's blood vessels from damage, potentially opening a new path toward Alzheimer's treatments.

The discovery builds on research the team published two years ago involving APOE ε4, a gene variant strongly associated with Alzheimer's risk. The scientists found that some people carrying APOE ε4 also possess a rare protective mutation in the fibronectin (FN1) gene.

Fibronectin is a protein that helps support the structure of tissues. However, in people with APOE ε4, excessive amounts can accumulate around blood vessels in the brain, potentially compromising a critical protective system known as the blood-brain barrier. The rare FN1 mutation appears to limit this buildup and the damage associated with it.

That earlier discovery suggested an intriguing possibility: What if scientists could develop a treatment that recreates the protection provided by this rare mutation?

A new study published in Nature Aging brings that possibility closer by identifying how excess fibronectin damages the brain's protective barrier and revealing potential ways to interrupt the process.

"This new study takes us from a genetic clue to a disease mechanism," says Caghan Kizil, the study's corresponding author and professor of neurological sciences in the Department of Neurology and the Taub Institute for Research on Alzheimer's Disease and the Aging Brain at Columbia University Vagelos College of Physicians and Surgeons.

"We knew that changes in fibronectin could protect against Alzheimer's, but we didn't know why excess fibronectin was harmful in the first place. This study gives us that detailed mechanism -- and with it, ideas for how to reproduce that natural protection."

How an Alzheimer's Risk Gene Weakens the Blood-Brain Barrier

The blood-brain barrier serves as a protective boundary between the bloodstream and the brain. Made up of tightly connected cells surrounding the brain's blood vessels, it carefully regulates which substances can enter brain tissue while helping maintain conditions necessary for removing potentially harmful waste.

When this barrier becomes damaged or excessively permeable, substances that would normally be kept out can enter the brain more easily. Such disruptions may contribute to inflammation and other processes involved in neurological diseases, including Alzheimer's.

To investigate the role of fibronectin, the Columbia team combined several advanced research approaches. They examined human brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid, the clear liquid surrounding the brain and spinal cord, using genetic and epigenetic analyses to explore changes in DNA and gene activity.

Their investigation also included single-cell sequencing, which reveals the activity of individual cells, and spatial imaging to examine where biological changes occur within tissues. Additional experiments involved human stem-cell models, three-dimensional cultures of blood vessels, and studies in zebrafish and mice.

Despite the variety of methods, the findings consistently pointed to the same mechanism: APOE ε4 promotes the production and accumulation of fibronectin around the brain's blood vessels.

In mice engineered to carry human APOE ε4, researchers measured nearly twice as much fibronectin in the brain as in mice carrying APOE ε3, another common version of the gene. The greater accumulation was accompanied by evidence that the blood-brain barrier had become leaky.

Scientists Discover How Fibronectin Causes Brain Damage

One experiment provided especially important evidence that fibronectin does more than simply accumulate in damaged blood vessels.

The researchers increased human fibronectin specifically in astrocytes, specialized support cells that help maintain the blood-brain barrier and keep the brain functioning properly.

Increasing fibronectin in these cells was enough to disrupt the barrier, allowing it to become more permeable. Conversely, reducing fibronectin improved the barrier's ability to function normally.

These results provided experimental evidence that excess fibronectin can directly contribute to blood-brain barrier damage rather than merely appearing alongside it.

"The animal experiments allowed us to move beyond association," says co-first author Prabesh Bhattarai, an associate research scientist in the Kizil lab. "When we increased fibronectin in astroglia, the barrier became permeable. When we reduced fibronectin, barrier function improved."

The team then investigated the molecular events responsible for the damage.

They found that excessive fibronectin interferes with communication between cells by activating abnormal signals through integrins, proteins that help cells sense and respond to their surroundings, and an enzyme known as focal adhesion kinase.

These abnormal signals disrupt a network of growth factors called VEGF, HBEGF, and IGF1. Although growth factors are often associated with cell development and repair, these particular signaling molecules also help astrocytes and blood vessel cells coordinate their activities to maintain a healthy blood-brain barrier.

When their communication becomes disrupted, the barrier can lose its integrity, potentially leaving brain tissue more vulnerable to damage.

"Single-cell analysis allowed us to examine this pathway in the different cells that maintain the brain's blood vessels," says co-first author Elanur Yilmaz, an associate research scientist in the Kizil lab. "We observed the same biological pattern across experimental models and human Alzheimer's tissue, making the mechanism especially compelling."

Evidence From Human Brains Strengthens the Alzheimer's Connection

Importantly, the researchers found signs of similar biological disturbances in human samples, suggesting that the process identified in laboratory experiments may also contribute to Alzheimer's disease in people carrying APOE ε4.

Analyses of brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid showed that elevated fibronectin levels were associated with inflammation in astrocytes.

Genetic and gene-regulation analyses also connected FN1 to blood vessel disease and changes involving VEGFA, a growth factor that plays an important role in maintaining healthy brain blood vessels.

Together, these findings provide multiple lines of evidence linking excess fibronectin to the vascular damage associated with APOE ε4.

"The convergence of these independent human datasets strengthens the case that fibronectin is part of the APOE ε4-related vascular disease process," says Badri Vardarajan, associate professor of neurological science in the Department of Neurology, Gertrude H. Sergievsky Center, and Taub Institute, who collaborated on this discovery of the FN1 mutation.

A Rare Genetic Defense Could Inspire New Alzheimer's Treatments

The potential implications are significant because APOE ε4 is the strongest common genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease. Approximately one in five people carries at least one copy of this gene variant.

Although carrying APOE ε4 does not guarantee that someone will develop Alzheimer's, it increases their likelihood of developing the disease. Understanding why certain carriers remain unaffected could reveal new opportunities for prevention and treatment.

The latest findings suggest several possible approaches.

One strategy would be to prevent harmful amounts of fibronectin from accumulating around the brain's blood vessels. Another would involve blocking the abnormal cellular signals triggered by excess fibronectin. A third possibility would be to restore the growth factor signaling that allows astrocytes and blood vessel cells to maintain the blood-brain barrier.

Researchers are already investigating these possibilities through laboratory experiments.

However, translating the discovery into a treatment will require considerable care. Fibronectin performs essential functions throughout the body, including supporting tissue structure and helping damaged tissues heal. Any future therapy would therefore need to target its harmful accumulation or effects without disrupting these beneficial activities.

By identifying the molecular mechanisms involved, scientists now have a more precise framework for designing such treatments. The findings may also help researchers determine when interventions would be most effective and how to measure whether they are successfully protecting the brain.

"By showing how fibronectin contributes to early vascular damage, this study gives us new therapeutic targets and potential ways to measure whether treatments are working," says Richard Mayeux, chair of the Department of Neurology and a collaborator on the project. "Clinically, it connects a major genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's to an early disease process that may be possible to modify."

Although these potential therapies remain experimental, the discovery offers a promising new direction in Alzheimer's research. Rather than focusing only on the damage that develops as the disease progresses, scientists may eventually be able to reproduce a natural genetic defense that helps protect vulnerable brains before significant damage occurs.

The marriage was a disaster, the couple finally separates, and then — once they’re no longer sharing a mortgage, a bathroom, and the exhausting daily business of being married —they discover that they actually like each other. Maybe they even have great sex. Real life, of course, is not always so neatly plotted. But there is something psychologically plausible about the experience you describe. Marriage and co-parenting ask people to do different things. You may have been fighting about the dishes, but the argument was rarely only about the dishes. Over time, every small disagreement can become a referendum on the whole relationship.

Now you have a different arrangement. You each have your own home, your own routines, and some degree of control over your own lives. You can be attentive and affectionate during the time you choose to spend together, and then go home. You’re no longer trying to make every part of your lives fit together. It may be easier to be generous with each other when you’re not also negotiating the entire architecture of your family. Also, there’s another possible ingredient: distance can make room for curiosity. When you were married, you knew each other in the exhausting, unvarnished way people know someone they live with. You saw each other sick, irritated, distracted, and overwhelmed. You had to deal with the consequences of each other’s habits and choices every day. Now, even with children in common, you may be encountering each other in smaller, more deliberate doses. Your ex can be a person you’re attracted to again, rather than the person who forgot to sign the permission slip or left you to handle a family crisis alone. You can have a good evening without needing it to prove that your whole life together is working. That doesn’t mean the sex is fake, or that you’re necessarily fooling yourselves. It may be real pleasure, real affection, even a new kind of intimacy. But good sex can mean many things, and it doesn’t automatically mean that the conditions that made your marriage unhappy have changed. You may have become better at being co-parents, or better at being exes, without having become more compatible as spouses. Those are meaningful accomplishments in their own right. You don’t have to turn them into a reunion story.

The question I’d pay attention to isn’t just whether you can keep having sex without fighting. It’s what happens when the situation becomes less convenient or less clear. What if one of you starts dating someone else? What if one of you wants more commitment, or less? What if one of you begins to feel jealous, or hopes the sex means you’re getting back together, while the other sees it as a pleasurable arrangement with no larger implications? And what happens if the old arguments return — not over household chores, perhaps, but over the kids, the schedule, or the meaning of what you’re doing? Because your children are part of this, even if the sex itself is private. You don’t need to share adult details with them, and you certainly don’t need to explain your sex life. But if the renewed relationship starts to look like a reconciliation to them, or if it changes the emotional temperature between their parents, they may notice. Children can be hopeful about their parents getting back together, even when nobody has promised that. They can also feel unsettled if a warm, cooperative co-parenting relationship suddenly becomes tense again. Protecting them doesn’t require you to avoid intimacy with your ex at all costs. It does mean taking care not to let uncertainty between the two of you spill into the family’s routines without talking about it.

All that said I’d suggest having a conversation that may feel less sexy than the sex itself, but is probably more important. #3459. How to Organize an Adaptive Triathlon for Kids (and Why It’ll Change Your Perspective)

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Over 25 children participated in the adaptive Kids Tri for Champions event. (Photo: Courtesy Kevin Webb)

For this story, Triathlete spoke with the two organizers of the Kids Tri for Champions and one parent of a participant to understand what it takes to put on an adaptive triathlon for kids.

This whole swim-bike-run experience is supposed to be fun, but chasing PRs and podium finishes can make that easy to forget. If you’re overdue for a reminder, consider helping with an adaptive triathlon for kids.

An adaptive triathlon is not your typical race, and to find out why, as well as learn what it takes to successfully organize one, we spoke with Erik Wanberg, Mayo Clinic Alix School of Medicine student and the co-founder of Kids Tri for Champions: Adaptive Triathlon, and his co-organizer, Kevin Webb.

In 2024, Wanberg and several other Mayo Clinic medical students founded the first annual Kids Tri for Champions, an adaptive triathlon for children with developmental, intellectual, or physical special needs in grades kindergarten through 12th.

The race was part of the students’ second year of medical school, during which they’re provided with a variety of volunteer opportunities that they can receive school credit for. While they can choose to give back through existing community events and community service, Wanberg and the other students decided to create a new event, bringing their own interest in triathlon to children with disabilities when they realized the sport was inaccessible to them.

Nearly a dozen participants of all ability levels swam, biked, and ran their way to the finish line of the inclusive event at Soldier’s Field Park in Rochester, Minnesota.

“We started this race in an effort to fill an equity gap for children with disabilities in Southern Minnesota,” Wanberg says. “There are excellent resources here for adaptive physical education in our schools and fun after-school activities in our communities, but no real chance for the kids to participate in an actual competition that allows them to have that community race experience.”

Nearly one in five children in Minnesota and 3 million children across the U.S. live with a special health need or impairment. What’s more, physical activity levels are 4.5 times lower for disabled children. And for 1.5 million public primary and secondary school students with a physical disability, athletic competitions remain inaccessible.

“So we set out to create the first adaptive triathlon in this region where kids on the entire spectrum of intellectual and physical disabilities could swim, bike, and run,” Wanberg explains. Rather than emphasize competition, the Kids Tri for Champions focuses on inclusive recreation and self-achievement.

Now in its third year, the triathlon was held on July 11. Webb estimates that over 25 children between the ages of 4 and 18 years old participated.

“Over the past few years, our adaptive triathlon has accommodated children with capabilities ranging from Down syndrome to physical disabilities,” Webb says, “including hearing-impaired athletes and wheelchair users.”

“Adaptive events allow all kids to feel a strong sense of accomplishment,” says Anna Strand, whose daughter Ella is a Kids Tri for Champions’ finisher. “It gives them a chance to connect with friends and to work through a challenge in a way that raises their heart rate and also exposes them to a whole community that is supporting them and cheering them on.”

Inspired to offer an adaptive triathlon for children in your own community? Wanberg and Webb have some invaluable tips for organizing an inclusive event that is fun and safe for kids of all abilities.

1. Do set a date and a rain date, especially if your event will take place during thunderstorm season.

2. Do start planning early. For the Kids Tri for Champions in July, planning usually begins in January or February. Wanberg suggests sending out a Google form/survey that includes a direct question about each participant’s functional/ability level and event needs, including any specific accommodations that they will require during the event, such as an adaptive bike. The form should also include a section where participants can request that an athlete buddy partner with them for the entire race if they don’t want to bring their own. For Kids Tri for Champions, a link to the form is included in a promotional PDF for the event, which sponsors and supporters share on their websites and social media accounts.

3. Do establish a financial support system once you have a sense of how many participants to expect. Webb estimates that the Kids Tri for Champions costs about $400 to $500 annually for finisher medals, shirts, juice, and snacks, most of which is covered by donations from local nonprofits.

4. Do find a pool for the swim and plan your course around that. Kids Tri for Champions books the Soldier’s Field lap pool through Rochester Parks & Recreation, which provides a few lifeguards as well.

5. Do make sure that the course is in a contained environment that is publicly available, legal for the race, and straightforward. Wanberg suggests walking the course you’re considering to ensure there are no bumps, craters, or cracks that might make it hard or unsafe for adaptive bikes or individuals with poor balance.

6. Do put a team of volunteers together. Webb starts by emailing Mayo Clinic medical students.

“We get about 10 medical students, as well as people from the nonprofits We Bike Rochester and EA Therapeutic Health for a total of around 25 volunteers,” Webb says.

7. Do get help borrowing adaptive bikes (which can otherwise be expensive) by building a relationship with a local bike company or non-profit that can help with e-bikes, handcycles, tandems, recumbents, and upright bikes with training wheels. For the Kids Tri for Champions, EA Therapeutic Health brings a trailer of their physical therapy bikes to the race.

8. Do assign a buddy to swim with the athlete and run alongside them while they bike and run if it’s requested in the survey.

“Make sure that a couple of the buddies you recruit can run fast,” Webb advises. “Two years ago, a kid with an electric bike requested a buddy, so I ran with him, but he was doing 5:30 miles! So the next year, I recruited a couple high school cross-country runners just in case we had anyone on a fast bike.”

9. Do be flexible while putting safety first! Some of your participants may not be swimmers, which is why it’s crucial to have lifeguards on duty and floaties available. Have helmets available and insist that every athlete wear one while cycling.

“On race morning, mark the course, put out cones, and position volunteers at key points to keep athletes on the right path, but be ready to let them do their own thing, as long as it’s legal and safe,” Webb says. “One year, we marked the course to go clockwise, but the kid who finished the swim first began biking the other way and that’s what everybody else did. So we went counter-clockwise that day. It’s organized chaos and you have to embrace it.”

1. Don’t charge money. Keep the event free for families to offer a truly accessible triathlon.

2. Don’t limit the event to participants based on their abilities. Tailor the course to children with special needs but realize that part of what makes an adaptive triathlon so memorable for them is the opportunity to participate in an event with their siblings and peers of all abilities.

“This year there were two kids that were just planning on going to the pool that day,” Webb says. “They saw the triathlon and wanted to participate even though they hadn’t signed up, so we let them. That experience exposed them to people who have abilities that are different from their own, which is so cool!”

3. Don’t have a set distance … or order. Start the race in the pool but leave it up to each child to decide how far they feel comfortable swimming, whether that’s one lap or several. When the participant finishes swimming, their buddy can help them transition to the bike.

“In our triathlon, the kids bike on a trail around the pool, and their buddy runs alongside them,” Webb says. “What’s important is that each kid has a buddy with them and can choose for themselves if they want to bike or run a couple laps or turn around.”

4. Don’t underestimate the flexibility needed.

“There will be kids with varying intellectual and physical disabilities,” Wanberg says. “As such, there will be unforeseen obstacles on race day. Be ready to change things on the fly, typically at an individual participant level, if necessary to keep the race safe and fun.”

“We’ve had kids that swim, bike one lap, and jump back in the water instead of running,” Webb says. “Two years ago, I was a buddy for an athlete with Down syndrome who was maybe 10 years old. We were running along the trail and passed a public group doing yoga. He decided that he wanted to join them and the group was cool with it, so we took a yoga break.”

5. Don’t forget how much of an impact an adaptive triathlon can have on the kids – and you!

“Ella ran hard and she was tired, but seeing people cheer for her put a smile on her face,” Strand says. “The most meaningful part was seeing her sense of accomplishment as she finished the race!”

A few years ago, Webb remembers there was a teen at the finish line when he saw his friend starting the run. He just turned around and went with the other kid.

“It’s the opposite of the races I participate in, where it’s all about being competitive,” says Webb, who was part of the University of California, Davis’ Triathlon Club and now competes in trail races. “An adaptive kids triathlon is all about giving these children a sense of belonging in our communities. No matter what their pace or how far they go, I guarantee that they’re having more fun than we do at our races. It’s an awesome reminder to not take being able to run, bike, or swim for granted. It can reframe your perspective on sport.”

One of the biggest myths in all of turkey hunting is that male turkeys respond to calling only in the spring. The fact is, toms gobble all fall, too, and are sometimes more vocal in autumn than in spring. And they can be very responsive to a call.

Just as in the spring, a hunter’s job is to get gobblers excited, because that’s when they make mistakes. And to do that, you have to sound like a gobbler when you call, imitating a newcomer that’s challenging the old boys’ club. It’s a pecking order thing. They come to the calling because they want to see who you are, and because they want to kick your behind.

Unlike in spring, turkeys are driven by food in the fall rather than the need to reproduce. Therefore, scouting food sources is one of the best ways to find fall toms. In early autumn, look for soft-mast foods such as hawthorn, dogwood berries, wild grapes, persimmons and crab apples.

Rounding out the fall smorgasbord are a variety of insects, assorted seeds, grasses, cherries and agricultural crops of clover, beans, small grains and waste grain that’s left on the ground in freshly harvested fields.

Later in the autumn, turkeys feed primarily on hard mast dropped by a variety of oaks and maples, as well as other trees that produce nutlike seeds. However, trees don’t produce these every year, so the trick is to know the foods turkeys prefer and to find which foods are available in your hunting area.

As long as the habitat and food sources don’t change, neither will fall roost locations, and these bedrooms are the best places to begin your fall hunt.

Always set up tight on a roost way before first light and on the same side or in the same direction as the previous evening’s fly-up. Listen for the first turkey to call, and then duplicate whatever the turkeys do, but with more enthusiasm. If turkeys yelp, you yelp; if they cluck, you cluck, but with more feeling. Work them into a frenzy.

When it comes to cutting, putting, purring and other sounds, there is no reliable difference between a hen and a tom; they all do it. When a gobbler gets excited, it will sometimes yelp 30 times in a row and do a lot of high-pitched putting.

One way to tell the difference between hen and gobbler yelps is the rhythm. A hen’s note is relatively faster and shorter in length. When a gobbler is yelping, each note is more drawn out, with a slightly slower rhythm. It can also sound coarser, or “throatier,” than a hen’s.

A quality slate or glass call is one of the best devices to use on fall turkeys. The slate will produce great soft tree talk, purrs and hen yelps, as well as realistic gobbler yelps. My favorite is the mouth call. I can imitate almost any turkey sound with the diaphragm call, including jake kee-kees, gobbler yelps and cutting.

Cartwheeling seems like it should require both arms and legs. But some itty-bitty snakes in Southeast Asia manage it without either.

Scientists first shared snapshots of a dwarf reed snake (Pseudorabdion longiceps) cartwheeling away from danger in 2023. Now, videos of three Pseudorabdion species hightailing it down a racetrack in the lab have offered a closer look at this rare and strangely acrobatic behavior.

Each snake starts with its body curled into a C shape, head lifted off the ground and facing the way it’s going. It then whips its tail out behind it, briefly becoming airborne. The snake flips its tail forward and down past its head. When the snake’s tail hits the ground in front of it, another roll begins, researchers report October 19 in Current Biology.

“What a cool snake behavior,” says Jessica Tingle, a biologist at Brown University in Providence, R.I., not involved in the work. “I find it especially amazing that an elongate animal like a snake can manage to roll, especially given that they do it in such an explosive way, which probably asks a lot of their muscles and bones.”

Yosuke Kojima was equally surprised when a colleague in Sarawak, Malaysia recounted witnessing this “very weird” behavior in 2019. So the researchers collected snakes from Malaysia’s Kubah National Park. They scooped up 11 P. saravacense, one P. collaris, one P. longiceps, plus six Calamaria reed snakes for comparison.

Finding the snakes was no easy task. They were only about 11 to 28 centimeters long. “They always hide under the leaves,” says Kojima, an ecologist at Toho University in Chiba, Japan. But were any of the snakes able to cartwheel away from the researchers? “No,” Kojima laughs. “We are quicker than them.”

The team set up a 2.2-meter-long racetrack and placed the snakes one at a time at one end. A gentle tap coaxed the snakes to move and cameras above and beside the track recorded the action. None of the Calamaria snakes cartwheeled, but all of the Pseudorabdion snakes did. At full tilt, they tumbled at 1.7 to 3.7 meters per second — about six to 13 kilometers per hour.

Through it all, the snakes held their heads fairly steady — reminiscent of how flying snakes hold their heads stable while launching into the air, the researchers say.

A cartwheeling snake first rolls its body along the ground to whip its tail out behind its head. Then, it swings its tail up and over its head. Finally, it brings its tail down on either the left or right side of its head. Once it the tail hits the ground, the snake is ready for another roll. In the cartwheel seen on top, the snake brings its tail down on the left side of its head, angling its whitish belly toward the viewer. In the cartwheel on the bottom, the snake’s tail lands on the right side of its head, showing its darker back to the viewer.

“I am very curious how such a strange and possibly demanding movement evolved … whether dwarf reed snakes have something special in their anatomy, physiology or neural control that lets them do it when their close relatives apparently can’t or don’t,” Tingle says. “Are their brains different in some way to let them coordinate a fast and unusual movement? Or do all snakes have the basic equipment, and they just don’t use it?”

Those may be open mysteries. But catching snakes cartwheeling on camera does tie up one loose end for Tingle.

“Some writers in the 1800s talked about ‘hoop snakes’ and described their motion as rolling, but that seemed pretty fantastical to me, and I chalked it up to overly inventive imaginations,” Tingle says. Those 19th century authors may or may not have been describing the exact behavior Kojima’s team recorded. “Either way, it’s neat to find out a real animal does something reminiscent of a fantastical old story.”
