#2975. Buy The Finished Home, Skip The Remodeling Misery

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

At almost 50, I’m never doing a gut remodel again.

Everyone who has survived one has the same thousand-yard stare. It’s the look of someone who spent two years arguing about grout color and waiting on inspectors who never show up when they say they will.

Remodeling might be the most unpleasant thing you can voluntarily sign up for. Nobody makes you do it. Yet every year, homeowners line up to hand over hundreds of thousands of dollars for the privilege of being miserable.

Cost overruns are common. So are time overruns.

If you’re brave enough to build a house or do a gut remodel, budget for the project to cost at least 50% more and take 50% longer than expected. If you do, you might come out only slightly bloodied and bruised. If you don’t, and you trust your general contractor’s estimate, your silent stress will go through the roof and detonate every relationship around you.

Here’s a great chart compiled by ResiClub from a report by University of Chicago and Princeton professors titled “Where Is It Slow To Build, And Is It Getting Slower?”

I’m not shocked to see San Francisco at #1, averaging 1.1 years to get a permit and 3.8 years to build a single-family home. San Francisco, after all, is the most expensive city to raise a family.

Miami and Philadelphia are close behind at about 3.4 years, while Indianapolis, El Paso, and Colorado Springs get it done in roughly six months. The national average from permit application to completion is about 8.8 months.

So a San Franciscan could watch a family in Indianapolis build seven homes in the time it takes to finish one. And it’s getting worse. Nationally, single-family permitting time roughly tripled from 1.6 months in 2000 to 4.9 months in 2023.

I’ve gone through two gut remodels and a couple of bathroom remodels since first buying San Francisco property in 2003, and every one has been a hellish process.

My last gut remodel started in mid-2019 and wasn’t 100% done until the end of 2022. Granted, COVID delayed completion by about six months. But three years for a project I thought would take one year at most was brutal.

At least I was able to run out the upstairs portion of the house while the ground floor gut remodel languished.

Here’s what most buyers miss. The construction bill is only the visible part of the iceberg.

Let’s say you buy a $1.6 million fixer and budget $400,000 for a gut remodel over 18 months. Reasonable, right?

Now apply reality. The budget runs 50% over, so it’s $600,000. The timeline stretches to 2.5 years. Meanwhile, you need somewhere to live. At $6,500 a month in rent, that’s $195,000. You’re also paying roughly 1.2% in property tax on a house you can’t live in, about $48,000 over 2.5 years.

Add it up and your $1.6 million fixer actually cost you about $2.44 million. And I haven’t even counted the opportunity cost of tying up roughly $850,000 in cash that could have been compounding in stocks or private investments.

Then there’s the cost you can’t put on a spreadsheet. If your kids are five and seven when you start, they’re eight and ten by the time you move in. You don’t get those years back living out of boxes in a rental while texting your GC at 10 pm.

If you’re over 40 and plan to buy in a city where building takes longer than 1-1.5 years, I highly encourage you to buy a fully remodeled home. You’ll save so much time, money, and headache.

At least in San Francisco, the market undervalues fully remodeled homes. Given how painful remodeling is, they should trade at much bigger premiums than they do. I suspect the same is true in most slow-to-build cities. Take a look at this illustrative chart.

In this hypothetical example, ten years ago, buying a fixer and remodeling it yourself may have saved you roughly $250,000 compared to buying a finished home. That was real money for the pain.

Today, the savings have shrunk to about $60,000. Labor costs, material costs, and permitting delays keep eating away at the discount, while finished homes haven’t gotten much pricier relative to fixers.

Spread $60,000 over 2.5 years of construction and you’re earning about $2,000 a month to manage contractors, chase inspectors, and live in a rental. And that assumes nothing else goes wrong. One bad subcontractor or a three-month permit delay and the savings disappear entirely.

How hard could it be, they ask themselves? Get an architect, hire a reputable GC, pay them to pull permits, pick out the fixtures, and you’re good to go.

But unless you’ve gone through a full remodel or ground-up build, you have no idea how hard things are and how long they take.

Do not underestimate how painful remodeling or building is. Your college degree or fancy job title means nothing.

The second reason is more understandable, especially for quirky rich people. Most fully remodeled homes are designed to appeal to the most buyers possible. White oak floors. White quartz countertops. Gray accent walls. Matte black fixtures. Tasteful, safe, and about as exciting as plain oatmeal.

If you want a jade green kitchen, a speakeasy behind a bookshelf, or a Japanese soaking tub with a view of the fog rolling in, no developer is building that for you. You’ll have to do it yourself.

I get it. Your home should reflect who you are. But ask yourself how much you’re willing to pay to express yourself. Paint, lighting, furniture, art, and landscaping can get you 80% of the way to a unique home for a fraction of the cost and time. And the more personalized your remodel, the fewer buyers will want it when it’s time to sell.

Your dream home might be someone else’s gut job.

After all my remodeling experience, I’ll gladly pay a hefty premium for a fully remodeled home and live with someone else’s beige taste. Thankfully, the market hasn’t priced these homes at ridiculous premiums… yet.

Stop being so eclectic and picky. Buy the finished home and then make cosmetic changes if you want.

The noise bullet point is worth a closer look, because it goes both ways.

When you remodel, you’re the villain. Your crew shows up at 8 am with saws and jackhammers, and the retiree next door who just wants a quiet afternoon nap starts plotting against you.

I know because I’m that retiree now. As I wrote in the one negative of living in a nicer neighborhood nobody talks about, five neighbors around me have been doing major remodels in less than a year.

Nicer neighborhoods attract buyers with the money and ambition to rip everything out. So even if you buy a remodeled home, you’re not fully escaping construction. You’re just escaping the bill.

I’ll take that trade any day. Here's a podcast episode I did (Apple, Spotify) talking to my friend Harry on his new home building process.

The silver lining of any remodel is that when it’s finally done, you’ll feel relieved and maybe even thrilled to have survived the gauntlet.

It’s like hitting yourself in the head with a hammer. It feels amazing when you stop.

Even if your GC and subcontractors do everything right, they still have to get through the plumbing, electrical, and building inspectors, each of whom may inspect twice before the work can be covered up. Once you’re through it, you’ll marvel at all the hard work that went into making your home beautiful. Few things are more satisfying after a long journey.

And if you remodeled thoughtfully, by adding livable square footage or staying within a reasonable budget, you’ll likely get back at least 80% of your money. In cities with wealthier buyers or a booming economy, you may get back over 100% of the cost while enjoying the home in the process.

Just don’t forget to count the years it takes off your life.

#2297. Time Bought, Leverage Preserved: Outcomes of the Trump–Xi Summit in Washington

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

From September 23 to 25, 2026, Chinese General Secretary Xi Jinping visited Washington, DC, for a summit with President Donald Trump. A previous Heritage Foundation analysis expected the summit to be limited to tactical agreements rather than transformative breakthroughs.[REF] The outcomes largely matched those expectations. This Issue Brief reviews the Washington Summit’s deliverables, assesses its outcomes, and looks ahead at the U.S.–China relationship’s trajectory.

Outcomes

The Washington Summit marked the second series of in-person meetings between President Trump and General Secretary Xi in 2026. Shortly before the summit began, the United States and China announced that the Busan trade truce, which was reached in 2025 to stabilize tariff rates and the flow of rare earths, was extended from November 10, 2026, to January 10, 2027.[REF]

On September 25, the White House published a fact sheet detailing the summit’s outcomes.[REF] The outcomes include:

  • The United States welcomed Chinese progress on cracking down on fentanyl precursor chemicals while “strongly encouraging” additional actions, including the “permanent scheduling” of morphine and the imposition of “maximum sentences, including the death penalty where applicable,” for 21 Chinese citizens arrested for illicit drug production.
  • The United States welcomed two pandas destined for the Atlanta Zoo.
  • The two countries operationalized the Board of Trade by reaching a consensus for “more favorable tariff treatment for $30 billion of non-sensitive goods in each direction.”
  • China committed to importing “at least 10 million metric tons of coal from the United States in 2027 and again [in] 2028.”
  • The two countries “established” the Board of Investment but provided no additional operational details.
  • The two countries “continue to work on U.S. concerns regarding supply chain shortages related to rare earths and other critical minerals, with the goal of ensuring shipment levels return to appropriate levels.”
  • President Trump will visit China in November for the meeting of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) forum while General Secretary Xi will visit the United States in December for the G20.
  • The leaders “agreed that Iran can never have a nuclear weapon and no country or institution can be allowed to impose tolls on international waterways.”
  • While stating that the United States and China were “allies in World War II and fought side by side to win the war,” the fact sheet immediately follows up by stating that the United States’ “forward military presence has preserved a Free and Open Indo–Pacific contributing to generations of American prosperity.”
  • The leaders also discussed Russia, North Korea, “trilateral arms control,” and “super intelligence.”
  • The two countries “established the U.S.–China Super Intelligence (SI) Dialogue to exchange views on risks and benefits related to SI,” which includes a “bilateral communication channel for SI incidents.”

On September 26, the Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs published its own readout outlining eight outcomes:[REF]

  1. The two countries agreed to build “a constructive China–U.S. relationship of strategic stability on the basis of respect, fairness, and reciprocity.”
  2. The two heads of state intend to attend each other’s hosting of APEC and the G20.
  3. Both leaders agreed that Iran “should honor its commitment to not developing nuclear weapons, and no country or institution can be allowed to impose tolls on international waterways.”
  4. Both leaders “recall that China and the U.S. were allies in World War II and fought side by side to win the war.”
  5. The two countries reached an agreement on reciprocal tariff reductions on $30 billion worth of goods.
  6. The two countries highlighted cooperation on counter-narcotics and law enforcement operations.
  7. Both sides agreed to establish the “China–U.S. AI Dialogue” and “establish a bilateral communication channel for AI [artificial intelligence] incidents.”
  8. The United States welcomed two pandas arriving at the Atlanta Zoo.

Interestingly, the Chinese readout also claimed that the United States and China agreed to “conclude a memorandum of understanding on crisis communication and prevention as soon as possible.”[REF] The United States fact sheet did not include this reference, and a U.S. official has since stated that the claim “is not accurate and was not even discussed.”[REF] One issue not included in the White House fact sheet was addressing wrongfully detained Americans and other political prisoners, such as Min Zin, who was detained shortly after the May summit, and Jimmy Lai. President Trump has since confirmed to reporters that “I did” raise their cases during his meetings with General Secretary Xi.[REF]

Combined, the White House fact sheet and Chinese readout show that the U.S.–China relationship’s “floor” remains steady, but the “ceiling” remains constrained to short-term agreements that do not address the most contentious issues that underlie the bilateral relationship.

Analysis

The Washington Summit produced three main takeaways:

  1. The United States Did Not Concede on Strategic Policy Issues. Ultimately, this is perhaps the best U.S. outcome from the summit. Based on the agreements announced, the United States did not alter its policy positions on Taiwan, export controls, and strategic economic sectors. General Secretary Xi’s most recent attempt to have the United States oppose Taiwanese independence did not succeed.[REF] Unlike the May summit, President Trump has kept to a stricter script when discussing Taiwan policy publicly, only telling reporters that Xi “understands very much how I feel.”[REF] This should reduce some anxieties across the Indo–Pacific, including in Taiwan. Furthermore, through the Board of Trade, China is expected to import “agricultural goods, fish and seafood, logs and wood products, cosmetics, and medical devices,” while the United States will import “primarily consumer products, including appliances, toys, holiday decorations, and car seats.”[REF]

    This list is important because the goods with reduced tariffs are non-sensitive, with few national security implications. The Trump Administration rightly kept export controls “off the table.”[REF] Ultimately, the United States reached agreements that support its economic interests without sacrificing strategically significant policy positions or sectors to get an agreement across the finish line. That is a desirable and defensible outcome.

  2. Time Was Bought, But Structural Issues Are No Closer to Being Resolved. One of the summit’s most important outcomes is the two-month extension of the U.S.–China trade truce, which extends the deadline from November 10, 2026, to January 10, 2027.[REF] Initially, the United States reportedly preferred a six-month extension.[REF] By contrast, some analysts believe that the Chinese side was seeking a longer extension, perhaps up to a two-year extension, to the end of President Trump’s second term.[REF] The Administration was right to prefer a shorter extension because it allows the United States to regularly assess China’s compliance, and the Administration’s messaging suggests China has not been entirely upholding its end of the bargain. At the same time, this extension will not fundamentally alter U.S.–China relations. Both sides will continue to compete while “buying time” to strengthen their advantages and reduce external vulnerabilities. While the truce extension incentivizes compliance, it is unlikely to result in any breakthroughs.

  3. Optics Mattered, But They Were More Complicated Than Perceived. Much attention has been paid to General Secretary Xi’s reception in Washington. On the surface, many have criticized President Trump’s willingness to, literally, roll out the red carpet for the Chinese leader. This summit served as the first state visit for a Chinese leader in more than 10 years and an unusual instance of President Trump personally greeting a visiting foreign leader on the tarmac. A closer review, however, reveals that the optics may have been more mixed than some have perceived.

    As General Secretary Xi was welcomed by President Trump at Joint Base Andrews, two B-1B Lancer bombers flew over both leaders,[REF] sending a very different message than President Trump’s greeting in Beijing by dozens of children waving flags and singing songs. The next day, a B-2 Spirit bomber and four F-22 Raptors flew over the White House as President Trump and Xi participated in a military ceremony.[REF] Then, during the state dinner, performances included selections from Les Misérables, including “Do You Hear the People Sing?” which was censored in China following 2019 pro-democracy protests in Hong Kong.[REF] At the National Archives, President Trump showed General Secretary Xi America’s founding documents.

    A “last minute” change was made, however, to remove a display of Chinese artifacts after Beijing became “irate” over the planned inclusion of the 1951 Treaty of San Francisco, because “the U.S. and Japan signed it, but not China.”[REF] Notably, the treaty “leaves the status of Taiwan undetermined and does not state that the island belongs to Beijing.”[REF] The United States also reportedly considered showcasing the Six Assurances and the Three Communiqués, but the documents were “ultimately not included.”[REF] Overall, while it will not fundamentally alter the trajectory of U.S.–China relations, the summit’s pageantry carried different messages and symbolism designed to demonstrate American strength while potentially pressing into Chinese sensitivities, even as some actions may be perceived as unnecessary.

Looking Ahead

With the trade truce sustained and future meetings expected to occur in only two months, policy analysts should track five primary issues through the start of 2027.

  1. The Trade Truce. While it is possible for the truce to be extended further in 2027, analysts should also consider under which conditions the truce will not be extended. Both sides are emphasizing a “reciprocal” nature to “constructive strategic stability,” with rare earths, fentanyl, Taiwan, and trade already known to be potential flashpoints. Before the summit, the United States believed that China was only “fulfilling two-thirds of [its rare earth commitments].”[REF] Almost immediately after the summit, U.S. Ambassador to China David Perdue warned that “it’s going to be a bumpy road over the next year” as the United States seeks to “establish independence again on rare earth elements, pharmaceuticals, [and] commercial shipbuilding.”[REF] These statements raise the question of whether the United States will determine that extending the truce is no longer in its interest. China could also make the same decision if it believes it could extract U.S. concessions by reimposing significant export controls on rare earths. China could also reassess its position following the U.S. midterm elections. Therefore, while both sides currently have incentives to continue the truce, analysts should not automatically assume this will be the case, especially if evidence grows of Chinese noncompliance.
  2. APEC and the G20. It is possible for the leaders’ APEC and G20 meetings to result in additional deliverables, but if the September summit itself produced few unexpected outcomes, these two meetings will likely have even fewer outcomes. Instead, the purpose of these meetings will likely be to sustain a floor in the relationship, maintain leader-to-leader interactions, serve as a “safety valve” to address any immediate disputes, and lay the groundwork for 2027 interactions, including a potential trade truce extension. One emerging issue, however, could be establishing “reciprocal nuclear site visits” following “US concerns about China’s rapidly growing nuclear weapons program and after the last remaining arms control agreement between the US and Russia expired earlier this year.”[REF] If the reporting is accurate, the discussions demonstrate the issue’s increased importance to the Trump Administration, even if potential visits would provide only limited visibility into China’s broader and rapidly expanding nuclear arsenal.
  3. Taiwan Policy. On one hand, the Trump Administration did not offer any concessions on Taiwan. On the other hand, questions about a stalled $14 billion arms package for Taiwan remain. The additional planned meetings may complicate the timing of the package, potentially pushing action into early 2027, which is another reason why the two-month truce extension matters. To be clear: The Trump Administration approved nearly 40 percent more arms sales to Taiwan in 2025 alone than the Biden Administration did in its full four years.[REF] In fact, no U.S. President has approved more arms sales to Taiwan in American history than President Trump.[REF] Still, it is in America’s security and economic interests to approve the currently stalled arms sale package as soon as reasonably possible because it supports deterrence across the Taiwan Strait and helps to sustain a free and open Indo–Pacific. It is also important to avoid setting the precedent that Taiwan arms packages are negotiable.
  4. AI Policy. Debates within the United States about AI safety, namely about the potential need to “slow down” AI development or “pace the frontier,” are increasingly relevant to the U.S.–China relationship. China engages in widespread distillation attacks on U.S. large language models, such as Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini.[REF] This means that Chinese developers can generate large volumes of outputs from U.S. models and use those outputs to train Chinese models to replicate aspects of the U.S. models’ capabilities.[REF] In other words, China’s AI “advancements” are linked in important, though not exclusive, respects to U.S. AI advancements. The Trump Administration has in recent months grown more vocal in condemning these practices.[REF] Furthermore, as AI-related security incidents continue to occur, including breaking out of digital sandboxes and conducting autonomous hacking,[REF] the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) must tackle a significant challenge: How can China advance AI without potentially compromising the CCP’s grasp on power?[REF] Thus, the establishment of a U.S.–China Super Intelligence Dialogue is a reasonable starting point to address shared interests and concerns about AI developments, but it should be viewed as merely the starting point for bilateral conversations regarding AI. Additionally, as with other issues, a “dialogue” will not solve significant and ongoing policy challenges about AI, and mistrust between the two countries will severely restrict cooperation. To this point, the United States should be wary of Chinese AI commitments and operate under the assumption that China will seek to gain advantages while protecting the CCP’s legitimacy in any cooperative arrangement.
  5. Wrongfully Detained Americans. While it is good news that President Trump raised Min Zin’s case directly with General Secretary Xi, it is unfortunate that no additional breakthrough has yet been announced. It did take multiple weeks following the May summit to secure and announce Pastor Ezra Jin’s release, however, so it is possible that discussions are ongoing through diplomatic channels. More broadly, it is important that “hostage diplomacy” does not become a routine part of China’s playbook as a means of extracting concessions from the United States. If a suitable deal, in accordance with U.S. national security interests, cannot be reached to secure Min Zin and Dr. Youlin Chen, the United States should consider imposing costs in response to Beijing’s unjust actions against American citizens.

Conclusion

The Washington Summit met expectations. While diplomatic interactions will continue, the United States and China remain locked in a state of competition, and meetings can only hide so much of the deep mistrust between them. In a highly competitive relationship, avoiding bad outcomes and limiting the escalation of tensions can be a meaningful success. Even within a narrow operating lane, the Trump Administration’s China policy should continue to prioritize holding China accountable to its commitments, safeguarding U.S. advantages, and putting the American people first.

The way I see most organizations roll out AI feels more like a farce than a strategy. If it were turned into a movie, reviewers would say it was unbelievable. Nobody could be that stupid.

Yet here we are. And what makes it all the more frustrating is that we have been here before.

Back in 2014, I wrote a book for Smashing Magazine called Digital Adaptation, looking at the mistakes being made in how organizations were approaching digital transformation and what they should be doing instead. The similarities between then and now are striking.

So in this post, I want to look back at Digital Adaptation and see how the lessons in that book apply to the AI era and what we can learn from the past to help us navigate the challenges of AI today.

The biggest mistake, both then and now, is approaching each as a technological challenge.

Sure, both digital transformation and AI are technological advances, and yes, some level of technological implementation is required. But that is the tip of a very large iceberg.

Back in the midst of the digital transformation era, IT departments were rolling out CMSs, CRM systems, intranets, and so on. Meanwhile, on the customer-facing side, organizations were rolling out websites, apps, and personalization. But they were putting almost no emphasis on how people were going to use these systems.

Internal systems were hard to use, and customers either didn’t care what was being built or wanted something else entirely.

The same is happening with AI. Senior management are buying into the hype, and IT teams are rolling out Copilot Studio licenses company-wide and considering the job done. Sure, there is a bit of organization-wide generic training, but that’s about it.

Just like before, staff are left wondering what to do with these new systems and feeling under pressure to use them and deliver results.

After all, this time around, it is even worse, because this time, staff fear for their jobs. They have read the same headlines that you have, and no amount of promises from senior management is going to make them feel any better.

Meet “TypeScript in 50 Lessons”, our shiny new guide to TypeScript. With detailed code walkthroughs, hands-on examples and common gotchas. For developers who know enough JavaScript to be dangerous.

I mean, think about it from a staff member’s point of view. AI offers them very little personally, even setting aside the fact that management may be intending to use it to replace them.

The main reason senior management are excited about AI is that it provides additional productivity. People can do more in less time. But that does not benefit staff. They are still required to work 9 to 5, but now they are expected to do more in the same time. They don’t get any time back, and they don’t get paid more, so why would they embrace AI?

One strategy is to help staff identify the parts of their job they hate and how AI can help them do those tasks more quickly and less painfully. People tend to be a lot more receptive to change when it is presented as an opportunity to improve their working lives.

But, that should just be the beginning. Organizations may need to consider providing incentives for staff to adopt AI in terms of career progression, compensation, or shorter working hours.

Also, adopting AI is not a simple task. It involves challenging every working practice they have built up over (in some cases) decades. All of that procedural knowledge has to be rebuilt. That is a lot to ask of anybody in even the best of circumstances, and most employees do not find themselves in the best of circumstances.

Most are overworked and struggling to stay on top of their to-do lists. They don’t have time to sit down, learn a new tool, and figure out how it fits into their workflow.

That doesn’t just take time. It also takes a lot of consideration and experimentation. It is not enough to just send a member of staff on a training course and expect them to be able to use AI to its full potential.

At a minimum, staff need training specific to their roles, such as the AI training I provide to UX teams. But organizations should be going even further.

They need to develop sophisticated training programs that help staff from different departments understand how to use AI in their specific roles. They need ongoing support and the time to experiment with AI to find the best ways to use it.

But formal training will only take you so far. In my experience, the most effective support comes from colleagues, which is why I would start with the enthusiasts rather than the resisters. Every organization has a handful of people who are already experimenting with AI, often without anybody asking them to. Bring those people together, give them the time and space to share what is working for them, and let their enthusiasm spread to everyone else.

I saw this work during the digital transformation era too. In Digital Adaptation, I described an internal conference where staff showed their colleagues what they had built, and it did more for adoption than any memo from the top ever could. People are far more likely to try something when they see a colleague in a similar role using it successfully than when they are told to by senior management.

Unfortunately, experimentation comes with a big downside… mistakes will be made.

Many organizations have, to be frank, a culture of fear where mistakes are seen as a sign of incompetence. However, by its very nature, learning a new tool like AI will involve making mistakes, and this can be a massive barrier to adoption.

I saw this during the era of digital transformation. Senior management would demand digital transformation and were confused when it didn’t happen.

What they didn’t realize was that they were sending mixed messages to staff. Sure, they were asking for digital transformation, but they were still demanding that the same key performance indicators (KPIs) be met, and they still pointed out any mistakes in their annual reviews.

There was no space to fail, as the pressure to deliver was relentless.

This time around, it seems to be even worse. I hear horror stories of staff being reprimanded for using too many tokens just weeks after being told to adopt AI. Or staff being lectured so much about what they should or should not do with AI that they are afraid to try anything. It is safer to just do what they have always done.

Look, I get it. AI cannot be rolled out without some governance in place, but again, this is an area that organizations have typically handled poorly.

In the midst of digital transformation, governance would either be non-existent, or it would be so strict that it would stifle innovation, and unfortunately, I see the same pattern emerging with AI adoption.

Staff need some governance to help them know where they stand. It helps them understand what is expected of them and what they can and cannot do.

But too often organizations have been so strict that it is easier not to do anything at all. Pages of instructions about what people should and should not share with AI create that sense of fear that I mentioned earlier.

Of course, mistakes will get made, but that is the cost of change, and so organizations need to accept that and do what they can to mitigate that in how they roll out the technology.

That said, organizations will need to be careful in how they roll out AI from an IT perspective. Once again, it is easy to lock everything down and stifle the adoption of AI.

Again, this is something I highlighted back in 2014 with digital transformation. Overstretched IT teams would pick a set of technologies and roll them out organization-wide, often with little or no consultation with the individuals who would be using them. Anything outside of that set of technologies had to go through a complex approval process, normally involving a steering committee. The result was a glacial pace of change that was one of the major contributing factors to the failure rate of digital transformation projects.

Today we are seeing the same thing. IT teams are opting into a tool like Copilot and calling it a day, but that often falls massively short.

Take, for example, a UX team I am working with at the moment. Management is pushing hard for AI adoption, and the team is excited about the potential of creating an AI-powered research repository and even AI personas. It sounds like a wonderful case study in AI adoption, with enthusiastic management, an excited team, and innovative ideas.

Unfortunately, the reality is that nothing is happening because Copilot cannot deliver on the team’s vision, and so they are currently bogged down in bureaucracy as they try to persuade the AI steering group that they need to use a different toolset. Understandably, the team’s enthusiasm is waning. Why bother?

And that team is one of the more patient ones. In many organizations, staff are not waiting for the steering group at all. Just as we saw shadow IT during the digital transformation era, with people signing up for Dropbox and Google Docs because the official tools could not do what they needed, we are now seeing shadow AI. When the in-house AI tool will not let people do their jobs, they open a personal ChatGPT or Claude account on their phone or home laptop and get on with it.

That is far more dangerous than the problem the restrictions were meant to prevent. Company data ends up in tools the organization has no control over, with no oversight and no record of what has been shared. In trying to lock everything down, organizations end up pushing the risk somewhere they cannot see it.

The instinctive reaction is to clamp down with stricter policies, blocked websites, and warnings about disciplinary action. But that rarely works, and it drives the behavior further underground. Shadow AI is a symptom, and a useful one, because it shows exactly where the official tools are falling short.

Look, I understand the cost of a messy, out-of-control tech stack. It is not ideal. However, in the early days of adoption, we may have to live with a bit of chaos while people explore the edges of what is possible and work out common use cases.

Organizations need to allow staff to explore different uses of AI and even different AI apps to find what suits their specific needs in their specific roles. They can always start locking things down later as the real organizational needs become clearer.

I admit all of this feels dangerous to some organizations. They want to control the AI adoption process centrally and make sure nothing goes wrong. I understand that. But change comes with a degree of risk, and the risk of moving too slowly is greater than the risk of moving too fast.

However, a bigger danger still is when organizations see this as a technological challenge and overlook the human side of things. AI adoption is not simply about rolling out new technology. Just like digital transformation, it is about people, culture, and change management. The sooner organizations realize the scope of what they are dealing with, the better.

Conventional advice around rest tends to follow predictable narratives. While all of this is good advice, it only helps our minds and bodies feel rested.

I grew into an adult who was uncomfortable with conflict and felt responsible for others' emotions. I suffered from low self-esteem, which didn’t help my plight to stand up for myself against mistreatment, even by people who were supposed to be my friends. Years of keeping my emotions and my opinions stuffed inside stifled my voice and also contributed to my struggles with my mental health. And when I became a mom, it became even more challenging.

“Growing up, there was a lot of ‘take care of other people’s needs before your own.’ I think that led to a lot of people-pleasing, a lot of being treated like a doormat for a while, and a lot of codependency,” says Grimes. “Then as a mom, once I had my kids, they obviously had really strong needs. Many of the women Gbadamosi works with initiate therapy when they hit that burnout point, of not knowing who they are outside of what they do as career women and/or as mothers.

“They did what they were supposed to do, but something feels off, whether they don’t feel like they’re at peace, or like they’re missing something,” she says. “They will come in kind of confused and sometimes feeling like they were misled, or even lied to.”

“It could [also] be increasing empathy. Like, ‘Hey, that person’s upset. Why do you think they’re upset?’” she says, “[Focus on] creating a sense of empathy and understanding without having to fix it. A big part of conflict avoidance is fixing it so that you’re more comfortable.”

Parts of the U.S. are facing an increased risk of flooding. Here’s your no-panic playbook for if it ever happens to you.

As the warm air from a super el Niño makes its way across the North American continent, parts of the U.S. are facing increased risk of flooding in 2026 and 2027, according to Newsweek. The potential for disastrous wind and rain events during the winter is so great that the governor of California had already declared a statewide state of emergency as early as September. Southern states, from California to Florida, face the possibility of heavy rain, making flood response preparations even more important for people in these areas than in previous years.

Of course, you don’t have to wait for torrential rains to be at risk of flooding. A plumbing problem, like a toilet overflow, a burst pipe or a backed-up floor drain, can be just as serious. Whether your house flooded because of a natural disaster or a plumbing problem, timely response is of the essence to avoid mold and structural damage to your house and belongings as well as denials on your insurance claims.  The following checklist — informed by insurance and home restoration experts — will help you get ready.

“Water and electricity are the biggest immediate safety risk after a flood,” says damage remediation expert Sam Hickerson. Water flooding your basement or ground floor may come into contact with wall receptacles or other electrical equipment. If the power is still on, the water may be electrified.

Shut off the power yourself if you have a dry pathway to the main panel. If you have to wade through water to get to the panel, call the power company to have them do it remotely. Stay dry until you know the power is off.

Even after you’ve shut off the power, it’s important to stay out of the water, because it could be contaminated with sewage, animal waste and agricultural runoff. The bacteria, viruses and toxic chemicals these contaminants carry are obvious health hazards.

If the water is deep, there could be all sorts of debris in it that you can’t see, including broken glass and sharp metal. One false step, and you could suffer a cut that would be instantly infected if there are pathogens in the water.

“Many policies have specific windows for reporting flood damage, and delays can complicate a claim,” warns Hickerson. To avoid delays, call the insurance company as soon as it’s practical to do so and file a claim.

You don’t need to provide any details at this point; you’re just getting the claims process started. It will be the adjuster’s job to assess those details at some point in the (hopefully) near future.

Houston Chronicle/Hearst Newspapers/Getty Images

You’ll need photos to document the damage, and foundation repair specialist Shane McClintic recommends taking them before you start the cleanup. “Go around your house and film everything that suffered damage,” advises Gulf Coast flood insurance specialist Brad Spurgeon. “Once you have cleaned up your damage your pictures won’t be as useful to the insurance company.”

In particular, advises restoration expert Vinnie Guaderrama, take a picture of the highest point of water on the wall. “Always photograph the data plates with the model and serial numbers before you remove water heaters, furnaces, washers and dryers. Many people forget to do this, and it can be difficult to find these numbers at a later date.”

Try to have a restoration professional on site quickly. Restoration pros are in short supply during widespread disasters, however, so you might be working by yourself. If so, “work fast,” says McClintic.

According to the EPA, mold probably won’t grow on water-damaged materials if they dry within a 24-48 hour period. Once mold gets a foothold, it’s much harder to eradicate, so time is of the essence.

Because you need to work quickly to prevent mold from getting a foothold, water extraction should begin as soon as you can do it safely. “A wet/dry shop vac works for smaller amounts,” says Hickerson,  “but a sump pump is worth renting for anything beyond a few inches of standing water.”

When you can’t reach a hose into corners and under cabinets, don’t be afraid use a bucket and sponge to get the water out. Any standing water you leave will raise the room humidity and promote mold growth.

When you uncover previously submerged carpet, McClintic recommends either taking it outside or, if you can’t, pulling it back to air it out. “Prop up furniture on wood blocks to stop wicking,” advises Hickerson, “and pull baseboards, not just carpet, in flooded rooms.”

Drywall is a porous material that wicks water upward, and once waterlogged, you need to replace it. To avoid having to replace entire walls, McClintic advises cutting the drywall about a foot above the water line.

If you have valuable documents or photos,  Guaderrama suggests putting them in the freezer (if it’s working) until the house dries out. Strangely enough, the freezer is a low-humidity environment.

Run fans and dehumidifiers and, if it isn’t raining, open doors and windows to get air circulating and dry the space as fast as possible. Hickerson warns that things may look dry when they really aren’t, so he recommends keeping the fans running for at least 48 hours, and perhaps as long as 72 hours.

Once the area is dry, Florida restoration pro Darren Impson advises cleaning and sanitizing with an anti-microbial cleaner. He says you should call a restoration company for this, but it might be hard to schedule one immediately after a disaster.

If you do it yourself, the CDC recommends using dish detergent and water or one cup of bleach per gallon of water. Use bleach only on hard, non-porous surfaces. It doesn’t penetrate porous materials like wood and won’t reach mold embedded in them, so scrub porous surfaces with detergent until the mold is gone.

People often forget to look in HVAC vents, says Hickerson, and if they’re wet enough to support mold, the blower could contaminate the rest of the house as soon as the HVAC system turns on, despite your sanitation efforts.

Uncover any vents that were submerged, ventilate the ducts to dry them out, then sanitize the metal with a bleach solution (metal is non-porous, so bleach is effective). It’s best to wait until you’ve done this before you operate the HVAC system.

We know what to leave out so no one can ascertain where our kids go to school, open a credit card in their name, or guess at their future passwords.

But the magnitude of the risk of sharing at all has skyrocketed in the past year alone, thanks to AI, which can turn a completely innocent school photo of a child into the unthinkable. According to the Internet Watch Foundation, “realistic full-motion AI video content is now commonplace. In 2025, the IWF identified 3,443 AI-generated child sexual abuse videos, an increase of 26,385% compared with the previous year, when just 13 such videos were recorded.” If the AI landscape in 2026 is any indicator, those numbers (and the risk it can happen to our kids) are going to be astronomical by year’s end.

Deepfakes and AI-generated images have also become a common bullying tactic. I can’t imagine trying to survive middle school after some zit-faced twerp generated fake nudes of me based on a completely innocent photo of me from my mom’s Facebook, but it happens now.

Aside from this, there are also less tangible downsides to sharenting, ones that may or may not dissuade a parent from posting. What if your child is embarrassed by the post, now or in the future? What if they don’t want your friends from high school to know they’ve had a stomach bug all week? What if they want to be the ones to tell their grandparents what their Halloween costume was instead of you? Stolen thunder is real when you’re a kid.

We know all of these things, and still we post.

Even after so long not sharing my son online, I still feel the urge to post him. When he earns a medal at soccer, gets an adorable new haircut, snuggles up to the dog on the couch — I want to shout it all from the internet’s rooftops. Look at my kid! Tell me he’s the cutest, the sweetest, the smartest... and by extension, that I’m doing a really good job being his mom.

Research has identified a number of common reasons parents post their kids online: to chronicle life events to remember them later, to “advocate for particular parenting philosophies,” to connect with family members, to gather support from their community, and in some cases, for financial benefit. But “the desire to receive ‘good mother’ approval as an indicator of parenting skills emerges as another sharing motivation,” states a study from all the way back in 2015.

In a 2024 review of 61 studies about sharenting behaviors, researchers identify this need to be viewed as good parents as a main driver behind why we post our kids, even against our better judgment. As modern parenting demands a more intense devotion to every aspect of child-rearing — emotional regulation, developmental milestones, creating core memories — sharing snippets of our lives online has become “both a perpetuator of and a coping mechanism for these pressures,” the authors state. Posting your kid screaming in the backseat for the world to see is, at least in some small part of your brain, about getting your followers to message you in solidarity. “OMG, I’ve been there. You’re doing great, Mama.”

The same article found there is a consistent underestimation of the risks of sharenting among parents, perhaps because we are also weighing the costs of not sharing:

“In relation to children’s privacy, studies suggest that the occurrence of sharenting behavior is not related to parents’ potential knowledge of risks, digital literacy, or competencies... A UK mixed-methods study highlighted a paradox where informed mothers continued to sharent despite privacy concerns, suggesting a complex decision-making process where the psychological need for social interaction may outweigh privacy risks.”

There can be a deep shame for parents who understand all of the risks and still want to post their children (and do). What’s always missing from the conversation about sharenting, and from our own assessments of why we personally do it, is this acknowledgment that we’re doing such intensive parenting with so little support. In a world where your village is spread around the country, sharenting is a way to strengthen connection, to pull your far-flung friends and family into your motherhood journey where you need them. Having someone DM you that you’re killing it, even if it sounds stupid, can be the boon that gets you through bedtime.

Unfortunately, studies also show that while we may feel more connected for sharenting, our children don’t.

A study from 2019 found that when parents shared photos of their children to show their pride, or to archive memories, kids responded more positively. However, when the post was more about “impression management” — trying to shape others’ opinions of their parenting for the better — their children had a more negative view. Overall, “adolescents largely disapproved of sharenting. They mainly considered it as embarrassing and useless,” researchers concluded.

A focus group study from the same year, involving interviews with 46 adolescents ages 12 to 14, found that kids believed “parents should respect a couple of boundaries concerning what types of posts can be shared, how often, and with whom. Moreover, they should ask permission before actually posting about their children.”

Without the external validation of social media, I focus on the actual proof I’m a good mom: when my son says he worried about me all day because I had to get my flu shot, when he spills his guts as we lay in bed and cuddle at night, or when I ask about the rose and thorn of his day and they are “seeing you” and “leaving you,” respectively. These were the only measures of my motherhood that ever really mattered, and they’re the only ones I bother with now.

I may never be rid of the urge to share my son with the world — I’m a pretty big fan of him. But there’s immense comfort in knowing photos of my kid will be very hard to come by for bullies or creeps, and that he has the freedom to define himself as he grows up without having to navigate anyone’s preconceived notions about him because of my posts.

That said, not everyone is as lucky as I am. My family and friends have all been completely on board with our sharenting boundaries. They also all live nearby. We have a village, unlike so many other American parents. I don’t judge or begrudge anyone’s choices around what they do or don’t share of their children online. But if you’ve ever felt an uncomfortable tension around the question of “to post or not to post,” just know you’re nowhere near alone.

The trouble is, when fall arrives and the first frost is on its way, all of those lovely pots? Well, they need a little extra attention if you want them to make it through the winter unscathed. (Something I learned the hard way during my early attempts at gardening; it was devastating).

So, if you've been wondering what to do with your container garden before temperatures drop, these are the three jobs that my husband recommends prioritizing.

When it comes to overwintering your container garden, my husband's first piece of advice is simple enough: figure out which of your plants can handle the cold and which can't.

"If you've got tender plants in containers, don't wait until the first frost is forecast to start thinking about where they're going to spend winter," he says. "Move them somewhere protected while the weather is still mild enough for them to adjust."

Plants in pots are more exposed to cold than plants growing in the ground, because their roots aren't insulated by the surrounding soil. Tropical plants, tender perennials, and many plants that have spent summer outside may need to be moved indoors or into a frost-free greenhouse, conservatory, or garage, depending on their light requirements.

Top tip: Before bringing plants inside, check their leaves, stems, and potting mix for pests. It's also worth moving them gradually into their new environment where possible, rather than taking them straight from a sunny patio into a dark corner indoors.

If you've got containers that need to stay outside, my husband advises you group them together in a sheltered spot. (Well, what he actually said was to treat them like penguins and get them in a huddle, but close enough).

Why? "It means they're less exposed to cold winds, and you can give the pots a little extra protection," he says. "Just make sure you're grouping plants with similar needs, and don't block drainage holes or leave containers sitting in water."

Grouping pots against a sheltered wall can help reduce their exposure to wind and make them easier to protect. It won't make tender plants frost-proof, but it can be a useful measure for hardy plants that are staying outdoors.

Top tip: Try to choose a spot that offers protection without becoming waterlogged, and raise pots slightly on pot feet or bricks if necessary to help excess water drain away. Good drainage is particularly important during winter, when cold and wet compost can put roots under additional stress.

One thing people often forget is that it's not just the foliage you need to think about. "The roots are sitting in a container that's exposed to cold air on all sides, so I always recommend giving them a little extra protection before the first frost arrives," says my husband. "You can wrap the outside of the pot with bubble wrap or hessian, and add a layer of mulch over the surface of the compost to help insulate the roots from temperature fluctuations."

Terracotta pots can be particularly vulnerable to cracking when moisture freezes and expands, so insulating the container can be helpful in exposed conditions. Bubble wrap provides an extra layer of insulation around the pot, while hessian is another option for wrapping containers.

Mulching the surface of the compost adds another layer of protection, helping to reduce temperature fluctuations around the roots. Well-rotted garden compost, manure, bark chips, straw, or shredded leaves can all work well, depending on the plant. "Just be sure to keep the mulch away from stems and crowns, and make sure excess water can drain freely," my husband adds.

Top tip: the important thing to remember is that insulation can help protect roots, but it won't turn a tender plant into a frost-hardy one. If you're growing plants that can't tolerate freezing temperatures, you'll still need to move them somewhere sheltered or frost-free.

It's important to remember that insulation is not a substitute for moving a frost-sensitive plant somewhere safe; the right approach depends on the plant, the container, and how cold your local winters become.

So, before the first frost arrives, check which plants need to move indoors, group the hardier containers in a sheltered spot, and protect exposed pots from the worst of the cold. It doesn't need to be an enormous job, either; honestly, just little time spent preparing now could help protect your plants through winter and leave you with a healthier collection to enjoy when spring comes around.
