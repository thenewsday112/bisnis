#672. NASA’s Artemis I Reveals a Surprising Radiation Mystery Near the Moon

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Radiation near the Moon can pass straight through the body, yet preliminary Artemis I measurements suggest a smaller-than-expected increase in exposure compared with low Earth orbit.

Beyond Earth’s protective magnetic field, astronauts face a constant bombardment of high-energy particles from deep space. But how much radiation would they actually absorb during a journey around the Moon? NASA’s Artemis I mission offered a rare opportunity to find out. Aboard the uncrewed Orion spacecraft, two human-shaped mannequins carried more than 10,000 tiny crystal detectors that recorded radiation exposure throughout their simulated bodies.

Surprisingly, radiation doses near the Moon were only moderately higher than those in low Earth orbit, a smaller increase than researchers might have expected.

Among these detectors were experimental lithium fluoride crystals developed by physicists at the Institute of Nuclear Physics of the Polish Academy of Sciences (IFJ PAN) in Cracow. Flown as part of the Matroshka AstroRad Radiation Experiment (MARE), the crystals captured microscopic traces of cosmic radiation during Orion’s journey in late November and early December 2022. A new study published in Radiation Measurements explains how scientists decoded those traces, revealing new details about radiation exposure in deep space and demonstrating a promising technique for detecting particles that conventional instruments can overlook.

“Low-energy protons, trapped by our planet’s magnetic field within the Van Allen belts, can account for the majority of the radiation dose received by astronauts in low Earth orbit. In the vicinity of the Moon, this substantial contribution disappears. Our measurements from the MARE experiment indicate that, although total radiation doses near the Moon are higher than those in Earth orbit due to the considerable levels of galactic radiation, the difference is not as large as one might intuitively expect,” says Prof. Pawel Bilski of IFJ PAN, a co-author of the paper.

Inside the Artemis I mannequins, known as human phantoms, the recorded radiation dose was essentially uniform. Galactic radiation is more prominent near the Moon, and its particles have such high energies that they can pass through an entire body, depositing only part of their energy along the way. That pattern differed from measurements made during the MATROSHKA experiment aboard the International Space Station more than a decade earlier. There, low-energy protons from Earth’s Van Allen belts contributed part of the dose and lost their energy at different depths inside the phantoms, producing an uneven distribution of radiation exposure.

Both MARE phantoms carried the experimental fluorescent lithium fluoride detectors, some highly enriched in the isotope lithium-7. The Cracow team also supplied nearly 600 crystalline detectors of another type, known as thermoluminescent detectors.

“The fluorescent radiation dose detectors based on lithium fluoride, developed at our institute, are passive in nature. In other words, they work like photographic film: once the crystals have been exposed to radiation, the tracks left by individual particles must be analyzed optically under a microscope,” explains Prof. Bilski.

As particles of ionizing radiation pass through lithium fluoride, they create defects in its crystal lattice, the orderly arrangement of atoms within the material. These defects act as color centers. During laboratory readout, blue light excites the centers, making them emit red fluorescence that can be observed under a microscope. Conventional plastic track detectors require chemical etching before their tracks can be read, but lithium fluoride needs no such treatment. A standard fluorescence microscope allowed the researchers to examine the tracks from the lunar mission quickly and without difficulty.

Extracting quantitative measurements required more complex calibration methods because cosmic radiation bears little resemblance to a controlled laboratory beam. Typical physics experiments expose detectors to particles of the same type, with well-defined energies and a precisely known direction of arrival. Cosmic radiation contains all naturally occurring types of particles and atomic nuclei, spanning a very broad energy range and arriving from every direction.

With tracks running through the crystals at different angles, the microscope brings a segment approximately one micrometer in size into focus, while its camera also captures a blurred glow from the remaining portions. That extra light affects fluorescence intensity, the primary measurement used to assess ionization density, or how densely a particle knocks electrons out of atoms along its path. Track shape also matters. Highly ionizing particles leave continuous, clearly defined lines, while weakly ionizing particles can leave a series of separate dots, further affecting the measured intensity.

“After applying a series of corrections and developing software to appropriately remove the background interfering with the measurements, our results differed by no more than 15% from those obtained using conventional track detectors across the entire overlapping range of energy deposition values. Moreover, we also recorded numerous particle tracks corresponding to energy deposits below the detection threshold of the detectors used to date,” Prof. Bilski emphasizes.

IFJ PAN developed its fluorescent lithium fluoride detectors a decade ago and has continuously improved them since. The crystals used on Artemis I were grown using the Czochralski method, in which a crystal is pulled from molten material. Their dimensions are measured in millimeters rather than centimeters, making them convenient for people to wear. They also resolve tracks more finely than conventional chemically etched plastic detectors, allowing higher radiation doses to be recorded while individual tracks remain distinguishable. Their broad range of detectable particle types and energies enables a better assessment of total radiation exposure.

Lithium fluoride detectors can also record neutron doses, particularly when enriched in lithium-6. Neutrons react with lithium nuclei inside the material, producing secondary charged particles locally. Those particles then interact with the crystal much as incoming ionizing radiation does, leaving a detectable record.

Reference: “Application of LiF fluorescent nuclear track detectors for measurements of cosmic radiation at the lunar orbit within the MARE experiment” by P. Bilski, A. Kilian, M. Sankowska, T. Horwacik and T. Berger, 1 September 2026, Radiation Measurements. DOI: 10.1016/j.radmeas.2026.107806

The research conducted as part of the MARE project was supported by a grant from the National Science Centre, Poland (NCN), under its OPUS program.

#350. United Airlines Says Its Basic Business Class Strategy Is Working, And More Is Coming

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

United Airlines says passengers are buying up to more expensive premium fares at very high rates, and it plans to keep expanding the strategy. I do not doubt that removing benefits from the cheapest tickets can persuade people to spend more, but I remain fully convinced that this represents an annoyingly punitive strategy for passengers who have already paid for first class or business class and will not cultivate “brand loyal” customers.

During United’s October 21 earnings call, Chief Commercial Officer Andrew Nocella offered an enthusiastic assessment of the airline’s tiered fares. Asked about the early results, Nocella said United was pleased with the rollout, would expand it further in the following weeks, and had redesigned its website to sell the options more effectively.

His assessment of customers choosing higher fare tiers was particularly direct:“The buy-up rates are incredibly high.”

Nocella also argued that the choices benefit customers and that United carefully explains what passengers are purchasing. He compared the process to the development of basic economy and indicated that fare segmentation in the other cabins remains in its early stages.

You can read the exchange in the earnings call transcript. Here are his remarks in full context:

“Yeah. I will say that we are really pleased by the launch of the tiered fare products, and we continue to expand them and are doing a bit more in the next few weeks. I think it’s been great for our customers. It provides more options for them and great for the airline as well. We redesigned united.com specifically to sell them effectively, and we’ll continue to refine our approach on that I think has even more upside.

“The buy-up rates are incredibly high. Consumers know what they’re buying, and we disclose it very carefully. This has worked for years. It started with basic economy a little more than 10 years ago. We’ve learned a lot of lessons on how to do this, and I just think it’s been really monumental for us, more so than people realize, and we’re glad to pave the way on this.

“It was a very good idea, and we look forward to refining it. If you recall, the early days of basic economy took a lot of refinements, and I think we’re just at the early days of this phenomena in the other cabins as well.”

United did not disclose a specific buy-up percentage or quantify the additional revenue attributable to these fares during that answer. Nor did Nocella identify precisely which additional markets or restrictions would follow, though you can expect most domestic, transborder, and other shorthaul international fares to soon also carry these restrictions.

As I previously covered, United is extending its Base fare structure beyond Polaris business class and Premium Plus to domestic first class and eligible shorthaul international business class.

The seat and onboard service remain the same: the differences concern benefits surrounding the flight. United’s published United First fare comparison lists advance seat selection for a fee and one checked bag with Base, while Standard includes seat selection. Base also restricts voluntary itinerary changes, which is what grouses me.

I understand why people would pay more to avoid those restrictions and I will certainly do the same on many trips.

Someone buying first class has already agreed to pay more for a better experience. A couple who wants to choose seats together or a business traveler who needs flexibility now faces another decision about whether to spend 20-30% more within the cabin. It’s a “genius” move to dramatically raise fares.

Look, there is a reasonable argument for a genuinely cheaper restricted fare. A traveler with firm plans, no checked bag, and no seat preference might welcome it. I am not suggesting every passenger needs the same ticket. What I would like to quantify with data is whether comparable trips with the same benefits have become less expensive, stayed the same, or become more expensive since United introduced the tiers…and I can tell you the anecdotal answer, at least with the domestic premium transcend and international longhaul fares I have tracked: there is no discount in price…the cheapest ticket now just comes with much less than before.

Those fares certainly benefit United, but there is absolutely no benefit to any customer with these more restricted fares. These fares will not build loyalty.

I never doubted United could make money doing this. My concern has always been what it does to the experience of buying a premium ticket. First class should be a relatively straightforward purchase. Instead, passengers must now examine another comparison chart to determine which ordinary ticket benefits accompany their more expensive seat.

I do accept Nocella’s point that clear disclosure matters. Passengers should know what they are buying, and United should explain the restrictions prominently, which it does. But explaining an unattractive restriction clearly does not make it attractive.

Of course I’m not asking United to make every premium ticket fully refundable. Discounted, nonrefundable business class fares have existed for years. There is a perfectly sensible distinction between preserving a ticket’s value for future travel and getting all your money back.

My objection is to making reasonable flexibility another upsell after the passenger has already chosen the premium cabin…I go back again to this implicit promise during the pandemic that change fees would be gone forever. And that the introduction of basic fares that do not allow any modification to the ticket are simply a re-introduction of change fees (arguably even worse than before) under a different name.

Remember what Kirby said in 2020 in stating that change fees were the “number one thing” customers complained about:

“Following previous tough times, airlines made difficult decisions to survive, sometimes at the expense of customer service. United Airlines won’t be following that same playbook as we come out of this crisis. Instead, we’re taking a completely different approach – and looking at new ways to serve our customers better.”

United may have immense wiggle room to do what it wants on a legal basis, but United received $7.7 billion in bailouts during the pandemic…I think for that it has a moral obligation to keep tickets flexible.

Asking me to pay more to modify a first class ticket, when it costs the airline nothing, feels like moving in the opposite direction.

The immediate revenue may justify the strategy to United. Whether it makes passengers feel better about buying a premium ticket is another question, and I would not confuse the two…it makes me more determined to be a free agent.

Berentson told AdExchanger that Stagwell is not acquiring the publishers in Freestar’s network, just the tech they use to monetize and the existing client relationships. Stagwell will retain all of Freestar’s employees and its executive leadership team.

The Freestar acquisition is the latest in a series of moves by Stagwell, which launched 11 years ago and has evolved rapidly because it “doesn’t have this enormous legacy holding us back,” Berentson said.

Just this year, Stagwell rolled out its own curation platform, Stagwell Curate; reorganized and rebranded its global media business as Stagwell Media; and brought The Media Machine to market.

Berentson said the Freestar deal is a continuation of Stagwell’s mission to serve advertisers while delivering more working media spend to publishers.

Freestar’s partnerships with premium news publishers, including the Associated Press, Reuters, Fortune and The Boston Globe, should help Stagwell advance its Future of News initiative to direct bigger media budgets to news orgs, Berentson said. With Freestar, Stagwell can curate higher-fidelity audiences across these publisher properties than it could before, Berentson said. It can also use the Freestar network to add audience extension for wider media buys and to improve its own principal media buying strategy.

Stagwell also sees the Freestar acquisition as a way to shore up its own managed services strategy.

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Publishers have long wished agencies would put their money where their panel talking points are. Stagwell has done something adjacent, buying Freestar, the company that helps the likes of Fortune and Boston Globe Media sell their inventory.

The independent holdco announced the deal today (Oct. 26) and is putting Freestar inside Owned Media, a unit that has picked up pace since Stagwell gave it a CEO in February. Ink, the travel publisher, and airport network ReachTV, are among the handful of properties there that will now lean on Freestar to run their programmatic sales. Freestar’s own publisher clients come out of it with something too, namely a shorter path to the advertisers and tools Stagwell has across its network.

“Publishers are essential to a healthy media ecosystem, and we believe that their long-term success is critical to the future of our industry,” said Ben Berentson, CEO of Owned Media, Stagwell. “Freestar has built an exceptional global business helping publishers increase revenue and improve operational efficiency in a complex programmatic landscape. Together, we’ll continue investing in that mission, while bringing new capabilities to support their growth and their clients.”

Freestar is the latest piece in a supply-side buildout that it has been working on all year. In July, it launched Stagwell Curate, an in-house marketplace to package publisher inventory before it ever reaches the bid. Stagwell’s AI agents plug straight into it. That same month, CEO Mark Penn told analysts more owned media acquisitions were coming. The pressure to make them is easy to spot, since Media and Commerce, the segment Owned Media sits in, grew 1% organically in the second quarter. Owning the company that runs ad sales for dozens of publishers gives Stagwell a far more direct line into their inventory. It also raises questions about how Stagwell will keep its buying and selling interests apart.

“Principal is central to holdco rev generation,” said Ciaran O’Kane, general partner at venture capital fund FirstPartyCapital. “If holding companies are carving out a meaningful share of open internet spend for principal-based deals, it makes sense for them either to own the infrastructure or to lock in strategic partners to run it. What should raise alarm bells is an agency that routes all its media through an execution layer except its principal media. Outside of principal media buying clients will need to know why that spend is running through its O&O stack. Transparency will be even more top of mind.”

Concerns aside, the logic behind the deal is hard for any holdco to ignore. The big networks have spent the past couple of years building their own orchestration and agentic buying systems, betting that AI agents will handle a large share of programmatic spend by the end of the decade and wanting those agents to run on infrastructure they own. Meanwhile ad tech keeps collapsing in on itself. The Trade Desk build Open Path to buy direct from publishers sans supply-side platforms (SSPs), and those some vendors now let buyers skip the likes of The Trade Desk altogether, Each shortcut is a scrap over who keeps the fee in the middle and the holdcos have learned from principal media just how much that fee is worth. Owning the company that already runs ad sales for hundreds of publishers put Stagwell at the supply end of the chain without having to negotiate its way there.

“Freestar has always been focused on helping publishers unlock more value from their businesses,” said Kurt Donnell, CEO of Freestar. “The best outcomes happen when advertisers know exactly what they’re buying, and publishers are rewarded for the quality they deliver. Stagwell understands that. Joining them lets us offer publishers new capabilities, while staying true to the publisher-first approach that has always been our north star.”

Working with ad tech companies like this — the ones that help publishers monetize their inventory — has become table stakes for holdcos. The aim is to decide for themselves which publishers’ ad slots get bundled up and sold to clients, a job that has traditionally belonged to SSPs and companies like Freestar. Steering budget toward publishers where they’ve already negotiated preferred rates gives holdcos leverage at the table and a proof point for clients. And once an agency knows what a publisher actually gets paid, it becomes much harder for anyone in the middle to tuck away a hidden fee. Every piece of that work brought in-house leaves a bit more of the programmatic margin with the holdco.

“It’s logical for agencies to own the stack,” said O’Kane. “When you live and die by margin, paying sizeable opaque ad tech fees to a third party simply doesn’t add up. That’s why the row between The Trade Desk and Publicis became such a public spectacle: it exposed how much value sits in the tech layer. Going forward, ad tech fees will become a genuine USP, and agencies that can offer cleaner, leaner economics will be in a stronger position.  Economics drives this industry, and decisions on M&A and partnerships will be heavily predicated on it.”

Cleaner economics for agencies won’t necessarily mean better ones for publishers, though. Freestar’s clients now have an ad sales partner owned by one of the companies on the side of the negotiating table, and they’ll want to see the new arrangement pay off before they take Stagwell’s word for it.

As one commercial exec at a global publisher put it to Digiday on condition of anonymity:  “I don’t see a generic publisher benefit, particularly for the largest publishers, but I can see the strategic rationale. It gives Stagwell direct access to some quality publisher environments and can offer its advertisers curated access to that audience data and inventory.”

Ultimately, what matters is who controls the publishers’ data and their relationships with advertisers.

Alessandro De Zanche, founder of media consultancy ADZ Strategies, put a finer point on it: In my ideal view of digital advertising, premium media companies would own and operate their alliance and platform where you can exclusively and directly buy that premium inventory, rather than in dozens if not hundreds of different environments. Failing that, it is only natural that the buy side will want to control it themselves.”

It says a lot about the conflicting business models of the different parts of the digital advertising ecosystem, De Zanche continued and how little is understood of the fact that it is the dynamics and the relationships that must change, before technology is implemented.

#1197. <i><em>Wicker</em></i> Is Hornier, Happier Than Its Short-Story Counterpart

Here’s how the new film compares to the 2008 fable, “The Wicker Husband,” by Ursula Wills-Jones

It’s a simple premise, really: A fisherwoman, deemed ugly by her village of judgy idiots, saves up enough coin to ask the local magical basket weaver to make her a husband. A month later, he delivers a willow-woven hunk, wholly and completely devoted to her needs. Physically. Mentally. Emotionally.

The short story “The Wicker Husband” was first published in 2008 in an online journal by Ursula Wills-Jones and is now a horny feature film starring Olivia Colman as the fisherwoman and Alexander Skarsgård as the Wicker Husband.

Before you see the film, you might want to read the short story for comparison. Tor Books recently picked it up for traditional publishing in the United States. It’s currently available as an e-book with a background introduction by the author and as an audiobook under the same title as the film. It will also be released in hardcover in April 2027, bound with a collection of Wills-Jones’s other short stories.

So how do the two differ? What’s remarkable about the one-hour-and-45-minute film is how closely it follows the short story, which clocks in at 26 pages in e-book form. Much stays the same: the village people, the plot, and the fairy-tale-esque lack of logical explanation for a wicker husband’s existence. The overarching themes of jealousy, societal and cultural disruption, otherness, and authentic love remain.

The film builds on the story’s foundation with strokes of humor and eroticism. There is no mention of explicit sex in the original story, but the film’s marketing has leaned heavily on the sex appeal of a Wife Guy vaguely shaped like Skarsgård, woven from willow. It’s not enough onscreen sex for some fans, though, who have already taken to TikTok to complain about the lack of “full-frontal wick.” The sex scenes are wrapped in comedy here, from the rickety bed breaking underneath them to the sound of wicker creaking as it expands and hardens. Wicker is a step in the right direction for fans of monster romance and sentient-object romance, but it won’t satisfy the horniest among them.

The film strays furthest from the story in how it fleshes out tertiary characters like the Tailor’s Wife, the Baker Woman, and the Innkeeper’s Wife. It paints a picture of the bleak reality of many traditional marriages in a patriarchal society—one where women are expected to live a life devoid of pleasure and partnership, physical and otherwise. As you get to know the townspeople better, their growing jealousy of the fisherwoman and her husband feels earned. Who wouldn’t become jealous? Who wouldn’t turn to desperation? As the viewer, aren’t you jealous too?

Spoiler warning ahead. When the wicker wife is delivered in the short story, the Butcher’s Wife tussles with her on the ground after catching her kissing (though really mauling) her husband. At the end of the short story, the village men, enraged by the basket maker’s deception, gather their pitchforks, knives, and shears to hunt him—an angry mob, out for blood, only to discover the basket maker, fisherwoman, and her wicker family have vanished. In the film, the Butcher’s Wife sees her husband being mauled and simply … lets it happen. Point, women. The villagers do too, slowly turning their backs on the bloody scene before them. It ends the same way, though this unlikely family isn’t on the run. They leave on their own terms. It’s a justified, happier ending for women and magical wicker people everywhere.
