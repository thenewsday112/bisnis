Live Science spoke with comparative psychologist Christian Frings to discuss a new theory for why orcas are attacking yachts.

Over the past six years, orcas have made headlines for a unique behavior: Some individuals have repeatedly damaged or destroyed rudders on boats. The behavior has prompted a wide range of explanations, including that it's playful, a form of revenge, a warning against getting too close to the pod, or even a type of cultural transmission, in which one orca observes another interacting with a boat and then imitates it.

But Christian Frings, a cognitive psychologist who studies how humans control their actions, thinks it could be deliberate.

While Frings, who is also chair of general psychology and methodology at Trier University in Germany, does not study orcas directly, he believes that theories developed to explain human behavior could provide insight into this orca behavior. Orcas (Orcinus orca), he said, may be a "test case" for whether a wide variety of species can anticipate how their actions will change the environment and then take those actions deliberately, with the goal of making those changes.

He published his theory as a commentary Sept. 8 in the journal Animal Cognition.

This doesn't necessarily mean orcas have wicked motives or are ramming boats with the goal of sinking them and scaring or harming people. It may be that orcas just like the sensation of smashing into a rudder or being in the water with a wonky, disabled boat. Once they learn this, they anticipate the effect — for example, how a boat moves or feels when the rudder is damaged — and then carry out the learned action sequence that creates that effect.

Frings' theory doesn't attempt to explain why orcas (also called killer whales) would seek to damage a boat or rudder. "Why they want this effect is a secondary question and while of course interesting it touches a quite different topic," Frings told Live Science via email.

Christian Frings is a cognitive psychology professor at the University of Trier in Germany and the director of the Institute for Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience. His research explores how the human mind turns perception into action and why our recent experiences can shape what we do next, often without us noticing.

Live Science spoke with Frings about his hypothesis and why theories that describe how humans execute a series of motor behaviors — called action control frameworks — might help us understand how other complex animals behave in the wild.

Kenna Hughes-Castleberry: Why is it important to study orca behavior, and what can studying orcas tell us about animal cognition?

Christian Frings: In truth, I'm the wrong person to get that question because I should mention right from the start that I'm a cognitive psychologist. So usually, I'm studying human behavior. So, it's not really that I'm interested in orcas, per se, more in the question of what is actually comparable between human action control, human cognition and animal cognition.

KHC: Can you explain the action-control framework for our readers?

CF: It's a group of theories. The most important point is that agents — humans and maybe orcas — control their actions by actually anticipating the behavioral and the sensory consequences of their actions.

So let's take the example of turning the light on. The action starts with anticipating how it will look when the light is turned on. It does not start with pressing the light switch. It starts with the visual effect you're going to produce. This visual effect then actually retrieves from memory a motor program, and this motor program actually produces the anticipated effect. So it's all about anticipating action effects.

In the human action-control literature, that is the most dominant view from different theories of how humans actually control their behavior: by anticipating the effects they're going to produce.

KHC: How does that apply to the orca behavior?

CF: The typical explanation for [the boat-attacking behavior] is more stimulus response or cultural transmission. Stimulus response would mean, in this case, the rudder elicits an approach or attack behavior. So any rudder should just elicit this behavior. [Editor's note: Stimulus-response theory is a psychological concept in which a stimulus triggers a particular response from an individual. Examples include a cat being sprayed with water when on the couch and jumping off the couch in response, or a dog salivating when it smells food.]

Cultural transmission would actually be observational learning. That means one orca sees how another orca is attacking a rudder and then just imitates the behavior.

I'm not saying that these explanations [stimulus response or cultural transmission] are wrong, by the way. I just want to add an additional layer of explanation.

I suggest that they actually anticipate the sensory consequences of their action. So maybe they anticipate how it would look and how it would feel if they destroy the rudder. I would not call it [their behavior] play, but maybe they're just testing or probing what kind of effect they're going to produce.

It's a little bit like human babies, when they start to move — or move their arms or move their legs — they're also typically trying to produce effects, right? It's also when they develop language, this kind of babbling, right? That is a kind of testing or probing action effects.

Maybe this is something the orcas also do. I don't know. But at least it's [another theory].

Whale-watching tours help the public see orca behavior from a close-but-safe distance.

KHC: So would the orca be anticipating something like the boat becoming impossible to steer?

CF: Actually, no. Let's assume for a moment it [that a broken rudder is] what they want to produce — because they somehow anticipate how it would look and how it would feel if this yacht cannot be steered anymore.

If they can anticipate, then this anticipation of this effect will retrieve from their memory what they have to do to actually produce this effect. And it's not about [higher-order] thinking. That is a very important thing.

So we do not need human-like intentions to impose that on the orcas. I'm not saying that, because I really think it's, in a way, comparable with turning a light on. When you turn your light on, you also do not have big thoughts about that. You just want to turn your light on. So you want to produce an effect, and you do not think about all the different, quite complex muscle movements you have to do if you actually press the light switch.

I would say, again, it does not really need higher-order cognition or something like that. We do not have to impose motives or complex thoughts on the orcas for that.

My point is just to look at this behavior from this somewhat different angle, adding this layer about action effects.

KHC: What are the bigger implications if your theory is correct?

CF: My point is just to look at this behavior from this somewhat different angle, adding this layer about action effects. Because if you, for a moment, take my ideas for granted and say, "OK, maybe he has a point," then this opens up the possibility to manipulate the orca behavior.

Because if the orcas anticipate effects and this action effect produces a certain behavior, if you change the effect — let's say the rudder [material] or the shell of the boat so that the orca thinks he's producing a certain action effect — then he shows his behavior, but this behavior does not produce the action effect anymore. So the orca will probably change the behavior. It might be dangerous because it might elicit somewhat different — maybe even more dangerous — behavior of the orcas, but it might also stop it.

But it gives you a natural angle to manipulate the orca behavior. [Editor's note: In a follow-up email, Frings noted that this could involve testing a different version of the boat rudder. If the original rudder, when damaged, leaves the boat floating in a "helpless" way, a second one could be tested that allows the boat to be maneuverable even when attacked. If Frings' hypothesis is right, then by breaking the link between the orcas' planned actions (ramming the boat) and the outcome they want (helpless boat), you could change the orcas' behavior.]

But again, if you try to change it this way, it might be a little bit dangerous. So I would not suggest doing that with a boat with real people on it, because if you change the environmental consequences, they [the orcas] might actually go a little bit crazy. Maybe they might try to produce this effect with a different kind of behavior, and that could be even more aggressive. But I don't know. I can't predict that.

KHC: So the orcas are a case study to test this framework, right?

CF: Exactly. And for the general approach, it's important to try to find action frameworks that can travel across species that are not specific for one particular species. Because at the moment, we might have action-control models for humans or action-control models for the great apes.

But maybe we can just have a family of action-control frameworks, because maybe orcas and humans, and at least the great apes, control their behavior concerning action effects in quite the same way, to be honest.

Another important point here that makes it, for me personally, very interesting: This is behavior in the wild. So a lot of the research on action control is typically in the lab under very controlled conditions, so in a way, this is moving from humans to orcas but also from lab into wild behavior.

I think one misconception is actually that the models from the lab are not applicable to behavior in the wild. That's not only true for orcas; it's also true for humans. And I don't like that.

And again, this paper is a nice showcase that lab models can actually be used to explain behavior in the real world.

Fire starters make it easy for anyone to start a roaring fire. Rather than having to wrestle with damp wood or crumpled-up newspapers to get the first flames going, users can employ a fire starter to achieve this with a single match or the flick of a lighter. The catch is that these outdoor living aids aren’t all created equal.

Some fire starters stand out for their ability to help ignite a fire rapidly and effortlessly, while others are difficult to light or won’t stay lit long enough to ignite a wood fire. We wanted to know which ones were worth buying, so we tested them in our own fireplaces, wood-burning stoves, and fire pits, eventually choosing the Superior Trading Co. Our main goal was to determine which fire starters performed the best in their relative categories. We didn’t test matches or lighters, but we did use them to light the accelerant fire starters. Sidewinder and worm drive refer to motor alignment and position on a circular saw. A sidewinder model’s motor is installed in line with the blade, enabling it to run at high speeds in a compact footprint.

A worm-drive circular saw has a motor in the rear of the saw and uses a set of worm gears (so-called because of their spiraling worm shape) by the blade. Worm-drive saws, which are usually longer, larger, and heavier than sidewinders, tend to reduce speed but increase torque (force). For speed, size, and ease of maintenance, a sidewinder model is the way to go. For power and torque, worm-drive saws reign supreme.

Amperage is the amount of electrical power a motor can withstand without its inner components failing. In the past, electric motors were weaker and not as capable, so amperage (amp) was an important specification to tout. Nearly all modern corded circular saws feature 15-amp motors.

A saw’s speed, however, can be a consideration since the faster the blade spins, the quicker it can cut through a material. But speeds tend to be relative because a 15-amp motor can spin a 7¼-inch blade faster than a 10¼-inch blade. Generally speaking, when it comes to 7¼-inch saws, speeds between 4,000 and 5,500 revolutions per minute (RPM) are common and should be adequate for fast, accurate results. Worm drives may offer slightly lower speeds but come with more torque (spinning force), so while they may cut more slowly, they won’t overheat or bog down during big cutting jobs.

Keep in mind that blade speed has very little to do with the density of material a saw can cut. This is largely dependent on the blade type and quality, and you may need to change a circular saw blade to a higher-quality version to cut denser substances.

Dianjunus rex’s mushrooms can be half a meter tall and weigh over two kilograms

Dianjunus rex (shown) grows in southwestern China and its yellowish, pillar and tentacle-shaped fruiting bodies may be the largest known among sac fungi.

A pineapple-sized fungus has quietly grown undetected in the forests of southwestern China – until now. The massive mushroom may set a size record for a major branch of the fungal family tree.

The fungus — named Dianjunus rex — may also represent a previously unknown family, researchers report September 28 in Frontiers in Fungal Biology. Fittingly, D. rex is part of a lineage that split off from other fungi right after the dinosaurs — like T. rex — went extinct.

We summarize the week's scientific breakthroughs every Thursday.

In 2024, researchers in China were conducting a survey of fungal diversity in a tropical rainforest in Yunnan Province when local farmers tipped them off to an unusual fungus in the area. The farmers included photos, says Indunil Chinthani Senanayake, a mycologist at the Kunming Institute of Botany in China. Some of the farmers who’d made the discovery took a bite of the mystery fungus, Senanayake says, and the tipster “wanted to confirm whether it was edible or not.”When Senanayake and her colleagues went to the location, they found strange, yellowish, tentacle-like and spatula-like mushrooms growing out of decaying tree roots. These were some kind of ascomycete, or “sac fungi”. Sac fungi like morels, truffles and molds make their spores in microscopic bladderlike receptacles, unlike the bowling pin-shaped reproductive structures in club fungi, the group that includes most culinary mushrooms.The mushrooms we see growing out of the ground are called fruiting bodies and make the spore-producing cells for the often hidden or subterranean fungus. Most sac fungi have relatively small fruiting bodies. But the mushrooms growing off these mysterious Yunnan fungi were enormous, with some reaching 50 centimeters tall. The team took samples of the fungi back to the lab for further study and found that they weighed up to 2.2 kilograms. By their estimates, these are the largest spore-making fruiting bodies among all sac fungi.

Torda Varga — a mycologist at the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew in Richmond, England who was not involved with the research — notes that some truffle species are known to get close to the bulk. D. rex appears to be the largest sac fungal fruiting body he’s aware of. “What I find particularly remarkable is that a fungus with such an enormous fruiting body appears to have remained scientifically undocumented until now,” says Varga, though he adds his experience has mostly been within Europe.

The researchers compared samples of the fungus’s DNA with those from other fungi to determine its evolutionary relationships. They found that it was a relative of species like candlesnuff fungus and dead-man’s-fingers, which make fruiting bodies only a few centimeters tall, but that it was probably in its own, unique family. The team gave the new species the name Dianjunus rex, referencing both the Latin “rex” for “king” and the ancient Dian kingdom of the region.

The team’s genetic analysis suggests D. rex’s family first evolved in the aftermath of the mass extinction at the end of the Cretaceous Period that wiped out the dinosaurs, some 65 million years ago. “During the dark, cold, postimpact years after the mass extinction, fungi that decompose dead organic matter could keep functioning better than light-dependent photosynthesizers,” Senanayake says.

Varga wonders if searching databases for bits of DNA shed into the environment could help uncover other members of D. rex’s royal family in the absence of physical specimens.

“Some of these [genetic] sequences might belong to previously undocumented relatives of Dianjunus rex,” he says. Finding these hidden species could tell researchers more about the biology and evolutionary history of this king of the sac fungi.

#4403. How solid-state transformers could help power the AI boom

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

New technology could help cut power losses and reinvent a staple of the grid.

Transformers are a crucial but nearly invisible part of our grid. The devices help efficiently move power across long distances, but they’re complicated and in short supply. And given that their basic design hasn’t changed much for about a century, they could use an upgrade.

As the world races to meet rising electricity demand from EVs, data centers, and more, researchers and companies are looking to modernize transformers. New, solid-state versions could help relieve some of the supply concerns and make sure the grid can handle the growing pressure.

Conventional transformers generally rely on huge amounts of specialized steel alloys and miles of copper wire to do their work of transferring electricity between circuits. Solid-state models, in comparison, combine magnetic components with high-power semiconductors, making them more compact and allowing for more control over the flow of electricity.

These devices are still in development, and can’t replace all traditional transformers today, especially the largest ones that work at the high voltages necessary for long-distance transmission. But a new generation of transformer technology could be a crucial part of our future grid.

Moving electricity through the grid isn’t perfectly efficient: As electricity flows through power lines, some gets lost as heat. How much varies—the higher the voltage, the lower the current, and the less electricity gets lost. So a key function for the grid is stepping voltage up and down.

Consider, for example, your local solar plant. For that electricity to efficiently travel to your home hundreds of miles away, it needs to reach a higher voltage than what’s generated by the solar plant. Enter transformers.

In its path from the power plant to your outlets, electricity might go through one transformer at the solar farm and another at a substation nearby, stepping up the voltage twice to reach a high enough level to travel on high-voltage transmission lines. Solar panels also produce direct current (DC) electricity, which needs to be converted to alternating current (AC) via an inverter before it flows into a transformer. Closer to its destination, the voltage will be stepped down again, probably at least twice: once at another substation and again at a smaller transformer in your neighborhood.

If you’re in the US, electricity from a solar plant might be converted to AC by an inverter at around 480 volts, get stepped up to 115,000 volts for the transmission lines, and finally get stepped back down to 120 volts and 240 volts for your home.

Conventional transformers do this work using what essentially amount to loops of wire. The core of a transformer is typically made of a special type of steel called grain-oriented electrical steel. Coils of copper wire sit on either end of that core.

The number of loops in each coil determines the voltage on either end of the transformer: If there are more loops on the secondary coil than the primary, the voltage steps up, and if there are fewer, it steps down. When incoming electricity passes through loops on the upstream end of the transformer, that forms a magnetic field in the core. That field induces a voltage in the loops on the other side of the device, which is what allows the energy to continue flowing at a new voltage. This process is called magnetic induction.

Transformers form the backbone of electricity transmission and distribution, says Srdjan Lukic, a professor of electrical and computer engineering at North Carolina State University. In addition to doing the job of stepping up and down voltage, transformers also often provide what’s called galvanic isolation, meaning there’s no direct electrical path flowing through them. That can help limit disturbances from moving through the grid, improving safety.

The problem is that today’s transformers are big and bulky, and they’re also difficult and expensive to replace, Lukic says.

Solid-state transformers essentially add so-called power electronics—semiconductors that convert and control electricity to the typical blueprint of today’s transformers.

While a conventional transformer operates at the frequency of the part of the grid it’s connected to, a solid-state transformer actually controls the frequency of the current. Typically, a solid-state transformer first converts incoming AC power from the grid to DC power, and then to a higher-frequency AC power using switches made of a semiconductor like silicon carbide.

From there, a magnetic component changes the voltage. Because the advanced semiconductors allow transformers to exert so much control over the frequency and stability of the current, the magnetic core can be much smaller than in a conventional transformer. Some come in at roughly one-tenth the size but have the same capacity, Lukic says.

Finally, additional power electronics convert the electricity from high-frequency AC to its required form—generally back to DC power and finally to lower-frequency AC.

Today, stepping voltage up and down is many transformers’ only job. But solid-state transformers can do much more. They absorb the jobs of devices like rectifiers and inverters (which convert AC to DC power and vice versa), shrinking the total footprint of the electrical equipment and potentially cutting the amount of power lost in the system.

In August, the New York Power Authority and the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) tested a one-megawatt solid-state transformer under realistic utility conditions at EPRI’s test site. The device was built in Lukic’s lab, and it was the first such test for a transformer of that capacity, he says.

The prototype was roughly 98% efficient—meaning it lost only 2% of the energy fed in. An equivalent conventional transformer and rectifier, by comparison, would be about 96% efficient together, Lukic says. A difference of 2% might seem small, but it is significant, since it means the solid-state transformer could cut power losses from conventional equipment in half, Lukic says.

One major goal of the project was to help utilities gain more confidence in these new components, Lukic says. That’s crucial, since these devices could be used on the power grid in the future. In the near term, solid-state transformers could be used within data centers to provide electricity for server racks.

The prototype is still a relatively low-voltage system, operating at about 15,000 volts. Industrial facilities like larger data centers often require higher-voltage connections, and transmission lines typically operate at hundreds of thousands of volts. Adapting solid-state transformers to these higher voltages will require further engineering development. But the test was a successful demonstration that solid-state transformers can work under real conditions, Lukic says.

Companies developing solid-state transformers are largely focused on data centers right now, Lukic says. There’s an influx of money to support developing the new technology, and the voltage requirements aren’t as high as they will be for larger transformers that will be needed for long-distance transmission.

In February, Heron Power raised $140 million to commercialize its solid-state transformer, which the company calls the Heron Link. The technology could be used in data centers, solar power arrays, and grid batteries, says Drew Baglino, the founder and CEO. The company is starting to test its technology at larger scales now, Baglino says. Ten megawatt-hours’ worth of batteries at Heron’s headquarters in Scotts Valley, California, will soon undergo testing with Heron transformers. The company is also planning a pilot with a nearby solar farm, Baglino says.

One challenge for solid-state transformers is that their power electronics have more failure modes than conventional transformers. In a traditional device, a failure means that the entire device will typically be taken offline, resulting in power interruptions. But Heron designs with that in mind, Baglino says: The company uses a modular design, with many small devices that work together. If one fails, the rest of the system keeps working until the affected bit can be replaced, which doesn’t require shutting the whole thing down.

Heron Power is currently building a $100 million factory in California to make advanced transformers. The facility should begin production in 2027 and reach its full production rate of 40 gigawatts’ worth of transformers per year in 2029.

In recent years, companies that make grid equipment have put $1.7 billion into the US supply chain for power transformers, which are used in high-voltage transmission, says Benjamin Boucher, a supply chain analyst at Wood Mackenzie. There’s been an additional $1.3 billion invested for distribution transformers, the smaller devices that help transform power for homes and businesses, he adds.

But even this investment isn’t keeping pace with the rapid growth of the grid. Wait times for large conventional transformers—the kind needed to connect new power plants, long-distance transmission, and high-demand sites like data centers—are stretching as long as five years.

“Transformers have become one of the biggest bottlenecks for utilities, generation developers, and even data centers,” Boucher says. The big challenge is that it’s difficult to get new transformer manufacturing up and running, because it’s a quite technical process that requires a lot of specialized labor. So even facilities announced years ago are still years from opening, Boucher says.

Solid-state transformers could help ease some of that pressure. But high-voltage devices needed for power plant connections and transmission lines are the holy grail for solid-state transformers, Lukic says. Existing solid-state technology can’t be used for those applications yet, so the industry will need to develop new materials and designs to support high voltages and handle electricity safely in a compact device.

“We’re facing a big risk related to grid infrastructure,” Boucher says. “Without the transformers, you don’t really have an electrical grid.”

InfoQ Homepage News AWS Shows Use of AI Agents to Triage Linux Kernel CVEs

Oct 27, 2026 4 min read

Engineers from Amazon Web Services (AWS) have described how the team that maintains the Linux kernel for EC2 infrastructure handles kernel CVEs. The process combines an automated filter, an AI agent and human review. The team also argues that it needs to move to newer long-term-support (LTS) kernels, as older branches collect fixes that are never backported.

Dylan Johnson and Justinien Bouron gave the talk at the Linux Plumbers Conference in Prague earlier this month. At the start of the recording, Johnson explains that the team maintains the kernel used within EC2 infrastructure. This is separate from Amazon Linux and from the kernels that customers run in their virtual machines.

After the kernel project became a CVE Numbering Authority in February 2024, the team saw about two kernel CVEs a week before 2024 and about 16 a day afterwards, with 848 published on 15 August 2026. The kernel CVE documentation states that the project cannot know how a given system uses the kernel, so users must decide which CVEs apply to them.

The first stage of AWS's process does not use AI. Bouron explained that kernel CVE records are published in JSON and list the affected versions and source files. A script discards a CVE if the kernel version is outside the affected range, or if none of the listed files are built into the image. In AWS's environment this removes about 70% of CVEs, although Bouron said the figure depends heavily on kernel configuration.

The remaining CVEs go to an AI agent. It receives AWS's kernel source tree, the configuration for each platform, a description of the environment and threat model, and a severity rubric. The agent produces a table that records the affected subsystem, the vulnerability type and the attack vector. Bouron said the team ignores CVSS scores, because they are not specific to its environment.

Two engineers usually review the table. According to the slides, the agent needs about 3.4 seconds per CVE, compared with minutes for a person. Bouron said review is now the slowest step in the process, and that the team does not intend to remove it.

When asked about accuracy, Bouron estimated that between 5% and 10% of the agent's decisions were problematic. This was an informal estimate, and the talk did not describe a formal evaluation.

The team also disabled kernel features it did not need, to reduce the number of applicable CVEs. Bouron called this approach "deceptively difficult". The slides list more than 32,000 configuration options, and removing the wrong one can cause bugs or performance regressions that appear months later.

The team's data also shows that older LTS branches receive fixes later and carry more unfixed CVEs. For CVEs affecting all six branches studied, the median time from a mainline fix to an LTS release was 31.8 days for Linux 5.10 and 18.1 days for 6.18. On 16 September, 1,709 known CVEs were unfixed in 5.10, compared with 229 in 6.18. These are counts for each branch, and they do not show how many affect AWS's own kernel.

Backporting fixes in-house brings its own risks. Bouron pointed out that CVE records often name only the final fixing commit. A team that misses the prerequisite commits may still be vulnerable after applying the listed fix.

AWS has shortened its own upgrades. Johnson said the move from 4.19 to 5.10 took about two years, measured from the start of porting work to full production deployment. The next upgrade took about a year, and the move to 6.18 took about six months. During the 5.10-to-6.12 upgrade, 43% of tracked issues were fixed in userspace, where workloads depended on older kernel behaviour. Johnson said the team will now run stable kernels in testing through the year, and move the fleet when each new LTS release appears.

Microsoft's Azure Linux team described a similar pipeline at the same conference. Its triage verdicts come from a rule table, and AI only suggests inputs to it. The team uses agents for backporting, and reported that a build-and-test feedback loop raised its success rate from about 50% to 80%. Google DeepMind's CodeMender, which InfoQ covered in October 2025, focuses on writing fixes. Its announcement stated that human researchers review every patch before it is submitted upstream.

Berend de Boer, CTO of Process Focus, commented on LinkedIn about the work involved in running cloud infrastructure. He listed host patching and Linux CVEs among the work that organisations take on when they build their own cloud. "Focus on what makes your business valuable to your customers," he wrote.

AWS has not released the tools. Asked about open-sourcing them, Johnson said the team would like to explore sharing the filtering tool. He also acknowledged that AWS's internal users make it easier to disable features than it would be for a general-purpose distribution.

Time poverty and overconfidence do the damage. Researching an investment takes hours you don’t have, so you trust people, and you assume that mastering the coagulation cascade somehow qualifies you to underwrite private equity. It doesn’t.

Physicians I know have poured money into restaurant groups that folded within 18 months, real estate “opportunities” that smelled faintly of Ponzi, and crypto tokens launched by a nephew’s roommate’s brother.

Index funds are boring. They work. Put $50,000 a year into low-cost index funds for 30 years at a 5% real return and you end up with about $3.32 million.

Lose 2% a year to fees and the same contributions grow to about $2.38 million, close to a million dollars handed to people in nicer suits than yours. If your portfolio needs a 40-page binder to explain, you’re the mark.

Most physicians blow past the taxable wage base ($184,500 in 2026) months before December, which puts you among the few groups who routinely qualify for the maximum benefit. Claiming age is a bigger lever for you than for almost anyone.

According to AARP, a worker at the 2026 maximum who files at 62 collects about $2,969 a month. Wait until full retirement age and that rises to $4,152. Hold out to 70 and it’s roughly $5,181.

Dr. Lucky filed at 62 because he “wanted the money while the government still had it.” Six months later he picked up locums at $200 an hour, and because Social Security withholds $1 for every $2 you earn above $24,480 before full retirement age, his own paycheck ate his benefit, which the SSA hands back after full retirement age in small monthly crumbs.

If you’re married, the higher earner’s claiming age also sets the survivor benefit, so delaying to 70 works like inflation-adjusted life insurance for your spouse at a premium no insurer will match. A serious diagnosis can justify claiming early.

For a healthy 62-year-old with a seven-figure portfolio, it’s usually the wrong call. You’ve delivered enough prognoses to know which patient you are.

Most physicians stuff nearly everything into pre-tax accounts during peak years, which makes sense at 37%. Trouble arrives when that’s the only move for 25 years, because a $4 million pre-tax portfolio is a smaller number dressed up for the party.

If you need $180,000 a year to live and pull it entirely from pre-tax accounts, you might withdraw $240,000 to get there. Your 4.5% withdrawal plan just became a 6% plan.

Required minimum distributions start at 73 (75 if you were born in 1960 or later) and stack on top of Social Security. Then Medicare notices.

IRMAA surcharges for 2026 begin at modified AGI of $218,000 for married couples, and at the top tier Part B jumps from $202.90 a month to $689.90, plus a $91 Part D surcharge, roughly $6,900 a year per person, a fine levied for the crime of saving diligently in the wrong bucket.

Medicare looks back two years, so the income you report at 63 sets your premiums at 65.

But if you retire at 58, delay Social Security, and you may get a decade of taxable income lower than anything since residency, a window for converting chunks of pre-tax money to Roth on purpose, in measured doses, the way you’d titrate a drip rather than push a bolus.

While you’re still working, fund a backdoor Roth IRA ($7,500 limit in 2026) and send part of your savings to the Roth side of your employer plan. If you’re 50 or older and earned more than $150,000 in FICA wages last year, the IRS already decided for you: your 2026 catch-up contributions go in as Roth.

Asset location matters too, and municipal bonds can help outside retirement accounts.

At 37% plus the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax, close to 41 cents of every taxable bond interest dollar goes to Washington. Keep bonds inside the 401(k) or 403(b). Hold broad stock index funds in taxable.

Two physicians can retire with identical portfolios and identical withdrawal rates and still get completely different endings. Timing decides which. Finance calls it sequence of returns risk, and you’ll call it something less printable.

Retire in February 2020 and you watched the Dow fall from about 29,550 to under 19,000 in roughly six weeks. Retire into 2022 and the S&P 500 dropped about 19% as inflation spiked.

Even 2025 had teeth: the S&P 500 fell almost 19% between its February high and early April as tariff headlines rattled markets.

Dr. Smart retires into three good years and coasts. Dr. Lucky (whose name keeps aging badly) retires into a bear market, sells near the bottom to fund two years of travel, and never gets those shares back.

William Bengen’s 4% rule, published in 1994, tries to account for that, and Bengen has since nudged his own number up to about 4.7%. The real mistake is treating any figure as carved on a tombstone.

Retirees who trim spending after bad years, or who keep two to five years of withdrawals in cash and short-term bonds, give their stocks room to recover instead of selling them on the way down.

Dr. Addams checked her allocation at 59 and found 95% stocks, 5% cash, and no plan for a crash. She moved to 60/30/10 stocks, bonds, and cash and started building a three-year buffer. Her mix won’t fit everyone, but “I’ll figure it out when it happens” fits nobody.

Don’t overcorrect into a bond basement either. Retire at 57 and your money may need to last 35 years, so stocks still have work to do.

CPI peaked at 9.1% in June 2022. Inflation cooled. Prices didn’t.

A $200,000 retirement budget becomes about $361,000 in 20 years at 3% inflation, and any income stream that doesn’t adjust, whether a fixed annuity, a pension without a cost-of-living rider, or a practice buyout paid in level installments, shrinks every year you hold it, rotting quietly from the inside the way pumpkins do.

You already know this feeling from the reimbursement side. The AMA estimates Medicare physician pay has fallen by roughly a third since 2001 after adjusting for practice-cost inflation.

Give your retirement plan the same respect for erosion you’ve watched eat your fee schedule.

Your brother needs $50,000 for a business. Your sister needs $30,000 to stop a foreclosure.

A few years later your kid asks for $80,000 toward a house, because you’re the rich doctor, and the rich doctor is who people call, and nobody in the history of families has ever called the rich doctor to pay him back.

Treat money to family as a gift with no strings, sized to what you can afford to never see again. Dr. Addams lent her brother $120,000 for a restaurant. It failed. He filed for bankruptcy, and they haven’t spoken in six years.

Long-term rental losses usually land in the passive bucket, where they can’t touch clinical income. Short-term rentals play by different rules.

If the average guest stay runs seven days or less and you materially participate, most commonly by logging more than 100 hours a year and more than any other individual, the losses turn non-passive and can offset W-2 or 1099 income (a separate route from Real Estate Professional Status), the closest thing the tax code offers to a garlic necklace for your paycheck.

Pair that with a cost segregation study and 100% bonus depreciation, which the One Big Beautiful Bill Act permanently restored for property acquired after January 19, 2025.

A $500,000 short-term rental might produce $150,000 to $200,000 of year-one depreciation, worth roughly $55,000 to $74,000 in federal tax at 37%.

Locums, telehealth, medical director stipends, expert witness work: if any of it lands on a 1099, a Solo 401(k) usually beats the SEP-IRA most contractors open by default. For 2026 you can defer up to $24,500 as the “employee” (shared with any W-2 plan you also use), plus an employer contribution of roughly 20% of net self-employment earnings, up to $72,000 combined, with $8,000 in catch-up at 50 or $11,250 between 60 and 63. The SEP also poisons your backdoor Roth through the pro-rata rule.

Older 1099 physicians with steady income should price out a cash balance plan on top, where contributions often reach six figures for doctors in their 50s. Those deductions pull double duty because of the QBI cliff.

For 2026, married couples get the full 20% qualified business income deduction at or below $403,500 of taxable income, and it disappears at $553,500. A married 1099 doc with $500,000 of taxable income who funds both plans can slide below $403,500 and watch the deduction rise from the dead.

Medicine breeds a specific condition. You hit your number, decide you need a bigger one, and a market dip talks you into another year, then another, until you’re 68 with $9 million and a partner gently asking whether you’ve considered slowing down.

Every year worked past enough gets paid for with a year you’ll never get back at that age, a year of knees that still handle a trail and a back that still lifts a grandkid, traded for a margin of safety your spreadsheet stopped needing long ago, which makes one more year the only prescription in medicine with no ceiling dose.

Physicians who bolt without a plan for Monday get haunted by the opposite problem. You were Dr. Johnson, the cardiologist. Now you’re Bob, with 50 empty hours a week and no idea what to put in them.

Easing out tends to go better than slamming the door. Dropping to 0.6 FTE, picking up locums, or teaching keeps income flowing and lets the portfolio grow untouched a few more years.

Watch for a malpractice tail bill on a claims-made policy, and remember that governmental 457(b) plans carry no 10% penalty once you separate from service, at any age.

In my experience, physicians who retire toward something stick the landing far more often than those simply running from the pager.

Dr. Addams caught her mistakes at 58 instead of 68, and that gave her room to work. She hired a fee-only fiduciary advisor, wrote a plan, surrendered the bad policies, sold the vacation home, and maxed every account she had.

She retired at 64 on about $18,000 a month instead of $35,000. Plenty, it turned out.

Several year-end moves for 2026 can’t wait for the week between Christmas and New Year’s:

For the rest of the year, check every beneficiary form, add up 12 months of actual spending, swap whole life for adequate term and disability coverage where it makes sense, and bring your spouse into the conversation. Then raise your savings rate 1 to 2 points every year until you hit 25% to 30% of gross.

You’ll make mistakes. Everyone does. What matters is catching them at 58, the way Dr. Addams did, while the options are still standing in the room, instead of at 70, when most of them have left the building and switched off the porch light behind them.
