Pennsylvania’s potential new state record archery elk, a massive 8×8 typical bull out of Centre County, has come under scrutiny after two of the guides on the hunt face trespassing citations. OL reported on the hunt for this bull, which happened September 15. The citations were issued October 7 after a property owner said the hunting party didn’t have permission to be on his land.

“At this point, charges are only going to be filed on the guides,” Harold Malehorn, Pennsylvania Game Commission northcentral region law enforcement supervisor, told Go Erie. “[As hunters], we’re expecting [guides] to take us places where we have permission to hunt. The hunter was not aware that they didn’t have permission to be there.”

The state agency sent a warden to talk with the successful hunter, Mike Skvarla, October 16. Skvarla harvested the estimated 900-pound bull with his crossbow on his 40th birthday. Out of 50,000 applicants in this year’s lottery, he’s one of the 15 who drew. It’s a once-in-a-lifetime hunt so Skvarla hired a guide and a caller.

“It was a once in a lifetime opportunity,” Skvarla told Outdoor Life in September. “I’ve been bowhunting deer for 20 years, but this was an elk – not a whitetail. Because I don’t know much about them, I decided to hire an outfitter for my hunt, and I’m sure glad I did.”

Guide Mike Conaway and caller Abner Druckenmiller, both of Trophy Rack Lodge, helped Skvarla during his hunt. Skvarla’s sister-in-law was also on the two-day hunt. Skvarla was not cited, but he was surprised when a warden showed up at his house with questions and a camera to shoot photos of the rack.

“We didn’t sneak onto the property or anything,” Skvarla said. “The game warden assured me. I’m not getting charged. I can keep the elk. I’m in the clear as far as the Game Commission is concerned.”

Conaway and Druckenmiller have both pleaded not guilty to charges of trespass, according to a statement issued by their lodge. They go to court November 19.

The potentially new state-record antlers are still in their 60-day drying period. Skvarla estimates a 428-inch green score and a 395-inch net score. The current Pennsylvania typical archery record is 381 1/8 inches harvested in 2021 by Christopher Sebulsky, according to the Pennsylvania Big Game Record System. The largest typical elk taken by any method in the state, according to the B&C books, is a 415 2/8 bull taken in 2022 in Clinton County. Pennsylvania Game Commission scorer Bob D’Angelo says the bureau has yet to decide how to handle the new entry. Regardless, the bull is clearly one of the biggest ever taken in the state.

“I’ve seen a lot of elk in my work as a researcher for Penn State,” Skvarla told OL. “But the size of this bull was just incredible. It took all four of us to load it into a pickup truck.”

About 1,400 elk live across 3,000 square acres in northcentral Pennsylvania, including Centre County where the potential new record comes from. The 2021 record comes from the same county.

Rocky Mountain Elk Foundation started partnering with Pennsylvania in 1991 to restore the state’s elk population. Since then, RMEF and partners have completed nearly 600 projects in Pennsylvania with a combined value of more than $33.7 million.

#4025. What does an Old Master smell like? Meet the perfumer commissioned by Sotheby's

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Lyn Harris qualified as Britain’s first classically trained female perfumer in the 1990s Courtesy Perfumer H

Art of Luxury magazine, published twice per year by The Art Newspaper, explores how grande marque fashion, jewellery, travel and lifestyle interact with artists, the art market and the museums and heritage sector.

“It’s about the earth, because everything comes from the earth,” says Lyn Harris, describing Clay, the latest scent from her fragrance house Perfumer H. The perfume was launched late last month, but the idea emerged over a year earlier during a visit to a wood kiln in a bamboo forest on the outskirts of Jingdezhen, the city that has been home to China’s porcelain industry for 1,700 years. For Harris, the smells—and the memories they triggered—evoked a heady mix: juniper, birch tar, musk, burnt ash, minerals, malt; the sweetness and warmth of charred cedar and sandalwood; and the effervescence of orange flower and mandarin. The resulting scent captures the universality of the ground beneath our feet, as well as its relationship with everything around it—the nature embedded in it and the light that bounces off it. “It’s an interesting fusion—quite young, it has a gentle sweetness and depth, as well as a twist,” Harris says.

Most of us would struggle to describe a scent in even two words, let alone the dozens that make up Harris’s baked clay accord. But as a classically trained “nose” (and the first British woman to achieve this), Harris is among the rare few who can break down a smell into its multiple constituent parts, describe it and reconstruct it—not just materially but also emotionally. It has led to a 30-year career, in which she has built a brand that creates perfumes, incense, candles and cosmetics, as well as teas, jams and oils, that sell in stores all over the world.

Perfumer H’s flagship store in London’s Marylebone Photo: Tom Fallon Photography

Of all the luxuries, scent is the most evanescent. Ephemeral and invisible, its success depends heavily on how it is delivered, something Coco Chanel knew only too well when she developed the disarmingly modern Chanel No.5 in its minimalist bottle. Harris communicates to those with less gifted noses by working with a range of collaborators to craft a sensory experience through other media.

The glass bottles that contain the 100ml of eau de parfum are hand-blown in Michael Ruh’s London glass-blowing studio (and cost £650). She is accompanied on all her travels by João de Sousa, a Portuguese film-maker who provides unique visual stories. Studio Frith, the chosen graphic designer of everyone from Artangel to Valentino, creates the clean packaging, while the Retrouvius studio, which specialises in clever reclamation, has designed the interior of her flagship store in London’s Marylebone. Together, these tell a story that words alone cannot.

In 2022, Harris was invited by Sotheby’s to develop three scented candles around an important auction of 17th-century still-lifes and landscapes by Dutch Old Masters—like perfume, priceless in their emotional impact. “What I found particularly clever was Lyn’s ability to draw out subtle aspects of a painting through scent,” says Alex Bell, chairman of Sotheby’s UK and Old Masters worldwide, who commissioned the project. “It was the first time we had explored scent as a way of interpreting and enhancing the experience of an exhibition in our galleries. We introduced Lyn to the paintings in the collection and invited her to respond to them in her own way. It was fascinating to see how she translated visual elements, atmosphere and mood into scent.”

Harris created the bespoke Smoke in Woods fragrance to perfume Sotheby’s in London during the preview and sale of Abraham Govaerts’s early 17th-century painting A wooded river landscape with travellers and peasants on a path, men in a boat and a village beyond Courtesy Sotheby’s

Rather than responding literally to each artwork, Harris teased out alternative readings. In a painting by Abraham Govaerts of peasants in a clearing by a river (A wooded river landscape with travellers and peasants on a path, men in a boat and a village beyond (early 17th century)), she homed in on the flickering light coming from a distant fire for a scent called Smoke in Woods. For Osias Beert the Elder’s floral still life of a fading floral bouquet (Still life of roses in an oriental lacquer and canework bowl, on a ledge with a butterfly and dragonfly (around 1615)), she picked up on the butterfly and dragonfly and created a fragrance called Rose and Insects—it’s one she often wears herself and contains no trace of rose, but honey, immortelle, papyrus and vetiver. “It’s about beauty, with imperfections,” she says.

She traces her love of fragrance back to her childhood in the Yorkshire countryside. “Nature has always been my thing,” she says. “From the minute I was born, I loved being outside.” Holidays were spent with her grandparents on their smallholding in the Scottish Highlands. “There was the smell of cooking, of jam, of the fire, of my grandmother’s garden with sweet peas and geraniums,” she recalls. “There isn’t a day goes by that I don’t think about that place.”

Her career has partly been shaped by the rhythm and simplicity of that carefully crafted life. “My grandfather was a carpenter. He had a place where he made furniture, and he always wore the same jacket, dungarees and boots,” she says. “My grandmother made our clothes. They didn’t buy a lot of things, but the way they curated their life with authenticity and dignity intrigued me.”

Clay, Harris’s latest perfume, captures the earth, the natural world embedded in it and even the light that bounces off it Photo: João Sousa

Recently she was inspired by her late grandmother to create a new fragrance called Soap for her friend Nick Wakeman’s fashion brand Studio Nicholson. In an episode of the label’s in-house podcast, Wakeman says the starting point of the project was sniffing fabrics and asking, “What does flax smell like? What does wool smell like? What does cotton smell like?” Then the conversation turns to how fabrics feel against the skin. “We wanted the perfume to feel like it was like a fabric that would veil you,” Wakeman says, while Harris tells me Soap is designed to be “a layer between your skin and your clothing. It’s clean, utilitarian and easy, with a whiteness—a starched smell that I’ve used sparingly.”

As a teenager in the 1980s, Harris was a rebel. She bleached her hair and dyed it red, and was eventually asked to leave her strict school. Her parents encouraged her to work in a perfume shop in their home town, and it was there she saw how scent could transform a person. “Women would come into the store and I could see how fragrance defined them—like lipstick, but more so,” she recalls. Today her work is still about how people feel when they wear her scents. She often tests then on any available “subjects”, including her son—her fragrances are intended to be unisex.

In 1992, she started training with Monique Schlienger, a renowned perfumer who had set up her own school in Paris in 1976. Perfume-making was traditionally a male-dominated field, but Harris was determined to be taught by a woman. “Monique taught me not to be scared of my emotions when being creative,” Harris recalls. She did further training at Robertet, in Grasse, near Cannes, before co-founding the luxury fragrance line Miller Harris in 2000. In 2015, she set up her own house, Perfumer H.

Harris describes her career as a continuous education. “In your first ten years you’re completely an innocent, finding who you are,” she says. “When your fragrances start to work in the marketplace, you become much more confident. In 20 years, you know your materials and your palette—you streamline, you cut back.” It has been a gentle progression. “Everything’s so rushed in today’s world,” she continues, “so a lot of new perfumers don’t have the luxury of time that I had. I would spend weeks floating around, dreaming.”

This summer, the first visitors to the fancy new St Clement Hotel in London have engaged inadvertently with Harris’s work. The hotel’s elegant foyer, hung with artworks by Richard Prince and Mark Grotjahn, is infused with a bespoke fragrance by Perfumer H. While the establishment takes its name from the nearby church of St Clement Danes in Aldwych, Harris has derived the scent’s bergamot and bitter orange notes from the opening lines of the nursery rhyme “Oranges and lemons, say the bells of St Clement’s.”

For the newly opened St Clement Hotel in London, Harris devised a bespoke citrus-based perfume, which infuses the building from foyer to penthouse Courtesy St Clement Hotel

The hotel’s founder Nick Jones, who also opened private members’ club Soho House in 1995, wanted something fresh, and told Harris that he especially likes the smell of freshly baked bread in the morning. She has blended that with toasted sesame, cardamom and tonka to develop what she calls a “moreish” smell.

“Lyn’s far cleverer at this than I am,” Jones says. “I’d have stopped at oranges and lemons; she’s put all this warmth underneath. When you walk in and smell it, you relax. You don’t know why, but you do.” Beyond the foyer, the same fragrance is used in all the soaps, hand creams, body lotions and haircare products throughout the hotel—in refillable glass bottles, at Harris’s insistence.

“It feels real and useful,” Jones adds. “It’s not scent as decoration, for its own sake. It does something.” And indeed for Harris, scent is anything but a decorative layer; it is an integral part of everyday living. It is not just in her grandmother’s garden, the soil of Jingdezhen or the stylish lobbies of St Clement, but “in my blood, my breath, my heartbeat”.

#4034. All six of Edward Hopper’s early oils, long thought to be original works, revealed to be copies

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Edward Hopper's Old Ice Pond at Nyack (around 1898-1900, top) is in fact a copy of Winter Sunset by Bruce Crane (bottom), which was published in the December 1890 issue of Art Interchange magazine Hopper: Copyright 2026, Heirs of Josephine N. Hopper/Licensed by DACS, London; Image courtesy of Heather James Fine Arts. Crane: Photo by Louis Shadwick

Around the age of 13, Edward Hopper converted the attic of the Nyack family home into a makeshift painting studio and private haven. Here he put into action his dream of becoming a painter. Though he had attempted some watercolours in the early 1890s, Hopper now began painting in oils, demonstrating a newfound artistic seriousness.

The first oil that Hopper painted was probably Rowboat in Rocky Cove, a quaint image of a placid stretch of water replete with distant ships, signed and dated 1895. He produced at least five more finished oils before enrolling in art school, all left in the attic, remaining unseen for decades. When they were eventually discovered and exhibited after the artist’s death, they were heralded as his first original compositions in oil. But the truth of their origins revises the accepted story of Hopper’s artistic development.

The most intriguing of these early oils is Old Ice Pond at Nyack, made between 1898 and 1900. The painting has been regarded as one of Hopper’s only confirmed depictions of Nyack in oil, seemingly offering a window into his formative environments. Depicting a snowy landscape presided over by an evocative sunset, the work has been cited as evidence of the young artist’s talent, his romantic connection with the American scene from an early age, and his prophetic interest in sunlight as an agent of mood.

The title of the painting refers to the old ice pond in Nyack, which until the 1950s existed at the top of Main Street, a short walk from the Hopper family home. Postcards and photographs of the pond reveal its placid beauty in warmer weather and frenetic activity in the winter season, when it froze over and was thronged with skaters and ice cutters. Hopper’s illustration A Pair of Slippers, of 1899, alongside several other early drawings depicting ice skaters, suggests his participation in this annual tradition, since it portrays two boys—one a likely self-portrait—slipping about on a frozen pond.

Old Ice Pond at Nyack certainly does function as a window into the little-discussed cultural environment of Hopper’s youth, albeit not in the manner previously thought. The work’s title, placing the landscape in Nyack, did not come from Hopper at all. Rather, it was bestowed by the painting’s first owner, Reverend Arthayer R. Sanborn, shortly after the artist’s death. Sanborn had discovered Old Ice Pond at Nyack and Hopper’s other early oils in the attic of Hopper’s childhood home in the late 1960s, alongside a trove of unseen early drawings, oils and watercolours. Rowboat in Rocky Cove was stowed here, too, and was titled Rowboat on Hudson by Sanborn—once more linking the painting to the Nyack scenery of Hopper’s childhood. The title was later altered, but the original affiliations to Nyack stuck, with writers continuing to relate both paintings to Hopper’s childhood environments.

Neither Old Ice Pond at Nyack nor Rowboat in Rocky Cove bear any relation to Nyack. They are actually faithful copies after paintings by other artists. Old Ice Pond at Nyack is a copy of A Winter Sunset by the then-popular tonalist artist Bruce Crane, while Rowboat in Rocky Cove is a copy after the watercolour Lake View by an unknown painter. Crane’s Winter Sunset depicts a landscape in Long Island or Connecticut, while Lake View is inscribed “Athelstane” and so possibly depicts a lake near Athelstane, Wisconsin, though this may also suggest the signature of the unknown painter.

Both paintings appeared as collectable colour plates in the Art Interchange, a widely read periodical for amateur artists that encouraged its readers to copy from its pages. Crane’s Winter Sunset appeared in the December 1890 issue and Lake View in February 1891. In both cases, the colour plates were accompanied by comprehensive sets of copying instructions, which explains the existence of numerous other extant copies of A Winter Sunset by amateur artists. The instructions for Lake View stated that the painting was “a very simple subject and well adapted to beginners”. There is little mystery, then, as to why Hopper copied the plate for likely his first serious attempt at oil painting; indeed, his beginnings as a painter seem to have been much more tentative than previously imagined.

Lake View by an unknown painter, which appeared in the February 1891 edition of Art Interchange, is the basis for Edward Hopper's painting Rowboat in Rocky Cove (1895) Photo by Louis Shadwick

These were not the only copies Hopper made. All of his early oils before he enrolled in art school, long deemed original compositions, are in fact copies. His painting Country Road (around 1896) is a copy after a painting titled A Landscape Study in Color by the American tonalist artist John Francis Murphy. This work again appeared as a colour reproduction in the Art Interchange on 27 March 1886. Hopper’s Ships, meanwhile, produced around 1898, was a copy after Marine View by Edward Moran, which appeared in the Art Interchange’s issue of mid-August 1886. Another early Hopper oil, originally titled Winter Scene by Sanborn and later renamed Church and Landscape, was again a copy, not a portrayal of a “snowy Nyack Baptist church”, as one writer has claimed. Though its precise source remains unknown, its status as a copy is confirmed via the existence of two paintings of the same scene by the painter Edgar Barnes Gilson that date to 1893, as well as a Victorian porcelain plaque on which this same scene is depicted.

Across these works, Hopper made no acknowledgment that any were copies, signing them either with the initials “E.H.” of “E. Hopper”. Only Church and Landscape remained unsigned. Rather than any attempt to mislead, the signatures suggest the artist’s youthful pride in his earliest achievements in oil that were never intended to be seen publicly. In any case, it is possible that Hopper did not produce an original oil painting until he enrolled at the New York School of Art in the autumn of 1900. What’s more, contrary to prior belief, no boyhood oils of Nyack by the artist exist.

Of course, all aspiring artists copy. It has long been established that Hopper made sketches from printed material and that eventually, when he reached art school, he was encouraged to copy after other artists. But knowing that all of Hopper’s first oil paintings are copies challenges prevailing myths surrounding the artist—namely, that his gift for painting emerged during adolescence with essentially no instruction and that he was little exposed to American art groups or movements until art school.

Hopper’s copies reveal the artist’s close study of contemporaneous painting from an early age, most specifically his interest in a now overlooked style known as American tonalism that achieved widespread dominance around the turn of the 20th century. These discoveries open a window into the much broader cultural fabric of Hopper’s youth, not least the centrality of illustrated periodicals to both the values that the young Hopper absorbed and to his formative conceptions of American art and culture.

#4046. Hilma af Klint painting could break artist's record next month in Stockholm

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

An abstract painting by the pioneering Swedish artist Hilma af Klint will be sold at the Stockholm auction house Bukowskis on 19 November, with an estimate of SEK6m to SEK8m (around $600,000 to $800,000).

Ser III No. 5, which measures 38.5cm by 29cm, was bought by a private collector in 1995, when it was deaccessioned by the Hilma af Klint Foundation, and has remained in the same hands ever since.

The oil and graphite on canvas is dated January 1920, a time when af Klint’s was at her most productive, making 85 works in just a few months, a flurry of subtly different arrangements of triangles, circles, and rectangles punctuated with symbols and signs, including variations of the Theosophical Society’s seal. However, this spurt of hard-edged geometric abstraction came to an abrupt stop in March 1920 when af Klint took a break from painting to study Goethe’s colour theory before restarting in 1922 with looser, wet-in-wet watercolours inspired by anthroposophical artistic principles.

It is highly unusual for af Klint’s work to come to auction, as her foundation (established in 1972 by her nephew and heir, Erik af Klint) tightly controls the supply of her abstract paintings. Those that do come onto the open market tend to be earlier, more traditional landscape paintings, such as Vid ankdammen, a watercolour on paper of a mother and child watching ducks from 1910, which sold for £40,640 at Christie’s in March, far above the £6,000 to £8,000 estimate. Such a dearth of supply also means the record for af Klint’s work is relatively low, at $165,825 for The Power of the Spleen, a watercolour on paper from 1931, which sold at Stockholms Auktionsverk in 2019.

“The artist’s non-figurative legacy is preserved and administered by the Hilma af Klint Foundation, and her abstract paintings have consequently remained almost entirely beyond the reach of the public art market,” says Louise Wrede, the head of Bukowski’s art department, adding that only two abstract paintings (as opposed to works on paper) by af Klint have previously sold at auction, “both more than three decades ago.”

Inspired by natural sciences, spirituality and philosophy, af Klint (1862–1944) was an artist well before her time and her visionary abstracts were little appreciated in her lifetime. It was only in 1986, when some of her work (including this very painting, Ser III No. 5), was featured in the Los Angeles County Museum of Art’s show The Spiritual in Art: Abstract Painting 1890-1985, which brought af Klint’s work to an international audience.

Since then her star has risen, and particularly sharply since 2018, when the Guggenheim Museum in New York held Hilma af Klint: Paintings for the Future, a much-lauded show which became the most visited exhibition in the museum’s history with over 600,000 visitors. The public and institutional appetite for af Klint’s work show no sign of abating—the first major retrospective of her work in France took place at the Grand Palais in Paris over the summer, Hilma af Klint: Paintings for the Temple (1906–1915), and just last week, the National Gallery of Ireland opened Hilma af Klint: Artist and Visionary, which runs until 7 February 2027.

Quantum computers capable of doing a million reliable operations could bring about a new stage of quantum calculation. That “megaquop” regime — as it’s called — isn’t here yet. But scientists just took a significant step toward it.

A quantum computer built by the company Quantinuum has error rates so low that it could perform a million operations with only about five errors, researchers report in a paper posted October 19 on the company’s website and arXiv.org.

“These statistics are consistent with this being a good building block for that megaquop machine,” says quantum physicist David Hayes of Quantinuum, who is based in Broomfield, Colo. In order to reach that regime, however, such computers will need to be scaled up.

Quantinuum’s computer, known as Helios, is made of electrically charged atoms, called ions, hovering over a chip. Those ions serve as the 98 quantum bits, or qubits, of the computer. But, like all qubits, those ions are finicky. Their states are delicate and can flip unintentionally and mess up calculations, like a 0 randomly switching to a 1.

A technique called quantum error correction tackles this by combining multiple error-prone qubits into one, forming a supercharged unit called a logical qubit that is less prone to mistakes. (For those who grew up on 1990s TV, it’s a bit like how Captain Planet is born from the combined powers of the gang of Planeteers.)

Logical qubits come in different sizes, integrating a given number of physical qubits — the ions, in this case. Each time a logical qubit is scaled up, going, say, from seven physical qubits to 19, the error rate drops by a factor called lambda. A previous demonstration of error correction by Google, reported in Nature in 2024, had a lambda of about 2, meaning errors were cut in half each time the logical qubits were scaled up.

Quantinuum’s computer has a lambda of about 10. So errors dropped quickly when the researchers made larger logical qubits. A logical qubit composed of 37 physical qubits had just 5 errors in a million operations — close to what would be needed for the megaquop regime. However, that experiment involved only a single logical qubit. Reaching the megaquop regime could possibly involve around 50 to 100 logical qubits.

The large value of lambda means that achieving a desired error rate takes fewer physical qubits, says Ken Brown, a quantum engineer at Duke University and a scientific adviser for the company Logiqal who was not involved with the research. “That’s a huge savings in the overall number of qubits you would need. So that’s what’s exciting.”

The kitchen is more than a place to cook. It’s where mornings begin, and days end—a place for conversation, sharing meals, gathering, and working. It’s one of the most lived-in rooms in the house, and the most appealing kitchens tend to feel like rooms rather than collections of equipment. The balance is in choosing appliances that can either disappear into the architecture or earn their place within it.

For more than six decades, California-founded Dacor has been considering both sides of that equation, introducing a series of innovations that have since become familiar features of the modern kitchen. Its current collection spans contemporary and transitional styles, with appliances designed to integrate into surrounding cabinetry or take a most visible role in the kitchen. Across the range, advanced features—from steam cooking and convection technology to precision refrigeration—are designed around the way a kitchen is actually used, making daily life easier rather than more complicated. Because ultimately, the best technology in the kitchen isn’t necessarily the technology you notice first; it’s what makes the room work better once you’re living in it.
