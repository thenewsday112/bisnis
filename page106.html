STAT’s Tom Capper explained how to measure AI search visibility, distinguish brand mentions from citations, and connect prompt tracking with business outcomes.

An AI answer can recommend your product without linking to your website. A customer might then search for your brand, visit directly, or buy offline. The purchase still matters to the business, even when analytics cannot connect it to the earlier answer.

That was the measurement problem Tom Capper, Director of Search Product Strategy at STAT, addressed in Tactical Solutions For The Biggest AI Search Measurement Challenges.

In this webinar, Capper explained why zero-click search was also an attribution challenge, not simply a traffic problem. His response was to measure visibility alongside business outcomes rather than expect clicks to describe the entire customer journey.

Capper illustrated the distinction with an AI-generated car recommendation. Audi was the brand being recommended, while the cited links pointed to other websites. A report focused only on whether Audi’s domain earned a citation would miss the recommendation itself.

The implication was practical: define the brand entities you want to track, including relevant sub-brands, rather than assume your domain captures every valuable appearance. Apply the same discipline to competitors so the comparison is meaningful.

“So yeah, citations have URLs. That’s still valuable tactical data, but I don’t think it should be your KPI. Mentions have entities.”

— Tom Capper. Watch the webinar, 24:10–24:30.

For his measurement approach, being mentioned or recommended was the outcome to understand; the cited URLs helped explain which sources might be shaping it.

Different answers to the same prompt do not make AI visibility impossible to measure. Capper treated that variability as a sampling problem, drawing a comparison with the location- and device-specific results SEOs already tracked.

A small set of prompts can give an unstable picture, particularly when the business already monitors a much larger keyword set. The goal was not to reproduce every individual’s exact conversation. It was to sample the relevant situations broadly enough to recognize patterns.

That also meant asking realistic questions. Capper cautioned against simply putting a keyword list into a prompt tracker. He suggested People Also Ask questions as one possible starting point, then considering the personas, locations, budgets, and needs behind those questions.

The webinar’s prompt-measurement discussion also addressed why the environment used to collect an answer mattered: Capper said STAT used the web interface because API responses could differ from what users saw.

De-emphasizing citations as a headline KPI does not mean ignoring them. They can make campaign work more concrete.

Capper described starting with pages cited when competitors were recommended. Those pages revealed third-party sources worth investigating, such as review sites or industry publications. A team could then work to improve its representation on relevant sources and monitor whether those sources appeared alongside changes in brand recommendations.

This created a more useful question than whether the citation count increased: which sources changed, what work did the team do, and how did the tracked answers change afterward? Keep the prompt sample and collection context in view when making that comparison, rather than treating an isolated answer as the whole result.

The last distinction mattered even when an organic ranking had not changed. Capper showed examples where a lower-ranked result was visible above the fold while a higher-ranked result was pushed below it. Position alone missed that difference.

He also urged caution with sentiment scores. A chart that swings sharply among positive, neutral, and negative may be amplifying small differences in otherwise neutral answers. The qualitative themes behind a mention can be more useful than a volatile summary score.

During the Q&A, Loren Baker asked how teams could connect this visibility to a customer journey when multi-touch attribution could not observe the earlier exposure.

Capper’s answer was to combine evidence rather than expect one analytics model to explain everything. Post-purchase surveys could reveal customers who said an AI recommendation helped them discover or choose a brand. Campaign tracking could separately show whether that brand was being recommended more often.

He suggested passing survey answers into analytics as a dimension so the team could compare self-reported discovery with the channel credited by analytics. He also warned that showing survey options in the same order could favor the first option.

This was a fuller account of influence, not a promise to reconstruct every person’s path or to classify all direct traffic as AI-driven.

#27. UK poised to miss reduced aid spending target of 0.3% of national income

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Calculations from the Center for Global Development say spending next year is likely to be £410m less than anticipated

The UK is on course to miss its target of spending 0.3% of national income on international aid by an estimated £400m by the end of next year.

Calculations from the Center for Global Development (CGD) found the UK was on course for a large shortfall in promised official development assistance at the end of 2027.

The researchers said the central forecast from the Treasury’s comparison of independent experts implied UK aid would be £410m less than anticipated – at only 0.29% of national income.

However, they said it was “very plausible” that the shortfall would be £580m, at only 0.28% of national income. Even the most conservative forecast implies a £250m shortfall, according to the estimates.

Ian Mitchell, a senior fellow at the CGD and co-director of its Europe programme, described these as “material shortfalls in the spending power of UK aid”.

Making up a shortfall of even £400m would be enough to halve cuts to Africa that are likely to cost thousands of lives and mean the UK’s de facto exit from the south and east of the continent, he said.

The CGD attributed the shortfall to higher than expected inflation, which means national income will be higher than forecast at the spending review, and a 2025 policy change around the Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) budget.

The researchers called on ministers to consider allocating additional funds to international aid in this week’s budget, to avoid the embarrassment and criticism from missing a target.

The 0.3% spending target for 2027 is far below the 0.7% promised in Labour’s election manifesto, having been downgraded by Keir Starmer to boost spending on defence.

Fleur Anderson, a former minister who sits on the foreign affairs select committee, said she would like to see higher spending on development aid in the budget.

She said: “I’d like to see this government going further and reversing these cuts altogether in time, but at the very least we should be hitting the reduced target that was set.

“Falling short isn’t an abstract accounting issue. It means communities not having clean water, girls pulled out of school, vaccines that don’t get delivered, peace programmes in war zones cut.

“The upcoming budget presents real opportunity for the Treasury to address this and for the government to show that its words on Britain’s place in the world are matched by action.”

Charities have repeatedly said that the government’s deep aid cuts will hit children’s education and increase the risk of disease and death in some African countries.

The latest figures released by the FCDO in September showed that British aid cuts under Starmer’s government hit the world’s poorest countries the hardest, while higher-income countries’ share of aid increased. The data showed a 6.7% decrease in funding to the least-developed and low-income countries, amounting to cuts of £118m.

The UK’s overall official development assistance (ODA) budget in 2025 decreased to 0.44% of gross national income, compared with 0.5% in 2024, a drop of £987m.

The government has prioritised spending through multilateral agencies such as the World Bank’s development arm, the International Development Association, and the global vaccines initiative Gavi. It has also continued to fund humanitarian support.

The Conservative party and Reform UK have pledged to slash aid even further, from about £9bn a year to £1bn – a level that would leave the UK unable to cover existing contributions to the UN and World Bank.

An FCDO spokesperson said: “The publication of the FCDO multi-year ODA allocations in March 2026 set out our spending plans and priorities for the next three financial years based on GNI [gross national income] at the time of the 2025 spending review.

“Moving to a fixed budget reduces volatility and provides the necessary stability to deliver high-impact aid programmes.”

I love putting a movie on the “big screen”—AKA the TV. Much better and more atmospheric than a laptop. But I don’t love the idea of having a screen front and center in our living area. In fact, none of us on the Remodelista team do—we’ve written before about ways to hide the television, with tricks and sleights of hand that include camouflage, concealing it behind cabinetry, and making it part of a gallery wall. But this idea, spotted in new project by Studio Nick Spain, might take the cake.

In restoring a 19th-century Victorian farmhouse in Athens, New York, not far from the Hudson, writer/designer Nick Spain walked a design tightrope, “reimagining the home as both a functional family residence and a poignant narrative exploration.” For the palette and interior details, he drew on the home’s history—particularly the wreck of a steamboat that occurred nearby in 1845, leaving the then-inhabitant of the home a widow—and referenced artistic and literary works on grief and memory. At the same time, while paying homage to the past, the house had to be practical for a family of four with two small girls living in 2025.

It’s a careful balance exemplified in Nick’s solution for the screen in the family room. “Our clients are big TV people,” Nick says, “and due to layout and electrical connections there was really no other place it could live besides over the mantle.” But they didn’t necessarily want a black screen to be the focal point of the otherwise poetic space.

What to do? Take a look at his simple solution—equal parts pragmatic and poignant.

On the botanical design itself, Nick—who is a writer as well as a designer—looked to Joan Didion. “In The Year of Magical Thinking (both the book and the play), Didion speaks about the grieving process in terms of rhythm and repetition, of how she’s continuously looping back to habitualized patterns of interacting with her husband even as she’s desperately trying to escape them. In the design process, we thought a lot about how this could be represented visually through decorative scrolling patterns that felt in line with some of the home’s more ornate elements.

“Many of the patterns that we looked at were by the wunderkind Dagobert Peche, and the floral image that’s depicted is actually swiped directly from one of his fabrics. Peche himself also died tragically young at the age of 36, which made it feel all the more aligned with the narrative we were working within, and the materiality of the muslin speaks to the simplicity of the environments that Wyeth often captured in his work.”

#3637. Think Architecture's fragmented House on a Slope overlooks Lake Zurich

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A cluster of interconnected volumes in dark limestone and cedar gradually descend down a narrow and steeply sloping site overlooking Lake Zurich at House on a Slope by Swiss studio Think Architecture.

Designed to sit harmoniously within the surrounding terrain, the 1,000-square-metre private home is split into two distinct blocks connected by a “subterranean art corridor”.

“The brief was to bring a very large programme into a narrow, steeply sloping site with as much sensitivity as possible,” Think Architecture creative director Marco Zbinden told Dezeen.

“We were interested in the quiet rhythm of the neighbouring houses, their individual scale, and the way they sit within the landscape. Breaking the residence into smaller volumes allowed us to respond to that context without losing the generosity of the interior,” he continued.

“From the outside, the house feels less like a single monumental object and more like a group of familiar structures that have always belonged to the hillside.”

Intended to emulate the surrounding landscape, the lakeside home was divided into two blocks, a “family house above, and a more grounded, protective house for children and guests below”.

“They are intimately connected through a corridor – filled with art – and the central garden level, which becomes the social heart of the home,” said Zbinden. “There, the architecture opens up, materials continue from outside to inside, and the different parts of the family’s life naturally meet.”

The larger, upper block, which is entered at street level, contains all the main living spaces including the kitchen, living room and dining room. These spaces are arrange over the first and lower floors, with large cedar-clad-timber windows giving views down the hillside. Several bedrooms are located on the top floor.

The lower level of the home opens up to a garden and large pool with a planted roof terrace sitting on the roof of the lower block. Clad in dark-limestone, this lower block is more enclosed with additional bedrooms for the family.

Carefully positioned openings within both structures allowed for the planting of native trees, ensuring a seamless transition into the mature foliage of the hillside.

Think Architecture designed the upper interior spaces to be open and airy, while the spaces in the lower block were more enclosed and calm.

“The interiors were conceived as a direct continuation of the architecture, extending its material language into the most private and lived-in spaces,” said Zbinden.

“We worked with a limited palette of bog oak, cedar, oak, Belgian bluestone, terrazzo, and patinated brass. Materials that are tactile, honest, and become more beautiful as they age,” he continued.

“The aim was to create spaces that felt warm and enveloping rather than decorated: quiet rooms shaped by light, texture, and proportion, where one can truly slow down and feel at home.”

House with a Slope is the most recent addition to a number of homes overlooking Lake Zurich completed by Think Architecture in recent months, including a minimal hilltop home and a home next to a vineyard.
