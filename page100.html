#1031. What Causes Bad Breath?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Bad breath is embarrassing enough. Not realizing you have it is worse. The cupped-hand breath check and a trusty pack of mints aren’t exactly foolproof—and the last thing you want is your breath to sabotage an important meeting or a first date.

Here’s the annoying part: You might have your oral hygiene routine fully dialed in and still deal with some funk. “You can have beautiful teeth, brush religiously, and still have breath that isn’t doing you any favors,” says Arthur Glosman, DDS, a restorative and cosmetic dentist in Beverly Hills, California. So what gives?

Ahead, experts explain what causes bad breath, how to get it under control, and when it’s time to call in a pro.

About 80 to 90 percent of bad breath (aka halitosis) originates in the mouth. The most common culprit? Excess bacteria. “Bacteria naturally exist throughout the oral cavity, but when they accumulate on the tongue, between teeth, or beneath the gumline, they produce volatile sulfur compounds responsible for odor,” says Graig Fischgrund, DMD, a periodontist at Apa Aesthetic in New York City. The back of the tongue—which many people don’t clean thoroughly—is prime real estate for buildup. According to Glosman, this area “is practically a five-star resort for odor-producing bacteria: warm, moist, and an endless buffet.” How appetizing.

Saliva—your mouth’s built-in cleaning crew—also helps determine how fresh or funky your breath smells. As Fischgrund explains, it helps regulate bacteria, neutralize acids, and wash away food debris. When saliva production drops, odor-causing bacteria can thrive. Mouth breathing, dehydration, smoking, and certain medications can all contribute to dry mouth. It’s also why morning breath is pretty universal (and perfectly normal): “Saliva production drops while we sleep, giving bacteria more opportunity to misbehave,” says Dr. Glosman.

Your diet can also leave its mark, though it’s usually temporary. Familiar offenders include coffee, alcohol, garlic, and onions. But if odor sticks around, it could point to a bigger problem, like gum disease or an untreated dental infection, says Fischgrund. Tonsil stones, sinus problems, and acid reflux can also contribute, Glosman adds, though these are less common.

First, nail down the basics: Brush your teeth twice daily, floss at night, and stay hydrated throughout the day. Replace your toothbrush or brush head every three to four months, or sooner if the bristles begin to fray. And you don’t need fancy flossing gadgets to get the job done: traditional string floss can clear away the plaque and debris your toothbrush misses.

The low-effort upgrade both experts recommend adding to the mix? A tongue scraper, which is designed to clear away gunk, especially toward the back of your tongue. “Just don’t attack your tongue like you’re sanding furniture,” says Glosman.

Mouthwash can help, too, but don’t mistake a blast of mint as a long-term fix. “Mouthwash can temporarily eliminate odor, but it can't reverse gum disease, treat an infection, or correct chronic dry mouth,” says Fischgrund. You’ll also want to be selective. Dr. Glosman advises opting for alcohol-free formulas, especially if you’re prone to dry mouth, and products designed to neutralize odor rather than simply cover it up. While mouth sprays, gum, and mints can do damage control throughout the day, Glosman says it’s “like spraying cologne on a sweaty gym shirt since you haven’t really solved anything.”

If you’re still dealing with bad breath after tightening up your routine, it’s time to consult a professional. Persistent bad breath can signal an underlying issue—not just a missed spot with your toothbrush. Your dentist can check for common oral causes and recommend the appropriate treatment. And if it turns out your mouth is healthy, the next step may be to consult your PCP or a specialist such as an ENT or GI doctor, depending on your symptoms.

While mints and mouthwash are quick fixes, the point is to find out what’s driving the funk—not just masking it.

#485. Oct. 26, 2004: Cassini at Titan

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On Oct. 26, 2004, the Cassini spacecraft made its first close pass by Saturn’s planet-size moon, Titan (later known as Titan Flyby A). After a seven-year journey — the last four months of which were spent in orbit around Saturn — Cassini plunged within 745 miles (1,200 kilometers) of the world’s surface. It snapped hundreds of photographs, made its first radar observations, and even scooped up traces of the moon’s outer atmosphere.

The first close-up images showed a surface with lots of contrast and a complex interplay between the bright and dark expanses, and numerous bright streaks running parallel Titan’s to equator. The surface appeared young, as there was no immediate evidence of impact craters, even though Saturn’s other moons bear innumerable impact scars. In addition to imaging, instruments on Cassini collected scatterometry, radiometry, and magnometer and spectrometer results. Those traces of Titan’s atmosphere were also analyzed, in preparation for the Huygens probe to conduct a full study when it landed on the moon in January of the following year.

#3676. Museum acquisitions round-up: a Joan Jonas dance performance and a squirrelly readymade

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Squirrel (1960, edition 1969) by Meret Oppenheim

Meret Oppenheim’s playful Squirrel, an assisted readymade first created in 1960 and produced as an edition in 1969, is one of more than 2,000 Modern and contemporary works of art that have been donated to the Detroit Institute of Arts (DIA) by the estate of the property developer and art collector Gilbert B. Silverman and his wife Lila. The scope is enormous, reflecting the Silvermans’ eclectic tastes, ranging from decorative arts and jewellery to performance pieces and time-based media, though there is a particular focus on works by Fluxus artists from the 1960s and 70s such as Joseph Beuys, George Maciunas and Yoko Ono, among others. These items join 130 other Fluxus works that were given to the DIA by the Silvermans’ foundation in 2008.

Courtesy of The Gund at Kenyon College and the Smithsonian’s Hirshhorn Museum and Sculpture Garden

The Gund at Kenyon College, Gambier, Ohio, and the Smithsonian’s Hirshhorn Museum and Sculpture Garden, Washington, DC

Wind is one of two works by the American performance artist Joan Jonas (born 1936) that have been jointly acquired by The Gund and Hirshhorn, using funds donated by Agnes Gund and the Joseph H. Hirshhorn Purchase Fund. Wind, a silent black-and-white film and one of Jonas’s first collaborations with dancers, shows a group of performers battling strong winds on a snowy beach. Playing with ideas of perception, reality and viewer participation, it features two performers wearing mirrored costumes. Mirrors are integral to the other acquired work, Mirror Pieces Installation II (1969/2014). This multimedia piece centres on mirrors that both reflect the viewer and a television playing another of Jonas’s filmed performances, Nudes with Mirrors (1969), showing a man donning and taking off a mirrored jacket in front of yet more mirrors held by other performers.

The Van Eeghen drawing book (17th century)

Although successful in his lifetime, the Dutch artist Rombout Uylenburgh (around 1580–around 1627) fell into relative obscurity after his death, and few of his works survive. However, the rediscovery of this book of around 30 drawings by Uylenburgh and his workshop significantly adds to his oeuvre. The book has been donated to the Rijksmuseum by the Van Eeghen family, which has owned it for more than three centuries, though it was only around 20 years ago that the link to Uylenburgh was made. The book includes fully worked biblical scenes alongside quicker informal sketches of, for example, birds and animals. Most books of drawings from the period have been broken up, the sheets sold off separately, so this is a rarity and, having barely been exposed to light, the colours remain remarkably fresh.

#3685. Art Povera’s Marisa Merz honoured with trio of Turin shows

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Marisa Merz, pictured installing Harp (1967-68) in Turin in 1973 Photo: Paolo Pellion; Courtesy Eredi/Heirs Paolo Pellion di Persano, Torino

A major survey celebrating Marisa Merz (1926-2019), Arte Povera’s only female artist, will open across three institutions in Turin this week. Marking 100 years since her birth, The Dance of the Hours will take place at the Castello di Rivoli Museo d’Arte Contemporanea; the Fondazione Merz; and the Galleria Civica d’Arte Moderna e Contemporanea di Torino (GAM). It is reckoned to be an undertaking of such magnitude that it is unlikely ever to be repeated.

Though it will be by far the biggest exhibition ever dedicated to Marisa Merz, the project is not unprecedented. In 2005, the same three institutions joined forces to honour Mario Merz, her husband and collaborator, following his death in 2003.

Now, the centenary presents an opportunity for those institutions to explore the career and legacy of Marisa Merz, says Francesco Manacorda, the co-curator, along with Marianna Vecellio, of the exhibition at the Castello di Rivoli.

Marisa Merz's Untitled is made up of eight heads on metal table and is going on show at the Fondazione Merz Photo: Renato Ghiazza; courtesy Fondazione Merz

The artist’s estate, represented by Fondazione Merz, retains the majority of her works, which include sculpture, works on paper and documented actions, but the collaboration offers more than exceptional access, Manacorda says. “It’s really three different approaches, and this will give you three different Marisas, which is appropriate, because she created a mystery around herself, and hid herself behind it. There is a ‘polyphonic’ Marisa that it’s possible to bring out.”

The ten contemporary artists commissioned to respond to Merz at Castello di Rivoli are essential to this multi-voiced approach, Manacorda says. Contributions from artists such as Leonor Antunes, Thea Djordjadze and Lara Favaretto, and a film by Tacita Dean, will amount to “a piece of art history”,Manacorda says.

Despite achieving considerable acclaim, including a Golden Lion for Lifetime Achievement at the Venice Biennale in 2013, Merz tended, often deliberately, to slip from view. The details of her life prior to her marriage in 1960 are sketchy and so are the reasons for her decision in the mid-1980s to step back from solo shows. Theories range from a need to concentrate to a dislike of competition or attention. “Some people claim it was a feminist choice—she had a daughter, and she writes about the fact that she wanted to work with [her daughter] Beatrice when she was a toddler,” Manacorda says.

Living Sculpture (1966), made of aluminium and foil stapled together, was Marisa Merz's first major work Gam—Galleria Civica d’Arte Moderna e Contemporanea di Torino

The domestic nature of Merz’s practice set her apart from her fellow poveristi and will provide the focus of the presentation at GAM. The family home was her studio, and her materials and techniques—from kitchen foil, staples and peas to sewing and knitting—were unassumingly homely.

“The home as a workspace was not a matter of practicality but of methodology,” says Beatrice Merz, who is now the president of Fondazione Merz, whose exhibition will explore process and transformation. “The home itself was an opportunity for making and working”.

Merz’s first major work was Living Sculpture (1966), a sprawling construction of aluminium foil, stapled together in a process that Merz said—surely with irony—was “as humble and modest as embroidery.” Now held by GAM, it was captured most memorably in photographs of her apartment, where its shiny tubes spilled like alien entrails from behind the television, almost engulfing the dining table. Made during hours at home caring for her daughter, Living Sculpture makes visible, and monstrous, maternal labour in a radical rejection of conventional domesticity. The sculpture frightened Beatrice as a child and in 1967 it featured in the avant-garde horror film Il Mostro Verde (the green monster).

Merz's Untitled Photo: Renato Ghiazza; Courtesy Fondazione Merz

The apartment was left intact following Merz’s death in 2019 and contains unknown quantities of works in progress, “some of which have never been seen, not even by Beatrice”, Manacorda says. The exhibition’s title was taken from one of many Post-it notes that Merz stuck around the apartment.

Even works that made it into the public sphere could lapse into obscurity. The focus of the Castello di Rivoli show is a reconstruction of E il Naufragar M’è Dolce In Questo Mare (roughly translated as “to be shipwrecked is sweet in such a sea”, a line borrowed from a Giacomo Leopardi poem). The work is a complex, multi-part installation that disappeared after its second outing at the Venice Biennale in 1980. Reconstructing it has been a process of recovery, with the arrangement of original elements, such as sheets of brown paper found in the apartment, being guided by photographs and an eyewitness account. Although if Merz were alive today “she would probably make it differently”, concedes Manacorda.

The provisional nature of much of Merz’s work and its inherent fragility makes its ongoing care particularly challenging. Conservation decisions are made on a case-by-case basis and in collaboration, Manacorda says. “The precarity that you perceive really is part of the work, but conservators and collectors are not necessarily ready to accept that fragility.”

#2276. Harassment Restraining Order Based Largely on Speech Critical of Zoo Veterinarian,

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

vacated on First Amendment grounds by a Massachusetts appellate court.

From E.B. v. J.R., decided Aug. 20 by Massachusetts Appeals Court Justices Sabita Singh, Rachel Hershfang, and Chauncey Wood:

The defendant appeals from harassment prevention orders issued pursuant to G. L. c. 258E (258E orders), by a judge of the District Court, requiring the defendant, among other things, to stay away from a city zoo where she had regularly protested the treatment of animals in its care. The plaintiff, a veterinarian who worked at the zoo, sought and obtained an ex parte 258E order against the defendant, which was later extended following a hearing after notice….

When reviewing whether the evidence was sufficient to support the issuance [or extension] of a 258E order, we consider [consistently with the statutory requirements] whether, "by a preponderance of the evidence, together with all permissible inferences," the judge could have found "that the defendant had committed '[three] or more acts of willful and malicious conduct aimed at a specific person committed with the intent to cause fear, intimidation, abuse or damage to property and that [did] in fact cause fear, intimidation, abuse or damage to property.'" …

Where the harassing acts consist of speech, each act must be the type of speech that is not protected by the First Amendment to the United States Constitution, i.e., generally either "true threats" or "fighting words." True threats include "direct threats of imminent physical harm" and "words or actions that—taking into account the context in which they arise—cause the victim to fear such [imminent physical] harm now or in the future and evince intent on the part of the speaker or actor to cause such fear." Fighting words are limited to personal insults that are inherently likely to incite a violent reaction….

The plaintiff's affidavit described the following four alleged incidents of harassment.

First, in July 2024, while the plaintiff was working at the zoo, the defendant began "yelling and screaming" at the plaintiff that she "should lose [her] job and right to be a veterinarian[,] … that [she] was abusing [her] power[,] and that [she was] abusing and killing the animals."

Second, in September 2024, while the plaintiff was working at the zoo, the defendant began "telling other [zoo] visitors falsehoods about the elephants being beaten." The plaintiff told the visitors that the allegations were not true, but the defendant "immediately yelled at [her] over [her] explanation to the visitors and got within inches of [her] face to continue yelling" at her. About forty-five minutes later, the defendant reapproached the plaintiff and got "in [her] face yelling at [her] about how [she was] a bad veterinarian and [her] cruelty towards animals." The plaintiff alleged that she "felt like [the defendant] was trying to intimidate [her] to quit [her] job." The defendant was "in [her] face videotaping" the plaintiff, so the plaintiff called 911. While the plaintiff was on the phone, the defendant "chased after [the plaintiff]."

Third, also in September 2024, the defendant "formally fil[ed] a written complaint to the [veterinary licensing] Board" that "was all lies and falsehoods."

Fourth, in November 2024, a television news crew was at the zoo reporting on the elephants when the defendant disrupted an interview and began to "yell and scream at [the plaintiff] that [she was] not a licensed veterinarian and that she [did] not have a license to practice in [Massachusetts], and that [she was] abusing and murdering elephants." The defendant also "threaten[ed] [the plaintiff's] career."

Of the four incidents alleged, only the second arguably constituted harassing conduct—getting within inches of the plaintiff's face while yelling and chasing after the plaintiff. The remaining alleged conduct constituted protected speech, not rising to the level of fighting words or true threats…. [T]hreats containing ideas or advocacy causing fear of economic loss or unfavorable publicity are not "true threats" …. As a result, the judge erred in finding that the incidents described by the plaintiff amounted to three requisite acts of harassment pursuant to G. L. c. 258E….

The court also concluded the court violated plaintiff's due process rights:

[At the motion hearing, t]he plaintiff's attorney recited the procedural background and the judge began to review the plaintiff's affidavit. The plaintiff's lawyer then started to relate that the defendant had been charged with violating the ex parte order, when the defendant stated, "I object to that, Your Honor." The judge admonished the defendant, "No, no. First of all, … you're going to understand what the rules are; okay? … The rules are, you're not to interrupt at any time." The judge then explained, "You don't, in a civil case, have the opportunity to say you object…. It doesn't work that way…. That's in a criminal case."

The judge then turned to the plaintiff and asked her about the Federal trial in which the plaintiff had testified the previous year. [This appears to me to be Rowley v. City of New Bedford (D. Mass. 2024). -EV] The plaintiff explained that the defendant had filed a lawsuit against the city for violation of the Endangered Species Act and that the suit was resolved in favor of the city. The plaintiff's counsel offered to provide the judge with a copy of the decision.

When the judge asked whether the defendant had a copy, the defendant replied, "Yes, ma'am, I do. Yes, Your Honor," and added, "I object to it being relevant." The judge responded, "What did I say about objections?" and "I decide whether something is relevant[,] right?" The defendant then stated that she disagreed that the Federal court decision was relevant to the plaintiff's request for a 258E order. The judge replied, "You can disagree all you want, but—and you can voice that, I don't want you [to] think you can't voice it…. But you can't say you object."

The judge then said that she had reviewed the defendant's motion to vacate and asked why she should not extend the 258E order. Just as the defendant began to explain that the plaintiff's claims did not meet the legal standard, the judge cut off the defendant and asked a series of questions, pointedly asking why the plaintiff would accuse the defendant if the allegations were not true. When the defendant attempted to explain the context and her theory that it was in retaliation for the Federal lawsuit, the judge again cut off the defendant, telling her to "[s]top talking," and that she (the judge) did not need to know about the elephants at the zoo or the plaintiff's background as a veterinarian.

The judge then invited the plaintiff's counsel to cross-examine the defendant. The cross-examination centered around the Federal lawsuit (concerning the elephants) and the plaintiff's status and licensure as a veterinarian. The plaintiff's counsel then presented the testimony of the plaintiff, who affirmed the truth of her affidavit. The plaintiff expressed how the "entire situation [was] causing [her] immense stress."

At the conclusion of counsel's direct examination, the judge asked the plaintiff, "[Y]ou are concerned for your safety because of the actions of [the defendant] physically, verbally, and on social media; is that right? … And there have been several instances where she has intimidated you; is that right?" After the plaintiff answered in the affirmative, the judge stated, "I have no doubts that you're a competent veterinarian or [the zoo] wouldn't have hired you," and "don't you for one minute second guess your training. Nobody in this room is a veterinarian and knows anything about what you do, okay? … You got your degree. You worked hard for it. Move on with that." …

[T]he judge appeared to shift the burdens by accepting the plaintiff's affidavit and then challenging the defendant to disprove the allegations contained within it. The defendant had no burden to testify or present evidence. Furthermore, due process required that she be heard on her legal argument that the plaintiff's claims did not meet the elements for a 258E order.

Instead, the defendant was repeatedly shut down when attempting to make her valid legal points, incorrectly told that she had no right to make objections, and subjected to questioning pointedly challenging her veracity while assuming the veracity of the plaintiff. Then, the plaintiff's counsel was invited to cross-examine the defendant. The plaintiff, who had the burden, testified after the defendant and was not cross-examined at all. The record reflects that the defendant did not have a meaningful opportunity to be heard. Collectively, these irregularities raise troubling due process concerns….
