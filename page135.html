#3762. Shanghai tea shop Basao draws on "Rockbund’s historic buildings"

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Photography by Jonathan Leijonhufvud

Architect Keiji Ashizawa drew on the surrounding traditional architecture, as well as British studio David Chipperfield Architects‘ restoration of the district, when designing this tea shop in central Shanghai.

The flagship store for Chinese tea brand Basao combines a retail space with a tea counter where visitors can enjoy the tea.

Located in Shanghai’s Rockbund area, it is surrounded by historic architecture but sits in an area that was part of a restoration by David Chipperfield Architects.

Ashizawa, founder of Keiji Ashizawa Design (KAD), aimed to design an “open and inviting retail space” that would have a natural connection to the street.

“When designing an interior, we place great importance on understanding the building’s location, history, and relationship with its surroundings,” he told Dezeen.

“The restoration and regeneration work led by David Chipperfield Architects has also played an important role in shaping the district’s character.”

The studio used metal structure and details to reinterpret some of the area’s traditional architecture in a more contemporary way for the interior of Basao.

“Drawing inspiration from the arches and repeated architectural details of Rockbund’s historic buildings, we introduced a series of vaulted steel ceiling elements,” Ashizawa said.

“Together with the delicate steel shelving, they bring a sense of lightness and openness, while subtly connecting the retail and seating areas, each with its own distinct atmosphere.”

In a nod to the buildings in Xiamen, Fujian, where the Basao brand originated, KAD used a “plaster-like finish” inspired by rammed earth for the shop area.

“Rather than drawing directly from the traditional tea room, we explored the use of earth, a material deeply connected to tea culture, as a defining element of the interior,” Ashizawa explained.

“The retail area is bright and open, with earthen walls inspired by the traditional rammed-earth architecture of Fujian Province,” he continued.

“In contrast, the seating area features darker tones reminiscent of smoked timber, creating a more intimate atmosphere, much like the living room of a friend’s home.”

While the main materials used in the space are timber and steel, KAD also added a brick floor in the shop part of the 108-square-metre space, which is juxtaposed with the wooden floor in the hospitality area.

“We chose brick for the flooring, a material commonly found in Rockbund’s historic architecture, particularly on building facades and occasionally on floors,” Ashizawa said.

The studio aimed for the material to tie the store into the neighbourhood while grounding the interior with its gridded pattern.

“The regular pattern of the brickwork creates a sense of rhythm and order, allowing the material to feel at home in a contemporary setting,” Ashizawa said.

In the retail area, the studio used a light colour palette to create an airy atmosphere that would underline the natural light from its floor-to-ceiling windows, while the seating area has deeper wood tones.

“Rather than relying on strong colours to define the space, we focused on the natural tones of wood and brick, along with the subtle interplay of light and shadow,” Ashizawa said.

“Bronze-colored metal finishes were also introduced as a reference to the historical use of copper in traditional Chinese tea utensils, creating a subtle connection to Chinese tea culture.”

The walls of the tea room were decorated with traditional Chinese lacquer Da Qi Hua pieces.

“The artistic concepts reflect Basao’s brand identity, while their colours were carefully selected to harmonise with the overall atmosphere of the space,” Ashizawa said.

Overall, the studio hopes that it has created a peaceful space in a busy part of the city.

“In a vibrant city like Shanghai, we see value in creating a space that remains connected to its surroundings while offering a sense of calm,” Ashizawa said.

“One of the most rewarding parts of this project was the opportunity to create such an experience through the culture of tea.”

KAD has previously designed a Nagoya coffee shop clad in locally produced tiles and created a restaurant that draws on the “whiteness of tofu”.

#917. Copycat Little Caesars Cheesy Bread

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Skip the trip and bake a sheet pan of copycat Little Caesars cheesy bread—it's enough to serve a crowd.

Little Caesars understands that people will happily make a detour on the way home for a box of soft, garlicky bread smothered in melted cheese. I’ve been one of those people more times than I’d like to admit. This copycat Little Caesars cheesy bread recreates the chain’s Italian cheese bread (not to be confused with its equally popular Crazy Bread) with store-bought pizza dough, garlic butter and a thick layer of cheese. When it’s fresh from the oven, the bronzed, cheesy top bubbles at the edges. You’re basically guaranteed to get Instagram-worthy cheese pulls.

The most common problem I see with homemade bread recipes like this one is a gummy, underbaked center. When butter and cheese go onto raw dough, the fat and moisture sink in, and the middle never really bakes all the way through. This recipe fixes that with a quick parbake, which sets the crust before any toppings go on. It’s the same trick I use for sheet-pan pizza, and it guarantees a golden bottom with a little crunch. A single pan yields 20 pieces, which makes this a no-brainer for game day, kids sleepovers, and salad dinners that need a warm, shareable side.

Preheat the oven to 375°F. Press the pizza dough into a greased 15x10x1-inch baking pan. Cover it and let it rise in a warm place until it’s doubled in size, about 30 minutes.

Editor’s Tip: If the dough keeps springing back from the corners, cover it and let it rest for five minutes. The gluten will relax and the dough will stretch much more easily.

Prick the dough all over with a fork. Bake it until it’s lightly browned, 10 to 12 minutes.

In a small bowl, combine the melted butter and garlic. Brush the mixture over the warm dough.

Sprinkle the dough with the mozzarella, Parmesan and Italian seasoning.

Bake the bread for 20 minutes or until it’s golden brown. Cut it into 1-inch rectangles. Serve it hot with pizza sauce, if desired.

Let leftover cheesy bread cool completely, then transfer the pieces to an airtight container and refrigerate them. Don’t let it sit out for more than two hours, since the cheese topping makes it perishable. A sheet of parchment paper between layers will keep the pieces from sticking together.

Copycat Little Caesars cheesy bread lasts three to four days in the refrigerator when stored in an airtight container.

You can get a head start on copycat Little Caesars cheesy bread by parbaking the crust (following the recipe through Step 2) up to a day ahead. Let the crust cool, wrap it so it’s airtight and keep it at room temperature. When you’re ready, warm the crust in the oven for a few minutes, then brush on the garlic butter, add the toppings and bake it as directed.

Yes, copycat Little Caesars cheesy bread freezes well for up to three months. Wrap cooled pieces individually in airtight packaging, then store them together in a freezer container. Reheat the pieces straight from the freezer on a baking sheet in a 375º oven until they’re hot all the way through and the cheese has melted again.

Little Caesars keeps its exact seasoning blend a secret, but the chain describes its Italian cheese bread as mozzarella and Muenster topped with garlic, butter, Parmesan and its own Italian spices. For copycat Little Caesars cheesy bread, a store-bought Italian seasoning with dried oregano, basil, thyme and rosemary gets you very close. Add a pinch of garlic powder to the blend if you want an even stronger garlic hit.

Yes, homemade pizza dough works in this recipe for copycat Little Caesars cheesy bread. Use about 1 pound of dough so it fills the 15x10x1-inch pan to the same thickness, and let it rise until it’s doubled in size before you parbake it. Dough made with a little olive oil will bake up slightly crisper on the bottom.

Low-moisture shredded mozzarella and grated Parmesan are the best cheeses for copycat Little Caesars cheesy bread because they melt well and brown without turning greasy. Little Caesars pairs mozzarella with Muenster on its restaurant version, so swapping shredded Muenster for part of the mozzarella brings the copycat even closer to the original. Skip fresh mozzarella, which releases too much water and leaves the crust soggy.

#2931. Pain And Penalty: Why A Dad Bod Challenge Works When Willpower Doesn’t

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you want to lose weight and gain muscle, join a dad bod or a mom bod challenge. The social pressure to hit your goals will be immense. And if you put a financial penalty for not meeting your goals by a certain date, you will be even more motivated to get fitter.

Since graduating from college in 1999, I've weighed between 164 and 173 pounds at 5'10” tall. I'm currently about 165 pounds, 45 days after the start of my Dad Bod Challenge. The lightest I weighed as an adult was about 153 pounds, which lasted all of one year before I gained the freshman 10+ pounds during college.

After college, my doctor also suggested I should be around 155-157 lbs to hit my ideal weight. So for over 25 years I've been telling myself I'd like to lose 10 pounds. And I've never succeeded, only coming close when I grew to 173 pounds during the first year of fatherhood in 2017, and then again during the first year of COVID. After both periods, I ended up losing about 7 pounds at most.

But after hearing about some fellow dads at my kids' school entering the Dad Bod Challenge and losing a ton of weight while also gaining muscle, I was impressed. This could finally be the impetus I was looking for to lose that stubborn 10 pounds!

Let me explain our setup, so you might be incentivized to create your own. After all, once you're financially on track, you should get physically on track as well.

There are actually 10 of us in the Dad Bod Challenge. Here are the conditions:

$5,000 might sound steep, or to some, it might not sound like much. But overall, the penalty needs to feel painful enough to motivate you to lift weights, exercise, and reduce food consumption. A $1,000 penalty would be enough to get me motivated, while another might need a $50,000 penalty.

$50,000 would be a scary number to lose, especially as cash strapped as I am. However, on the flip side, with such a high penalty, there is no way I wouldn't meet my goals.

Overall, we agreed that $5,000 was a high enough penalty. After about 45 days, the judge also offered dads the option to buy an extension for $500 a week, for up to four weeks. If a dad buys the extension and meets his objectives, he gets all his money back and doesn't have to pay the $5,000 penalty.

This lifeline was a great relief for me given my slower-than-expected progress. But I'm also reluctant to take it because I need PAIN AND PENALTY to keep me motivated.

If you think losing weight is hard, try losing fat and gaining muscle at the same time. Way harder.

Muscle needs protein and calories to grow. A calorie deficit, which is how you lose fat, starves your muscles of the fuel they need. So the two goals fight each other.

Losing weight on its own is relatively simple. Track what you eat and run a daily calorie deficit large enough to hit your fat-loss target by a certain date. You don’t have to do more. You just have to eat less. Exercise helps, but roughly 80% of weight loss comes from diet. And if you exercise too much, you might overeat.

Building lean mass, which is mostly muscle, requires lifting weights. And not casual lifting. You need to lift to failure, where you can’t squeeze out another rep or two. That usually means at least three sets of 8 to 12 reps, adding weight over time to discover what you can really do.

When I was young, piling on more plates was a fun challenge. Now my joints and tendons ache when I push to the max, and that’s when the injury risk spikes.

Fellow dads have torn biceps, triceps, and pecs, and thrown out their backs lifting. My 70-year-old uncle, who looks like He-Man, tore a shoulder muscle this year and needed surgery. He’s out for at least two months.

If I get seriously injured, I will most likely fail the dad bod challenge. To win, or at least not lose, every dad must hit both his fat-loss and lean-mass goals at the final scan concurrently. You can’t hit your muscle target two months in and then lose it during the final 45 days because you focused only on cutting.

Body recomposition is far harder than doing either one alone. And this is my first time doing it

I hadn’t regularly lifted weights in over 10 years before this challenge.

The reasons were mostly laziness and boredom. I also have a shoulder injury that’s nagged me for at least six years. During COVID, I couldn’t throw a softball overhand without pain, so I mostly played first base and occasionally third. I’d rather not have surgery.

My hope was that going from zero to one would produce big gains. But I needed to be careful. If I hurt myself further, I might not be able to lift at all.

For 45 days, I watched what I ate. I cut way back on sugar and carbs and loaded up on protein shakes and chicken. On top of playing tennis and pickleball three times a week for about 1.5 hours a session, I added 30 to 40 minutes of walking a day.

After all that, my DEXA scan showed I’d lost only 2.6 pounds of fat and gained 1.4 pounds of lean tissue. I expected at least 4 pounds of fat loss, so I was disappointed. But I’m pleased with the lean tissue gain.

The good news is I still have about two months to go.

Over the next two months, I need to gain another 1 pound of lean mass and lose 11 pounds of fat. That’s a net loss of 10 pounds, which would put me at about 156.5 pounds. But a DEXA scan can easily fluctuate by half a pound, so my safer target is 156 or less.

I haven’t been under 160 pounds in 26 years, but I’ve dreamed about it, just like how I've dreamt of buying a mansion in paradise. Then reality sets in and bludgeons my hopes into the ground.

Kei Nishikori, a pro tennis player I’ve followed, has played at around 155 pounds at 5'10”. If he can do it, so can I. Of course, he’s a professional athlete who works out five hours a day and I’m a 49-year-old dad who writes. Still, I accepted the challenge.

As a tennis and pickleball player, I want to be lean. Big muscles are nice, but they slow down my speed and hands. It’s one reason you don’t see muscular players on the pro tour. To each their own.

The best part of a Dad Bod Challenge is the group. Everybody is supportive and brings a different nutrition and fitness background. We cheer each other on and answer each other’s questions. Although, some remain completely silent.

I don’t think anybody is primarily motivated by winning the other dads’ money. Everybody seems motivated by the chance to improve their health.

What’s interesting is that the goals aren’t uniform. We entered knowing the penalty and our own targets, but once the challenge started, we could see everyone else’s.

The judges tried to set fair goals and meet each dad where he was. But after seeing all the numbers, I probably should have negotiated a smaller fat-loss target.

For example, my buddy is two inches shorter than me and started 38 pounds heavier. He has to get down to about 196 pounds while I have to get down to 156 pounds. Our body compositions are different, as he has more lean mass, but losing a similar percentage of fat when you have less of it is harder.

That's life. Which brings me to the lessons a Dad Bod Challenge teaches about money and life.

Getting ripped and wealthy doesn't just happen. You've got to make it happen. Nobody is coming to save you.

The only reason I exercise is because tennis, pickleball, and softball are fun. If they weren’t, I wouldn’t. I don’t run for fun. I don’t walk for fun, although I do enjoy a nice hike.

This lack of fun is the main reason I stopped lifting weights. If I’m going to lift something heavy, I want to be productive, like hauling rocks and bags of mulch for a landscaping project. Curling a dumbbell in a gym felt pointless.

A Dad Bod Challenge forces discipline when fun isn’t enough. It gets you out the door for a 6 am run before work, or to the gym at 9 pm once the kids are in bed. The challenge also helps you get used to that throbbing feeling of hunger, which may make you more appreciative of the things you have.

Make the challenge long enough, 100 to 180 days, and the discipline compounds until it becomes part of your weekly routine.

Building wealth requires the same consistency. You need the discipline to save as much of each paycheck as possible, and the courage to put those savings at risk. Historically, stocks have gone up in roughly 70% of years. That’s when you feel great about your efforts, like losing fat and gaining muscle. The other 30% of the time, you lose a little or a lot and curse your timing, like getting injured.

Stick with both routines for 10 years and you’ll be blown away by the results. But staying disciplined for that long is hard. That’s where a workout buddy more gung-ho than you helps. It’s also where an automatic 401(k) contribution from every paycheck and an AI investment advisor helps.

Some people are born to wealthy, loving parents. Others are born into poverty, given up for adoption, or lose their parents as babies. Some people befriend the founders of a hot AI company and join before it hits it big. Others join startup after startup that goes bust.

The body is similar. Some people are born with fast metabolisms and uncanny strength. Others pack on weight despite working out constantly. Tall, skinny people may struggle to add muscle. Shorter, stockier people may bulk up with ease.

At 49, I'm one of the oldest dads in the group. The youngest is 39. Ah, I remember 39. I was five years into FIRE, traveling the world, and recovering much faster than I do now. I also didn’t have my shoulder and knee injuries. But the challenge gives older dads no handicap. No easier fat-loss targets, no lower lean-mass goals, no extra free time.

Compare your goals to someone else’s and you might get annoyed, because you have completely different genetics. The judges try to set fair goals, but they won’t get it perfect every time.

We can’t control the hand we’re dealt. We can only control our effort and attitude. So compare your progress to your old self and nobody else. If you hit your own goals, you won’t lose. If you happen to win some money too, great. The money was never the point.

What I love about my Dad Bod Challenge is that the creators and judges are fitness fanatics. They’re smart, enthusiastic, kind, and extremely fit. They’ve experienced the benefits of being in shape, so they’re paying it forward by organizing a challenge for 10 dads who are nowhere near as fit.

I’ve learned a ton about how to train, what to eat, how to recover, and how to supplement with protein. One example: spread your protein out, eating about 20 to 25 grams every few hours instead of cramming it all into one meal for better utilization.

Oh, and did you know a tablespoon of olive oil is around 120-140 calories?! Nuts!

I also lost all my excuses. I used to tell myself only people without kids or demanding jobs could get extremely fit. But the judges are dads and successful entrepreneurs. Excuses gone.

Paying it forward is also why I’ve published three times a week on Financial Samurai for over 17 years. No courses, no paywalls, just free content based on my experiences to help you reach financial freedom sooner. When I find a great strategy, like negotiating a severance if you plan on quitting anyway, I find it impossible not to share it.

Before this challenge, I had never measured my lean mass. I just stepped on a scale. But a scale lies by omission. You could lose two pounds of muscle and think you’re making progress. That's no good, especially for folks over 40 who begin to lose muscle more rapidly.

The DEXA scan showed me the truth. After 45 days, I’d lost 2.6 pounds of fat and gained 1.4 pounds of lean body mass. The scale only showed a 1.2-pound difference, which would have been demoralizing. The DEXA showed real recomposition, but also still a bummer because I thought I would have made greater gains.

Finances work the same way. Plenty of people only look at their income or checking account balance and think they’re doing fine. But income is just the scale. Net worth is the DEXA scan. It shows whether you’re building assets or just shuffling money around while debt quietly piles up.

I’ve tracked my net worth since 1999 and it’s one of the best financial habits I’ve ever built. Whatever you measure regularly, you naturally start to improve.

Empower is what I use to track my net worth once I moved on from spreadsheets in 2012. Mezzi is what I use to track my public investments and gain powerful financial insights to better optimize my portfolio.

At the end of the day, I want to build muscle and lose fat to increase my chances of living a longer, healthier life.

That’s primarily why I think more people should join a Dad Bod or Mom Bod Challenge. Even if you miss your exact targets, you’ll likely finish in better shape than when you started.

In high school and college, I stayed in shape to have the widest dating pool possible. My four years at William & Mary were a blast.

Once I started working, good dieting and regular exercise went out the window. During my first five years, staying under 170 pounds was a struggle. Eventually I settled into the mid-160s, playing competitive USTA tennis for 10 years and wanting every edge I could get.

Now, I want as much energy as possible to play with my kids. And I want to live long enough to meet my grandchildren.

So if you need a reason to get in shape and change your body composition, organize a Dad Bod or Mom Bod Challenge. Even if you lose, you’ll probably make new friends and end up fitter.

Here’s to greater strength and 156 pounds or less by December 23, 2026. More importantly, here’s to a lifetime of healthier habits going forward.

#982. 40 years later, Chernobyl's nuclear fallout is not what scientists expected

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Four decades after the Chernobyl (known today as Chornobyl) nuclear disaster, microscopic radioactive particles remain scattered throughout the surrounding soil. The explosions that devastated the reactor released enormous quantities of debris, leaving behind contamination that continues to pose serious health risks. Even after 40 years, particles measuring just 8 to 50 micrometers remain highly radioactive, and entering affected areas still requires protective clothing.

Scientists have now taken a closer look at these tiny remnants and uncovered a surprising finding: some have preserved much of their original internal structure despite decades of exposure to the environment.

"There are three classes of these particles," explains Tobias Weissenborn, a physicist and doctoral candidate at Leibniz University Hannover. "First, there are particles that are chemically and physically still very similar to the nuclear fuel uranium dioxide. Then, there are particles that are partially or fully encased in, or completely fused with, their zirconium layer."

The second category originated during the extreme temperatures reached inside the reactor. As the nuclear fuel melted, it combined with zirconium, a highly resistant metal used in the fuel's protective outer layer.

"The third type of particle was created when the graphite-moderator caught fire," Weissenborn adds.

Under normal operating conditions, the graphite moderator slows fast neutrons, helping sustain the nuclear chain reaction. During the disaster, however, the graphite ignited and continued burning for ten days.

"In the process, the fuel oxidized into various uranium oxides" - such as U3O8.

Unlike more stable materials, this uranium oxide is mechanically fragile and can readily break apart into microscopic fragments. Wind can then carry these radioactive particles through the environment, where inhaling them presents serious health hazards.

Scientists Examine Chornobyl's Radioactive Dust in Unprecedented Detail

Despite decades of research, scientists still do not fully understand why radioactive particles from Chornobyl break down at different speeds. As these materials weather, they can produce several forms of uranium oxide, making their long term behavior difficult to predict.

Investigating these changes requires exceptionally precise equipment, including synchrotron X-ray diffraction, a technique that reveals the arrangement of atoms inside crystalline materials.

Dr. Christoph Hennig, a crystallographer at the Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf (HZDR), has now completed the first successful structural analysis of radioactive fragments of this kind at the Rossendorf Beamline in Grenoble.

Working with Weissenborn, Hennig analyzed six particles recovered from the Chornobyl region. By identifying the different crystalline phases within them, the researchers gained information that could help determine how quickly radioactive substances escape into the surrounding environment.

"The colleagues from Hanover and I used various methods to isolate the particles from Ukrainian soil samples and attach them to tungsten electrodes," Weissenborn explains.

Because the samples were highly radioactive, they had to be enclosed within several layers of containment before being transported to Grenoble. There, Hennig's research team used X-ray diffraction to examine their internal structures by measuring how X-rays scattered as they passed through the material.

"First, we had to figure out how to examine the particle structure," Christoph Hennig explains. "Ultimately, we fully rotated the particles in an X-ray beam focused down to 100 micrometers - about the thickness of a human hair. We measured each particle from 2,000 different angles to seamlessly capture all the reflections."

The measurements allowed the scientists to distinguish different uranium oxide phases within the radioactive fragments, revealing details that had previously been inaccessible.

A Surprising Discovery About 40-Year-Old Nuclear Fallout

The results revealed something unexpected. Despite spending four decades in the environment, the nuclear fuel inside the six examined particles had retained much of its original crystal structure.

This suggests that at least some of the radioactive debris produced by the Chornobyl disaster is more chemically stable than researchers previously believed.

That stability could have important environmental implications. Rather than rapidly releasing radioactive substances, the particles appear capable of retaining fission products within their structures. If this behavior occurs more widely, it could help limit the movement of radioactive contaminants into nearby soil and water.

However, the researchers emphasize that their findings should not be interpreted as evidence that Chornobyl's radioactive contamination is becoming harmless.

"However, every single particle has a different structure," warns Weissenborn, "and our experiment only studied six such particles from two different locations."

The relatively small sample means the findings cannot yet establish how radioactive particles behave throughout the entire contaminated region. To understand their stability more comprehensively, scientists would need to investigate many additional particles collected from a much broader range of locations.

Why Chornobyl's Radioactive Legacy Remains Unpredictable

Even with substantially more data, predicting the long term dangers of Chornobyl's radioactive debris would remain challenging. Individual particles can differ in composition, structure, and resistance to environmental weathering, potentially affecting when they release radioactive materials.

"And even if we obtained some averages at some point, we still wouldn't be able to make universal statements about health risks in the region," Weissenborn cautions. "Because even if the particles decay in a largely uniform patten, there will always be outliers, more persistent particles, that will release radionuclides at a later point in time."

These persistent particles could continue releasing radioactive substances long after other remnants have broken down, complicating efforts to assess future contamination and health risks.

For that reason, the new findings do not suggest that restrictions on the Chornobyl Exclusion Zone can be removed anytime soon.

Research into the disaster's enduring consequences is continuing. Weissenborn and Hennig are already conducting additional experiments focused on highly radioactive transuranic phases found in the remaining debris. Their work could provide a clearer picture of how Chornobyl's nuclear materials change over time and how long their environmental effects may persist.

The largest collection of exploding white dwarf stars ever compiled is providing fresh evidence that dark energy, the mysterious force responsible for the Universe’s accelerating expansion, may change over time. The findings challenge a central assumption of modern cosmology and could help scientists better understand how the Universe evolves.

An international research team led by scientists at the University of Queensland’s School of Mathematics and Physics brought together observations of 2,884 Type Ia supernovae. These powerful stellar explosions serve as important tools for measuring distances across the Universe, allowing astronomers to trace its expansion history.

PhD candidate Ryan Camilleri said the project establishes a new international standard for supernova cosmology. By bringing decades of measurements into a unified dataset, researchers have created their most detailed picture yet of how cosmic expansion has changed over time.

“We’ve rebuilt 3 decades of astronomical observations into a single, consistent framework,” Mr. Camilleri said.

“We combined our data with other cosmic measurements including relic light from the Big Bang and maps of how galaxies are distributed through space.

“Instead of confirming the standard model of cosmology which assumes dark energy is fixed and unchanging, we have more evidence that dark energy may change over time.”

The new collection brings together historical supernova measurements and findings from the Dark Energy Survey (DES) published in 2024.

To make observations collected over different periods directly comparable, the scientists revisited older data using updated analytical methods. Advances in understanding supernova behavior allowed the team to improve the consistency and accuracy of measurements gathered over decades.

“Over the years we’ve learned a lot more about how supernovae behave so we’ve been able to go back and apply that improved understanding to older data,” Mr. Camilleri said.

The researchers also had to account for differences among the telescopes used to collect the observations. Other factors, including cosmic dust and the masses of galaxies, can influence the light astronomers receive from distant supernovae and therefore affect distance measurements.

“Extensive work has been done to link observations from different telescopes with different capacities and take into account issues such as cosmic dust and galaxy mass which can affect the light coming from a supernova.

“We also incorporated more subtle effects such as gravitational lensing, which is the bending and magnification of light around large objects as it travels from a supernova to Earth.”

Astrophysicist Professor Tamara Davis said the expanded dataset represents an important advance in efforts to understand the nature of dark energy.

Under the standard model of cosmology, dark energy is treated as a constant property of the Universe. However, recent observations have raised questions about whether that assumption fully explains how cosmic expansion has evolved.

“Our supernova data from DES in 2024 first showed hints that dark energy may be time varying, and this new compilation also sees a deviation from the standard model although in a slightly different direction,” Professor Davis said.

The findings are especially interesting because a separate astronomical project has also produced evidence suggesting that dark energy might not remain constant.

“Similarly results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) found hints of variations in dark energy in its surveys of relic sound waves from the early Universe.

“So, 2 completely independent measurements have found hints of time variation in dark energy, challenging the standard model that dark energy doesn't change.

“All of this research may also hold the clue to explain how gravity and quantum physics fit together.

A new optical sensor costing just $10 could bring laboratory-level precision to wearable health monitors, potentially allowing people to track blood glucose without needles. The technology could also transform food testing, industrial quality control, and environmental monitoring.

Researchers at the University of Cambridge and GlitterinTech (a start-up founded by the same research group) have developed a new type of optical spectrometer that combines exceptional accuracy with a remarkably small size and low cost. Measuring just centimeters across, the device could be incorporated into portable electronics and wearable health technology. Its potential applications range from monitoring important health markers in real time to identifying materials and detecting changes in chemical composition.

Optical spectrometers work by analyzing light to reveal information about the substances it interacts with. They are essential tools in medicine, manufacturing, chemical testing, and environmental research. However, making these instruments smaller has traditionally meant compromising their performance. Compact spectrometers often have limited measurement ranges, reduced resolution, or lower accuracy, making them better suited to identifying substances than performing precise measurements.

The researchers believe their new design could overcome these limitations. Known as a convolutional spectrometer, it uses a mathematical principle called the convolution theorem to analyze light in a fundamentally different way. According to the team, this approach delivers performance that surpasses existing types of spectrometers in several key areas, including designs based on light dispersion, Fourier transforms, and computational reconstruction.

A New Way to Analyze Light With Extraordinary Precision

Traditional spectrometers generally separate incoming light into its component wavelengths or use complex calculations to reconstruct its spectrum. The new device takes another approach by performing a mathematical operation called convolution directly on the incoming light.

To accomplish this, the researchers arranged optical components with repeating spectral responses in a sequence. These components can include unbalanced Mach-Zehnder interferometers or micro-ring resonators. By adjusting them in proportion to one another, the system can shift its response to different wavelengths in a controlled, linear fashion. Fast Fourier transforms are then used to accurately recover the light spectrum.

"The key insight was to go back to the mathematics and ask whether there was a fundamentally cleaner way to retrieve spectra," said Dr. Chunhui Yao, a lead author of the study. "By using the convolution theorem directly in the optical domain, we avoid many of the limitations that have held miniaturized spectrometers back. This gives us high precision, strong noise tolerance, and very low computational overhead, all in a compact and low-cost system."

The researchers built the spectrometer using a silicon nitride photonic platform and integrated it with onboard electronics. It operates across a broad range of near-infrared wavelengths (1200-1700 nm) and can collect and process measurements in less than a second.

Another important advantage is its ability to expand its measurement capabilities without major hardware changes. Because the system uses repeating optical patterns, its spectral bandwidth can theoretically be extended almost without limit. Adding more optical components in sequence can also increase its resolution exponentially.

A $10 Sensor That Could Transform Food Testing and Healthcare

To evaluate the device outside purely theoretical measurements, the researchers tested it on a variety of materials and substances. The results demonstrated its potential for applications that normally depend on larger, more expensive laboratory instruments.

When used to identify plastics, pharmaceuticals, coffee, flour, and tea, the spectrometer achieved a 100% classification success rate in the reported tests. It also measured concentrations in water-based and organic solutions with accuracies of approximately 0.01%, exceeding the performance of commercial benchtop spectrometers in those experiments.

The healthcare demonstrations were particularly promising. Under conditions designed to reflect real physiological environments, the device accurately measured several human biomarkers, including skin moisture, blood alcohol, blood lactate, and blood glucose.

The researchers also demonstrated noninvasive glucose tracking over an extended period in one participant. Although further testing would be needed before the technology could become a practical medical product, the finding suggests a possible route toward monitoring blood sugar without repeatedly piercing the skin.

"These biomedical demonstrations are particularly exciting," said Prof. Qixiang Cheng, who led the project. "What makes this work stand out is not just performance in the lab, but technical readiness. Dr. Chunhui Yao's contribution was crucial in translating a mathematical concept into a fully packaged, robust system that operates reliably across temperature extremes and real-world conditions. That combination is what opens the door to practical deployment."

The sensor also demonstrated impressive resistance to temperature changes. It continued operating reliably between -20°C and 80°C, an unusually wide temperature range for a miniature spectrometer. This durability could be especially valuable for wearable devices, factory equipment, and sensors operating outdoors.

Why a Simpler Design Could Be a Major Breakthrough

The new spectrometer's advantages extend beyond its accuracy, size, and price. Its internal design also reduces the amount of computing power needed to analyze incoming light.

Many existing miniature spectrometers depend on complicated calibration procedures and demanding algorithms to reconstruct spectral information. These methods can become unreliable when measurements contain noise, and they often require substantial processing resources.

The Cambridge team's convolution-based approach avoids much of this complexity. Because the measurements follow a linear mathematical relationship, the system can recover spectra rapidly and consistently while using relatively little computational power.

"This is a beautiful example of how elegant engineering can unlock real impact," said Prof. Richard Penty, who contributed to the photonic system design and integration. "The architecture is simple, scalable, and manufacturable, yet it delivers precision that rivals or exceeds much larger instruments. Dr. Chunhui Yao played a central role in bringing together the photonic design, system integration, and experimental validation that made this possible."

Its relatively simple construction could also make the technology easier to manufacture on a large scale, potentially bringing sophisticated optical measurements to devices where conventional spectrometers would be too bulky or expensive.

Bringing Laboratory-Grade Measurements to Everyday Devices

The researchers envision a future in which accurate optical sensing becomes a routine feature of everyday technology rather than something largely confined to specialized laboratories.

In industrial settings, inexpensive spectrometers could be installed throughout manufacturing facilities to continuously check material composition and product quality. Similar sensors could monitor food freshness, identify substances, and analyze environmental conditions on a much larger scale than is currently practical.

Healthcare could see equally significant changes. Compact sensors might eventually enable wearables to measure hydration levels, detect signs of alcohol intoxication, track biomarkers related to physical fitness, or continuously monitor glucose levels for people managing diabetes.

"Our vision is to make spectrometry as ubiquitous as temperature or motion sensing," added Prof. Qixiang Cheng. "This work shows that high-quality spectral information doesn't have to be confined to the laboratory -- it can be embedded directly into the technologies people use every day."

As photonic technology becomes smaller and more advanced, the convolutional spectrometer provides a promising example of how mathematical insights and practical engineering can make sophisticated scientific instruments simpler, cheaper, and more accessible. By combining high precision with a device that costs around $10, the researchers have opened a potential path toward a new generation of smart sensors capable of measuring the hidden chemistry of our bodies and surroundings.

There are a lot of paradoxes in psychology—the harder you try to fall asleep, the less likely you will; the more you try to avoid thinking negative thoughts, the more likely they will pop into your head; and according to newer research, your greatest meaning in life can come from your greatest pains.

As a clinical psychologist specializing in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), I’ve spent many therapy hours straddling the spaces of pain and meaning. As Steven Hayes, founder of ACT, shared with me on Your Life in Process Podcast, “You hurt where you care.”

Let’s try an experiment to illustrate this coupling. Consider the following activities: Go through the list below, mentally check off those that are both uncomfortable and meaningful.

Which activities did you check? What else would you add? Where else in your life do you do difficult things that are also important to you? What is Discomfort Tolerance? The mindful practice of working with difficult feelings rather than avoiding them, recognizing that meaningful growth often requires discomfort.

Core Insight: A meaningful life isn’t free from discomfort but comes from pursuing what matters despite difficulty.

The more you engage in meaningful activities, the more likely you are to experience discomfort. If you love someone or something, you might know this by now. Not only is discomfort associated with purposeful activities like parenting, service work, owning a pet, and social justice, but experiencing pain can be pleasurable in its own right.

For example, hot yoga, deep tissue massage, eating spicy food, and running long distances can be enjoyable because, according to Paul Bloom, author of The Sweet Spot: The Pleasures of Suffering and the Search for Meaning, they create contrast, provide a break from self-rumination, and socially signal strength and competence.

Pain may also play an important role in our dopamine balance. Our brain seeks homeostasis by upping our pain to balance out too much pleasure. Taking a “dopamine detox” and residing more on the pain side of the equation paradoxically resets our brain to experience pleasure more fully.

As a therapist, I ask my clients to choose pain in the service of supporting what is most meaningful to them. Exposure therapy is at the foundation of some of the work I do, and much of it is based on the principle of moving toward pain to increase your “zone of flexibility.” With exposure, you flexibly welcome what is uncomfortable when it allows you to live a richer and fuller life.

Most mornings, I get up early to run, lift heavy things, or practice sitting in silence—all in the service of health and well-being. If someone were to force me to do any of these activities, I might feel misery. Having a choice is a powerful psychological tool. Choosing to experience pain instead of resisting it changes your relationship with it. You can even take on the mindset of choice with activities that feel “inflicted on you” by shifting your mindset to “yes brain,” as Dan Siegel calls it. Consider attending a social event: How would your relationship with the event shift if you changed your mindset to choosing to be there versus being “forced” to go? Choose to take a step toward what is uncomfortable, especially when what you care about is in the same direction.

When we are experiencing pain, we often are so engrossed in avoiding it or being overwhelmed by it we lose track of what’s important in the moment. . Imagine you have two dials—a pain dial and a meaning dial. When you turn the meaning dial, it automatically turns the pain dial. They are tethered! Should you turn down the meaning dial? Of course not. Instead, we can practice making space for discomfort while focusing on the meaning dial’s benefits. You may have greater distress tolerance and resilience when focused on meaning and purpose.

Often the anticipation of pain can be worse than the pain itself! Our minds can tell us terrible stories about what is coming next. When choosing to enter an uncomfortable situation, stay in the present moment with the discomfort: Just this minute, just this moment. Present-moment focus helps free you from your mind’s doom and gloom about the pain that is to come. You do not need to tolerate future pain; you just need to accept the discomfort of this moment. Now, that is doable.

One way we try to control pain is to worry and ruminate. Physicializing is an effective practice to help you get out of your head and into the sensations of your body. When you notice a physical or emotional discomfort, try turning toward it in your body. How might you describe the discomfort’s shape, color, movement, or weight? By turning toward and observing your discomfort, you do three things: You give your mind a job other than ruminating and worrying; you develop an observer-self who can recognize you are not your pain even as you experience pain; and you befriend your discomfort. Pain is less scary when you welcome it.

Building mastery, growing relationships, and learning new skills are uncomfortable for everyone. Remember that discomfort is part of the human experience.

And choosing to practice compassion in relationships can be painful at times. When you choose pain in the service of compassion, you grow a more resilient, courageous mind and heart. No one is immune to suffering and our suffering is interconnected. Turning toward the suffering of others with the energy of compassion, understanding, and care is the greatest gift you can give to another being and to our planet.

Pain and discomfort are part of a well-lived life. Practice opening yourself to your full experience, and you may discover greater freedom.

The Supreme Court on Thursday afternoon cleared the way for New Hampshire to bar the use of student IDs as the valid photo ID required to obtain a ballot there. In a brief, unsigned order, the justices paused a ruling by a federal judge in New Hampshire, citing the principle (often known as the Purcell principle) that “lower federal courts should ordinarily not alter the election rules on the eve of an election.”

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson was the lone dissenter. She contended that U.S. District Judge Joseph Laplante had merely “issued a targeted order” that was “reasonably designed to address alleged misinformation, not to change the law.”

Legislators in New Hampshire passed the law, known as HB 323, in April, and it went into effect in June. The Coalition for Open Democracy, a nonprofit that (among other things) helps high schools to promote and run voter-registration drives, went to federal court, where it argued that the law violates the Constitution because it burdens the right to vote and because it was intended to discriminate against young voters.

In an order earlier this month, Laplante declined to block the provision of HB 323 that, he said, “removed student ID cards from the forms of identification that are expressly valid to obtain a ballot.” However, he barred the state from enforcing a directive, issued by Secretary of State David Scanlan in April, “to the extent it prohibits or discourages election officials from considering student IDs as evidence of voter identity” under a separate “safety-valve provision that permits a voter without qualifying photo identification to establish identity by other means.”

After the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 1st Circuit turned down the state’s request to pause Laplante’s ruling, Scanlan came to the Supreme Court, asking the justices to step in. Laplante’s order, he said, requires the state to undo all of the steps that it has taken since April, when he issued his directive, to prepare for the election. Among other things, he said, his office “published student voting guides, updated polling place posters, updated its website, conducted social media outreach, produced and ran radio ads, and spoke at public events to explain how identity verification would work.” Moreover, Scanlan continued, the district court did so, “based on its own interpretation of state law, while generating more questions than it answers. These are the precise circumstances in which the considerations animating” the Purcell principle “are at their apex,” he concluded.

The Coalition for Open Democracy countered that, unlike other cases involving the Purcell principle, Laplante did not block a state law; instead, his order “enforces the legislature’s statutes against an executive gloss exceeding the statutes’ reach.” It further argued that Scanlan has already made the changes required by Laplante to his website, which now indicates “that supervisors may consider a student ID along with other evidence. The Secretary’s revised guidance is now the status quo,” the group emphasized.

In its one-paragraph order on Thursday, the justices stayed Laplante’s ruling while the state’s appeal moves forward.

In her dissent, Jackson argued that “the relief the District Court provided actually promoted the interests Purcell sought to protect. When a federal court determines that material misrepresentations are being made about a State’s election rules, an injunction to prohibit that conduct does not contribute to public confusion, it prevents that very problem.”

Yet these two stories are too often kept apart. Histories of American Jews understandably focus on topics such as immigration, religious life, economic achievement, antisemitism, and cultural assimilation. Studies of the Founding, meanwhile, emphasize constitutional design, republican theory, federalism, and democratic institutions but too rarely ask what those arrangements meant for religious minorities like the Jews. The result is a serious gap in the national story: What does the American Jewish experience reveal about the republic the Founders created, and how should Americans talk about that in an age of rising antisemitism?

That question matters because antisemitism is not only a security problem or a social prejudice. It is also a symptom of historical amnesia and civic decay. When Jews are cast as strangers to the national story, or when American history is reduced to a permanent struggle between oppressors and victims, the actual record disappears.

The Jewish experience in America included real prejudice and exclusion, but it was never defined by them alone. Over time, the country widened membership and drew a vulnerable minority into responsibility for the nation they now regarded as their own.

The United States did not erase prejudice, exclusion, or suspicion from public life. At various points in time, Jews faced antisemitism, social barriers, university quotas, professional discrimination, and moments of political hostility. The difference was not that America had no prejudice. It was that Jewish life no longer depended on charters, royal favor, or the whims of rulers. In America, the ground gradually shifted. Jews entered public life and eventually assumed responsibility for many of the country’s most important institutions.

This report is not simply a catalogue of notable Jewish achievements. The deeper question is what those achievements say about America. The answer, drawn from the historical record, is striking. The Founders created a system in which Jews could move from formal equality to full participation in American public life. American Jewish history is therefore part of the history of the republic itself, not merely the story of one minority community.

This framing offers a grounded conservative alternative to the identity-driven accounts that increasingly dominate discussions of minority life in America. Recovering this history is not group self-congratulation. It is historical truth. Jews were not guests in the American story. From the Revolution forward, they helped to finance independence, defend the nation, interpret its laws, build its institutions, strengthen civil society, expand commerce, advance science, and enrich the country’s shared culture.

America offered a form of belonging that did not require Jews to surrender their faith or identity. It enabled Jews to become defenders and guardians of liberty, something they had rarely, if ever, experienced throughout the centuries in the Diaspora. That matters for America 250 because it underlines what makes this country truly unique. Restoring this history is therefore an act of civic renewal.

For that reason, this report is organized around institutions rather than biography alone. Biography matters because men and women make history and because antisemitic narratives so often erase the humanity and patriotism of actual Jews. Still, the larger purpose is not biographical. Each figure discussed here illuminates a different part of American life: the Constitution, the armed forces, the judiciary, voluntary associations, free enterprise, scientific inquiry, and national culture.

A list of Jewish accomplishments may stir pride, but it will not necessarily strengthen constitutional literacy. It can even miss the deeper lesson if those achievements are presented as isolated curiosities rather than evidence of how a free republic works. The task is to connect individual lives to the laws, practices, and institutions that made their public service possible.

For policymakers and civic educators, this history should be taught as constitutional history because America’s religious freedom became a key source of national strength. Equal citizenship became meaningful through actual participation in the courts, the armed forces, commerce, education, and public life. America’s inheritance remains worth defending because it allowed different communities to join a common national project without dissolving into sameness.

From Toleration to Citizenship: The Constitutional Transformation of Jewish Political Life

The emergence of the United States opened a new chapter in Jewish political history. For centuries, Jewish communities had lived as persecuted minorities whose standing depended on rulers, charters, and the arbitrary and often uncertain mercy of toleration. The American Founding did not erase that inheritance at once. But it began to change the terms of Jewish political life in a way that was historically extraordinary.

The key change was the replacement of conditional toleration with citizenship grounded in law rather than indulgence. That change can be seen in the Constitution, in the language used by national leaders, and in the conduct of early American Jews themselves, who already behaved as if the republic was theirs to defend and build. That is why the Jewish experience in the Revolutionary and early national periods matters far beyond the small size of the Jewish population at the time.

By the eve of the American Revolution, Jewish life in the colonies remained modest in size but meaningful in impact. The approximately 2,500 Jews living in British North America[REF] represented only a tiny fraction of the colonial population, yet their experience reflected broader features of colonial political culture that would later shape the American framework. Unlike much of Europe, the colonies lacked a single, uniform religious establishment. Authority was divided among separate colonies, local institutions exercised real influence, and commercial relationships often encouraged cooperation across religious boundaries. These conditions did not produce civic equality in the modern sense, nor did they eliminate legal disabilities or social prejudice. But they did create an environment in which religious diversity could increasingly be accommodated without threatening political stability.

The 1787 Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia. The delegates who assembled for the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia in May 1787 faced the challenge of designing a stronger national government. They had to create a political home for a remarkably diverse people. Their answer was bold: National unity would not rest on a common church but rather on a shared American framework. Article VI of the Constitution declared that “no religious Test shall ever be required as a Qualification to any Office or public Trust under the United States.”[REF] The sentence was brief, but its implications were enormous.

Ratified in 1791, the First Amendment carried the same idea further by denying Congress the power to establish a national church and by protecting the free exercise of religion.[REF] Together, Article VI and the First Amendment gave Jews a national language of belonging that could be claimed not as a favor, but as a right. That marked a decisive break from the older world of permissions, privileges, and revocable toleration. The new order did not depend on the mood of a monarch, the convenience of a local magistrate, or a life of dhimmitude in Muslim lands. It rested on a public commitment.

The federal achievement of the Founders becomes even clearer when placed against the uneven record of the states. The Constitution did not instantly abolish every state-level religious restriction, and the early republic still contained remnants that elevated Protestant political culture above all others. But the national framework changed the argument. Once the Constitution rejected religious tests and protected free exercise, state-level exclusions based on religious identity looked increasingly inconsistent with the country’s stated principles. That gave minorities a vocabulary with which to challenge inherited disabilities and press the republic toward upholding its own commitments. Religious discrimination increasingly had to be justified in light of those principles rather than accepted as the natural order of things.

James Madison and Thomas Jefferson. James Madison, a principal architect of the Constitution, and Thomas Jefferson, the author of the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom, matter here because their defense of religious liberty created a public language broad enough to include Jews. Madison’s Memorial and Remonstrance, which he penned anonymously in 1785, argued against coercive religious assessments, while Jefferson’s statute declared that civil rights should not depend on religious opinion. These arguments helped to move religious liberty from prudence to justice. For Jews, that shift mattered profoundly. Civic standing could now be defended as a right, not requested as a concession.

But legal provisions tell only part of the story. One of the most revealing examples is the correspondence exchanged between the Hebrew Congregation of Newport, Rhode Island, and President George Washington in August 1790. Moses Seixas, the congregation’s warden and a civic leader in Newport, praised the new government for affording “liberty of conscience, and immunities of Citizenship.”[REF] Washington’s reply sharpened the point. “It is now no more,” he wrote, “that toleration is spoken of, as if it was by the indulgence of one class of people, that another enjoyed the exercise of their inherent natural rights.” The passage rejected the older logic of toleration and replaced it with the language of natural rights and citizenship.[REF]

Washington’s letter has endured because it stated a clear republican standard: Jews were not to be tolerated as useful outsiders but protected as citizens whose rights were inherent. The difference was not semantic. Toleration implies hierarchy; one group permits another to exist. Citizenship implies equality under law; members of the political community possess rights by virtue of their humanity and their status as citizens. That shift became one of the defining achievements of the American Founding.

The Newport exchange was more than a gracious note sent to a small congregation. It publicly defined the relationship between the new government and its Jewish citizens. Washington did not ask Jews to prove that they deserved toleration, nor did he describe their security as a gift from the Christian majority. He placed them within the same framework of natural rights that protected every other citizen. For American Jews, that language offered both reassurance and a standard by which the conduct of government could be judged.

Haym Salomon. Washington’s words carried special weight because they reflected Jewish actions during the Revolution itself. Haym Salomon, a Polish-born Jewish immigrant, merchant, and broker, joined the Sons of Liberty, was arrested by the British, escaped confinement, and helped to marshal the funds that sustained the Patriot cause. He was not merely a donor. He used his commercial skill, linguistic ability, and credit networks to turn patriotic conviction into material support when the cause was on the brink. Legend has it that in 1781, when Continental troops threatened mutiny over unpaid wages, it was Salomon who arranged for $20,000 to fund the Battle of Yorktown, which decided the war for independence.[REF] From 1781 to 1784, Salomon worked closely with Robert Morris, the Superintendent of Finance, negotiating bills of exchange and raising badly needed funds for the government. Salomon’s name appears in Morris’s diary at least 75 times, and later accounts credit him with providing or arranging roughly $650,000 in support of the American cause.[REF]

Francis Salvador. Francis Salvador offers a different Revolutionary example. A Sephardic Jew from a prominent family, Salvador settled in South Carolina, entered colonial political life, and became the first Jew elected to a colonial legislature in British North America. He died in combat in 1776 during the struggle for American independence. His life joined political participation and military sacrifice at the birth of the republic. For a community often treated elsewhere as politically suspect, Salvador’s service made a powerful point: Jews could be not only beneficiaries of the American cause, but actors willing to pay the ultimate price in its defense.[REF]

Mordecai Sheftall. Mordecai Sheftall of Savannah provides a third example. A religiously observant Jew, merchant, and communal leader, Sheftall served as a colonel and Commissary-General for Patriot forces in Georgia. His work required logistics, trust, and responsibility for provisioning troops, all of which were central to the survival of the army. His career shows that Jewish participation in the Revolution extended into the practical, unglamorous work of sustaining the war effort.[REF]

No Footnotes to the Revolution. These figures, as well as others, were not footnotes to the Revolution. They risked their lives, spent their fortunes, and sacrificed for the American cause because they believed the new republic offered Jews a different future. They financed, fought, supplied, and served because the American cause promised an alternative to the insecurity Jews had known elsewhere.

The timing of their service is important. Salomon, Salvador, and Sheftall acted before the durability of the American experiment was known and before Jews could be certain what place they would occupy within it. Their loyalty was therefore not a response to generations of settled acceptance. It was a wager on the promise of the Revolution. They believed that the principles being proclaimed could create a country in which Jews would no longer live at the sufferance of rulers, and they helped make that country possible.

The early republic did not produce full equality overnight, but the direction of political life had changed. Article VI, the First Amendment, and Washington’s reply to Moses Seixas gave Jewish citizenship a language sturdier than toleration. The Revolution had already drawn Jews into finance, military service, public administration, and civic life. The Founding gave those acts a firmer home.

Nowhere was this new belonging tested more clearly than in military service. Jews were willing to risk their lives for the republic, and the republic increasingly entrusted them with arms, command, and military responsibility.

Citizenship, Service, and the Defense of the Republic

Military service made the meaning of Jewish citizenship unmistakable. It asked Jews to accept discipline, risk, and sometimes death for a country they regarded as their own. It also required the United States to entrust them with arms, command, and responsibility, duties it would never place in the hands of permanent outsiders.

In many societies, Jews served in armies without gaining equal political standing. The American experience developed differently. Jewish service increasingly reflected belonging rather than conditional acceptance, even as prejudice persisted and the armed forces slowly adapted to religious difference.

The Revolution itself established the pattern. Jewish participation in the Patriot cause made clear that the struggle for independence did not belong to one denomination or one ethnic group. The republic was defended from the beginning by men whose presence complicated older assumptions about political belonging. That fact did not end prejudice, but it set a standard against which prejudice could be judged.

Military service did not by itself eliminate antisemitism or guarantee advancement. Jewish officers and enlisted men could still encounter suspicion and hostility. Yet service gave them a direct claim on the nation whose dangers they shared. It also confronted the country with a practical question that prejudice could not easily answer: How could men be treated as outsiders after they had accepted the same discipline, endured the same hardship, and faced the same enemy as their fellow citizens?

Uriah Phillips Levy. The Navy occupied a special place in the young republic, and Uriah Phillips Levy’s career is tied to that setting. Levy, born in Philadelphia in 1792, was a pioneering Jewish naval officer who rose through the ranks despite antisemitic hostility and became the first Jewish commodore, then the Navy’s highest rank.[REF] He is also remembered for purchasing and preserving Thomas Jefferson’s Monticello in 1834, eight years after the Founding Father’s death, a striking act of patriotic remembrance by a Jewish officer devoted to the author of the Declaration of Independence.[REF]

Levy’s importance did not lie only in his rank but also in the reform he championed. His long campaign against corporal punishment[REF] sought to bring a coercive arm of the state closer to republican ideas of discipline, dignity, and lawful authority. He understood that military effectiveness did not require degrading treatment. Levy’s key contribution was that he worked to make the Navy more consistent with the principles of the republic it defended.

Hyman G. Rickover. A century and a half later, the same pattern appeared on a much larger stage in the career of Admiral Hyman G. Rickover. Rickover, a Jewish immigrant from Poland who arrived in the United States as a child, rose to become a four-star admiral and the architect of the nuclear Navy.[REF] By the mid-20th century, American security depended on scientific innovation, administrative rigor, and relentless attention to safety. Rickover supplied all of them. He built a culture that shaped one of the most important strategic programs of the Cold War.[REF]

The United States relied on Rickover at the highest level of national defense. A Jewish immigrant was entrusted with a program central to deterrence, naval power, and national survival. Trust of that kind cannot be faked.

Jacob Frankel. Jewish military service cannot be measured only by command or innovation. It must also be judged by whether the armed forces made room for religious conscience. During the Civil War, federal law initially limited chaplaincies to ordained ministers of “some Christian denomination,” thereby excluding Jews by statute. The successful effort to change the law and commission Rabbi Jacob Frankel in 1862 as the first Jewish chaplain showed that religious liberty could enter even the most hierarchical institution of the state.[REF]

Frankel’s appointment deserves attention because it turned principle into military practice. Frankel was a prominent Philadelphia rabbi, cantor, and communal leader, and his commission made clear that Jewish soldiers did not shed their religious identity when they put on the uniform. They remained citizens with consciences, and the Army recognized that fact. The change in the law showed that Jewish soldiers could serve as Jews, not despite being Jews.

The Army also acknowledged that Jewish soldiers had legitimate religious needs. Accommodating those needs did not undermine military unity; it brought military practice closer to the nation’s constitutional commitment to liberty of conscience.

Jewish Service in World War II. The experience of American Jews in World War II further strengthened the bond between citizenship and service. About 550,000 American Jews[REF] served in the armed forces during a war against regimes that had made antisemitism a central doctrine of state power. Their service joined American patriotism and Jewish survival in a particularly direct way. They fought not as a separate interest group, but as American citizens defending their country against tyranny.

World War II also exposed the difference between a nation that protects religious liberty and regimes built on racial ideology. American Jewish soldiers could serve as Americans while remaining Jews. They could carry prayer books, seek chaplains, observe religious duties when possible, and fight under the flag of a country whose principles rejected the blood-and-soil nationalism that had consumed Europe. Their service gave moral force to the American cause because it embodied the civilization the enemy sought to destroy.

Accommodating Jewish religious practice did not weaken military unity. It allowed Jewish soldiers to serve without pretending that their faith no longer mattered. Soldiers need discipline, but discipline in a republic is not the same as enforced uniformity of belief. A free country asks its citizens to serve without demanding that they erase the deepest sources of their identity.

That point is especially important for understanding Jewish service. Jewish soldiers did not prove their patriotism by becoming less Jewish. They proved it by serving faithfully as Americans while remaining members of a religious community with its own obligations, calendar, and memory. That balance is one of the achievements of American life. It allowed service to deepen collective belonging rather than demand personal self-negation.

Full citizenship also meant being trusted to interpret and administer the nation’s laws.

The Rule of Law and Constitutional Self-Government

Military service was one measure of belonging. Another was whether Jews could take part in interpreting and administering American law. For minorities, confidence in impartial law was especially important because legal insecurity had long been one of the clearest signs of exclusion.

Over time, American Jews entered the judiciary, executive administration, and constitutional advocacy. They were no longer simply asking the law for protection; they were helping to interpret, to administer, and to defend it.

The Founders understood that law would have to restrain power as well as express public will. Alexander Hamilton’s defense of the judiciary in The Federalist Papers emphasized the need for courts independent enough to guard the Constitution and the rights of individuals. That principle mattered for all citizens, but it carried special importance for minorities whose security depended on impartial law rather than popular indulgence.[REF]

Louis D. Brandeis. By the late 19th and early 20th centuries, that principle was being tested under the pressures of industrialization, administrative growth, and concentrated power, which forced Americans to ask whether older constitutional principles could govern modern conditions. Jewish lawyers and jurists entered that debate not as outsiders but as Americans arguing over the shape of the common legal order. Louis D. Brandeis became one of the central figures in that struggle. Born in Louisville to Jewish immigrant parents, Brandeis became a leading lawyer, reformer, and public advocate known as the People’s Lawyer before his appointment in 1916 as the first Jewish Justice of the U.S. Supreme Court.[REF]

As a lawyer and jurist, Brandeis sought to define liberty in a modern republic. He challenged monopolies, defended privacy, argued for public accountability, and insisted that concentrated private power could threaten republican freedom as surely as government overreach. His career showed how American Jews helped define liberty under the Constitution.

Benjamin N. Cardozo. Benjamin N. Cardozo brought a different temperament to American law. Where Brandeis was often a reformer, Cardozo became known for careful reasoning, judicial restraint, and respect for legal continuity. He came from a distinguished Sephardic Jewish family rooted in New York’s oldest Jewish community and served as chief judge of the New York Court of Appeals before his appointment to the U.S. Supreme Court in 1932.[REF]

Cardozo rejected both mechanical formalism and judicial free will. His warning that a judge is “not a knight-errant, roaming at will in pursuit of his own ideal of beauty or of goodness” remains one of the clearest statements of judicial humility in American legal thought. In a republic governed by law, judges must interpret law, not substitute personal preference for public authority. Cardozo helped to explain why courts deserve trust only when they are disciplined by law.[REF]

Felix Frankfurter. Felix Frankfurter’s career centered on the proper limits of judicial power. He believed that courts preserved democratic government partly by respecting the authority of the elected branches. Born in Vienna and brought to the United States as a child, Frankfurter became a Harvard Law School professor, an influential public intellectual, a close adviser to President Franklin D. Roosevelt, and an Associate Justice of the Supreme Court from 1939 to 1962.[REF]

Frankfurter’s answer was a jurisprudence of judicial restraint rooted in concern for boundaries and democratic legitimacy. Later generations have not agreed with him in every instance, but that is not the central point here. He argued over the proper limits of power from the center of American legal life. That shows how far the American Jewish experience had traveled. A community long accustomed elsewhere to legal vulnerability had produced figures trusted to define the responsibilities of the nation’s highest court.

Oscar Straus. Judicial service was only one part of this story. Oscar Straus illustrates the executive dimension. Straus, a German-born Jewish immigrant, served as a diplomat, American representative to the Ottoman Empire, and in 1906 became the first Jewish Cabinet secretary when President Theodore Roosevelt appointed him Secretary of Commerce and Labor. His appointment showed that the federal government could entrust one of its highest administrative offices to an American Jew on the basis of competence and trust.[REF]

The executive story is important because constitutional government is administered as well as interpreted. Straus’s service demonstrated that Jews could be entrusted not only with rights, but also with the ordinary burdens of governing. That was a crucial marker of belonging. A citizen is not fully integrated into a republic unless he too can be trusted to serve the state and the public faithfully under law.

Louis Marshall. Louis Marshall points in the same direction from the world of constitutional advocacy. Marshall, a prominent New York lawyer and longtime leader of the American Jewish Committee in the early 20th century, defended minority rights, religious liberty, and constitutional principles in public controversy and litigation. He framed Jewish claims not as sectarian exceptions but as claims tied to the rights of all Americans. That was essential. In the American system, minority security is strongest when it is tied to principles that protect everyone.

Jews as Loyal Custodians of Public Office. The presence of Jews in the legal profession, the judiciary, and executive administration also helped to refute an old antisemitic suspicion: the claim that Jews could not be loyal custodians of the nation’s courts and public offices. Brandeis, Cardozo, Frankfurter, Straus, and Marshall answered that charge with service. They interpreted law, administered policy, defended rights, and argued over constitutional limits as Americans responsible for the public order.

Their success did not mean that prejudice had disappeared from American law or public life. Brandeis faced bitter opposition, and Jewish lawyers continued to encounter barriers in firms, universities, and professional circles. The deeper point is that such barriers no longer defined the limits of Jewish public service. American Jews could reach the highest levels of the legal system and take part in the country’s most serious arguments over liberty, authority, and constitutional restraint. Their presence changed both the institutions they entered and the public understanding of who could be entrusted to lead them.

Their careers showed that Jews were no longer merely protected by American law. They had become responsible for shaping and administering it.

Jewish participation was not confined to government and the courts. It was equally visible in schools, charities, hospitals, and other voluntary institutions.

Civil Society, Philanthropy, and the Social Infrastructure of Liberty

Much of American life is built outside government, in schools, charities, hospitals, congregations, and other voluntary institutions.

The American Jewish experience in this sphere is especially revealing because Jewish communal life had long depended on voluntary organization. In America, those traditions did not remain confined to self-preservation. They moved outward into institution-building that strengthened the nation. Jews did not have to disappear into the broader culture to contribute to it.

Alexis de Tocqueville, the French observer whose Democracy in America remains one of the classic accounts of American self-government, famously saw the “spirit of association”[REF] as indispensable to democratic life. Tocqueville was not writing specifically about Jews, but his insight helps to explain why American Jewish communal life fit so naturally into the country’s traditions of self-government. Early American Jewish communities built congregations, schools, burial societies, benevolent organizations, and mutual-aid networks. Their significance lay in the practices they cultivated: deliberation, trusteeship, responsibility, and organized service.

These associations were also schools of self-government. Their members raised funds, selected leaders, settled disputes, kept records, and decided how limited resources should be used. In doing so, they practiced the ordinary disciplines on which democratic life depends. Jewish communal institutions preserved religious life, but they also prepared immigrants and their children for participation in a society where citizenship required initiative rather than passive dependence. The habits learned in a congregation, charity, or school board could readily be carried into business, local government, and national service.

Rebecca Gratz. The 19th-century American Jewish educator and philanthropist Rebecca Gratz offers an especially revealing early example. Gratz, born into a prominent Philadelphia Jewish family, became a pioneer of Jewish education and charitable organization. She helped to establish institutions devoted to women, children, orphans, and religious education. Her life shows that citizenship cannot be reduced to officeholding or suffrage alone. Much of the work on which a republic depends is performed through voluntary institutions built outside formal political power.[REF]

Gratz’s significance is also moral. Her work strengthened Jewish communal life while serving broader needs. That combination is central to the American Jewish experience. Religious distinctiveness did not prevent public contribution. In many cases, it supplied the traditions and obligations that made such contribution possible.

Julius Rosenwald. Few figures better illustrate the civic force of Jewish philanthropy than Julius Rosenwald. Rosenwald, the son of German Jewish immigrants, built Sears, Roebuck and Company into a national enterprise and became one of the most consequential philanthropists in American history. His importance lies in the way he used private wealth to strengthen the social conditions on which republican life depends.[REF]

The Rosenwald Schools, built in partnership with black communities and public authorities across the South, showed philanthropy at its best: not condescension from above, but partnership, local initiative, and educational opportunity. Rosenwald’s model required community participation and local commitment. Durable institutions are built when private generosity, local responsibility, and public purpose reinforce one another.[REF]

Felix Warburg. The philanthropy of Felix Warburg illustrates a related development: the organization of large-scale giving. Warburg, a German-born banker and member of the prominent Warburg family, became a major figure in Jewish and general philanthropy in the early 20th century. His support for hospitals, educational institutions, relief organizations, and wider philanthropic networks showed that civil society in a free republic depends on good governance, fiduciary discipline, and administrative capacity.[REF]

More than Generosity. Effective philanthropy required more than generosity. Jewish institutions also developed the financial discipline, professional leadership, and administrative skill needed to build organizations that lasted. Their work showed that voluntary association could operate at scale without becoming an arm of the state.

The growth of Jewish hospitals, charities, and educational institutions also responded to real social needs. Immigrants often arrived poor, disoriented, and vulnerable. Communal institutions helped them adjust to American life without forcing them to sever their religious and cultural roots. That process mattered not only for Jews. It strengthened the wider republic by helping newcomers become self-supporting citizens through voluntary initiative rather than permanent dependence on state power. The result was an integration rooted in confidence rather than self-erasure.

At their best, these institutions also served people and causes beyond the Jewish community. Hospitals treated patients regardless of race or creed, schools formed citizens, charities relieved suffering, and cultural organizations enriched public life. Jewish civil society did not remain a closed system of group preservation. It became part of America’s social fabric. The skills and forms of cooperation that developed in one community became assets for the whole country.

Civil society also helped American Jews to navigate the tension between integration and identity. Congregations, schools, charitable agencies, and other institutions preserved Jewish life while connecting Jews to the broader public. These institutions preserved Jewish identity while helping immigrants and their children enter American public life.

This is one reason why identity-driven accounts of American life miss the mark. They often treat group identity as a claim against the nation. The American Jewish experience shows something different: that a distinct religious community can strengthen the nation precisely because it brings traditions of memory, obligation, charity, and learning into public life. The result is not fragmentation, but a richer form of common citizenship.

American freedom also opened professional and economic opportunities that Jews had often been denied elsewhere.

Innovation, Economic Life, and the Productive Uses of Liberty

The United States gave Jewish immigrants and their descendants opportunities in commerce, finance, medicine, and science that had often been closed to them elsewhere. Its legal and social framework allowed initiative, intellectual inquiry, private property, and voluntary association to flourish with unusual freedom.

This allowed Jews to turn talent, hard work, and practical ideas into enterprises that served the wider public. The examples here should therefore be read not as isolated success stories, but as evidence of how freedom worked in practice.

America, of course, did not guarantee success or eliminate prejudice. It did, however, make it harder for religious identity to determine how far a person could advance. Talent could be tested in the marketplace, the laboratory, or the university rather than stopped in advance by law. That distinction helps to explain why Jewish achievement in America was so broad. The country did not assign Jews a single economic role. It gave them room to move across fields and to apply their abilities wherever opportunity and need met.

Paul Warburg. Paul Warburg’s contribution was institutional. Warburg, a German-born banker whose brother Felix was discussed earlier, immigrated to the United States and became a leading advocate of banking reform. He helped to shape the financial architecture of the modern American state. In the wake of recurring financial panics, he argued for a more coherent central banking structure and became one of the intellectual forces behind the Federal Reserve System.[REF]

Whatever one thinks of the later history of the Federal Reserve, Warburg’s role showed that America could rely on a Jewish immigrant for designing institutions central to national economic life. That was a remarkable act of trust. The American economic system allowed his expertise to enter national service.

Jacob Schiff. Jacob Schiff illustrates the link between capital formation and responsibility. Schiff, a German-born banker who became a senior partner at Kuhn, Loeb & Co., was one of the most important financiers in the United States in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. His career involved railroads, investment banking, philanthropy, education, and Jewish communal leadership. He understood capital not simply as accumulation but also as obligation.[REF]

Levi Strauss. Levi Strauss illustrates the practical side of American enterprise. A German immigrant, he traveled to San Francisco during the Gold Rush era in the mid-19th century and built a company around durable clothing that became part of American life. His story highlights the opportunities the country offered to people with initiative and a strong work ethic, regardless of their religious creed.[REF]

The power of the Strauss example lies in its ordinariness as much as its success. Blue jeans became associated with workers, miners, farmers, cowboys, students, and eventually global American culture. A company founded by a Jewish immigrant produced an article of clothing that became an icon of American practicality and rugged individualism.

Julius Rosenwald. Julius Rosenwald, a philanthropist, must also be understood as a builder of the national market through his role at Sears, Roebuck and Company. His importance in commercial history reflects more than private success. Sears helped to integrate the American economy, expanded access to consumer goods, and helped to organize modern commercial life on a national scale. Rosenwald’s later philanthropy also demonstrates that free enterprise and civic obligation need not be opposing principles. In his career, the productive energies of the market were joined to a serious sense of duty.[REF]

Milton Friedman. Born in Brooklyn to immigrant parents from Central Europe, Milton Friedman became one of the most influential economists of the 20th century and a dedicated defender of free markets and limited government. His influence did not arise from wealth, office, or communal representation. It arose from the force of his argument.[REF] Friedman insisted that economic liberty was inseparable from political liberty and that excessive concentration of power in government threatened both prosperity and freedom.[REF]

Friedman’s importance lies in the way his ideas reinforced a central insight of the Founding: Liberty depends not only on rights written in law, but also on institutions that limit coercion, protect voluntary exchange, and preserve personal responsibility. He did not write as a representative of a minority group but as an American economist arguing about the conditions of freedom. That itself is part of the story. America made it possible for a descendant of Jewish immigrants to shape national and global debates about liberty.

Friedman challenged prevailing assumptions, defended unpopular conclusions, and trusted open debate to test his ideas. That intellectual independence was itself a product of the freedom he defended. His career shows that Jewish participation in American life was not limited to entering existing institutions. American Jews also helped to reshape the country’s understanding of the proper relationship among government, markets, and individual choice.

Jonas Salk. Jonas Salk’s development of the polio vaccine reveals the same pattern in science. Salk, born in New York to a Russian immigrant mother and a father born in New Jersey to immigrants from Eastern Europe, became a physician and medical researcher whose work helped to protect millions from one of the most feared diseases of the 20th century. His achievement mattered because it saved lives. But it also mattered because it grew out of a free society’s ecology of universities, laboratories, philanthropy, and private initiative.[REF] Salk’s work therefore belongs not only to medical history, but also to the civic history of the republic.

Albert Einstein. Einstein’s arrival in the United States demonstrated America’s ability to provide refuge to scholars driven from Europe by tyranny. Born in Germany and already world-famous for his scientific achievements, he came to the United States after the Nazis rose to power. His presence showed that a free society could shelter inquiry and benefit from it.[REF]

Einstein was not a conventional political figure, and his views did not align neatly with American conservatism. But his American refuge reveals a vital truth: Societies that protect liberty attract talent that closed and tyrannical societies drive away.

The Same Underlying Reality. These men worked in very different fields, but their careers reflected the same underlying reality: America allowed Jewish talent to contribute directly to the nation’s economic, scientific, and intellectual strength.

These histories suggest that innovation depends not only on talent, but also on law, stability, free inquiry, private initiative, and moral responsibility. The American Jewish experience is especially revealing because it shows how a historically vulnerable minority could help to build the financial, commercial, scientific, medical, and economic capacities of the nation. America allowed Jewish success to become part of the nation’s strength.

These contributions also raise an important question about how America remembers its own history during its 250th anniversary.

Civic Memory, Constitutional Identity, and the American Future

Institutions cannot be preserved if citizens do not understand how they arose or why they matter. Arguments about history are therefore never only about the past. They shape the inheritance through which a people understands its future.

American Jewish history shows how constitutional liberty allowed a once-vulnerable minority to become deeply invested in the country’s future. Over time, American Jews became involved in the military, legal, scientific, economic, philanthropic, and cultural life of the republic. That is not a marginal story. It is one of the best tests of the American promise.

The American Jewish experience confirms that America’s commitments to equal citizenship, liberty of conscience, and the rule of law were not empty slogans. Over time, they changed lives, opened doors, and drew Jews into responsibility for the nation itself.

This matters directly in a period of renewed antisemitism. Historical ignorance does not create hatred by itself, but it gives hatred room to breathe. A truthful account of the American past narrows that space by showing that Jews have long helped to build, defend, and sustain the republic. The rights of Jews, like the rights of all citizens, do not depend on a ledger of contributions. But memory must rest on truth, and truth requires acknowledging that Jews have been part of the American story from the beginning.

Teaching the Full Story. Schools should teach about American Jews not only as victims of persecution, but also as soldiers, judges, entrepreneurs, scientists, philanthropists, and public servants. Lessons about persecution, immigration, and the Holocaust remain important, but they are not the whole story. Teaching only the history of victimhood leaves students with an incomplete picture and can unintentionally reinforce the idea that Jews stand apart from the American experience. A fuller account shows the obstacles that Jews faced and the responsibilities they assumed.

The same point applies to contemporary identity politics. If American history is taught mainly as a struggle among fixed groups competing for recognition, the meaning of the Jewish experience is easily lost. The better lesson is that a free republic can preserve unity without demanding uniformity. It can allow religious distinctiveness while building common citizenship. It can call minorities not merely into recognition, but into responsibility.

Irving Berlin. Irving Berlin’s patriotic music gives that truth a melody. Berlin, born Israel Beilin in the Russian Empire, immigrated to the United States as a child and became one of the great composers of American national feeling. He did not erase his origins, nor did he make patriotism the property of any one group. He helped to give musical form to an American identity broad enough to include those who came from elsewhere and made the country their own.[REF]

Berlin’s example is especially powerful because he reached Americans in a way that legal and historical arguments often could not: through music that became part of the country’s shared culture. His patriotic songs showed that American identity could be adopted, loved, and strengthened by those who came from elsewhere and wholeheartedly embraced their new country.

America at 250. The semiquincentennial should be more than an anniversary. It should prompt Americans to consider what made the country’s experiment in liberty possible and why it remains worth defending. “America 250” will fail if it becomes only a sequence of ceremonies. It will succeed if it strengthens historical understanding and renews gratitude for liberty under law.

The American Jewish experience belongs near the center of that inquiry. It shows that the United States, despite its failures and limits, gave Jews the security and freedom to take an active part in national life. Recovering that history during America’s 250th anniversary would strengthen civic literacy, answer antisemitism with truth, and reaffirm religious liberty and equal citizenship.

A truthful account of America must acknowledge its failures without allowing those failures to swallow the whole story. The American Jewish experience helps to preserve that balance. It shows that prejudice was real, but it also shows that the country’s founding principles gave citizens the means to challenge it.

This understanding has practical consequences. Schools, museums, civic organizations, military academies, law schools, and public officials should not treat American Jewish history as a narrow ethnic supplement. They should present it as constitutional history. Jewish contributions matter not merely as a record of achievement, but because they reveal what religious liberty and equal citizenship made possible.

Acknowledging Jewish contributions should not become merely a parade of famous Jewish names. The individuals discussed in this report matter because their lives illuminate larger features of American government and society. George Washington’s letter explains the difference between toleration and rights. Jewish military service shows how belonging is proved through sacrifice. Jewish jurists demonstrate the importance of impartial law, while philanthropists and entrepreneurs reveal what voluntary initiative can accomplish. The aim is not ethnic pride for its own sake, but a clearer understanding of the country.

That lesson is badly needed now. Antisemitism thrives when Jews are portrayed as alien, parasitic, or detached from the national good. The historical record shows the opposite. American Jews helped to finance independence, defend the country, interpret its laws, build its institutions, expand commerce, advance science, and enrich national culture. Recovering that record will not end antisemitism by itself. But it deprives antisemitism of one of its most useful lies.

The challenge for America 250 is not simply to add Jewish names to an existing commemorative calendar. The challenge is to tell the national story more truthfully. When Jewish contributions are presented as part of the central American narrative, citizens can see how religious liberty and equal citizenship enabled Jews to identify with the country and contribute to its future.

What Policymakers, Educators, and Historical Societies Should Do

In order to strengthen civic education and counter antisemitism with an accurate account of American history, think tanks, universities, and historical societies should:

Commission a series of focused studies on the Revolution and Founding, military service, constitutional law, free enterprise and philanthropy, and science and innovation. Each study should be written for policymakers and public leaders, not only for scholars, and should show how American Jewish history illuminates the practical achievements of the Founding.

Civic education organizations and public history institutions should:

Develop policy briefs and educational toolkits for lawmakers, state officials, educators, museums, and community organizations. These resources should shape the historical findings into useful materials for public programs, lectures, exhibits, classrooms, and antisemitism education.

Congress, state education agencies, military academies, law schools, bar associations, and public history institutions should:

Incorporate American Jewish history into their curricula and programming. It should be taught as part of the nation’s constitutional development, not as a detachable minority sidebar.

Public officials, civic leaders, and Jewish communal organizations should:

Respond to antisemitism not only with condemnation, but also with historically grounded explanations of the role that American Jews have played in building and defending the nation. Condemnation addresses the moral offense. Historical literacy addresses the falsehood beneath it. Both are necessary.

Foundations, universities, think tanks, and philanthropic organizations should:

Support further research and educational work on the role religious minorities have played in American self-government. The American Jewish experience should be a central case study because it links religious liberty with full participation in national life.

Free societies endure when citizens regard the country and its institutions as their own. American Jewish history shows that the United States has repeatedly made that kind of belonging possible. Recovering this record is an act of civic renewal.

The American Jewish Experience and the Meaning of the American Founding

The history examined in this report points to a clear conclusion: America gave Jews a form of citizenship that had rarely been available to them elsewhere. Jews did not merely find refuge in the United States. They became active participants in its public life and defenders of the country.

This progress was neither immediate nor complete. It occurred because the country’s constitutional principles gave Jews the means to challenge exclusion and claim their place as equal citizens. In that sense, the American Jewish experience vindicates one of the Founding’s most ambitious claims: Liberty secured by constitutional government can produce citizens who regard the nation as their own.

That lesson is urgent as the nation celebrates America 250. Antisemitism is in part a symptom of historical amnesia and civic decay. When Americans forget the role Jews have played in building the republic, they become more vulnerable to narratives that cast Jews as strangers to the national story. Historical truth is therefore an essential element in the fight against antisemitism.

Recovering the record of American Jewish service, leadership, and patriotism can provide a fact-based and constitutionally grounded alternative to identity-driven accounts of American life so common on the Left. It can show that the Founders’ principles of religious liberty, equal citizenship, freedom under law, and civic responsibility made possible one of the most remarkable stories of minority integration in modern history.

National memory does not preserve itself. Each generation must learn the history of the country it has inherited. If America 250 is to be more than a commemoration, it should remind citizens that the Founding allowed religious minorities to enter national life without erasing their identities. That achievement is not incidental to the American story. It is one of its strongest proofs.
