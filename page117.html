It didn’t take long for fans to figure out that the fourth season of "Star Trek: Strange New Worlds" was going to be unlike any season of "Star Trek" before it. The entire season was one giant gimmick, and that gimmick was shoving "Trek" into as many genres as possible and often directly adapting classic films from those genres.

You could argue that an entire season of gimmick episodes is a bold risk: putting the characters in fun, wacky situations to see how they react. It could be interesting, but the season we got never made the most of the concept. It was all so safe and paint-by-numbers, and, most importantly, it didn't have anything profound or interesting to say.

That should trouble any Trekkie. "Star Trek" — a show built on progressive ideals, thoughtful characters, and boundary-breaking television — was completely and totally afraid to say anything at all.

To be clear, "Star Trek" is no stranger to genre mashups, and they're not inherently a bad thing. Trek has been running for nearly 1,000 total episodes, and they've been doing mashups since the very first season of "Star Trek: The Original Series", which featured gangster, historical fiction, and war-themed episodes.

The holodeck took things further, giving the writers an excuse to set the action anywhere they could dream up. No more alien planets that look suspiciously like Earth were necessary to completely flip the series’ genre; just work up a hologram malfunction and suddenly "Trek" is a Sherlock Holmes-esque mystery or a noir thriller. And even without the holodeck, genres are fluid things that often take from each other to tell stories within each other’s realms.

But while "Trek" has always dabbled, it's never done an entire season of genre episodes… in fact, that might be unprecedented in all of science fiction TV itself, let alone "Star Trek". This season of "SNW" didn’t use occasional gimmicks to take a break from space fare; it was all gimmick, all the time.

* Some of you are screaming that Episode 4 was a noir. It was not. Not everything in black and white with fedoras is noir.

"Strange New Worlds" season 4 was genre-blending at scale. This wasn’t a hint of another genre influencing the episode; it was full-blown immersion into each and every one.

In the past, when other genres have come into "Trek", the series still felt tonally consistent; the characters never bent to the will of the genre, and its direction stayed the same. That was not true of this season. Characters, directorial style, and tone all bent to the will of the selected genre instead of fitting into the already established universe. There were multiple episodes this season that felt nothing like a "Star Trek" episode.

Also — and I may be in the minority here — there were only two episodes this season that I’d argue were executed well (Episode 6 and Episode 8), meaning that the relentless gimmicks paraded out each week felt not just detrimental to the show itself, but they weren’t much fun to watch either.

The real question that crops up from this season is why they did it at all. If you listen to the show’s producers, it’s because they love "Trek" so much and they thought it would help move the characters along.

"I really do respect and hear the criticism that: 'When you have 10 episodes [per season], should they be more homogeneous?’”, co-showrunner and executive producer Akiva Goldsman told TV Line when addressing the criticisms for the season. “For us, the answer was no, because the original series always jumped around in genre … Part of what 'Star Trek' did well was use genre for storytelling, and that was what we wanted to explore in this series.”

He is, of course, absolutely correct; that is exactly what "Trek" has traditionally used genre episodes for. When "The Original Series" went to the Nazi planet, it was one of the first and earliest examples of directly confronting fascism and Nazism on American television. When the show played with Greek gods, it took the moment to have a groundbreaking interracial kiss. Even when it went broad comedy in “The Trouble with Tribbles,” it at least kept its characters consistent and its universe coherent.

For the most part, that practice continued throughout classic "Trek" and even into the modern era, with genre being used to aid storytelling, not overwhelm it.

Season 4 of "Strange New Worlds" does none of this. It isn’t using genre as elevation; it’s using it as a distraction: a shiny gloss over a collection of episodes too afraid to say anything that will get the series accused of being “woke” or progressive. Later in the interview, Goldman claims that they can’t please everyone, but that seems like exactly what they were trying to do this season-- resulting, of course, in pleasing no one.

This season is running scared from the current moment, and it’s doing so by hiding behind gimmicks. Any previous series of "Trek" (or any great science fiction) would be tackling subjects such as immigration, fascism, war, or any other societal issue that is overwhelming us right now, but "SNW" is doing none of that, instead presenting farcical genre fare

It’s hard not to draw a direct line from this approach to the purchase of Paramount by Skydance last year and the company’s new leadership, who are more than cozy with President Donald Trump. There seems to be little other reason that a franchise and series that were being bashed for being “too woke” and overly progressive would veer so hard into gimmickry that barely hides the hollow shell this show has become.

Rewatching the first three seasons of "SNW" shows how hard a turn this series has taken since the re-election of Trump and the steady march of companies trying to kowtow to a government that wants to squash progressive ideology.

The show has gone from telling powerful stories for both its characters and society to a series of running gags that do little more than slap the Federation delta onto people’s chests so people think it's still "Star Trek".

It isn’t. This season of nothingness is exactly what you get when you ask "Trek" not to push boundaries, not to be hopeful, not to be bold, and not to progress forward. When a show founded on speaking out and saying something spends an entire season running away from doing just that, it’s not just a condemnation of the show itself; it’s a condemnation of the creative process behind it.

The real issue with this season isn’t the attempt to play in genre for ten episodes straight; it’s that the reason they did it was fear, not hope.

AI companies know they are being held hostage by each other, and that they need to take action to self-regulate before they suffer major reputational and legal consequences. There is a blueprint on how they can do it.

The largest artificial intelligence (AI) firms increasingly understand that they are hostages of one another. If one firm pushes ahead recklessly, others may feel compelled to follow. If one deploys a dangerous model without adequate safeguards, others may see it as a green light to do the same.

The debate over AI has centered on what governments should do to regulate this frontier technology. That remains essential. But a more immediate question is emerging: Can the companies building these powerful systems take action independently before they lose credibility and suffer legal consequences?

That question no longer looks theoretical and is being recognized as an urgent challenge. We've already seen AI firms lose control of their models — for example, in Australia, a rogue AI hacked a government healthcare website. If we are not careful, it's only a matter of time before one of these incidents causes large-scale, real-world harm, like shutting down an electricity grid or municipal water system, or a mass misdiagnosis in a health system. If this trend continues, the entire industry could become tarnished.

But there is a blueprint for self-regulation in a risky business: the nuclear industry, which has built up layers of oversight, controls and self-scrutiny over decades.

Frontier AI may require something similar: private regulation among competitors that do not trust one another but have a mutual interest in preventing outcomes that could damage them all. This does not appeal to the better angels of human nature — it's self-preservation for the industry itself.

There is a historical precedent for this kind of arrangement. The nuclear sector did not become safe because every actor suddenly became benevolent. It became safer through a layered system of peer scrutiny, technical standards, information-sharing and external oversight. International organizations such as the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), which was established in 1957, emerged to coordinate monitoring and verification, while national regulators and industry bodies gradually built professional norms around catastrophic risk.

The result was not perfect safety, as the Chernobyl disaster in 1986 clearly attests. But nuclear governance architecture is designed around the recognition that in high-risk technological systems, one actor's failure can become everyone's problem. This was seen following Chernobyl: global nuclear safety increased significantly after the disaster, with upgrades to reactor designs, improved training and international monitoring networks.

In the aftermath of the Chernobyl disaster, safety and regulation in the global nuclear industry increased.

And this movement to self-regulation also occurred in the U.S. nuclear sector, following the Three-Mile Island nuclear accident near Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, in 1979. The reactor suffered a partial meltdown after a relatively minor cooling malfunction. Investigations later revealed design flaws in instruments and alarms, along with inadequate emergency response training, were the root cause of the incident.

The event nearly killed the nuclear energy industry in the U.S., causing public support to fall from a record high of 69% in 1977 to 46%t in 1979. The entire industry suffered because of failures at one facility. In response, the U.S. nuclear industry established the Institute of Nuclear Power Operations (INPO) in December 1979 to improve operational safety.

The analogy to AI should not be overstated. Nuclear materials are easier to count than algorithms, and nuclear facilities are harder to hide than model weights. The outcomes of a catastrophic AI blunder are also less existential, although its use in weapon systems is not without risk. In September, an AI hallucination almost led to the U.S. intercepting a Chinese ship suspected of carrying nuclear material to Iran.

Yet the comparison remains useful because the core governance problem is similar. In both cases, competitors operate in an environment where the downside risk of unchecked rivalry is borne by society while the upside is private gain for these firms. The challenge is to create institutions that make restraint the rational default — even when no actor fully trusts the others, and the inner workings of the industry are not readily visible to outsiders.

For AI, that could mean reciprocal evaluation of frontier models before deployment, secure mechanisms for sharing safety information, agreed red lines around certain capabilities, and independent third parties empowered to audit compliance. Such mechanisms would not replace government regulation. They would instead serve as an intermediate layer: stronger than voluntary ethics statements but more adaptive and technically informed than legislation alone can often be in fast-moving domains. The state would act as a background enforcer.

AI governance is often discussed as if the only choices were laissez-faire innovation or heavy-handed state control. History suggests a third path.

AI executives appear willing to look for solutions too, with Elon Musk recently suggesting a peer-review system before advanced model release, and Demis Hassabis, co-founder and chair of Google DeepMind, putting forward a framework for an AI Standards Body, similar to the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority.

Skeptics will object, with reason, that industry-led governance can become cartel behavior or public-relations theater. Companies may use "safety" language to entrench incumbency, slow rivals or deflect binding regulation. Those are real dangers. The lesson from nuclear history is not that private coordination works automatically. It is that private coordination works only when nested inside a broader system of transparency, verification and public authority. Without those elements, private regulation becomes little more than trust-me rhetoric.

Still, dismissing private regulation altogether would be a mistake. Governments do not yet possess the technical capacity, speed or international alignment needed to oversee frontier AI on their own. And if the leading labs already see themselves as bound together by shared exposure to catastrophic risk, that recognition can become the basis for institutional design. The question is how to turn mutual fear into durable rules.

That is where the nuclear analogy is most powerful. It reminds us that industries facing existential hazards do not need perfect trust in order to cooperate. They need mechanisms that make cooperation preferable to defection. In practice, that means inspections, peer review, incident reporting, professional norms and a state-backed enforcement perimeter. AI governance is often discussed as if the only choices were laissez-faire innovation or heavy-handed state control. History suggests a third path: structured private coordination under public shadow.

The real opportunity, then, is not to celebrate the newfound (apparent) prudence of technology executives, it is to recognize what their comments reveal. When even fierce rivals begin to argue for mutual scrutiny, they are admitting that competition alone cannot govern the frontier. That admission should be treated not as reassurance but as evidence that the risk has become collective.

The policy task now is to build institutions that fit that reality. Governments should push for mandatory reporting, independent evaluation and legal backstops. But they should also encourage enforceable forms of peer oversight among the handful of firms whose systems may shape the trajectory of the technology. Frontier AI is becoming an industry in which the leading actors are hostages of one another. The nuclear age showed that such conditions can produce serious governance. The hope is that AI firms learn that lesson before they learn the cost of ignoring it at the peril of the public at large.

Here's what it finds that cProfile misses

Python developers have had access to a code profiler for a long time. cProfile ships with Python, records every function call and tells you exactly how often each function ran. That makes it useful for scripts and batch jobs whose execution you control.

It’s less convenient for situations you can’t control. For example, when a web service is already running and starts behaving badly. You can’t just attach cProfile to that running process to see what’s going on. Restarting the service under a profiler may make the problem disappear, and the tracing overhead can change the behaviour you’re trying to measure in any case.

Python 3.15 introduces another option. The new profiling.sampling module, known as Tachyon, can start a program itself or attach to a process that’s already running. It reads the process externally, so the application doesn’t need to import a profiling library or restart.

Tachyon can also examine every thread, distinguish CPU work from waiting, show which code holds the GIL and mark execution inside native extensions. If a process appears stuck, it can print each thread’s current stack without running a full profile.

In this article, we’ll try each feature against a small HTTP service. Every profiler command has a matching endpoint, so the results are visible and obvious.

Python 3.15 places its profiling tools under a new package:

profiling.tracing is the deterministic profiler previously exposed through cProfile. It records every function call, return and exception. The resulting call counts are exact, including functions that finish in a few microseconds.

profiling.sampling checks the program’s call stacks at regular intervals. A function that appears in many samples is probably responsible for a large share of the execution time.

These two approaches answer different questions.

Sampling doesn’t record every event, so its timings are estimates. If a function accounts for 10% of the samples, it probably accounts for roughly 10% of the sampled time. Results can vary slightly between runs.

Tracing is exact, but the profiler has to process every call. That extra work can become noticeable in an application that calls many small Python functions.

The Python documentation recommends statistical sampling for most investigations and deterministic tracing when exact call counts matter. The existing cProfile module remains available as an alias for profiling.tracing. The old pure-Python profile module is deprecated and is due to be removed in Python 3.17.

#798. Your blood could reveal inflammatory bowel disease 10 years before diagnosis

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Scientists have identified hidden changes in the immune system that can appear up to 10 years before a person is diagnosed with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). The findings, presented at UEG Week 2026, suggest that certain antibody patterns in the blood could eventually help doctors identify people at risk of developing the condition and even predict how severe their disease might become.

Inflammatory bowel disease refers to a group of long-term disorders, including Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), that cause persistent inflammation in the digestive tract. These conditions develop when the immune system responds abnormally, contributing to inflammation and damage in the intestines.

Scientists increasingly believe that IBD begins developing years before it is officially diagnosed. However, the immune system changes that take place during this early, largely hidden period have remained poorly understood.

Tracking Immune Changes a Decade Before Diagnosis

To investigate what happens before IBD is diagnosed, researchers examined 2,000 blood serum samples collected from 500 people. The participants included 200 who eventually developed Crohn's disease, 200 who developed ulcerative colitis, and 100 healthy individuals of comparable age and sex.

Blood samples were available from the time of diagnosis and from approximately two, four, and 10 years earlier. This allowed the researchers to look backward through a decade of immune activity and identify changes that emerged before the diseases were recognized.

The team analyzed antibody reactions to approximately 357,000 antigens associated with bacteria, viruses, food, and the immune system itself. Antigens are substances that the immune system can recognize and respond to, while antibodies are proteins produced by the immune system that help identify specific targets.

Across all 2,000 samples, the researchers detected 123,980 unique antibodies.

A striking pattern emerged. People who later developed Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis had significantly less antibody diversity than healthy participants, meaning their immune systems showed a narrower range of antibody responses. Distinctive changes could be detected in both diseases as far back as 10 years before diagnosis.

Different Blood Signals Reveal Crohn's Disease and Ulcerative Colitis

The researchers also found that Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis were associated with different immune signatures.

Among people who later developed Crohn's disease, antibody responses to herpesviruses and bacterial flagellin were elevated. Flagellin is a protein that forms part of the tail-like structures some bacteria use to move.

These elevated responses were especially noticeable in individuals who eventually developed complicated Crohn's disease or disease affecting the ileum, the final section of the small intestine.

At the same time, antibody responses to encapsulated bacteria gradually decreased as diagnosis approached. These bacteria are surrounded by protective outer coatings that can help them evade immune defenses.

People who later developed ulcerative colitis showed a somewhat different pattern. Their antibody responses to herpesviruses and autoantigens increased, while responses to encapsulated pathogens declined. Autoantigens are components of the body's own tissues that can mistakenly become targets of an immune response.

Together, these findings suggest that the immune system undergoes measurable changes many years before inflammatory bowel disease is diagnosed.

AI Identifies Hidden Disease Risk Years in Advance

The researchers then used machine learning, a form of artificial intelligence that identifies patterns in complex data, to determine whether these antibody signatures could distinguish people who would eventually develop IBD from healthy individuals.

Using blood antibody patterns from up to 10 years before diagnosis, the models distinguished future Crohn's disease cases from healthy controls with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.90. For ulcerative colitis, the corresponding value was 0.84.

AUC is a measure of how well a model distinguishes between two groups. A score of 0.5 indicates performance equivalent to chance, while 1.0 represents perfect discrimination.

The researchers also discovered that antibody patterns could help distinguish people who would develop complicated Crohn's disease from those whose disease would remain less complicated. This distinction was possible approximately 10 years before diagnosis, with an AUC of 0.85.

These findings raise the possibility that blood antibody testing could eventually help identify not only who is at increased risk of IBD, but also which patients may need more intensive treatment.

Commenting on the findings, lead author Dr. Arno Bourgonje said: "It is fascinating to see that complicated CD at diagnosis is associated with a distinct antibody profile years before the first clinical symptoms and is not simply the consequence of disease progression after diagnosis. Finding these patterns so early points to the potential of antibody profiling to support earlier risk detection, distinguish between disease subtypes and help identify patients who may benefit from more intensive therapy as early as possible."

Could a Blood Test Detect Gut Disease 10 Years Early?

Although the results offer compelling evidence that IBD has a detectable early immune signature, the researchers emphasize that the findings are not yet ready for use in routine medical screening.

The study establishes an important proof of principle, but additional research is necessary to determine whether these antibody patterns can reliably predict disease in broader populations.

Researchers must also establish whether antibody testing provides meaningful benefits beyond existing genetic information, clinical assessments, and other blood-based measurements. Any potential screening test would need to be accurate, affordable, reproducible, and ultimately shown to improve patient outcomes.

"The next priority is to validate these signatures in independent, more diverse and prospectively followed populations," concluded Dr. Bourgonje. "We also need to determine their added value alongside genetic, clinical and other blood-based markers, translate the findings into a reproducible and affordable targeted assay and, ultimately, demonstrate that using such a test actually leads to better clinical outcomes."

If future studies confirm these results, blood-based immune profiling could offer doctors a new way to recognize inflammatory bowel disease risk years before diagnosis, distinguish between disease subtypes, and identify patients who might benefit from earlier intervention.

#842. Scientists discover a hidden switch that burns fat and blocks new fat cells

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists have identified a remarkable protein switch that influences whether cells burn fat or store it. Disabling this protein increases fat consumption and interferes with the formation of new fat cells, raising the possibility of a different approach to treating obesity. Subsequent research has strengthened the discovery, revealing that the same protein helps regulate how much energy the body burns.

What if one of the keys to fighting obesity lies not in suppressing appetite, but in changing how the body's cells use energy?

Researchers at the Weizmann Institute of Science have identified a protein called MTCH2, nicknamed "Mitch," that plays an important role in controlling metabolism. When scientists disabled this protein in laboratory experiments, cells began consuming more fuel, including fats and carbohydrates. They also found that removing Mitch could interfere with the process through which immature cells develop into fat cells.

The discovery builds on earlier experiments in mice, in which switching off Mitch in muscle tissue increased energy expenditure, improved endurance, and protected the animals from diet-related obesity.

The findings, published in The EMBO Journal in January 2025, suggest that manipulating this protein could eventually offer a way to influence both fat burning and fat accumulation. Additional research published later that year has provided further evidence of its importance in regulating body fat and metabolism.

Why Scientists Are Searching for Better Obesity Treatments

Over the past decade, medications designed to promote weight loss have transformed obesity treatment. Drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide have helped many patients achieve substantial reductions in body weight, largely by influencing appetite and the body's responses to food.

But losing weight does not necessarily mean losing only fat.

Weight reduction can also involve a decline in lean body mass, which includes muscle and other tissues. Preserving muscle is especially important because it supports strength, mobility, and metabolic health.

A 2026 analysis of 20 clinical trials involving 15,782 participants found that lean mass accounted for approximately 25% to 39% of the weight lost with several widely used weight-loss medications. Importantly, the researchers found that comparable proportional losses also occurred with lifestyle interventions, while adding resistance training helped preserve more lean tissue.

These concerns have encouraged scientists to investigate ways to reduce body fat while protecting the muscles needed for long-term health.

Professor Atan Gross and his colleagues at the Weizmann Institute arrived at a possible avenue through an unexpected discovery involving Mitch.

Switching Off One Protein Protected Mice From Obesity

In earlier experiments, Gross's team reduced the activity of MTCH2 in the muscles of mice. The effects were striking.

The genetically modified animals showed increased metabolic activity and were resistant to obesity caused by their diet. Their muscles also developed more fibers with a high capacity for using oxygen, helping improve physical endurance.

The mice performed better in exercise and stress tests and showed improvements in heart function. In addition to resisting excess fat accumulation, they experienced favorable changes in their overall body composition.

The results suggested that Mitch was doing much more than controlling a single metabolic reaction. Somehow, this protein appeared to influence the body's ability to use energy, store fat, and sustain physical activity.

That raised an intriguing question: How could disabling one protein produce such widespread effects?

To investigate, the researchers turned their attention to mitochondria, the microscopic structures responsible for supplying much of the energy that cells need to function.

The Mitochondria Connection Behind Fat Burning

Mitochondria are frequently described as the powerhouses of cells because they convert energy from food into a usable form called ATP, or adenosine triphosphate.

ATP acts as a cellular energy currency. Muscles use it to contract, cells need it to grow and repair themselves, and countless biological processes depend on a steady supply.

However, mitochondria are not static structures. They constantly change shape, joining together into larger networks or separating into smaller units.

When mitochondria fuse, they can form interconnected systems that help coordinate energy production. When they divide, the resulting structures may operate differently, altering how efficiently cells produce and consume energy.

Gross's team had previously established that Mitch plays a major role in mitochondrial fusion, the process that allows separate mitochondria to merge.

Without this protein, the mitochondrial network becomes fragmented. Although the cells can still generate energy, they experience changes in energy efficiency and demand that can force them to consume more fuel.

This offered a possible explanation for the unusual metabolism observed in the mice. Instead of conserving energy efficiently, their cells appeared to be operating in a more energy-intensive state.

But would disabling Mitch have a similar effect in human cells?

Human Cells Begin Burning More Fat Without Mitch

To answer that question, researchers led by doctoral student Sabita Chourasia used genetic engineering to remove MTCH2 from human cells grown in the laboratory.

The change disrupted the normal structure of their mitochondrial networks. Individual mitochondria became more separated, and the balance between energy production and energy demand shifted.

The cells developed a persistent shortage of readily available energy relative to their needs, even as their mitochondria increased their oxygen-consuming activity.

To compensate, they began consuming more nutrients.

"After deleting Mitch, we examined, every few hours, the effect that had on more than 100 substances taking part in metabolism in human cells," Chourasia explains. "We saw an increase in cellular respiration, the process in which the cell produces energy from nutrients, such as carbohydrates and fats, using oxygen. This explains the increase in muscular endurance in previous experiments using mice."

Cellular respiration is the series of chemical reactions that allows cells to extract usable energy from nutrients. Increasing this activity generally requires a greater supply of fuel.

The researchers found that cells lacking Mitch consumed more fats, carbohydrates, and amino acids, the building blocks of proteins.

Fat metabolism was particularly revealing.

Rather than relying on their usual mix of energy sources, the altered cells increased their use of fatty acids for fuel. This indicated that MTCH2 influences not only how much energy cells consume, but also which nutrients they use to meet that demand.

"We discovered that deleting Mitch led to a major drop in fats in membranes," Gross explains. "At the same time, we saw an increase in fatty substances used to produce energy, and we realized that the fat was being broken down from the membrane to be used as fuel. In other words, we showed that Mitch determines the fate of fat in human cells."

Cell membranes are thin, flexible barriers made largely from fatty molecules called lipids. They protect cells and organize many of their essential functions.

The findings suggested that cells deprived of Mitch were changing how they distributed and used these lipids, drawing on some of their available fatty resources to support increased energy consumption.

Detailed analysis also revealed changes in stored lipids, demonstrating that the effect was more complex than simply eliminating cellular fat. Overall, the results showed that MTCH2 is a powerful regulator of how cells allocate nutrients between energy production and other biological needs.

The researchers then investigated whether Mitch also influences how fat cells develop.

This question was particularly interesting because previous studies had found elevated MTCH2 levels in fat tissue from women with obesity.

Fat cells, also known as adipocytes, do not appear fully formed. They develop from immature precursor cells through a process called adipogenesis, or fat cell differentiation.

As these precursor cells mature, they must build new cellular structures, produce fats, and activate genes that allow them to specialize in storing energy.

All of this requires considerable energy and raw materials.

Gross's team suspected that removing Mitch might disrupt the process by creating an environment in which cells were too busy consuming fuel to build the components needed for fat storage.

Experiments using mouse precursor fat cells supported this idea. When the researchers deleted MTCH2, far fewer cells successfully developed into mature fat cells.

In the published experiments, approximately 80% to 90% of normal precursor cells differentiated within six days, compared with only 5% to 10% of cells lacking MTCH2. The altered cells also accumulated substantially fewer lipid droplets, the structures used to store fat.

"When we deleted Mitch from the progenitor cells, we discovered that the environment created in these cells was not conducive to the synthesis of new fats," Gross explains. "Reducing the ability to synthesize membranes prevents the cells from growing, developing and reaching the point where differentiation is possible. The process of fat accumulation requires a large amount of available energy, but in cells without Mitch, there is a shortage of energy. In addition, the expression of genes necessary for differentiation is suppressed, and there is a shortage of the substances vital for this process to occur. As a result, differentiation of new fat cells is reduced, along with fat accumulation."

In other words, disabling Mitch appeared to affect fat metabolism in two distinct ways. It increased the consumption of energy-rich nutrients and interfered with the cellular machinery needed to develop new fat-storing cells.

Importantly, the fat cell differentiation experiments used a mouse-derived cell line, while the detailed analysis of nutrient consumption was conducted in human cells. The combined results suggest a broader role for the protein, but they do not establish that blocking MTCH2 would prevent new fat cells from forming in people.

Follow-Up Research Reveals Another Fat-Burning Mechanism

The importance of MTCH2 became even clearer in research published on October 3, 2025, in Nature Communications.

An international research team, which included Gross and Chourasia, investigated how MTCH2 influences metabolism in fat tissue. By analyzing human and mouse fat samples, studying cultured fat cells, and conducting experiments in genetically modified mice, the scientists identified another mechanism linking this protein to energy expenditure.

They found that MTCH2 helps regulate the movement of fatty acids into mitochondria, where these molecules can be broken down to produce energy.

The process involves an enzyme called carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1, or CPT1. This enzyme acts as an important checkpoint, helping determine how much fat can enter mitochondria for use as fuel.

Normally, CPT1 activity is regulated by a molecule called malonyl-CoA, which signals when cells should limit fatty acid consumption.

The researchers discovered that MTCH2 physically interacts with CPT1 and influences how strongly this regulatory signal affects the enzyme.

Removing MTCH2 altered that control system, allowing mitochondria to increase fatty acid utilization and energy expenditure.

This provided a more detailed molecular explanation for why disabling the protein could encourage cells to burn additional fat.

Mice Burned More Energy Without Eating Less

The follow-up study also produced an especially promising result.

When researchers removed MTCH2 specifically from the fat cells of mice, the animals burned more energy without increasing their physical activity or changing how much food they consumed.

Under a regular diet, the genetically modified mice gained weight at a rate similar to that of normal mice.

However, when the animals were fed a high-fat diet, a difference emerged.

Mice lacking MTCH2 in their fat tissue gained less weight and accumulated less body fat than untreated mice, despite consuming similar amounts of food.

Their lean body mass remained comparable to that of the control animals.

The genetically modified mice also demonstrated improved glucose tolerance, indicating that their bodies were better able to manage blood sugar after receiving glucose. In addition, their cholesterol levels were lower.

These findings strengthened the evidence that MTCH2 influences whole-body metabolism, rather than simply changing the behavior of isolated cells.

The scientists also found that its metabolic effects could occur independently of UCP1, a protein commonly associated with heat production in brown fat. Brown fat is a specialized form of body fat that helps generate heat by burning energy, whereas white fat primarily stores excess energy.

Discovering an additional pathway for increasing energy expenditure could be important because it offers researchers another potential target for treating obesity and related metabolic disorders.

Could This Protein Lead to Better Weight-Loss Treatments?

Together, the findings suggest that MTCH2 may be an important molecular switch linking fat storage, energy consumption, and the development of fat cells.

The original experiments showed that removing the protein could increase nutrient consumption in human cells and interfere with fat cell formation in mouse cells. The later research demonstrated that selectively removing MTCH2 from fat tissue could increase energy expenditure and protect mice against excessive weight gain on a high-fat diet.

That combination raises the possibility of developing treatments that change how the body handles energy, potentially complementing medications that primarily work by reducing appetite.

However, several major questions remain unanswered.

Scientists have not yet demonstrated that inhibiting MTCH2 produces safe, sustained weight loss in humans. The protein also participates in other important biological activities, including mitochondrial regulation and pathways involved in cell survival. Blocking it throughout the body could therefore produce unwanted consequences.

Preventing fat cells from forming is not necessarily beneficial in every situation, either. Healthy fat tissue plays an essential role in storing excess energy, maintaining blood sugar balance, and producing hormones. Disrupting its function could potentially redirect fats into organs where they may cause harm.

Researchers will need to determine whether MTCH2 can be targeted safely, which tissues would benefit most, and whether reducing its activity would preserve muscle health while promoting fat loss.

For now, established strategies such as resistance exercise and adequate protein intake remain important for protecting muscle during weight reduction. A 2026 review of clinical trials found that combining lifestyle changes with resistance training produced a more favorable pattern of lean mass preservation than weight-loss interventions without such exercise.

The research nevertheless points toward a compelling possibility: Rather than focusing exclusively on how much food the body takes in, future obesity treatments might also influence how cells decide whether to burn energy or store it.

And one protein, Mitch, appears to be deeply involved in that decision.

Most of what you’ve read about deer drives is not true. And the maps that you’ve seen published, with arrows indicating the directions deer will travel when pushed by drivers, bear little resemblance to real-life situations in today’s deer woods. As a result, I’d be willing to bet this week’s grocery money that the traditional, worn-out driving tactics you and your partners are currently using are largely ineffective.

In the least, the rather standardized deer drives so prevalent among today’s whitetail hunters don’t have much relevance when it comes to taking big bucks that have become programmed to react to drive situations in an entirely different manner than naive younger deer. In short, today’s big deer are panic-proof, and that means they’re not likely to get up and flee directly away from a conventional drive line.

Yet it took the unraveling of some unforeseen events one year for my own group of hunters to realize just how often mature bucks had undoubtedly been making fools of us.

These events took place in southern Ohio, where it’s common for small groups of hunters to stage drives all day, every day. Only a small percentage of hunters in this area patiently sit in tree stands and still fewer attempt still hunting. Making drives is the name of the game, and the driving typically begins at first light in the morning and continues until evening dusk settles in.

Well, as it happened one day, the six of us planned the logistics for 14 different drives, and by early afternoon we had attached our tags to one doe and two small bucks. Trouble was, by that time we had also completed all 14 drives. Because we didn’t have permission to hunt on adjacent lands, there were only two alternatives: Quit for the day, or return to the location of the first drive that we had staged that morning and begin driving each cover a second time.

With our standers in place, the spread-out drive line began working through the cover exactly as had been the case earlier. However, after he had progressed only about one-third of the way through the cover being driven, Tom Tomlinson popped a bootlace, requiring him to squat down to initiate a repair. It only took a few minutes, but in the meantime the drive line had advanced more than a hundred yards, leaving Tomlinson far behind.

When the hunter finally stood and took only a few short steps, he heard a slight rustling in the leaves behind him and a quick glance revealed a splendid eight-point buck slinking along in a head-lowered, sway-backed posture. Probably more by spontaneous reflex than conscious effort, Tomlinson snapped off a quick shot at almost point blank range, and the deer went down in a heap.

As Tomlinson admired his buck, he considered the hairy situation that might have presented itself had the buck jumped up in front of instead of behind him, because that would have required shooting in the direction of his departing friends—which is absolutely verboten. Right then he realized the importance of every hunter exercising the utmost restraint when it comes to staging drives to always ensure safe shooting at close range, at fully identified targets, and with no intervening cover even partially blocking the gunner’s view.

When the group later rejoined for a confab to discuss the unusual happening, voiced opinions differed widely. Several hunters speculated that the buck had moved into the cover sometime after we had driven the location earlier that morning, but this opinion didn’t account for the buck somehow being behind the drive line during the second attempt to push the cover.

Finally we came to a consensus that the big deer had been there all along and that he had simply refused to leave his chosen security cover, preferring instead to repeatedly circle and dodge any human intruders.

About then, Tomlinson stated his future intentions. “Next time we hunt together,” he said, “I’d like to volunteer to be on the drive line all day. And I hope you don’t mind if I occasionally lag behind a bit!”

Of course, Tom Tomlinson is only one of many hunters who have recently begun to accept the tenet that, aside from hunting scrapes during the rutting period, enacting forced movement is just about the only way to take truly big bucks.

The reason why is because most big bucks become nocturnal in their behavior due to conditions that tend to inhibit their daytime activities. Common examples of conditions that suppress daytime movements include the existence of a full moon (which allows for safer nighttime feeding), strong daytime winds, unseasonably warm temperatures and hunting pressure. Whenever one or more of these conditions exist—a situation that occurs more often than not—maintaining a vigil on stand is futile, with drives being the most viable alternative.

Obviously, the key to the success of any drive is learning how to predict the most likely escape routes the animals will attempt to use. Yet what most hunters fail to understand, especially if the terrain being driven is relatively large, is that though immature deer will likely vacate the premises posthaste, mature bucks probably won’t come out at all.

Big bucks are extremely security conscious. Moreover, they select their hiding places with craft and cunning and seem to instinctively know that rising from their beds and exposing themselves to full view increases their vulnerability. Consequently, when a hunter filters through a big buck’s area, the buck will quite often lower his chin right down onto the ground and then not even blink!

Georgia deer biologist Larry Marchington once told me about a time he was hiking through a wooded area on a routine survey of habitat types when he spotted a whitetail buck bedded in a brushy thicket. The buck quickly lowered his head, and Larry decided to have some fun by attempting to get as close as possible to the animal.

“I made no pretense about my intentions,” Larry explained, “and simply began walking straight toward the animal. He was facing in a quartering-away direction, and I got so close that I could have reached out with a stick and prodded the buck in the rump, but he never budged an inch. Finally I walked around him in a semicircle in order to directly face him. Only after we eventually made eye contact with each other from only scant feet away did the buck explode out of the cover like he’d been scalded!”

Recurring incidents like this make me wonder how many times each year hunters walk right past huge bucks. Therefore, after a drive has been concluded, if members of your group have reason to believe that there is still a buck in the cover somewhere, there’s only one thing to do. Drive the cover a second time, or even a third, until the buck is either routed out or everyone is convinced that he wasn’t there in the first place.

Yet don’t make the mistake of driving the cover in the same manner the second time, because the only thing that this will accomplish is to educate the deer in that particular area. Try something different, such as driving the cover in the opposite direction, with your standers placed in the vicinity of where the drivers started out the first time.

Take a plantation of thick pines, for example. This type of cover offers a buck a myriad of hiding possibilities, and if your drivers repeatedly approach from the west, it won’t take very long for all of the deer in residence to learn from the experience.

Therefore, good advice the second time you drive the pines would be for the drivers to approach from the south, the next time from the east, and so on. That way, especially if you are the lucky beneficiaries of windy weather, the deer will remain totally confused and not know which direction to expect danger from. Nor will they ever know either the surest escape route to take to avoid blundering into hunters on stand, or the intended travel routes of the drivers.

In going back to my friend Tom Tomlinson and how he had to credit a broken bootlace in the successful taking of a nice buck, it’s now a standard practice for every member of our drive line to make a fishhook pattern. This strategy capitalizes upon a wary buck’s preference to hide rather than run.

Draw upon your own gut-level instincts, for example, and imagine yourself trying to elude someone in a jungle. If your pursuer passed close by but you knew that you were well concealed, chances are you’d hunker down and freeze. Later, after that person had passed and continued on for some distance, you’d undoubtedly reason “this spot is no longer safe.” Then you’d probably begin sneaking in the opposite direction to increase the distance separating you and your adversary while simultaneously looking for a new and better hiding place where you wouldn’t again have to risk such a close confrontation.

A whitetail buck behaves much the same by first holding tight in his bed, allowing the driver to pass, and then rising to his feet in an attempt to slip out the back door. Because he is so closely tied to his chosen security cover, it’s not likely that the buck will leave the region entirely. Yet he will indeed feel his safety has been jeopardized and that it’s time to relocate his bed a slight distance away but still in the same immediate area.

It was understanding this behavioral trait of whitetails that prompted Tomlinson to invent his novel fishhook drive. For the purposes of illustration, imagine a brushy riverbottom thicket. One or two hunters have been placed on stand along the far edge of the thicket several hundred yards from the drivers. At the near edge, two hunters enter the thicket and begin a routine drive, making their presence well-known by carrying on a casual conversation as they work their way through the cover.

However, instead of the drivers actually proceeding all of the way through the length of the thicket to where their partners are stationed on stand, they only travel perhaps two-thirds of the way through the thicket and then reverse their line of travel and ever so quietly begin sneak-hunting in the opposite direction toward their starting points. Naturally, they both wear bright-orange safety clothing and always keep track of each other’s location.

Quite often, one of the drivers will have an almost ridiculously easy, close-range shot at a buck slipping away. But here’s what makes this drive so super-effective. Any buck that initially stayed put until the drivers had passed and then began slinking away in the opposite direction will invariably find a new place to quickly bed back down. If one of the drivers does not get a shot when the buck is on his feet, the buck will predictably hold tight in his bed once again when the drivers approach during the course of making their fishhook pattern. Then, when the drivers pass the second time, the buck will again rise from his bed and begin moving away from the drivers; this will take him squarely in the direction where the stand hunters are waiting!

A similar technique is known as “back standing,” in which hunters are placed on stand both ahead of and behind the drive line, with the drivers purposely spreading themselves far apart to actually encourage deer to slip back between them so that the rearmost stand hunters may have shots. Here again, it is imperative that all participants keep close tabs upon their partners’ whereabouts to avoid any possibility of risky shooting.

The intriguing thing about the fishhook drive is that it can be counted upon to produce bucks even on crowded public hunting lands. There have even been several instances in which our group has seen hunters piling into trucks shortly after they had just completed a drive somewhere, has stopped to briefly chat with them, and has then driven the same exact cover and taken a buck that had cleverly eluded the first drive party.

However, one thing that we’ve noticed about the fishhook drive is that it works best in level-terrain situations and especially if the hunting party is relatively small and only if two or three individuals are able to act as drivers.

If the group is small but is hunting in hill country, a still better drive strategy is one that I learned in Tennessee from John Marcks. He dubbed his innovation “the diagonal shift.”

“We rarely have enough hunters in our group to place several on stands and designate still others as drivers,” Marcks explained, “so we don’t use hunters on stand at all.”

If you were to have a bird’s-eye view of a diagonal shift drive, it would look like a line of individuals marching up and across the crest of a ridge, down through the next hollow, up the opposite facing slope, down the next and so on. The most obvious feature of the drive line, however, is that it is not straight but angular, like the longest leg of a right triangle. Thereby, the lead hunter at the forward end of the drive line may be as much as several hundred yards ahead of the trailing hunter at the opposite end of the drive line.

What makes this strategy so productive is that when a buck holds tight until a driver has passed and then rises from his bed and begins sneaking in the opposite direction, the deer has no idea that he might be skulking right into the lap of another driver trailing behind the first.

Moreover, this tactic is a very safe way to stage a drive because due to the way in which the drivers are staggered diagonally across the rolling terrain, a hunter presented with a shot can shoot straight ahead or straight back or to his immediate right or left—assuming that he knows that another driver isn’t directly in front of or in back of him and that the hunter “ahead” of him hasn’t lagged behind, nor the hunter “behind” him speeded up. (In a conventional drive, shooting in any direction other than behind the driver would be very unsafe because of standers placed up ahead and other drivers lined up to one’s right and left.)

A few other tips also are in order when it comes to driving today’s well-educated bucks. I’ve been describing how panic-proof bucks generally prefer to hold tight in their beds rather than make like jackrabbits and head for the next county. But this usually only applies to bucks in large, unbroken tracts of real estate where the animals have plenty of room to circle and dodge intruders.

In small or broken segments of terrain, just the opposite often applies, and the deer seem to know that their survival depends upon their ability to get out of a tight space as quickly as possible and into distant cover. My group has had many experiences, for example, in which members began driving a 10-acre woodlot, and heard shooting from the standers within only one minute of beginning to push the cover.

This type of situation makes timing crucial to success. If the drive begins only five minutes too early, before the standers are well situated at their intercept locations, the effort is almost certain to be doomed to failure.

By the same token, a hunter who is instructed to take a stand must exercise great care in hiking to his designated waiting location. If a buck is bedded close by and hears the hunter moving into position, he’ll undoubtedly attempt to skirt the hunter and thereby vacate the immediate area.

A hunter moving to his stand should therefore take the long, roundabout way, if necessary, and sneak into position as quietly as possible. Also of critical importance is that once the stander has selected his vantage point, it is imperative that he not budge an inch. Many standers, after being in position for a few minutes and having an opportunity to survey their surroundings, decide that they’d be better off 50 yards to their right or left. In not knowing that their partners may have already started the drive, they begin walking to the “better” location and any buck coming their way instantly spots their movement and veers sharply to avoid them.

Finally, strive to maintain a degree of flexibility in engineering various types of drives. Today’s whitetail buck lives in closer proximity to mankind than his predecessors, and this has fostered in him the ability to quickly pattern man’s various activities.

It is for this reason that big bucks find it quite easy to outfox unimaginative hunters who always work drives in the same manner day after day and year after year. Conversely, those big bucks that ultimately find themselves packaged away in someone’s freezer are deer that never expected the unexpected.
