#244. Gen Z protests in Germany: Is France-style violence looming?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Young people in Germany are preparing to protest over education and climate change. Politicians fear violence like in France, but an expert sees differences.

As politicians in Germany look ahead to the coming weekend, many fear that cities across the country could witness the kind of violent unrest that recently shook France. An initiative known as "Gen Z regelt" ("Gen Z sorts it") has launched a social media campaign calling for demonstrations in 23 cities nationwide.

"Gen Z" broadly refers to the generation born between 1995 and 2010. Many feel that their parents' generation has done too little to combat climate change and has allowed the gap between rich and poor to widen ever further. At the same time, they are concerned about their educational prospects and future opportunities.

In France, what began as largely peaceful protests recently spiraled into serious unrest. Cars were set alight in several towns, while in Limoges the town hall was torched. In Lille, Lyon, and Toulouse and elsewhere, police deployed tear gas, leading to numerous arrests.

Many politicians, particularly within Chancellor Friedrich Merz's center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU), are warning of similar scenes this coming weekend.

Alexander Throm, the CDU parliamentary group's domestic spokesperson, told the daily Handelsblatt newspaper: "Scenes like those in France must be prevented at all costs."

In Throm's view, far-left groups and criminals from immigrant communities could "hijack" the demonstrations. "An ominous alliance is emerging, particularly in Berlin," he warned.

Marc Henrichmann, also of the CDU, chair of the Bundestag's parliamentary oversight panel for the intelligence services, also expressed concern: "The far-left extremist scene is internationally connected and coordinated." He said that violent far-left activists now even cross national borders to engage in looting and acts of violence elsewhere.

But Sebastian Koos, sociologist at the University of Konstanz whose research focuses on youth and protest movements, told DW that he does not expect Germany to experience unrest on a scale comparable to that seen in France, even though events in the neighboring country helped spark the protests now being planned across Germany.

Koos sees key differences between young people in the two countries. "Unlike in France, these protests are not driven by the same level of anger," he said. "They have been planned, officially registered and carefully organized. The website, in particular, places strong emphasis on peaceful protest and repeatedly stresses a non-violent approach."

At the same time, the sociologist does not rule out the possibility that extremist groups could exploit the authorized demonstrations to stir up unrest: "It is certainly very important for the police and public authorities to be well prepared."

Quentin Gärtner, 19, studies biotechnology in Heidelberg and served until November of last year as secretary-general of the Federal Student Conference, which represents around 7.5 million school students in Germany.

"What unites us is that we have run out of patience with the education system. Many young people are deeply frustrated," he told DW. "The performance of German school students continues to decline, teaching positions are constantly being cut, and in many cases school buildings have seen little or no meaningful investment for years."

For Gärtner, the discontent reflects a broader problem with parliamentary democracy in Germany and many other Western countries.

"Politicians want to win elections. They focus on the groups that matter most to them electorally rather than on the future and the long-term sustainability of their policies," he said.

"And in our aging societies, older people and the baby boomer generation are the groups that carry the most political weight."

Opposition parties in Germany have also voiced understanding for the concerns of younger people. Speaking to Germany's public broadcaster ZDF, Green Party co-leader Felix Banaszak said: "The intergenerational contract is no longer working. Older generations have driven the climate into a dead end."

He argued that the social welfare system was also reaching its limits and that rents had become unaffordable. According to Banaszak, the only thing the state seems to offer young people are the letters sent to 18-year-olds asking them to consider joining the military. Germany is currently desperate to expand the Bundeswehr and is considering reintroducing national service.

By Tuesday, around 134,000 people had registered on the "Gen Z regelt" website to take part in the protests planned for the coming Saturday. With several days still to go before the demonstrations, that number could rise further.

#267. Sandy Hook families urge Supreme Court to uphold state and local bans on AR-15-style rifles

Relatives whose loved ones were killed in the Sandy Hook Elementary School shooting are making an impassioned plea to the U.S. Supreme Court to uphold state and local bans on certain semiautomatic rifles, citing the high body counts and devastating injuries the weapons can inflict.

The parents of five of the 20 first-graders killed in the 2012 Connecticut shooting and relatives of one of six educators who died filed a “friend of the court” brief on Wednesday, weighing in on two cases challenging bans in Connecticut and the Chicago area. Justices are scheduled to hear oral arguments in both cases on Dec. 2.

“The assault on Sandy Hook Elementary School, carried out by an untrained, 112-pound youth armed with an AR-15-style rifle, is graphic proof of the unusual and extreme lethality of that firearm and the harm it is capable of inflicting on American communities,” the relatives' filing says. They added that they “seek to protect other American families from becoming casualties in another AR-15 killing spree.”

Gun rights advocates and groups whose lawsuits led to the two Supreme Court cases are arguing that it's unconstitutional under the Second Amendment to ban semiautomatic rifles, which are legally owned by millions of Americans. Supporters of the bans say such rifles were designed for military use, are too dangerous for civilians and should be regulated like machine guns.

More than 40 other people and groups — most in favor of the rifle bans — also filed “amicus curiae” briefs on Wednesday. Such filings are submitted by parties who are not directly involved in the litigation.

Sandy Hook shooter killed 26 people with AR-15-style rifle

The 20-year-old Sandy Hook gunman, Adam Lanza, shot his way into the locked school in Newtown, Connecticut, and unleashed a fury of more than 150 bullets in about four minutes with a Bushmaster AR-15-style rifle fueled by high-capacity ammunition magazines. The massacre led Connecticut to ban such rifles in 2013 — the same law now before the Supreme Court.

Jennifer Hensel, whose 6-year-old daughter Avielle Richman was killed in the shooting, was quoted in the relatives' brief saying Avielle was shot multiple times in the face and authorities told her that she would not recognize her daughter if she saw her body.

“A moral imperative is truly at hand,” Hensel said in supporting weapons bans. “Please, do not allow more blood to soak our Connecticut soil from assault-style weapons. A fundamental duty to protect all in Connecticut is at stake.”

A similar brief filed Wednesday by Sandy Hook Promise, the gun violence prevention group started by Newtown families, said another victim, 6-year-old Dylan Hockley, was shot five times — “his body destroyed so completely, his mother was told not to touch him to say goodbye as he lay in his coffin.”

“The effects of these weapons on children’s bodies are catastrophic and obscene,” the filing said.

Dylan's mother, Nicole Hockley, a co-founder of Sandy Hook Promise, urged the Supreme Court to consider children's safety.

“I know firsthand the devastation a semiautomatic rifle can inflict in minutes on the bodies, hearts, and minds of children — and the families who love them,” she said in a statement. “No family should have to live with that pain. As the Court considers this case, the consequences being experienced by children and communities cannot be an afterthought.”

Gun rights advocates filed suit to block rifle bans

Gun rights supporters say that while they sympathize with gun violence victims' families and condemn mass shootings, the solution isn't to curtail Second Amendment rights. They say school shootings happen despite bans on so-called “assault weapons.”

“Solving or at least mitigating school shootings is not about banning a type of gun,” said Konstadinos Moros, director of legal research and education for the Second Amendment Foundation, a plaintiff in the challenges to both the Connecticut and Cook County, Illinois, bans.

“While some guns are obviously more capable than others, school shooters inflict harm not because of the capability of their firearms, but because of the helplessness of their targets,” he said in an email to The Associated Press. “The real solution lies in proper school security, which can both more quickly respond to shootings, and deter attacks from happening in the first place, given that the depraved individuals who commit these sorts of crimes tend to pick ‘soft targets.’”

Justices to weigh whether bans violate Second Amendment

The current Supreme Court, which has expanded gun rights, announced in June that it would hear appeals challenging bans on the AR-15 and similar semiautomatic firearms in Connecticut and the Chicago area. The ban in Cook County was first passed in 1993. Lower courts have upheld both laws.

Similar laws are in place in about a dozen states, covering major cities like New York, Los Angeles and Washington, D.C. Congress allowed a national assault weapons ban to expire in 2004, but Democrats have supported renewing it in response to mass shootings. States have also continued to pass their own laws, including recent measures in Virginia and Rhode Island.

It is the latest high-profile dispute over guns to reach the court since its conservative majority handed down a landmark ruling in 2022 that expanded Second Amendment rights and spawned challenges to firearm laws around the country. The court said that any gun laws must have a strong grounding in the nation’s historical traditions.

#798. Albania takes anti-riot crackdown cue from France

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Albania has imposed steep fines on people who take part in violent protests, citing a recent law imposed in France, in an attempt to quell weeks of clashes between police and protesters demanding the resignation of Prime Minister Edi Rama.

Under measures adopted Wednesday, remaining at a demonstration that turns violent will be a criminal offense, even for those not directly involved in clashes. Anyone who “chooses to remain in a crowd” engaged in violence or looting, or is found “concealing their identity,” faces fines of up to 500,000 leks ($6,130), over six-times the Balkan country’s average monthly salary.

Initially sparked by student anger over education costs and deteriorating facilities, the demonstrations have evolved into nationwide protests against inequality, soaring living costs, and cuts to public services.

Citing “urban anarchy” when announcing the crackdown, Rama acknowledged France’s controversial ‘casseurs-payeurs’ (rioters pay) bill, which would hold demonstrators liable for up to €10,000 ($11,160) in damages. The bill passed the French Senate on Wednesday and is now headed to the National Assembly, following weeks of protests and riots in which over 1,300 protesters were arrested in a single day.

Italy has also followed suit. Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni’s government approved measures last week that criminalize “violent participation in a demonstration,” punishable by up to five years in prison and fines of up to €20,000.

In Albania, a “special electronic register” will track suspected offenders, while police gain powers to restrict gatherings near schools, hospitals, and government buildings over potential violence. The measures – which also provide compensation for businesses damaged in the unrest – are already in place and require parliamentary approval within 45 days to remain in force.

Anti-government protests in the country began peacefully in May over plans for a luxury resort in the Zvernec nature reserve, a project linked to US President Donald Trump’s son-in-law, Jared Kushner.

By mid-October, the protests escalated into daily clashes with police amid growing anger over corruption, economic mismanagement, unemployment, and failing public services. Demands for Rama’s resignation took center stage, but the prime minister, who has been in power since 2013 and was elected to a fourth term last year, has refused to budge.

The unrest has caused over €2 million in damage, according to official estimates, as rioters torched ministries and attacked police stations, public buildings, and businesses. Clashes flared up again over the weekend, with protesters chanting “Revolution” and “Rama in prison” and police using tear gas, rubber bullets, and water cannons in Tirana.

Despite the protests being driven by grievances over domestic matters, Rama blamed the violence on a “hybrid war” by foreign actors, specifically Russia and Iran.

Russia has dismissed the accusations. Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova suggested last week that Rama should look closer to home. “These are citizens of your own country who took to the streets to protest. Don’t shift your responsibility onto others. Deal with your own domestic problems.”

#923. France planning new riot crackdown law

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

France’s Senate has backed a sweeping “vandals pay” bill that could force people involved in violent gatherings to cover up to €10,000 ($11,100) in damage caused not only by them, but also by other members of the group. The vote follows weeks of nationwide protests which spiraled into looting, arson attacks, and clashes with police.

The bill was initially drafted by the government over the summer after violence following Paris Saint-Germain’s Champions League victory, but it gained urgency after a new wave of mass public disorder. Demonstrations against soaring living costs and worsening quality of education turned violent in several cities this month, with rioters looting shops, torching vehicles, and destroying the 19th-century city hall in Limoges. Thousands of people were arrested during the unrest, with hundreds injured.

Senators passed the measure by 229 votes to 105 on Wednesday, backed by conservatives, centrists, and Marine Le Pen’s right-wing National Rally despite fierce objections from the left.

While presenting the legislation, Interior Minister Laurent Nunez argued that it seeks “to deter vandals and looters who burden taxpayers and demonstrators with their misdeeds, and… to better compensate the victims of this damage.”

He added that the bill is also “firmly based on fault; fault exists when one participates in a group formed with the intention of committing violence.” Meanwhile, centrist Senator Olivier Bitz reassured that “it should not be assumed that this text would allow for the prosecution of passersby or peaceful demonstrators.”

The bill – which included around 20 additional provisions compared to its original version – establishes joint civil liability for people who knowingly join a group formed to commit violence or vandalism, or remain in a crowd after police dispersal warnings while aware of ongoing attacks.  Those found liable could owe up to €10,000 for destruction committed by others, although the final sum is to be determined in court, and peaceful participation alone would not trigger the measure.

To recover court-ordered compensation, the legislation would also allow deductions from social benefits, including family allowances, housing assistance, and minimum-income payments. The bill would also exempt people who remain in a gathering for legitimate reasons, potentially including journalists and medical volunteers.

While centrists and right-wing supporters portrayed the bill as a more just measure to extract damages from those involved in vandalism and protect law-abiding taxpayers, socialist Senator Marie-Pierre de La Gontrie warned that the welfare measures risk penalizing entire families.

Nathalie Godard, Director of the Action Department at Amnesty International France, also argued that the bill “threatens to further deter people from taking to the streets to exercise their rights to freedom of expression and peaceful assembly,” adding that it comes on the heels of the government’s push to crack down on protests.

The Senate passed the bill ahead of the 2027 presidential race, with two key contenders taking contrasting positions over the unrest. Marine Le Pen, the frontrunner in recent polls, has championed the “vandals pay” principle, accusing President Emmanuel Macron’s government of failing to control the violence.

Her left-wing rival Jean-Luc Melenchon has backed the protests while condemning looting and vandalism and accusing the authorities of inflaming tensions through police brutality.

While the bill sailed through the right-dominated Senate, it now faces a much tougher test in the fractured National Assembly, where centrist and right-wing lawmakers together hold enough seats to outvote the left but remain divided over the proposed crackdown.
