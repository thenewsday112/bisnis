#507. France: Young voters blase about Glucksmann's Socialists

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Raphael Glucksmann wants to lead France's Socialists to power without working with the left-populist Jean-Luc Melenchon, who is particularly popular with young voters. How can the center-left candidate win them over?

The historically working-class Parisian neighborhood of Belleville has gentrified in recent years. It was here that Raphael Glucksmann, a member of the European Parliament for France's center-left Socialists, celebrated his victory in the party's presidential primary on October 11.

Two rounds of voting had been anticipated, but the 46-year-old son of philosopher and essayist Andre Glucksmann triumphed in the first round with a convincing 66% of the vote and will be the Socialist candidate in France's 2027 presidential election.

As he left the Cantine de Belleville restaurant, Glucksmann was flour-bombed by an unidentified assailant. The following morning, he called on his rival, the left-wing French populist Jean-Luc Melenchon, to condemn attacks carried out by his supporters, though it is not clear that the attacker was indeed a supporter of Melenchon's La France Insoumise (LFI).

The incident has highlighted the issue set to dog Glucksmann's presidential candidacy over the coming weeks: How can he win over Melenchon's supporters if he refuses to work with their candidate?

"Where do you stand on Melenchon?" That was the question that dominated much of the Socialists' primary campaign. Party leader Olivier Faure spoke in favor of leaving the door open for cooperation with the LFI, but Glucksmann called for a clear break with the far left. His decisive victory is therefore also seen as a vote in favor of a clear demarcation.

The other primary candidates questioned Glucksmann's political experience. Segolene Royal, a previous presidential candidate, compared his bid for the Elysee with that of an "intern" wanting to take charge of a company. She said Glucksmann had no experience in government or even running a local authority.

Despite Glucksmann's victory in the primary, his starting position is a difficult one. A poll by Toluna Harris Interactive, published the day after his win, projects that he would win 10–11% of the vote. The same poll put Melenchon on 18%, meaning the far-left man would be much more likely to reach the runoff. But the survey was conducted before Glucksmann won the primary, and so does not take into account any potential boost the nomination may give him. Still, 10% would be an improvement on the Socialists' performance in the last presidential election in 2022, when their candidate Anne Hidalgo won just 1.75% of the national vote.

There is also the complication that the Socialists are banking on a man who is not even a party member to lead them from irrelevance. Raphael Glucksmann actually belongs to a small center-left party called Place Publique.

Glucksmann has his work cut out for him — especially with young people. In an Ipsos survey in September, which posited Glucksmann as the social-democratic candidate and Edouard Philippe as the only candidate from the political center, Melenchon scored 41% among the under-25s. Meanwhile, 20% of this age group supported the far-right nationalist Marine Le Pen. France's young electorate is politically divided, but Melenchon still dominates on the left.

The 75-year-old is currently rallying alongside the students whose protests have drawn international attention. At a rally near Clermont-Ferrand in central France, he expressed solidarity with their movement and reiterated his calls for lowering the voting age to 16 and abolishing Parcoursup, France's national admissions procedure for higher education.

In an analysis for the Fondation Jean-Jaures, a Socialist-aligned think tank, the political scientist Antoine Bristielle distinguishes between a core group of committed LFI voters and the broader left. Melenchon's most loyal supporters, he says, are often young, educated city-dwellers, and many are under financial pressure. Four in 10 households in this group live on a net monthly income of less than €2,000 ($2,200).

Bristielle estimates that just under a third of the left-wing electorate in his study care more about a left-wing victory than their allegiance to any particular party. Almost half of the people in this group are younger than 35 and would consider voting for the Socialists, the Greens or LFI. Redistribution and an active government are important to them, but most would prefer politicians who are willing to compromise.

The pattern recently observed in Germany, where young women are more likely to vote for the left and young men for the right, does not apply in France. Political scientists Anja Durovic, Nonna Mayer and Noemie Piolat found that in the 2024 European and parliamentary elections, young French women were not significantly more likely to vote for the left, or less likely to vote for the far right, than men of the same age. It remains to be seen whether this pattern will be replicated in the 2027 presidential election.

Glucksmann says he would ease the burden on earned income by reducing France's CSG social security tax while increasing taxes on inheritances worth more than €2 million. He also presents himself as a committed supporter of European integration, while Melenchon has called the EU a neoliberal institution. Glucksmann's political profile combines social policy demands with foreign policy positions that could also appeal to voters from the camp of the current president, Emmanuel Macron.

France's Ukraine policy is one such issue. Glucksmann favors decisive support for Kyiv. Manon Aubry, a member of the European Parliament for Melenchon's LFI, says her party supports military assistance only under certain conditions, namely: The provision of aid to Ukraine must first be subject to democratic debate; it must not enable attacks deep inside Russian territory; and it must not weaken France's own defense capabilities. In 2024, LFI MPs voted against the French–Ukrainian security agreement.

The tension between the parties of the left was once again apparent in the aftermath of the flour attack in Belleville. The LFI MP Sebastien Delogu posted a declaration of support on X for the taxi driver who was going to have to clean his car.

Glucksmann must win over a lot of voters from Melenchon's camp if he is to have a chance of winning the presidency. This is likely to prove a much more difficult task than simply distancing himself from Melenchon.

#1592. New York eases part of Native American imagery ban

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

A core part of the mascot ban will remain in place, however, despite pressure from the Trump administration to overturn it as a form of racial discrimination.

New York is continuing its fight against Native American mascot imagery despite pressure from the Trump administration and local districts suing to keep their logos, which have in some cases been unsuccessful so far.

However, the state is backing down from implementing a portion of its ban that it put in place in 2023 amid those legal crosshairs, according to court documents.

The state in 2023 prohibited the use of Indigenous names, mascots and logos by public schools, saying in its proposal at the time that it was trying to "promote positive learning environments in schools" and that research has found such imagery could create a hostile environment for Indigenous students. Under the policy change, most districts were required to transition away from the prohibited mascots by 2025.

Some districts sued the state as early as 2023, with Massapequa Union Free School District saying in its lawsuit that its mascot references the "rich history" of its Long Island community and is "meant to honor the area's Native American roots."  Changing mascots can also come with hefty price tags for school districts, with one Minnesota school district affected by policies similar to New York's estimating last year that the change could cost $1 million.

As part of Massapequa's and another district's lawsuit against the state, brought by Wantagh Union Free School District, the New York State Attorney General Letitia James’ office told the courts in an Oct. 16 update that it was repealing a section of the state mascot ban that prohibits school employees on school property or at school events from wearing or promoting any Indigenous names, logos or mascots.

However, the broader ban on Indigenous school mascots will remain, while some claims brought by the Wantagh school district lawsuit and others are still ongoing in the appeals circuit.

New York State Department of Education spokesperson JP O'Hare, in an Oct. 21 email to K-12 Dive, described legal challenges brought against the state by Long Island school boards as "unsuccessful.”

"As schools complete the transition away from stereotypical Indigenous mascots, the Department will recommend that the Board of Regents leave decisions regarding employee clothing, including apparel displaying offensive Indigenous imagery, to local school districts, consistent with how other employee attire and personnel decisions are handled," O'Hare said.

The Massapequa district celebrated the change as a positive step forward, but called on New York Gov. Kathy Hochul and the Board of Regents to repeal the entire ban.

"This is a significant victory for Massapequa and for the principle of local control. From the beginning, we have maintained that the state’s Native American mascot regulation represents an unconstitutional overreach," said the Massapequa School District Board of Education in an Oct. 23 statement to K-12 Dive. "The state’s recommendation to return authority over employee apparel to local school districts is an important acknowledgment that these decisions should be made locally, not dictated by Albany."

New York's decision to walk back a portion of its mascot law while remaining staunch in its decision to prohibit Native American mascots comes despite pressure from the Trump administration, which has repeatedly backed the logos and said that prohibiting them is racial discrimination.

Last year, the U.S. Department of Education launched an investigation into the New York Department of Education for alleged Title VI violations. About a month later, the U.S. Education Department found the state agency in violation of the civil rights law protecting students from race-based discrimination.

“The U.S. Department of Education will not stand by as the state of New York attempts to rewrite history and deny the town of Massapequa the right to celebrate its heritage in its schools,” said U.S. Education Secretary Linda McMahon in an April 2025 statement. “While New York chooses to prioritize erasing Native Americans, their rich history, and their deep connection to the state, it is requiring schools to divert time and resources away from what really matters: educating our students.”

Within two months of the original investigation, the agency referred the case to the U.S. Department of Justice for enforcement proceedings, after the state refused to change its policies as requested by the administration. There has been no recent public update in that referral proceeding.

New York has been on the frontlines of the issue, joined by a handful of other states with similar restrictions. In 2019, Maine became one of the first to enact such a ban, according to Lutzker & Lutzker, which provides trademark and branding law services.

As of April 2023, more than 21 states had acted to or were working to address the use of Native mascots in their public schools, according to the firm.

#2098. Justice Department Sues to Stop Secret Gender Transitions at Anne Arundel County Public Schools

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

An official website of the United States government

The Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division announced today its lawsuit to stop Anne Arundel County Public Schools (AACPS) from secretly “gender transitioning” children without their parents’ knowledge or consent and blocking parents’ access to information about their children, which they are entitled to receive under federal law. The Department’s lawsuit enforces the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) and the Protection of Pupil Rights Amendment (PPRA).

“Schools should not discuss preferred pronouns or ‘gender identity’ with a student without parental notification and consent,” said Assistant Attorney General Harmeet K. Dhillon of the Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division. “Parents should not be lied to, stonewalled, or branded as ‘safety concerns’ when they ask to see the education records that reveal what schools are secretly doing to their children. This Department will end illegal school policies that keep parents in the dark.”

The Department’s complaint alleges that AACPS’s gender policies deny parents their rights under FERPA and PPRA. AACPS directs school staff to solicit, document, and act upon a student’s self‑identified name, pronouns, and “gender identity” without notifying parents. AACPS designates “gender identity” information as “confidential” and instructs staff to withhold it from parents based on the unilateral and unfounded assertion that informing parents would pose a “safety concern” to students. AACPS reports “gender identity” information to the Maryland State Department of Education without parental notice or an opportunity to challenge its accuracy.

The complaint also includes allegations about one anonymous family’s struggle with AACPS’s policies. During the 2025-2026 school year, AACPS questioned students, one-on-one, about their preferred names and pronouns. Thereafter, Jane and John Doe twice received emails from teachers referring to their daughter by a male name and pronouns. The Does confronted AACPS, but each time AACPS lied about helping their daughter pretend to be male at school. Although AACPS eventually admitting to lying, AACPS declined to give the Does details about their daughter’s “gender transition” and refused to turn over the child’s relevant education records.

Today’s lawsuit is the latest in a series of steps taken by the Civil Rights Division to end public school policies that trample on parental rights guaranteed under FERPA and PPRA. FERPA authorizes parents to inspect and review their children’s education records, as well as correct or delete inaccurate information within those records. Under PPRA, parents have the right to give or withhold consent before schools collect sensitive personal information from minor students.

Individuals may file FERPA and PPRA complaints with the U.S Department of Education at: studentprivacy.ed.gov/file-a-complaint.

#2154. Azerbaijan’s Hijab Restrictions Exclude Girls From School

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Three Azerbaijani women wearing headscarves in Baku, Azerbaijan. © 2019 Getty Images

School officials across Azerbaijan have been turning away girls wearing hijabs following the introduction of new government dress regulations, sparking protests across the country. Police have detained dozens of people amid the protests, while Instagram and TikTok users have reported difficulties accessing the platforms as public criticism of the restrictions has grown.

Azerbaijan’s Cabinet of Ministers amended school uniform regulations on September 21, prohibiting clothing "inconsistent with the principle of secular education." Although the regulations do not explicitly mention hijabs, schools began refusing entry to girls wearing headscarfs when the rules took effect on October 12. News reports showed distressed girls after being turned away from schools.

Separate dress requirements adopted on October 6 also affected teachers.

Authorities responded to the protests with widespread detentions. The Ministry of Interior acknowledged on October 12 that they had detained 37 people in Baku, alleging disruptions to public order. Independent media reported that more than 200 people were detained near a central Baku metro station that evening. Activists also reported 88 detentions in Nardaran village, with criminal cases opened against many of those detained.

People criticizing the restrictions online have also faced detention. Unidentified individuals reportedly took activist İlkin Hüseynov from his home on October 14, a day after he launched "Let Leyli Go to School," an online initiative connecting volunteer teachers with girls excluded from classrooms. He was subsequently sentenced to 30 days detention for allegedly disobeying police orders.

In Nakhchivan, a man reportedly received a sentence for 25 days detention following a TikTok post about the restrictions. Social media users across Azerbaijan also reported difficulties accessing Instagram and TikTok on October 12; some were only able to connect via virtual private networks.

Excluding girls from schools because they wear religious headscarves interferes with their rights to education and freedom of religion or belief, and raises serious concerns about discrimination. While governments may regulate school attire, any restrictions must be lawful, necessary, proportionate and non-discriminatory. Azerbaijan should not use the principle of secular education to deny girls access to schooling.

Azerbaijani authorities should immediately ensure that students and teachers wearing hijabs can attend schools, release anyone detained solely for peacefully protesting or criticizing the restrictions, ensure access to legal counsel for those in custody, and investigate reports of interference with social media access.

#2773. What 272 Climbing Accidents Reveal About the Risks Climbers Face

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

The 2026 Accidents in North American Climbing reports on accidents that occurred in late-2024 and 2025. (Photo: Maya Silver)

A couple weeks ago, the American Alpine Club released its annual roundup of misfortunes in the mountains. The 2026 Accidents in North American Climbing covers accidents reported across the United States and Canada that occurred in 2025 or late in 2024.

On a trip to Grand Staircase-Escalante National Monument last weekend, I read this year’s Accidents in North American Climbing (ANAC) cover to cover. Then I caught up with editor Pete Takeda to find answers to my questions about his reporting and uncover some of the stories and data behind the trends I observed. I also pulled from Climbing’s coverage of some of these incidents shortly after they took place to get the bigger picture.

In 2025, Pete Takeda said he received 30% more accident reports from climbers than in 2024, resulting in 16 more pages than last year’s book. That makes the 2026 ANAC the biggest edition on record in terms of number of accidents reported since the American Alpine Club (AAC) started printing the book in 1948. Takeda even had to omit a number of accidents because they simply ran out of space.

But that doesn’t necessarily mean that more accidents are occurring. Takeda told Climbing last week that he thinks the uptick is due to the fact that more climbers are reporting their accidents. “People are becoming aware of how their individual accident has significance and meaning if they push it out there to inform people,” Takeda observed. He added that climbers are no longer “a bunch of hardened” folks on the edges of society willing to put it all on the line for the send.

As the culture of climbing evolves to become more mainstream, climbers care more about risk and safety than in the past. “It’s a cultural shift,” Takeda said. He also added that the AAC saw increased accident submissions after Shane Johnson, their VP of Media, established the Prescription video series in late 2020 to analyze them.

The theory that more climbers are simply reporting their accidents also could explain why 2025 saw a notable increase in accidents and injuries, but a decline in fatalities. Perhaps climbers are becoming more likely to report less significant accidents as the culture shift that Takeda sees occurs.

But it could also be a fluke. “Year to year, you get significant swings,” Takeda said. “Some years, fewer people get killed and more people break their ankles.”

Aside from year-to-year swings, here are some of the key takeaways and trends from the 2026 Accidents in North American Climbing.

I noticed a number of incidents this year on the approach or in alpine terrain involving sketchy talus fields—and climbers approaching them perhaps a bit too casually. One involved a guy hiking with a coffee mug in one hand. He lost his footing and tumbled 20 feet down a scree field.

Another involved a climber who dislodged a rock that then landed on his foot while scrambling to a summit in the High Sierras. The rock threw off his balance and he fell. Wearing a helmet may have prevented further injuries beyond his concussion and whiplash. “I now plan to wear the helmet for any scramble ascent or descent,” the anonymous climber wrote in the report.

One fatal incident involved a climber scrambling off trail to pick up some litter on the way back to the car. He slipped and fell 25 feet, landing chest down on a rock. The climber’s shoes were untied. And in alpine terrain in British Columbia, a loose boulder even resulted in the amputation of one climber’s thumb.

Maybe it’s time for rock climbers to take a cue from ice climbers, who often gear up for the approach—and take it a lot more seriously. “Maybe put on some approach shoes, maybe wear a helmet, maybe have one to two hands ready,” Takeda recommended.

Yes, we said it: A helmet on the approach. While many climbers may still skip the helmet while climbing or belaying, the safety-oriented among us ought to consider adding a helmet on the approach if it involves sketchy scree fields, overhanging walls above, or rockfall risk.

Several accidents this year involved small gear ripping out. Two of them occurred in Joshua Tree. In one incident, two Black Diamond #.3s ripped, resulting in a 40-foot groundfall. The other involved three “marginal nuts” ripping out, resulting in a 40-foot deck onto a boulder. The nuts in question were DMM Peenuts, sizes 3, 4, and 5. And in the Leavenworth area, a #.2 ripped out of a horizontal crack. Climbing also reported not too long ago on a deck in Scotland that involved a #.2 Z4 cam failing.

“Like with any gear, with smaller pieces, the margins of safety are a lot smaller,” Takeda said. He observes that smaller cam placements aren’t as strong, require better finesse, and involve a steeper learning curve. “We’re just going to see more and more of this,” he predicted.

Derek Gustafson, who helped design Black Diamond’s Ultralight Camalots, recommends backing up small cams with a nut. With small cams, it’s even more important than with mid-sized cams to make sure your small cams are placed well with the ideal level of lobe retraction. Instead of the 25-75% lobe retraction you’d aim for with, say, a #3, Gustafson suggests an ideal spectrum for small cams of 30-60%. That’s because smaller cams have a narrower range of effectiveness, especially in softer rock like sandstone.

Takeda also advised more climbers to take the time to learn how to place nuts. “A few years ago, we reported 15 accidents involving pulled protection, novices to pros. All were cams,” Takeda said. He added that he’d rather trust a nut than a cam “any day, for a bunch of reasons.” He prefers nuts in the RP to finger range. “They’re simple, light, and easy to visually assess …  more so than tiny cams,” Takeda said.

One of the most memorable accidents in this year’s book involves four climbers bailing off an alpine route in Washington. As we reported last May, they decided to all rappel off a single piton on one of the pitches. The piton failed, sending all four on a 500- to 700-foot fall down steep rock and ice. Only one climber survived. This year’s ANAC goes into the tragic story in more detail, but the takeaway is clear: Don’t trust that piton without testing it and backing it up.

Two other incidents in 2025 involved pitons failing. In West Virginia’s Seneca Rocks, a climber fell over 60 feet when a piton ripped that a climber had tested by yanking on it. She carries the broken piton in her climbing pack as a reminder. And on a route in Utah’s Little Cottonwood Canyon, a piton—that a climber had hung on previously—failed under dynamic forces in a fall. “Backing up fixed pitons is a wise choice,” the climber concluded.

In September in Little Cottonwood Canyon, I in part decided not to lead a route for exactly this reason: the first possible piece of protection was an old piton with no opportunity to back it up. Rust or material deterioration aside, the cracks into which pitons are wedged often widen slowly over time due to melt-freeze cycles. For that reason, some recommend doing a bounce test. You can also re-hammer a piton in to get it deeper in the constriction (if you happen to carry an actual or improvised hammer).

Even if you think that piton is bomber, what these accidents show is that you shouldn’t trust your life to this antiquated gear without verifying its strength. When in doubt, back it up with at least one other piece.

In the intro to this year’s ANAC, Takeda called toprope soloing accidents “a growing trend” that he’s observed since at least 2024. As people with busy lives try to get outside, it’s difficult to coordinate their complicated work and family schedules with partners. That makes toprope soloing an alluring option. Especially with the rise of purpose-built products from companies like Avant, climbers increasingly see this as a casual and low-risk way to get out there.

But Takeda observes climbers getting in over their heads who don’t fully understand the mechanics of the systems they’re using. Pair that with a lack of self-rescue skills, and some toprope soloists are bound to get themselves into a pickle.

One accident, which ended up getting cut from this year’s ANAC due to space constraints, involved a climber in Devil’s Lake, Wisconsin. That climber was trying to get back into the sport. “He used to climb difficult routes,” Takeda said of the climber, who had set up a 5.8 or 5.9. “He ended up getting stuck and having to get rescued. Basically, the guy couldn’t do the moves and lacked the knowledge of how to escape his rig.”

Takeda recommends hiring a guide to help you dial your toprope soloing devices and setups before you go it alone.

This year’s report includes a couple of knees hopelessly wedged into offwidths. One even bordered on the possibility of amputation. This is unfortunately a topic that Climbing associate editor Sam MacIlwaine is all too familiar with.

Takeda once viewed this as a relatively ridiculous accident category that typically only befell the Brits—until his own knee got wedged into a crack.

We asked him to share his own knee epic from the early 2000s, guest starring the late great Hayden Kennedy. Takeda was climbing a 5.11 offwidth in Indian Creek when his knee became stuck. His friends showed up, including Kennedy. He recalls his knee was stuck for around 15 minutes—or “long enough for me to panic and then go, ‘Just be cool, dude.’” Eventually, Kennedy came to the rescue, tapping on Takeda’s heel with a hammer.

“This is going to happen more and more,” Takeda said of the stuck knee incidents, as offwidth climbing rises in popularity. Check out MacIlwaine’s tips for how to avoid a stuck knee—and what to do if it happens.

In a few cases this year, climbers decided to bail when the retreat from their bail point was far from ideal. Instead of finishing out the remaining pitches, they chose to rappel across rope-eating terrain without descent infrastructure. If they’d gone up, a safer rappel with fixed anchors or a walk-off awaited. While it may seem like the responsible thing to do is to retreat rather than continue on—especially in the event of encroaching weather—consider whether you may end up actually spending more time building marginal rappel anchors, dealing with stuck ropes, and losing gear in the process.

In one case on the East Buttress of Mt. Whitney, climbers who got off-route by following another party in the wrong direction actually made a good decision to continue upward rather than hazard a series of rappels with bail gear. “We made logistical, thought-out choices—the first was to bail up instead of down,” wrote Micah, one of the climbers involved.

Recently, we reported on an incident in the Wind River Range, wherein two climbers decided to bail off Pingora Peak due to weather, sickness, and dehydration. But the low-angle crack ate their rope. Without the skills to self-rescue, they were condemned to a night on the wall until rescuers could reach them. The safer, faster option would have been to carry on, top out, and descend via the bolted rappel anchors to the climber’s left of the summit.

Every year, fresh rappelling accidents make it onto the pages of ANAC and the web pages of Climbing. But while rappelling comes with well-documented risks, Takeda said that lowering accidents are increasingly becoming a cause of accidents, too. He attributed this in part to “a multiplicity of anchor styles,” particularly in the U.S. “People don’t know or misinterpret the anchor,” Takeda said. “It comes down to education and standards across North America.”

The justices will hear the case during the October 2026 term and will consider whether allegations that a school admissions policy is racially discriminatory under the 14th Amendment must first demonstrate the policies harmed a certain racial group’s enrollment rate in comparison to its applicant pool size.

#649. Scientists reveal the hidden danger of missing blood pressure medication

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Missing doses of blood pressure medication could significantly reduce its ability to protect against heart attacks, strokes, and other serious cardiovascular problems, according to findings presented at ESC Congress 2026.

An estimated 1.4 billion adults worldwide between the ages of 30 and 79 have high blood pressure (hypertension), a major contributor to illness and death. Although medications that lower blood pressure are proven to reduce the risk of cardiovascular complications, approximately half of patients do not take their prescriptions as directed.

"We have evidence that poor adherence to antihypertensive medication is associated with worse outcomes," explained presenter, Miss Qianqian Yang from the University of Oxford, UK. "However, previous data has come from observational studies and could be confounded by other factors. We undertook an analysis of randomized trial data to assess the true impact of adherence on the benefits of antihypertensive treatment."

Major Study Examines the Impact of Missed Blood Pressure Medication

To better understand how consistently taking medication affects its benefits, researchers analyzed individual patient data from 91,339 participants across nine clinical trials. Each trial compared a blood pressure treatment (intervention group) with an alternative approach (comparator group: either placebo or a less-intensive regimen).

The researchers divided participants into two categories based on how closely they followed their prescribed treatment. Those who took at least 80% of their assigned medication were considered to have higher adherence, while those who took less than 80% were placed in the lower adherence group.

Even with the regular monitoring and structured conditions of clinical trials, patients became less consistent about taking their medication as time passed.

"A decline in adherence over time was apparent, even in the structured setting of trials," noted Miss Yang. Overall, 88% of participants met the higher adherence threshold during the first year, but that figure had dropped to just 79% by the fifth year.

Skipping Blood Pressure Pills Reduces Treatment Benefits

The difference in medication adherence was reflected in how much patients' blood pressure improved.

Among participants with higher adherence, systolic blood pressure, the top number in a blood pressure reading, was 5.2 mmHg lower in the intervention group than in the comparator group. For those with lower adherence, the difference was considerably smaller at just 3.0 mmHg.

Researchers then examined whether these differences translated into protection against major cardiovascular problems. The outcomes included strokes, heart attacks (myocardial infarction), ischemic heart disease, and heart failure that resulted in hospitalization or death.

The findings revealed a striking contrast. Among patients who consistently followed their treatment plans, the intervention group experienced an 11% lower incidence of major cardiovascular events compared with the comparator group. However, among patients with lower adherence, there was no statistically significant reduction in these events.

"Notably, our results reinforce the saying that 'drugs don't work in patients who don't take them.' Patients with lower adherence derived little or no cardiovascular benefit, whereas those with higher adherence had greater reductions in blood pressure and prevention of cardiovascular events," said Miss Yang.

Helping Patients Get Lifesaving Heart Protection

The researchers believe that helping people take their medication consistently should become a greater priority in the treatment of high blood pressure. They recommend more routine assessments of medication adherence, along with better patient education and practical strategies to make treatment easier to follow.

Possible approaches include simplifying medication schedules, combining multiple treatments into a single pill, and prescribing longer-acting drugs that can help limit the consequences of an occasional missed dose.

Professor Felix Mahfoud, Chair of the ESC Communication Committee, emphasized that missed medication has received too little attention as a contributor to poorly controlled blood pressure.

"For years, we have neglected non-adherence to medication as a cause for uncontrolled hypertension. Assessment of adherence should become a routine part of hypertension care so patients do not miss out on life-saving benefits. As healthcare providers, we are here to have open, non-judgmental conversations with our patients about any barriers they may have to taking medication, to help improve adherence."

#2861. Where to Shoot a Deer: Everything Hunters Need to Know About Shot Placement

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 24, 2026 8:34 AM EDT

Get everything from whitetail news and opinion pieces, to advanced hunting tactics and cutting-edge gear reviews.

Knowing where to shoot a deer for a quick kill is the responsibility of every deer hunter. Most of us were taught that the best place to shoot a deer is the “boiler room,” which means the top of the heart and the lungs. For the vast majority of hunting scenarios, that’s still the best option. Penetrating these vital organs (with a bullet or an arrow) leads to massive blood loss and a quick death. It also results in very minimal meat damage.

So, if you know you want to shoot a deer in the heart and lungs, where exactly should you aim?

Put simply, if the deer is broadside, follow the front leg up the deer and aim just below the halfway point on its chest. Look for the “crease” that the deer’s shoulder creates. Shooting with a rifle, you can edge tighter to the deer’s shoulder. Bowhunters want to hit just an inch or two behind the crease, which allows a little more room for error (more on this later).

There isn’t much of a difference in aim point for rifle hunters or bowhunters, as you can see in the photo below.

The video below, created by the National Deer Association, does an excellent job explaining where to shoot a deer on an anatomically correct deer target. Look closely at the bone structure of a deer’s shoulder. It is not one solid mass of bone. First the bone angles forward of the leg and then the shoulder blade sweeps back. Understanding this anatomy is especially important for archers who generally want to avoid hitting these large bones with their arrow (well constructed rifle bullets will easily blast through bones). This is a key difference for bowhunters: There’s simply a much smaller margin of error.

Now, the discussion on where to shoot a deer gets more complicated when deer are quartering away from you or angling toward you. You still want to hit the vital area of the heart and lungs, but now you must change your aim point. The best way to do this is to visualize where the heart and lungs are placed within the deer’s body cavity and aim so that your bullet or arrow angles through them. In general, this means holding farther back on quartering away shots and farther forward on quartering-to shots.

Many hunters prefer quartering-away shots to broadside shots. In some cases, the angle creates a larger margin of error for hitting the lungs. Plus, the deer is facing away from you so it’s easier to draw or shoulder your gun without being detected.

Visualize an angle that takes your bullet or arrow through the deer’s heart and lungs and then continues into its offside shoulder, or just in front of it. One way to do this is to look at the offside leg. Follow the front of the offside leg up to the body.

The photo above is the aim point for bowhunters on this angle. Rifle hunters should aim a few inches farther forward.

Quartering-to shots are not ideal for bowhunters. This is because you must hold into the shoulder or risk hitting guts. Lighter arrows and lower draw weight bows will not penetrate bones in the deer’s shoulder. But for rifle hunters shooting quality bullets, the quartering-to angle still presents a good shot opportunity. You must hold into the frontside shoulder now, with your bullet exiting the middle or back of the rib cage. This shot will damage more meat than a well-executed broadside shot.

Frontal shots can be incredibly effective but they offer a smaller margin of error than broadside shots. When a deer is facing directly toward you, aim between the two shoulders, just below the half-way point on its chest. Executed properly, this shot typically drops deer very quickly. Because your target is much narrower at this angle, however, this shot should only be taken at close ranges.

Bowhunters who hunt from the ground and rattle in or decoy bucks commonly see frontal shots. If you’re a bowhunter and intend to take frontal shots, you should opt for a heavy arrow with a high FOC and heavy, durable fixed-blade broadheads. But most importantly, you must have your setup tuned properly and be able to shoot baseball sized groups without fail.

You will see some hunters bragging online about the “Texas heart shot” which means shooting a deer that is facing straight-away, through the anus. It’s true that when this shot is made properly, with a rifle, it can be quickly fatal. The bullet passes through the rear end of the deer and takes out its vitals in front. Essentially, it’s the same as the frontal shot but in reverse.

However hunters (and especially bowhunters) should pass on this shot angle. Deer are often angling away, not walking perfectly straight away. So it’s very likely that you’ll hit the deer in the ham and miss the vital organs. Also, a deer presenting this shot angle is likely moving, which only increases the likelihood of a marginal hit. The one exception here is when making a followup shot. If you know you hit the deer with your first shot (and are unsure of how well) then it’s wise to follow up with a going-away shot if you can.

Shooting a deer with a bow is more complicated than shooting one with a gun. As we’ve already discussed, there’s less margin for error with archery gear. Plus, since bowhunters are often hunting from treestands and taking close-range shots, they have to account for the sharper angle of the shot. Lastly, deer commonly jump a bow string on farther shots, meaning they duck or spin the instant you loose your arrow. In many cases, bowhunters should aim slightly lower on their target, even if they intend to hit a deer in the heart/lungs. Here’s what you need to know about where to shoot a deer with a bow.

Many bowhunters advocate for shooting deer through the shoulder in the “vital V,” instead of the crease. By this they mean, holding into the deer’s shoulder, where its humerus and scapula make a sideways V shape. If you place your arrow through this window, it’s unarguably deadly. There are no bones (besides the ribcage) in this V. Plus, bowhunters shooting heavy arrows, stout draw weights, and durable fixed-blade broadheads are able to penetrate these bones without issue anyway. The YouTube personality “Ranch Fairy” has been one of the most prominent modern advocates of aiming for the Vital V, and you can see his explanation in the video below.

Aiming for the vital V is problematic, however, especially for new bowhunters. If you pull your shot low or forward, you’ll hit heavy bones in the shoulder, which your arrow might not pass through. Plus, the lungs narrow in the deer’s forward body cavity. Most bowhunter’s education courses, and this author recommend holding farther back into the crease of the deer’s shoulder, and not aiming for the vital V.

As long as you’re using quality archery gear, a well-placed shot through the crease will be deadly, almost without fail. Arrow weight, broadhead style, and draw weight aren’t as much of a factor here. Plus, you have slightly more room for error by holding behind the shoulder. By aiming for the crease, you’re holding for the area on the deer’s body where the lungs are the tallest and widest. If you miss a little forward, you’ll likely hit the vital V, if you miss a little back, you’ll likely hit the back of the lungs and liver.

It’s also easy to see the crease and use it as a specific spot to aim for. Picking a singular aim point is crucial.

When shooting from a tree stand, your arrow will be striking deer at a downward angle. The higher your stand and the closer the deer, the steeper this angle will be. For example, if you’re 20 feet up, shooting a deer that’s 15 yards away, you’ll be shooting downward at an approximately 60-degree angle. When your arrow strikes the deer, it will look something like the photo below.

So for close shots at a steep angle, you want to aim slightly higher. This ensures your arrow will pass through both lungs. But the keyword here is “slightly.” Generally speaking, hitting a deer too high with a bow is bad.

The best thing you can do to master elevated shot angles to is practice them. Use  a 3D target and shoot from an elevated position, as if you were in a treestand. This will help you get an instinctual understanding of where to hold for different shot angles. You want your arrow to pass through the top of the near lung and the bottom of the far lung.

All of this gets a bit more difficult for bowhunters because so often deer jump the string or duck at the sound of the bow releasing. This is typically only an issue beyond 20 yards. Some bowhunters solve for this by simply aiming a little lower for farther shots. For shots beyond 20 yards, it’s a good idea to hold a little lower, but still in the vital zone. However there is no rule or formula for outsmarting string-jumping deer.

“Their reactions are so dynamic and different in every situation that you cannot predict what the deer is going to do,” says Aaron Warbritton of The Hunting Public. “Over all the video we’ve watched, we noticed that some deer would duck straight down to the ground. Some deer would drop a foot at 30 yards. Others would drop four inches at 30 yards. Still other deer would not move at 30 yards. Some would duck and roll away. So, they would be broadside at 30 yards, duck six inches, roll away and by the time the arrow impacted, the deer is actually quartering away instead of broadside.”

Every scenario is slightly different, but here are some basic tips and takeaways to deal with deer jumping the string:

Because rifle bullets carry so much more energy and generally do more damage to bones and organs, rifle hunters have more options besides the heart and lung area. With a heart/lung-shot, deer often run off even though they die quickly. A heart-shot deer can still make it 100 yards. Some hunters want to drop deer in their tracks, so should these hunters aim elsewhere? We asked a number of deer cullers—sharpshooters whose job requires them to kill deer quickly, for their perspectives on bullet placement. Their advice, detailed below, is: “It depends” on distance, bullet type, shooting ability, and your goals for meat retention. Besides the heart and lung area, other options include high-shoulder, neck, and head. There are pros and (major) cons to each.

Pros: The ultimate shock-and-awe shot. A big, fast-moving bullet will likely snap the spine, short-circuit the nervous system, break bones, and anchor a deer where it stands.

Cons: The volatile, upsetting bullets best suited for this shot damage a lot of meat, from the shoulder through the neck and upper backstrap. Plus, it’s easy to miss high when aiming here.

When you sharpshoot deer for a living, as Grant Woods did for 21 years, “you can’t afford misses or wounded deer running around,” he says. Both cost you time and money—especially a wounded, bleeding deer, running for its life and spooking other deer.

Woods, a noted whitetail biologist, did much of his deer-­control work on golf courses. There, shots usually ranged between 200 and 300 yards. His first choice was the double-shoulder shot, with a .308 round entering a shoulder blade on one side, slamming through the body and into the far shoulder blade.

“If you watch slow-motion video of a deer being shot this way, its whole body flexes when the bullet hits,” says Woods. “That snaps the spine. That deer’s never going to move again.” This shot can damage more meat than the heart-lung approach, but the benefit is that the deer will likely drop immediately.

Pros: A deer dies instantly when its brain takes a direct hit. Plus, there is very little meat lost to a head shot.

Cons: The brain is a tiny target, and it’s easy to miss the deer entirely or, worse, to wound it through the jaw.

How do you guarantee a drop-it-where-it-stands shot? For Anthony DeNicola, owner of White Buffalo, a top deer-control operation, it’s all about the brain.

“Draw a line from tear duct to tear duct, then go 2.5 to 2.75 inches above that line, centered,” says DeNicola. “That’s where you want to place your bullet—first and best option.”

A bullet in the brain instantly incapacitates the animal; death follows in seconds. Of course, DeNicola and his team have an advantage over hunters: They shoot at night with infrared optics, from raised mobile platforms, over bait, at known distances (usually 50 to 60 yards), and (where legal) with suppressed rifles.

DeNicola uses .223-caliber rifles, firing 50- to 55-grain frangible varmint projectiles that expend all their energy into the brainpan. In the urban and suburban environments in which he works, DeNicola can’t afford to have a round exiting an animal.

The second best shot placement for headshots is a brain shot from the side. DeNicola’s third choice is a shot just below the back of the skull in the first four cervical vertebrae of the spine.

“The deer drop immediately,” DeNicola says of the vertebrae shot. “Heart and lung functions will cease. They lose consciousness and die in eight to 12 seconds.”

For hunters, however, taking head shots is ill-advised.

“There’s simply no margin for error around a deer’s relatively small brain (about 3 inches on the average deer, as much as 4 inches on a large adult buck),” NDA’s Lindsay Thomas wrote for OL earlier this year in his column, Don’t Aim for a Deer’s Head. “Plus, a deer’s brain is a deceptively small target. Yes, a big whitetail might look like it has a large head. But for reference, compare a deer shoulder mount to a skull mount. With the skull mount, you’ll see that the cranial cavity only makes up a small portion of the skull. It would be easy for hunters to misjudge the location of the brain, within the skull, on a live, moving deer.”

Head shots should be reserved for sharpshooters only.

Pros: A correctly placed bullet will kill with massive shock to the spinal cord and vertebrae while damaging very little meat.

Cons: The vital area on a neck shot is quite small. Hit low, and you will wound a deer with very little chance of recovery. Plus, this shot often merely paralyzes a deer, requiring a second shot.

Like the head shot, the neck is a deceptively small target. This is especially true for bucks with swollen necks during the rut. Unless the deer is at close range and you are extremely confident in your marksmanship, avoid attempting neck shots and wait for a better opportunity.

The best shot on a deer is tight behind the shoulder, through the lungs and heart.

Deer can survive a shoulder shot if the projectile doesn’t penetrate through the scapula or humerus bones. Deer that are hit high behind the shoulder often survive, too.

At the risk of sounding like a broken record, shooting a deer tight behind the shoulder, through the lungs and heart, is ideal placement, and does not ruin meat.

Unless you’re a professional sharp shooter, the old school deer hunters had it right all along on where to shoot a deer. The best shot placement for deer is through the lungs and the top of the heart, just behind the shoulder. With any standard deer hunting cartridge or any quality broadhead, a well-executed shot here will mean a quick death for the deer, plus a highly visible and short blood trail for the hunter to follow. Study whitetail anatomy before heading to the woods. And when you see deer while hunting, pay close attention to their body angles and the resulting shot presentations. It’s good practice to visualize your aim point on real live deer, even when your not intending to harvest that particular animal. That way when it is time to shoot, you’ll know exactly where.

#1681. Trump administration: English learner, other minority protections ‘not enforceable’

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Seven federal resources are also being reviewed as a result of the Supreme Court’s 2024 decision overturning the Chevron doctrine.

The Trump administration is abandoning a slew of guidance documents, resources for districts, and civil rights enforcement approaches meant to make education more accessible for English learners, Black and other marginalized students, according to an update scheduled to be published in the Federal Register Oct. 23.

The U.S. departments of Education and Justice said seven total documents issued by the two agencies between 2015 and 2023 are "not enforceable" under various federal civil rights laws related to race, disability and other protections. Some of them were already previously quietly rescinded by the administration.

"The Departments will not rely upon these guidance documents in their enforcement of federal civil rights laws, including Titles IV and VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964," the notice said.

In one overturned document from 2015, which was also rescinded in 2025, the federal government sought to ensure that "school districts are equipped with the tools and resources to meet their responsibilities" for English learner students.

"It is crucial to the future of our nation that these students, and all students, have equal access to a high-quality education and the opportunity to achieve their full academic potential," said the Obama administration-era guidance.

It included requirements for states and districts to help families and students overcome language barriers, sufficiently staff programs supporting English learners, and assist districts in identifying and assessing English learner students in a timely manner and provide them with assistance.

The guidance required districts to enroll all students regardless of their parents' perceived or actual immigration status, to protect students from harassment based on their identity, and to allow students to speak their primary language during the school day, among other things.

The federal government is also walking back other guidance documents for districts and enforcement approaches meant to make education equitable for marginalized students, such as those issued in the wake of the 2023 U.S. Supreme Court decision Students for Fair Admissions, Inc. v. Harvard College and University of North Carolina.

The landmark decision overturned race-conscious admissions in higher education, and advocates said the ruling would impact K-12 trends such as practices for magnet school admissions, scholarship opportunities for high school students, teacher pipelines, and overall higher education opportunities for students of color.

In an effort to address those and other concerns, the Education Department and DOJ in August 2023 released a Dear Colleague letter, saying, "The Departments also reaffirm our commitment to ensuring that educational institutions remain open to all, regardless of race."

"To that end, it is important that students — particularly those who are underrepresented — feel a sense of belonging and support once on campus," the Biden administration’s accompanying guidance said.

An accompanying guidance encouraged colleges to still continue targeted outreach with the intention of maintaining a diverse student applicant pool and provide pathway programs, such as through high school career and technical education programs.

"Students should feel comfortable presenting their whole selves when applying to college, without fear of stereotyping, bias, or discrimination," the overturned guidance said. "And information about an individual student’s perseverance, especially when faced with adversity or disadvantage, can be a powerful measure of that student’s potential."

The Trump administration also abandoned documents issued following the height of the COVID-19 pandemic meant to address race-based discrimination in school discipline and school climate.

For example, one of the two documents included guidance for districts to prevent harassment of Asian American and Pacific Islander communities in schools, which spread to classrooms when broader harassment of these communities proliferated nationwide. The second document was issued as schools reopened, reminding districts that they should address behavioral or other needs of students instead of disciplining certain racial subgroups more harshly.

Trump’s Justice and Education Departments said that the guidance documents were under scrutiny in wake of Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo. The 2024 Supreme Court decision overturned the Chevron doctrine, which was, up until then, a 40-year old precedent that gave federal agencies wide powers to interpret and apply congressional statutes.

The decision was expected to bring sweeping consequences for how the Education Department approached civil rights enforcement, including bringing regulations and interpretations under scrutiny.

"Each of the guidance documents was issued prior to the Supreme Court’s decision in Loper Bright, which held that agency interpretations must be consistent with the 'single, best meaning' of the statute authorizing them," the departments said in their October notice walking back the documents.

The decisions come amid a broader push from the second Trump administration to change school support and federal civil rights enforcement approaches for Black and English learner students, among other historically marginalized student groups.

In July, the Education Department rescinded disparate impact regulations, a legal theory that was used for years to investigate districts with policies and practices that were neutral on their face but harmed Black or other historically marginalized student groups in practice. It was used to address unintentional discrimination or bias, such as in student discipline practices.

The Trump administration has also repeatedly sought to decrease access to educational services for English learner students.

This year, the Education Department shuttered its Office of English Language Acquisition, the federal agency’s only arm dedicated to ensuring that English learners and immigrant students gained English proficiency.

In its regulations proposed in August for Head Start programs, the administration would mandate that instruction be only in English, in addition to recommending significant reductions to federal government guardrails for the early childhood centers.

Rush is scheduled to be sentenced on Jan. 28, 2027. He faces a maximum penalty of 20 years in prison, three years of supervised release, forfeiture, restitution, and a fine. A federal district court judge will determine any sentence after considering the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines and other statutory factors.
