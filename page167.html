#2260. Guatemala: Prominent Anti-Corruption Lawyer Fatally Shot

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image People stand near a framed photo of Guatemalan lawyer and anti-corruption figure Juan Francisco Solorzano Foppa, who died after being shot by unknown assailants on Saturday while visiting a sports center, during his wake at a funeral home in Guatemala City, Guatemala, October 19, 2026. © 2026 Cristina Chiquin/Reuters

(Washington, DC) – Guatemalan authorities should carry out a thorough, prompt, and credible investigation into the killing of Juan Francisco Solórzano Foppa, a prominent anti-corruption lawyer, former prosecutor, and former head of Guatemala’s tax agency, Human Rights Watch said today.

On October 17, 2026, armed assailants shot and killed Solórzano Foppa at the Campo Marte sports complex in Guatemala City. Another man, identified by authorities as Gustavo Adolfo Aguilar Gamarro, was also killed in the attack. Guatemalan authorities arrested two men in connection with the crime, but prosecutors said there was no evidence linking them to the attack and a judge ordered their release. 

“The killing of Juan Francisco Solórzano Foppa is a shocking attack against a lawyer who played a prominent role in some of Guatemala’s most important anti-corruption efforts,” said Juanita Goebertus, Americas director at Human Rights Watch. “Guatemalan authorities should ensure a thorough, prompt, and credible investigation capable of identifying not only the gunmen, but also anyone who planned, ordered, or facilitated this crime.”

Armed attackers opened fire on Solórzano Foppa while he was at the sports complex coaching a football team, killing him at the scene, media reported. President Bernardo Arévalo condemned the killing and called for those responsible, including any who played a role in planning the crime, to be brought to justice.

Solórzano Foppa, 43, was widely known for his anti-corruption work in Guatemala. He served as director of criminal analysis within the attorney general’s office and later led Guatemala’s Superintendency of Tax Administration, from 2016 to 2018. He helped advance investigations into high-level corruption and tax evasion, contributing to efforts to recover millions in unpaid taxes and supporting major corruption cases, including work related to the criminal case known as “La Línea” (“The Line”), which led to the resignation of President Otto Pérez Molina in 2015.

After leaving public office, Solórzano Foppa continued to work as a lawyer and public commentator. He was known for denouncing corruption and links between criminal groups, politicians, and authorities. 

In 2023, he briefly joined the legal defense team of the well-known journalist José Rubén Zamora, who has faced arbitrary criminal prosecution since 2022. During Attorney General Consuelo Porras’s tenure, a campaign of arbitrary criminal prosecutions against critics included the detention of Solórzano Foppa, who was accused of “hindering criminal investigations” in connection with his defense of Zamora. Solórzano Foppa said the prosecution was arbitrary and pleaded guilty to obtain an early release.

“Solórzano Foppa’s killing is a chilling reminder of Guatemala’s long history of political murders,” Goebertus said. “Those responsible should be held accountable.”

According to cookware expert Alycia Johnson, Vice President of Merchandising at Sur La Table, hybrid cookware makes up a unique category of cookware in that the materials differ from pan to pan. Typically, hybrid pans combine a nonstick material such as ceramic with stainless steel ridges. This offers many of the benefits of both stainless steel and nonstick cookware in one singular pan.

“Hybrid cookware gets its performance from a combination of materials and surface design,” says Alycia. “Many pans feature a textured cooking surface with raised stainless steel elements and recessed areas containing a nonstick coating. The stainless-steel helps create a durable cooking surface, while the nonstick components help release food more easily.”

She notes that depending on the pan’s construction, additional layers of conductive materials such as aluminum help distribute heat evenly.

“Together, these features allow home cooks to tackle a variety of techniques, from browning proteins and sautéing vegetables to preparing delicate foods that might stick to traditional stainless steel,” she says. “It brings together the best of two worlds so you can sear your proteins to a beautiful golden brown and slide a delicate omelet right onto the plate without a hitch. No compromise required.”

Overall, the biggest benefit of a hybrid pan is versatility. Alycia explains that hybrid cookware bridges the gap between traditional stainless steel and nonstick. It offers a more forgiving cooking surface without sacrificing the ability to achieve a good sear. As a bonus, a single hybrid pan can often handle a wide range of everyday tasks.

“Depending on the construction and coating, durability and easier cleanup can be added advantages,” she says. “For home cooks who want cookware that performs well across daily cooking needs, hybrid cookware can be a smart investment that delivers both searing power and nonstick performance.”

To find the right cookware for you, Alycia advises that you consider what you cook most often. If you frequently sear meats, look for cookware designed to withstand higher-heat cooking and provide higher heat retention. For folks who cook a lot of eggs, fish, or other delicate foods, the type and quality of the nonstick surface is extra important.

“Shoppers should also consider compatibility with your stovetop, whether it is oven-safe, and what temperatures it can withstand. Understanding how to clean and maintain the pan will help extend its performance and lifespan,” she explains. “Look for hybrid cookware with sturdy construction, high-quality materials and a trusted nonstick coating. A good pan should feel solid but not too heavy, heat evenly and have a comfortable, secure handle.”

Ultimately, the best hybrid cookware should strike a balance. It should offer the durability and performance of stainless steel, plus the ease and convenience of nonstick.

#2873. 20 Years Ago, Reel Rock Set Out to Make Climbing Films More Interesting Than “Watching a Car Rust”

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Josh Lowell shows Chris Sharma some freshly shot footage in Céüse, France, circa 2006. (Photo: Courtesy Nick Rosen / Sender Films)

When this year’s Reel Rock premieres at the Boulder Theater on Thursday, October 22, it will officially mark the twentieth lap across the screens of mountain town theaters and climbing gym walls.

The three founders—Peter Mortimer, Nick Rosen, and Josh Lowell—met at Colorado College in Colorado Springs in the early aughts. Originally, they wanted to create an annual film tour for climbers like Warren Miller or the annual Teton Gravity Research set does for skiers.

Their first official film, First Ascent: The Movie, explored various pioneering exploits on rock. They followed Didier Berthod on Cobra Crack (5.14) and Dean Potter on Epitaph (5.13b). Their cameras rolled as David Lama put up deep water solo FAs in Thailand and locals sent untouched lines in the Black Canyon. And they captured Timmy O’Neill “doing Urban Ape-style climbs,” as Rosen puts it, referencing O’Neill’s moniker in Reel Rock films.

Two decades later, Rosen sees the Reel Rock filmmaking ethos as one that defies an old analogy that watching climbing is about as entertaining as observing a car rust. “What Pete and Josh started, and what we developed from there, was the idea of character- and story-driven films about climbers,” Rosen told Climbing. “Climbing really benefited from that because watching raw climbing can be slow. Josh brought cutting, shooting, and editing climbs to a new level to make them as exciting as possible.”

Alongside Reel Rock, Sender Films, the production company behind the tour, has also put out a number of award-winning documentaries, from The Alpinist to Valley Uprising. Most recently, the team brought The Dark Wizard to life on HBO this year. The four-part docuseries about the complicated and troubled life of free soloist and BASE jumper Dean Potter stirred controversy and won acclaim. Rosen says more unflinching documentaries like Potter’s are in the works.

Two decades in, we caught up with two of the three Reel Rock guys. We asked Rosen and Mortimer about their favorite films, how their approach to filmmaking has evolved over time, and what the future of Reel Rock looks like.

Peter Mortimer: We’ve made maybe 100 short films for Reel Rock over the years. The biggest challenge is keeping the films fresh, finding unexpected stories, unlikely characters, unreal climbs, surprising relationships. We’re always looking for that special something. Over the years, we’ve worked with more filmmakers and cinematographers to document the climbing journey, but in the end the spirit of the films is the same: Go deep, capture the magic, and bring the story to life.

Nick Rosen: In recent years, our films have gone beyond climbing into broader social ideas. For instance, in 2019, we started filming a group of African American climbers from Memphis Rocks expanding beyond the gym into ice climbing. That film came out during the Black Lives Matter movement and felt very resonant. The following year, we told a story about climbers in the West Bank of Palestine, which made a huge impact on our audience. We also filmed Ukrainian climbers holding a competition in Kyiv during bombings.

Rosen: Resistance Climbing is high on the list. Another standout is Point of No Return. Over 20 years, we’ve faced real tragedies during production. In 2008 in China, an avalanche killed our close friends: Jonny Copp, Micah Dash, and our 21-year-old crew member Wade Johnson.

It was devastating. Three years into filmmaking, I considered quitting entirely. But making Point of No Return helped us and the community process grief, offering context and closure through art.

Mortimer: I’m gonna go with an obscure one: United States of Joe’s, which tells the story of bouldering’s development in Joe’s Valley, Utah and how climbing came head-to-head with the local Mormon mining community. It’s a feel-good film about people from different walks of life building bridges to make the place better for everyone.

Rosen: That happened often because we committed to uncertain stories dependent on weather and injuries. A million things that can go wrong.

Mortimer: Oh man, there are so many. We’ve supported various expeditions over the years, where climbers were shut down by weather, conditions, or other outside factors. It’s tricky supporting expeditions because they’re so high-consequence, the motivation has to be pure, and you don’t want the climbers weighing the importance of a film crew waiting on them when making decisions.

Mortimer: Josh and Nick and the crew are basically my closest friends and we’ve worked together for 20-plus years now. We’re all opinionated and disagree on things, but we also respect each other creatively and appreciate what the others bring to the table whenever we don’t see eye to eye. I, in particular, tend to have a pretty overbearing intense personality (I’ve been told) and these guys are good at rolling their eyes when I drink too much coffee.

Rosen: It’s like a marriage, so we’ve had heated creative arguments over the years. As we get older, our egos get less in the way.

Mortimer: Definitely not with us running it. It’s so hard to make these films good every year, we put so much of ourselves into it and we’re totally gutted for weeks or months afterwards. We fantasize about young filmmakers taking over and carrying the torch, taking it in new directions, keeping it relevant for future generations, but so far that’s not happening. We all have kids—maybe some of them will catch the bug.

Rosen: I’ll be like 78 years old. We’d love for Reel Rock to continue, but someone has to make the films. Every year, we ask if there’s more to say, but new generations push limits and diversify the sport. The evolving culture keeps compelling us to document it. Twenty years later, we are still drawn to telling these stories.

For years, those examiners graded banks on “reputational risk,” a category with no financial definition or objective yardstick. While a loan defaulting is a pass-or-fail course, reputational risk is whatever a regulator wants it to be, with no financial impact required.

For those who believe in free markets and individual liberty, debanking was doubly offensive because it wasn’t a market failure but a regulatory distortion. Left to sound financial judgment, banks have every reason to serve profitable, lawful customers. It took government pressure to make turning away business look like the safe choice.

The Hida are widely regarded as Japan’s premier alpine terrain and feature the country’s most developed hut system, Yamagoya. These staffed huts offer hot meals, sleeping mats, water, snacks, and beer, allowing for multi-day runs and traverses with lightweight packs sans camping gear—perfect for a trip like ours.

Three years ago I ran across the Southern Alps on an ancient postal route called Nakasendo. While the Southern and Northern Alps offer remarkably different terrain and technicality, my first trip taught me much about Japanese culture and logistics that was helpful while preparing for my return.

Perhaps my biggest takeaway from that initial experience was that the language barrier is a major challenge—especially in rural places, where many people don’t speak a lick of English. While apps like Google Translate can help to order a quick meal or get assistance with navigation in a pinch, planning a trip and booking mountain huts requires someone not only fluent in Japanese, but who also understands the mountain culture. For us, that liaison was Kota.

I was connected with Kota at Japan Adventures Co. through a friend, and I honestly can’t say enough good things about him. He is an incredibly organized planner, a great communicator, and an avid hiker and trail runner. He even helped negotiate our stay with a few huts that didn’t believe our route was possible, ensuring the entire trip ran smoothly. Kota and his team lead a variety of hiking, food, and cultural trips around Japan, and are currently rolling out new trail running itineraries in the Northern Alps and elsewhere.

With help from Kota and advice from guides like Tatsuro and Paul from Nagano Adventures, I was able to refine our route to stay at unique huts, run the best trails, and find a balance between challenging and doable for each day. It’s important to note that mountain huts in Japan are warm and welcoming, but they run on rigid schedules. Breakfast is typically at 5 a.m. and dinner is around 5 p.m. If you don’t check in by 4 p.m., they will notify search and rescue, so it’s important to start early and keep an eye on your pace throughout the day.

My goal was to start in Kamikochi and end at the Seas of Japan, creating a single linkup by connecting a number of popular two- and three-day hiking routes—the Panorama Ginza Route, Mount Yari, Ura Ginza Panorama Route, the ridgeline from Mount Eboshi and Funakubo-dake, the Hachimine Kiretto, the Kaerazu no Ken (ridge of no return), and Tsugami Shindo. It’s a mouthful to say, but when drawn on a map, the routes piece together nicely, forming a (mostly) straight line running from north to south.

Getting to and from the Northern Alps isn’t terribly difficult. After landing in Tokyo (either HND or NRT), a bullet train, or Shinkansen, will take you to Matsumoto in just a few hours. From there you can take a smaller train, bus, or taxi into the mountains to begin your adventure. Returning to Tokyo is just as easy by hopping on a bullet train in a coastal city like Toyama. There are plenty of stations to choose from and you usually can find seats just a day or two in advance.

There are a few ways to shuttle your luggage from the start to the finish, but I recommend Yamato Transport. Other options include dropping bags at the post office, the front desk if you’re staying in a hotel, or even convenience stores like Lawson’s or 7-Eleven. Though this might seem risky at face value, it worked flawlessly for us. We walked into a convenience store around 10 p.m., spent a meager ten minutes filling out the paperwork, and walked out. Our bags arrived at our final hotel just after we arrived seven days later.

Despite plenty of challenges, the trip ran as smoothly as I could have imagined. We dealt with heavy rain, 50 mph winds, and thick mist for our first few days, which forced us to slow down on the scrambling sections, layer up to stay warm, and take plenty of breaks in huts along the way. Some days had tons of vert (often more than 1,000 feet per mile) and rocky trails that made running difficult, so we embraced the shuffle. On day four, when covering our most remote section, we didn’t see a single other person. Fortunately the trails are well marked, even where they are less frequented and overgrown.

The crux of the route, the Kaerazu no Ken, is a long and serpentine downclimb on Class 4 granite slabs, offering a chain for protection in some areas while others did not. This was one of the dozen or so sections that having a helmet was a welcomed addition to our kits. For those comfortable with alpine climbing, this will feel like a fun change of pace. For those afraid of exposure, it’ll be challenging. The huts themselves vary considerably from small ridgetop outposts like Funakubo to much larger and busier outfits like Hakuba Sanso. Regardless of size, every hut owner we met was incredibly kind. Japanese hospitality is something they take very seriously.

Overall, our experience in the Hida was more than we could have asked for. Steep and technical trails, unique and cozy huts, fickle yet fruitful weather, and no other Americans or Europeans along the way. It’s an undiscovered gem for anyone outside Japan. With the help of Kota, Tatsuro, and others, I’m already planning a return trip next year.

One of the lightest climbing helmets on the market, the Vapor was perfect for our trip. We frequently had sections of running between scrambles and wouldn’t leave it on because it breathes so well. The Vapor fits over a hat and under a rain jacket hood, making it great for wet days, too.

The latest from Garmin is designed for routes like ours with a solar-powered display that ran the full week on a single charge. The maps were critical on wet days when I didn’t want to use my phone, and the flashlight was perfect for early mornings trying to not wake up other guests.

On a week-long trip you end up eating a ton of snacks—gummies, chips, gels, and whatever you can buy at the huts—but nothing hit home like GU stroops. Whether it was with morning tea, on the top of a big climb, or preventing a bonk, stroops played a big role in our success.

But with such a trove to browse, how is anyone deciding what to buy? We asked home thrifting experts what each generation is most commonly hunting for on the shelves and in the aisles, and the generational markers are loud, to say the least.

“Thrifting is one of the rare types of shopping that feels like a sport, and it really does feel like you've ‘won’ when you find that special piece,” says Virginia Chamlee, a seasoned antiques dealer and author of Big Thrift Energy. “Then, there's the resale value of some items. Even if you aren't buying to resell, it's nice to buy something that you know might later increase in value.”

No one is immune to the thrill of thrifting, even Gen Z. Known for their commitment to sustainability, Gen-Zers are leading the second-hand shopping category, as a whopping 62 percent of the generation reports shopping thrifted or consignment goods in just the last year and 58 percent browsing pre-owned pieces before they even consider buying brand new. Their commitment to thrifting has even launched a social media movement in the form of #thrifthauls.

Beyond the sustainability and ecological benefits of second-hand shopping, designer Phoenix Grey says the real allure is an air of individuality. “Gen Z thrifts for personality. They’re less concerned with whether something is a ‘good investment’ and more interested in whether it’s fun, strange, or nostalgic. They want something that no one else has because they are so used to seeing everyone with the same items,” says Grey. “They gravitate toward the 70s and 80s: chrome, bold colour, and squiggly, sculptural forms. Grandmacore is a big part of it too, with floral prints, ruffled lampshades, and kitschy ceramics used with a sense of humour, because they want something that's ‘fun.’ Their style is maximalist and eclectic.”

Bargain hunters by trade, potential resale value is a major deal-breaker when Millennials are browsing. The second-hand market is a haven for Millennials, who have weathered the rising cost of living and fluctuating inflation rates for decades, making retail prices harder and harder to justify.

Not wanting to sacrifice beauty or flair, thrifting and DIY culture became a lifeline for personal style to prevail over the often bleak economic landscape. “I think Millennials are also interested in the hunt itself. Finding something amazing for a fraction of its retail price is part of the appeal,” says Katie Zamprioli, a design influencer. “Upcycling, furniture flips, DIY makeovers, and finding creative ways to give something old a completely different look became a huge part of the culture. Instead of seeing a dated dresser or an outdated lamp and passing it by, they saw the potential to paint it, reupholster it, change the hardware, or turn it into something entirely different.”

Millennials championed many of the popular design schemes big-box retailers race to emulate today, particularly midcentury modern sculptural styles and pieces with Victorian-era flair. Original art is also a big hit with the middle-ground generation.

While Boomers may make up the smallest slice of second-hand consumers, their style is pronounced. Like the younger gens, Boomers are chasing nostalgia, but unlike Gen-Zers and Millennials, they aren’t driven by whatever's hot in internet trends. Instead, Boomers are scanning shelves for pieces of their childhoods.

“I think Boomers are drawn to the pieces from their childhood homes as well as brands that made high-quality products in the 80s and 90s. Boomers have a good handle on what things used to cost, too, so they understand practical value,” says Chamlee.

According to Chamlee, their most wanted items include hot-air balloon embossed Pyrex dishes, candy-colored depression glass, and sturdy cast-iron kitchenware.

So, whether you’re shopping estate sales, thrift shops, or consignment, there really is something for every taste hidden on the shelves.

#2272. Failure Is No Option: The U.S. Space Force Has 10,600 Personnel and Manages GPS for 6 Billion Users

The newest American military service can fit entirely in a minor league ballpark but is already playing in the big leagues. The Albuquerque Isotopes (the Colorado Rockies’ AAA affiliate) host 13,500 nightly. The United States Space Force (USSF) is six years old and numbers 10,600 personnel. Those Guardians fight daily “above their weight class” as they manage several no “no-fail” missions of using and protecting America’s space assets.  

Consider the work of the 2nd Navigation Warfare Squadron (2 NWS), which manages the Global Positioning System (GPS) satellite constellation. Thirty-one GPS satellites, managed by a handful of brilliant 19– to 26-year-olds, are used by three-quarters of the world’s population for everything from ordering a pizza or finding the nearest emergency room to military applications that use precise targeting to minimize collateral damage. Every day, six billion users benefit from GPS worldwide, generating more than $1.4 TRILLION in economic impact.   

Or take the 2nd Space Warning Squadron (2 SWS). Their patch is an “All-Seeing Eye,” which is fitting, as they detect and track every ballistic missile launch worldwide. From illegal ballistic missile tests in North Korea to the thousands of missiles launched near-simultaneously by Iran earlier this year, 2 SWS tracks every single one of them and accurately predicts where they are going to impact.  

That predicted impact service is valuable for the obvious early warning of “get out now!” But it also preserves American and our allies’ missile interceptors because defensive munitions aren’t expended on missiles that land in the ocean or uninhabited areas. As an aside, 2 SWS can also detect any nuclear detonation anywhere in the world.  

Then there’s the 4th Space Operations Squadron (4 SOPS). It has the motto “Linking the Forces” because it protects all U.S. military satellite communications. By “all,” I mean all wideband, polar, ultra-high, and low-frequency communications worldwide for all U.S. military operations, simultaneously; from an embarked Marine near the Straits of Hormuz ordering a part for an Osprey, to a soldier using a map during a training exercise in the Mojave Desert, to the proverbial “nuclear football” that a military aide keeps near the president at all times.

Or take 1st Space Operations Squadron (1 SOPS), which protects all of America’s space-based assets. It operates maneuverable vehicles for “close-proximity engagement” and conducts orbital warfare.

It also breaks an adversary’s intelligence collection and surveillance capabilities in times of war.  

I sleep better at night knowing these Space Guardians are working every night. But then I lose sleep when I think about the challenges they face. Each of these critical capabilities operates at the intersection of national security and global commerce with a razor-thin margin of success.  

1 SOPS is bound by physics and orbital mechanics and must continually make “maneuvering without regret” decisions to manage orbital fuel consumption. 4 SOPS operates legacy hardware that is 15 years past its planned design lifespan. 2 SWS faces an unprecedented surge in threat activity; 10 years ago, operators might see eight to 20 missile launches in a three-year assignment—today they see that consistently in an eight-hour shift.  

4 SOS keeps it all running with a single, lean operational team. 2 NWS lives with the risk that a single day of GPS downtime could cost the U.S. economy a billion dollars.  

Every Space Force team is vulnerable to electrical infrastructure attacks and must defend legacy systems from by-the-minute, AI-generated cyberattacks while operating with a bare minimum of staff. I lose more sleep knowing the People’s Republic of China has increased its orbital presence 925% over the last 10 years to “…enable long-range precision strikes and [deny] other militaries the use of space-based information.”

“I would be really happy if somebody came to experience this work and then went home and sat on their own couch,” he says, “and started to see their own history and aesthetic evolution and the narrative it holds.”
