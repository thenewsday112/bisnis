InfoQ Homepage News Cloudflare Opens Quicksilver to Customers as Workers KV Instant, Inside a 1 MB Envelope

Oct 28, 2026 4 min read

Cloudflare recently made Quicksilver available to customers for the first time, as a new mode for Workers KV called KV Instant. Reads resolve in 1.62 ms at the 99th percentile, compared with 287 ms for classic KV. What determines whether a team can use it isn't the latency, but the envelope around it.

Quicksilver is Cloudflare’s own configuration store, introduced in 2020. Nearly every request to Cloudflare looks up at least one key in it, Rob Sutter writes, and it has never been a customer-facing product. Brendan Irvine-Broque, who works on Cloudflare’s developer platform, announced the change on X:

we have this crazy key-value store at Cloudflare called Quicksilver

instantly updates keys across our whole network. microsecond reads from every location. stupid fast

our customers have asked us for years to make it available

we finally did – in a way we think is quite simple - as a new mode for Workers KV

The published figures, measured to over 300 edge locations:

Cloudflare notes it has no sub-second fidelity for replication lag in classic mode, so the classic median and p95 figures are upper bounds rather than measurements. p95 reads in Instant mode are reported in microseconds.

The API is the Workers KV one, with three differences. Mode is set at namespace creation with a "mode": "instant" attribute, and cannot be changed afterward. Metadata is unsupported, so getWithMetadata always returns null. And list returns every matching key with no pagination.

Then the limits. A namespace holds at most 10,000 key-value pairs and one megabyte in total, with keys up to 300 bytes. Writes are capped at one per namespace per second, which Cloudflare contrasts with classic KV’s one write per key per second, on the grounds that a single system of record "makes it easier for you to reason about update order in your application." That difference is the one most likely to catch a team migrating: a pattern classic KV permits across many keys concurrently becomes a single serialised queue.

Pricing points the same direction, and Cloudflare says so plainly: "storage and Class A operations are significantly more expensive than Workers KV."

Reads cost 60% less. A write costs ten cents, against five dollars per million in classic mode. Storage runs $100 per megabyte per month, and since a namespace tops out at one megabyte, that is also the ceiling. Multi-key reads bill per key, so requesting three keys in one get costs the same three Class B operations as three separate calls.

Irvine-Broque set out the reasoning in the same thread:

just like how we use Quicksilver ourselves, it’s for small data that is read often and updated infrequently

pricing reflects this - reads actually cost 60% less than even Workers KV

Writes cost what they cost, he added, because writing a key replicates it to hundreds of locations. This is a store priced so that you read from it constantly and write to it rarely.

Benjie Malinao, also on X, read the fit the same way:

This is the config store agents actually need. Sub-2ms p99 reads and global writes landing in about a quarter second at p99, on the same KV API. Feature flags and agent settings without waiting on a TTL. I’d move hot-path config over first.

Feature flags, routing tables and agent settings fit that shape. Session state, user records and anything with a write path do not.

The comparison practitioners reached for was Vercel. Developer Shawn Mclean asked Irvine-Broque directly:

I’m looking into using Vercel Global Config for a middleware read. They claim 15ms. If I move my workloads to CF, are you saying I can read from middleware for a platform tenant domain mapping in 2ms?

even p50 of Workers KV (non-instant) from 2 years ago was ~5ms. KV Instant is even faster than that (see numbers in the post)

Cloudflare’s own worked example is a launch flag read on every request and flipped at a keynote, which is precisely the workload: one key, one write, read everywhere immediately.

Classic Workers KV, which launched during Birthday Week in 2018, remains the recommendation for large or frequently updated data. The two modes now sit under one API with opposite economics.

KV Instant is in private beta with a sign-up form, and Cloudflare says it plans to open it up more widely. No general availability date is given.

The law-professional-to-queer-media-founder pipeline is not a particularly common one.

Daniels studied entertainment and corporate law at Loyola Law School in LA. Turns out, a solid legal background comes in handy for building a sustainable business in monetizing multicultural programming. But the plan was always to work in the entertainment sector, she told me.

Specifically, Daniels saw an opportunity to build a sustainable business that addresses the lack of diverse representation on television. Revry’s content slate includes a comedy series called “Gayborhood,” a lesbian drama titled “To the Stars” and a new competition reality show called “King of Drag,” which is sponsored by e.l.f. STREAMLINED: What inspired you during your legal studies to become a business owner and found Revry?

ALIA J. DANIELS: My studies focused on business, entertainment and communications in mass media. I also spent some time doing employment law, which included working with folks starting small businesses, such as production companies.

The inspiration for Revry hit when I wrote my thesis about how minority groups are represented on TV, which included telling the stories of people who couldn’t see themselves in modern TV. At the same time, I always knew that the future of TV would end up online. So, ever since 2010, I knew what I wanted to do was to establish an online TV network – and I knew I wanted to work to create representation of the queer community. There was a massive untapped opportunity in LGBTQ+ storytelling because there simply wasn’t a TV network dedicated to queer media.

During my studies, I also met a colleague who would become another Revry co-founder, Chris Rodriquez. In 2015, we joined forces to form Revry. We started by establishing relationships with queer content creators who would wind up in our programming library. We officially launched as a company in 2016 during San Francisco Pride.

What expertise did you develop at law school that has helped you make Revry a profitable company while pursuing that vision?

Much of my studies focused on the intersection of law and the business of digital media. That business background was really helpful in terms of understanding the intricacies of standing up a new company and how to address any legal questions or concerns that may arise along the way.

Knowing how to get started from a business perspective and having a pulse on the developing trends in media and entertainment is what laid the foundation for Revry.

For example, we really leaned heavily into FAST before FAST became the buzzword that it is today. We wanted to make sure that queer, representative storytelling is widely accessible to audiences that don’t typically get to see themselves on the big screen on the wall – especially queer identities outside of gay cisgender white men who are centered in mainstream queer storylines. Plus, having a clear understanding of the legal considerations involved in content licensing and distribution was incredibly helpful in standing up a business that revolves around FAST, which was still very much the wild west when we were fleshing out our FAST business roughly 10 years ago.

What other developments at Revry are you especially excited about?

I’ve been really excited about the growth we’re seeing with PrismRiot, which is our programmatic ad exchange that connects advertisers with other diverse-owned, multicultural-focused publishers.

PrismRiot is our answer to helping brands who want to secure scale in their campaigns but without having to buy additional audience extension deals on random platforms. Because those platforms just represent eyeballs – and that’s not what our clients desire. The reach needs to be relevant.

For diverse-owned publishers, a simplified way to reach a wide swath of advertisers interested in their inventory creates ways for those publishers to generate incremental revenue. And that additional revenue stream gives them the ability to invest in creating more programming to grow their audience and business.

Turning to the programming itself, which content on Revry are you particularly amped about?

I’m very excited about “King of Drag” that recently premiered. The drag king community is small and it doesn’t get the love and spotlight that it deserves, so giving the community this platform and having drag kings see themselves represented on screen has been huge.

I also run a couple of newsletters on SubStack. Those are my creative outlets. After all, mobile is certainly poised to become an even more important piece of the media and entertainment world.

Taylor Loutsis edited by christina vergopoulou October 28, 2026 3 min

Located in Medora, North Dakota, the Theodore Roosevelt Presidential Library is embedded directly within the terrain of the Badlands. Designed by Snøhetta with architect of record JLG Architects, the library campus connects three institutions across expansive indoor and outdoor environments. Studio Loutsis was commissioned to develop the comprehensive signage, wayfinding, and donor recognition system spanning the site, treating graphic wayfinding as an architectural and landscape-driven extension of the campus.

To unify varied scales, from monumental exterior markers to pedestrian trail placards, Studio Loutsis collaborated with Commercial Type to engineer a bespoke typeface, paired with a custom iconography system. Exterior wayfinding incorporates heavy timber pylons, directional panels, campus maps, and dark metal dimensional lettering engineered to withstand North Dakota’s extreme climate while weathering alongside the building envelope.

Fabricated under stringent Living Building Challenge sustainability parameters, all materials, non-toxic coatings, and mechanical assemblies adhere to strict ecological standards. Indoors, signage mounts directly to vertically articulated timber partitions using custom-milled infill blocks, while the central lobby’s ‘Wall of Gratitude’ integrates flush donor wood panels into the primary architectural cladding.

#4320. The Benchmarking Crisis

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Goodhart’s law, that any measurable target stops being useful when people start optimizing for it, very much holds in database benchmarking. That is not new, of course. Famously, TPC-D got retired when people optimized so much for it that the query runtimes converged towards zero due to excessive caching. TPC-H fixed that by declaring itself to be an ad-hoc benchmark, and explicitly forbidding things like exploiting knowledge about the workload or caching results (and a number of other optimizations).

We now see the same problem with the ClickBench benchmark, heavily accelerated by today’s use of AI code generation. If you tell an AI to make the queries faster, it will make the queries faster, regardless of whether this is a generally plausible approach or not. For example, the pgrust implementation performs group-by aggregations on string columns purely on the hash value, ignoring the long string content. That is a great performance boost, of course, and works for this benchmark, but can produce wrong results. Is it faster? Yes. Is it a plausible optimization? Probably not. And it does many other things: For example, ClickBench repeats certain predicates across queries, and thus pgrust caches the predicate, completely eliminating the query cost the second time. Or for query Q17, which returns tuples grouped by userid, it makes use of the fact that a) tuples are roughly clustered by userid, and b) the query specifies a limit clause without order by. Thus any userid will do, and it never reads most of the data. A human would not do these tiny micro-optimizations, because they are unlikely to be relevant in general use cases, but an AI can of course look at each query in detail and tune for it.

Part of the problem is, of course, ClickBench itself. The benchmark is not well specified in what is permitted and what is not, there is no update stream, the queries are very simple, share predicates, are underspecified, etc. But the fundamental problem is independent of that concrete benchmark: The moment we start aggressively optimizing for a specific benchmark, the benchmark stops being very useful. Historically, that pressure was reduced a bit by the requirement that systems be generally useful. That is, they did not just want to perform well in benchmarks but also to be useful for their customers. That is, of course, not a concern for vibe-coded execution engines.

It is not clear what to do about it. Our SQLStorm proposal tried to address this by having tens of thousands of queries, which makes overfitting harder. No human would fine-tune their system to such a huge workload. But with AI tools we could even investigate ten thousand queries in detail if needed, so that is not a real defense.

I think that to be meaningful, a benchmark has to a) like TPC-H, be ad-hoc, and forbid exploiting prior knowledge and any form of intermediate result caching. If we don’t do that, execution times converge towards zero. And b) the benchmark has to include some form of update stream, as otherwise overly aggressive read-only or read-mostly systems will always outperform systems that offer full transactional support. And c) the benchmark has to be truly ad-hoc, in the sense that the systems tested have to be older than the benchmark. One way to fight Goodhart’s law would be to (automatically) come up with a new benchmark from time to time, and then measure the systems that existed before the benchmark. The caching/pre-computation problem still remains, of course, but at least that would prevent the over-aggressive adjustment to just one specific benchmark.

Interestingly, the machine learning community has struggled with exactly the same problem for years, and we should probably borrow some of their ideas. Kaggle competitions, for example, rank submissions on a hidden test set that participants never see, which makes overfitting on the public data pointless. Similarly, benchmarks like LiveBench regularly release new questions to make sure that models cannot have seen them during training. For database benchmarking that could mean keeping the actual benchmark queries secret and only publishing a representative sample for development, or, even better, generating a fresh workload for every evaluation round. SQLStorm already uses generated queries, so producing a new query set from time to time would be fairly easy. We would probably want a more generic approach (higher variety in schemas, update streams, transactions, etc.), but that seems to be a direction that would help keep benchmarks meaningful.

#4420. AI Emergency Alerts Are Coming. Humans Might Not Be Ready

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This story was produced by Grist and copublished with WIRED. It is part of Distorted, a Grist series exploring the spread of misinformation in the wake of extreme weather, and is published with support from the CO2 Foundation.

Earlier this month, after Hurricane Isaias made landfall on the Gulf Coast as a Category 3 hurricane, the emergency management expert Laura Myers got a call from a meteorologist in Alabama. Myers is director of the University of Alabama’s Center for Advanced Public Safety, and she talks to state and local emergency managers around her region all the time. The meteorologist told Myers that a local emergency manager had published a graphic showing where Isaias might threaten the Alabama coast with a “sting jet” of high winds.

The problem? The emergency manager had generated the graphic using an artificial intelligence chatbot, and it wasn’t accurate.

“That county emergency manager was trying to get his stakeholders to focus on the risk in that county, and so he had asked AI to help him,” she said. “It did what he wanted it to do, but it was not meteorologically accurate.”

Most emergency management agencies don’t permit employees to use generative AI, but Myers and most other emergency management experts agree that its usage is likely already pervasive at state and local disaster departments. There are hundreds of companies that are marketing AI tools to emergency managers for tasks like flood damage assessment and resource coordination, but public communication is one of the areas where government employees and startups have the most interest.

Emergency department personnel with the city of New York monitor the watch-command room in Brooklyn on February 23, 2026, ahead of a winter storm.

The reason? Most emergency managers aren’t very good at sending out alerts and quickly communicating about disasters with the public. That’s not because they’re emergency managers, but because they are human beings. They tend to write in jargon that non-experts might not understand, leave out information on the assumption that the public already knows it, and include irrelevant details that could distract people from what they actually do need to know.

“A lot of agencies are really limited in resources and the number of people that they have,” said Jeannette Sutton, a disaster researcher at the University of Albany who has created a comprehensive dashboard that provides templates for disaster alerts. “I think they recognize it as a priority, but they don't prioritize it until they realize they should have. They're just trying to get their job done without screwing something up.”

Emergency managers are the government officials who are in charge of disaster response and recovery. At the state and local level, It is these officials and their agencies, not the Federal Emergency Management Agency, that make up the first line of defense after an extreme weather event. As such, emergency managers often like to say that “trust is our currency,” and it’s in their communication with the public that they build and lose this trust.

As climate disasters become more frequent and widespread, there have been numerous examples of flawed human communication scrambling an emergency response. In 2016, for instance, adjacent counties in California issued a “voluntary evacuation” and a “mandatory evacuation” for the same wildfire. In Australia in 2019, officials warned about wildfires by using the phrase “Watch and Act,” which the government later said caused “significant confusion” because no one knew what it meant.

In some cases, inadequate communication can mean the difference between life and death. This happened in Montecito, California, after the 2017 Thomas Fire. A few weeks after the fire, a heavy rainstorm produced a deadly mudslide. The government of Santa Barbara County struggled to communicate the risk to residents still traumatized from the blaze and experiencing a phenomenon that Sutton calls “warning fatigue.” The county sent alerts to everyone in the “voluntary evacuation zone” for wildfires, telling residents to “stay alert to changing conditions and be prepared to leave immediately.” Most of those residents should have left immediately, but to many the evacuation notice was optional; others just didn’t want to evacuate for a second time in a month. The mudslide killed 23 people and injured more than 160 others.

Governments that put out bad warnings are apt to lose the public’s trust, said Sutton. “They're losing the opportunity to be that voice to help people to be safe.”

In theory, alerts are a perfect use case for artificial intelligence, and in particular for the generative AI systems that have developed at a dramatic pace over the past few years. FEMA provides training sessions for emergency managers to learn best practices for synthesizing and sending alerts, but many officials don’t remember all their training when an emergency arrives. A large language model is different. It can read hundreds of thousands of alerts in mere minutes, digest all the research on what works in similar scenarios, and then apply that to edit a human alert in seconds.

“If you’ve only sent one [alert] in your life, and that was 15 years ago, and you need to remember all your training, and you’re thinking ‘Hey, there's a chemical plume coming my way, the phone's ringing, the radio's blaring, I need help,’ the AI is probably gonna do a better job than you are,” said Rob Dale, a former meteorologist who now runs the emergency management department in Ingham County, Michigan.

Dale has already used AI to draft a few practice alerts behind the scenes, but he’s never had to send one out. Ingham County is a small county in central Michigan, and it’s immune from major weather events like hurricanes and fires. If such an event did happen, Dale said he would have no issue allowing a chatbot to draft his incident communication, but he’d still want control over sending the alert out.

Some experts believe the technology is already good enough to deploy. EM1, a startup that produces AI software for emergency managers, has used Sutton’s research to train a model that its executives say is already better than almost any human emergency manager. The company has tested this in-house model on a number of scenarios including wildfires and tsunamis. It can already match or exceed human capabilities on most benchmark tests.

Sutton gave Grist an example of how the software works: Grist provided her with a recent alert sent by a New Mexico county that was trying to warn residents about flooding on the San Juan River.

After a series of erroneous evacuation alerts to L.A. residents sparked panic during an active wildfire season in January 2025, the director of the county’s emergency management department apologized.

The original alert read, “Breeched banks. The water is rising. EVACUATE NOW. McGee Park open for people and pets, RV.” Sutton asked the EM1 model to evaluate and revise the alert. In a few seconds, it produced what she said is a far better alert, one that checks all the main boxes of disaster communication.

The new alert read: “San Juan County: Floodwater is rising near [location]. People in [evacuation area]: LEAVE NOW for higher ground. Do not walk or drive through floodwater. McGee Park is open for people and pets. Check [official update source] for updates.” The model identified and fixed several problems with the original text, which does not say who is sending the alert, name the specific location where flooding is happening, or clarify who needs to evacuate and give advice on how to do so safely. It misspells the word “breached,” and it doesn’t provide any guidance on where residents can seek further information.

EM1 has contracts with around a hundred government agencies and school districts, but none are using it to automate their extreme weather alerts just yet. For the moment, they’re only using it to workshop plans and internal preparations. Even so, all the experts interviewed for this story said they are certain that many emergency managers are already using chatbots to write messages, even if they won’t admit it. Sutton said that the emergency managers who take her alert training sessions often present her with high-quality alerts they have drafted in seconds using ChatGPT or Gemini.

“Official adoption is pretty low, but people are unofficially using it, absolutely,” said Sarah Miller, a professor of emergency management at Pierce College in Florida and a longtime instructor at the National Disaster Preparedness Training Center. “They're using their own personal accounts, or they're using free ones.”

It sounds like an easy trade-off: if AI is better and faster than humans, and the difference between a good alert and a bad alert can be life and death, we should be using AI for every heat wave and wildfire. Yet even researchers who are bullish on chatbot technology say that making the switch could backfire. Members of the public who are skeptical of AI or who don’t trust the government in the first place may spurn agencies that publish AI-generated graphics or social media posts. In Syracuse, New York, for instance, a union of public employees sued the local government for using an AI service to answer non-emergency 911 calls.

“We trust things that we perceive [are] authentic and care about us,” said Ginny Katz, the founder of HazAdapt, a consultancy that helps disaster officials figure out how to use technology. “AI-generated content does not feel authentic.” There’s also a risk that chatbots could allow emergency managers to send out more alerts, and faster, which could increase the risk of warning fatigue.

As is the case in other areas where AI is being deployed, disaster experts say that for the moment the software is only as capable as the person using it. A seasoned emergency manager might be able to speed up their communication with the public by having a chatbot draft a series of alerts, but an inexperienced one rushing to create an urgent alert won’t know how to evaluate the output of a model like ChatGPT or Claude.

Even though the technology is a ways off from being able to perform without a human expert in the loop, that hasn’t stopped many understaffed emergency management departments from using it. As for how the proliferation of AI-generated content is affecting public trust in government, experts say it’s too early to tell.

“If you had a human sending you a text message versus a computer sending you a text message, which one would you believe is being more accurate?” said Justin Snair, the founder of Preppr, another company that offers AI software to emergency managers.

The answer, he said, depends on the person receiving it.
