#349. Texas Republican group seeks to block certification of state’s election

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Republicans in a Texas county have filed a lawsuit asking for ballots in the state’s midterm races to be hand-counted.

Days before the United States’ pivotal midterm elections, a Texas group affiliated with the Republican Party has asked a federal judge to stop certification of the state’s upcoming election results.

In a lawsuit filed on Tuesday, the Potter County Republican Party and its chairman, Daniel Rogers, claim potential election interference by artificial intelligence (AI) or “malicious actors” that could compromise the results of next Tuesday’s elections in Texas.

The plaintiffs, who are asking for a hand count of every ballot from every precinct in the state before election certification happens, allege voting machines have components inside made by China that would allow them to be manipulated.

“If a component is tampered with during manufacturing, it could serve as a point of entry for unlawful and malfeasance hackers, allowing for the possibility of synchronized attacks across multiple election machines and jurisdictions,” the lawsuit read.

The legal challenge comes as several polls show Democrat James Talarico narrowly leading his Republican challenger, Ken Paxton, in Texas’ all-important US Senate race that could ultimately decide which party controls the chamber after November 3.

The Talarico and Paxton campaigns and the Potter County Republican Party did not immediately return requests from Al Jazeera for comment.

The legal complaint does not specify if any voting machines in Potter County, or elsewhere in Texas, have been compromised. The plaintiffs acknowledged in their filing that no previous election in the county was “altered” by a third party, arguing that the possibility of vote manipulation by AI makes the state’s election safeguards easily compromised.

“The real likelihood of a clever and undetected hack by recently developed AI programs, which conceal their activity to avoid detection, reinforces the need for a full verification based on hand-counting of all paper records or ballots in all Texas precincts, prior to certification of the federal results,” they wrote.

State law in Texas already requires hand-count audits in elections where electronic tabulators are used. But the plaintiffs argue that does not go far enough.

“The paper record or ballot provides an independent verification mechanism only if election officials actually compare a complete hand count of all of the paper records or ballots,” the filing said.

The lawsuit also claims US “adversaries”, such as China, Iran and Russia, are capable of accessing election systems.

US President Donald Trump in July declassified documents that, he claimed, showed Chinese interference in the US 2020 presidential election, reviving his attacks on US elections despite no evidence that Beijing impacted the vote he lost that year.

Last week, the US Department of Justice also sought to block certification of an election, claiming, in Alaska, that an independent candidate with the same name as the state’s Republican Senator up for re-election is running in order to mislead voters.

Alicia Pierce, a spokeswoman for Texas’s Secretary of State, whose office administers elections in the state, said it “does not plan to comment on the lawsuit at this time”.

#643. US proposes Venezuela elections for 2027, opposition leader Machado says

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Sign up now: Get ST's newsletters delivered to your inbox

CARACAS, Oct 29 - Venezuelan opposition leader Maria Corina Machado said on Thursday the United States has suggested the country hold presidential elections next year, a timeline she has previously supported, in her first substantive remarks since returning last weekend.

Machado's return to Venezuela risks straining her ties with the administration of US President Donald Trump, which captured then-President Nicolas Maduro in a January raid. Maduro is facing US drug trafficking and torture charges, which he denies.

Washington has said Machado, a Nobel Peace Prize laureate, has every right to be in Venezuela, but Trump has also praised acting President Delcy Rodriguez. The Trump administration supports negotiations between the Rodriguez government and opposition groups which do not include Machado's Vente Venezuela movement on overhauling Venezuela's electoral council and top court before the next elections.

Machado thanked Trump and US Secretary of State Marco Rubio for their roles in Maduro's ouster and said the relationship between the two countries is one of mutual respect and in both nations' best interests.

“Secretary of State Marco Rubio has laid out a sequence of steps for moving toward a democratic transition that makes clear that presidential elections will be held in 2027,” Machado said, adding she would ensure that all eligible Venezuelans can cast ballots in any future election.

The US State Department did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Rubio has previously said he expects Machado to support the talks.

At a press conference in Caracas, Machado avoided responding when asked about Trump's comments in January that she did not have sufficient support to immediately lead Venezuela, and this week, that Venezuela could become the 51st state.

Machado has cultivated Trump as an ally for years.

The opposition leader added she was pleased Rodriguez, in remarks earlier in the day, had also backed elections and that those who help the country transition back to democracy will need guarantees. Machado said a transitional justice process, a truth commission and a revamp of state security forces are necessary.

Machado, 59, returned to Venezuela on Sunday after several failed attempts this year. She was barred from running in the 2024 election but her proxy roundly won, according to tallies provided by the opposition at the time. Venezuela's institutions, however, backed Maduro.

Venezuela needs transparent contracts, open bidding for public deals and international arbitration to ensure investors and the Venezuelan people receive the most from future investments, Machado said.

Rodriguez's interim government has signed deals with a variety of companies interested in oil, gas and mining with support from the US. They include a deal between North American Blue Energy Partners and the US government to export production from 17 Venezuelan fields.

Machado said NABEP's contract, which she has not seen, "evidently does not reflect the levels of transparency that Venezuelan society wants," especially because of the company's CEO Alejandro Betancourt. Betancourt has been investigated in the United States, Spain and Switzerland, including for money laundering, but never indicted.

“I want to emphasize something to all those investors who are looking at Venezuela today, or who have entered Venezuela in recent times: every legally signed contract will be honored," Machado said, adding some contracts could be "changed to new terms that we will offer, which will be much better."

The country needs a deal with the International Monetary Fund to establish how the country will manage its huge debt load and attend to urgent humanitarian needs, Machado said.

Machado, who lived in hiding for more than a year before leaving the country as many of her close collaborators were detained, said releasing political prisoners is key to restoring democracy.

Venezuela's opposition and human rights groups have said for years the government uses detentions to stamp out dissent. The government has denied holding political prisoners.

Legal rights group Foro Penal says 322 political prisoners are incarcerated in Venezuela, and more than half of them have not been convicted. Some 11,000 people are under other restrictions such as house arrest.

Foro Penal is focused on ensuring "the destruction of the repressive apparatus," especially regarding the new top court, the immediate and full liberation of political prisoners, the overturning of repressive laws and setting up an eventual truth commission, its vice president Gonzalo Himiob told Reuters this week.

Rodriguez said earlier on Thursday that more than 9,000 people have been released or had legal restrictions withdrawn under a limited amnesty law.

On Monday, Machado visited her lawyer Perkins Rocha, who was arrested after the 2024 vote and held for 18 months in Caracas' Helicoide, where advocates and former inmates have alleged torture takes place. Rocha remains under house arrest and had his amnesty request rejected. REUTERS

#655. ‘A clear alliance’: Nations accused of undermining science at UN nature talks

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Sign up now: Get ST's newsletters delivered to your inbox

Saudi Arabia, Russia, Argentina and Brazil are said to be frustrating progress at the UN biodiversity meeting in Yerevan, Armenia.

YEREVAN - Political tensions are mounting in the final days of the United Nations biodiversity talks with a handful of countries accused of trying to weaken the science that underpins global nature policy.

The growing feud over the role of science has alarmed veteran negotiators and follows recent efforts by fossil fuel-reliant nations to delay reports by the IPCC, the global authority on climate change.

“The same thing is happening here as what is taking place with the IPCC,” said a French government delegate at the COP17 summit in Yerevan, Armenia.

The delegate requested anonymity to discuss ongoing negotiations.

The delegate said four countries – Saudi Arabia, Russia, Argentina and Brazil – were frustrating progress.

The pushback is directed at two scientific assessments by the UN-backed IPBES biodiversity panel on the overlap between the nature and climate crises and the economic forces driving them.

Nearly 200 nations were due to agree how these landmark findings should guide efforts to protect nature but some countries “did not want to acknowledge them”, the French delegate added.

A negotiator from another developed nation told AFP these four countries were also leading this push.

“There is a clear alliance of Russia, Saudi, Argentina and, to some extent, Brazil, all working together to try to water down IPBES reports, not welcome them, or use them as bargaining chips in other negotiation items,” the negotiator said on condition of anonymity.

Much of the dispute boils down to a few paragraphs of text in a draft agreement and whether countries should “welcome” or “take note” of the IPBES findings.

The latter phrasing is considered far weaker in the language of UN negotiations, where every word is closely scrutinised.

The current draft was “a text without ambition”, said the French delegate.

“If we no longer have a scientific basis to work from, then everything we discuss here is merely a matter of opinion,” the delegate added.

Academics and civil society activists at COP17 have already issued a joint statement of concern at the state of negotiations.

When approached by AFP in Yerevan, IPBES chair David Obura declined to comment as the talks were still ongoing.

Although the summit is due to end on Oct 30, a number of outstanding matters – including around the science – remain unresolved.

The dispute echoes a growing pattern in consensus-based global negotiations, where oil-producing countries are accused of frustrating efforts to tackle fossil fuels, plastic trash and other environmental harm.

Climate science has long been a flashpoint and the IPCC is currently deadlocked, as countries including Saudi Arabia and India push to delay publication of its next set of benchmark reports on global warming.

In an unprecedented decision in 2025, a crucial summary of the Global Environment Outlook, a flagship UN scientific report, was torpedoed as nations rowed over fossil fuels.

“We’ve pretty much always seen IPCC reports being politicised...,” said the negotiator from the developed country.

“But in the biodiversity world they’ve been much less contentious until the last few years, and this COP in particular.”

“They’re never easy, no decision is. There’s always nitpicking over language – but it’s gotten far worse.”

Bethan Laughlin, a senior policy specialist for the Zoological Society of London who observed the COP17 negotiations firsthand, said the trend was worrying.

“The point really isn’t this particular text, although it is highly significant. The point is the precedent it sets,” she told AFP in Yerevan. AFP

#369. Finland launches criminal probe of suspected environmental damage by Google

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The US tech giant is investing $15bn in a huge new data centre project in northern Finland.

Finnish police have launched a criminal investigation into suspected breaches of environmental law at the construction site of a planned Google data centre in northern Finland.

The project is part of a $15bn deal announced last month by Alphabet’s Google to invest in artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure in Finland, which the US company described as its largest investment in Europe.

Authorities have said Tuike Finland, a local subsidiary of Google, conducted forest clearing and drainage at a planned site near the town of Muhos, before the environmental impact assessments required under Finnish law had been completed.

“We are investigating whether the company’s operations have breached the Water Act and resulted in environmental damage,” Detective Superintendent Kari Pakanen told the Reuters news agency on Thursday, adding that a guilty finding could lead to a corporate fine.

Pakanen declined to say whether any individuals could become a target of the investigation, and a Google spokesperson said the company would cooperate with the police investigation and continue to focus on maintaining compliance at its sites.

Videos and photographs showing the scale of the forest clearance at the site of the data centre, and Google’s initial reluctance to address the issue in the days after the news broke, have prompted widespread anger in Finland, where there is a high level of respect for the natural environment among the public.

“All the outrage and dismay directed at Google, I can very much share that, especially when we are talking about the parent company valued as the third-largest in the world,” Finnish Green MP Oras Tynkkynen told Al Jazeera. “There is just no excuse. You have to know the legislation of the country you are operating in; you can afford to hire the best legal experts to help you navigate the local legal requirements.”

Finnish authorities ordered Google to stop work at the data centre site at the start of October, and the latest information from the police highlights shortcomings in the drainage and water management at the site.

Tynkkynen called Google’s actions “unforgivable”, but cautioned that while the anger being directed at the US tech giant was justified, it was also diverting scrutiny away from legacy Finnish forestry companies, which he says are “by far the biggest contributors to biodiversity loss in this country”.

Finland’s cool climate, low-carbon electricity and government endorsement of AI projects have contributed to making the country one of Europe’s main destinations for data centre investments in recent years.

Finland, similar in size to Germany – but with one-seventh of the population, less than six million people – has had less vocal opposition to data centre buildouts than other places, including in the United States.

AI data centres are among the main drivers of a projected rise of up to 40 percent in Finland’s electricity consumption in the coming years, if all agreed grid connection projects go ahead, grid operator Fingrid has said.

Google’s planned investments in Finland include data centres, electricity grid improvements and clean energy and battery projects to power services such as Gemini, Search, Maps and YouTube.

#379. Al Jazeera marks 30th anniversary with network overhaul

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Marking three decades, Al Jazeera unveils a new structure: three channels, 33 programmes, and tech-driven innovation.

Al Jazeera is marking its 30-year anniversary with a transformation of the network, introducing a three-channel structure for its Arabic-language broadcasting operation and 33 programmes aimed at providing audiences with more in-depth coverage and analysis.

Since its launch on November 1, 1996, the Qatar-based network has helped reshape Arab journalism, challenging dominant political narratives and providing a platform for political debate rarely seen on television in the region at the time.

Reflecting on this enduring legacy during a special anniversary episode of the network’s Arabic-language Baad al-Bath podcast, veteran anchor Khadija Benguenna detailed the foundational shifts the network brought to the Arab news industry.

Benguenna said that Al Jazeera had popularised practices such as breaking news alerts, continuous live coverage, and exclusive reporting, which were subsequently adopted by other Arab broadcasters.

Former Al Jazeera news director Ahmad al-Sheikh echoed that sentiment during his appearance on the same broadcast. He described the Arab world’s media environment as previously devoid of independent voices, and credited Al Jazeera with providing an example for other Arab broadcasters to improve standards across the board.

The anniversary brings a significant restructuring of the Al Jazeera Media Network’s Arabic-language news services, separating rolling breaking news coverage, live event broadcasting, and more in-depth reporting across three channels.

Speaking to the Qatari newspaper Al Raya, Asef Hamidi, the managing director of Al Jazeera Arabic, described the changes as a “new renaissance” for Al Jazeera. “We are talking about numerous paths undergoing significant development that the audience will notice,” Hamidi said.

Hamidi, who was appointed to his current position in September 2025, said the restructuring was intended to address the growing volume and complexity of news and the need to offer audiences different ways of following events.

In an interview with Qatari newspaper Al Watan, Sheikh Nasser bin Faisal Al Thani, Al Jazeera Media Network director general, explained the philosophical necessity behind the expansion.

“From the very beginning, Al Jazeera was more than just another television channel,” Sheikh Nasser said. “It was a broader project to deepen public understanding.”

He said the strategy for the network’s fourth decade would be guided by three principles.

“Our fourth decade will be defined by three priorities: immediacy, understanding and the human dimension,” he said.

Under the new structure, each of Al Jazeera’s three Arabic-language news channels will have a distinct editorial focus.

Al Jazeera Mubasher, which began broadcasting in 2005, will continue its concentration on uninterrupted live coverage of events, without commentary from presenters or correspondents.

For breaking developments, the new Al Jazeera Now service will focus on developments as they unfold. But unlike a conventional news channel, it will operate without scheduled bulletins or a fixed programme grid.

Meanwhile, the flagship Al Jazeera Arabic channel will retain its news bulletins while pivoting more towards in-depth analysis and investigative journalism, as well as other programmes.

The restructuring is accompanied by plans for 33 programmes, promoted under the slogan “Your questions are our questions”.

Sheikh Nasser said the expansion was intended to provide more context on news events to Arab viewers.

“The real competitor today is not another channel, but noise – the flood of information that makes understanding harder, not easier,” he said, adding that Al Jazeera wanted to fill that gap in understanding.

Hamidi said the new programming would include investigative documentaries, discussions of political and social issues, and programmes examining the historical roots of current conflicts.

The network’s ambitions extend beyond television broadcasting to strengthening Al Jazeera’s local and regional reporting through dedicated digital platforms covering Palestine, Egypt, Sudan, the Gulf and other parts of the Arab world.

The Syria Now platform, which offers documentaries, investigations, and field reporting focused on Syrian affairs, has quickly emerged as one of the primary digital outlets for news on the country.

Looking ahead, the network is also planning a project called Al Jazeera Cultural, provisionally scheduled for launch in late 2027.

The initiative will serve as a space to foster sustained debate about the Arab world’s cultural and intellectual life.

The expansion also forms part of a wider technological transformation, including the development of The Core, an artificial intelligence initiative announced in December 2025 in collaboration with Google Cloud.

The initiative aims to integrate AI into newsroom operations, helping journalists manage information, coordinate coverage and make better use of the network’s reporting archives.

One component, Orbit, is being designed to coordinate newsroom workflows, including monitoring developments, commissioning stories, organising interviews, translating material and managing reporting assignments.

Sheikh Nasser said the first phase of The Core is scheduled to launch on November 1.

Despite the increased use of AI, Sheikh Nasser insisted that human oversight would remain primary.

“Artificial intelligence enables the human and does not replace him,” Sheikh Nasser said. “The main standard for us is conscience and editorial judgement, and editorial judgement is human.”
