#3900. Apparatus Explores Persian Mythology in Act V: Afsaneh

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Five years after its last collection, Apparatus is entering a new chapter. The New York-based design studio has unveiled Act V: Afsaneh, a collection of lighting, furniture, and objects inspired by Persian mythology, personal history, and the cyclical nature of life. Conceived by artistic director Gabriel Hendifar, the collection is both a tribute to his mother and an exploration of the divine feminine as a source of creativity, knowledge, and renewal.

Gabriel Hendifar. Photography courtesy of Apparatus.

The name Afsaneh carries two meanings. In Persian, it translates to “fable,” but it is also the name of Hendifar’s mother. That dual significance provides the foundation for a collection structured as a modern fable, with four interconnected chapters tracing a journey from creation to connection.

“Life is shaped by cycles. Creation and destruction. The separation from self and the return,” Hendifar explains. “It’s in these cycles that the collection finds its rhythm. The seed becomes the flower. We gather to celebrate its dance with the sun and breathe in the gift of its scent, however fleeting. And at the center of this beauty is the divine feminine—the source, the creative force, the nurturer within each of us.”

Each chapter takes its name from a Persian word and explores a different stage of that cycle. “Esfand: The Seed” represents origins, fertility, and protection. Named for the 12th month of the Persian calendar, Esfand also references a seed traditionally associated with warding off the evil eye. Here, the motif appears in lacquered finishes that evoke the colors of nature, from milk and honey to pomegranate, sky, dusk, and night.

“Gol: The Flower” follows the seed into bloom. Its forms explore growth and transformation, with molded fiberglass used to create delicate, membrane-like surfaces. The floral theme extends beyond the physical objects through Afsaneh, a namesake fragrance combining damask rose, oud, and frankincense. Scent becomes another material in the collection, introducing an ephemeral dimension to objects designed to endure.

The narrative takes a different turn with “Rahvi: The Serpent,” which represents the pursuit and transmission of knowledge. Water-formed brass and blown glass trace sinuous lines, while semiprecious stones introduce color and symbolic weight. Tiger’s eye, black onyx, quartz, honey agate, red jasper, lapis lazuli, and aventurine appear as amulets, connecting the serpent’s shifting form to ideas of wisdom and protection.

The final chapter, “Jahaan: The World,” brings the narrative outward, toward community and gathering. Bronze and aluminum intertwine with cast glass, creating forms that reference the cosmos and the idea of shared experience. Where the earlier chapters focus on origins, transformation, and knowledge, Jahaan considers what happens when those experiences are brought together.

Throughout Act V, familiar materials from the Apparatus vocabulary are pushed in new directions. Brass, glass, and bronze remain central, but techniques such as water-forming and fiberglass molding introduce different possibilities for shape, surface, and movement. The resulting pieces move between the architectural and the organic, with curves, suspended forms, and layered materials giving physical expression to the collection’s symbolism.

That relationship between objects and the human experience has long informed Hendifar’s approach. Rather than designing individual pieces in isolation, he imagines complete environments in which lighting, furniture, and decorative objects contribute to a shared atmosphere. Act V takes that philosophy further, using a narrative structure to connect the collection’s materials and forms.

The story also extends beyond the objects themselves. Apparatus collaborated with the Martha Graham Dance Company on five short films that interpret the collection through movement. For Hendifar, whose work often explores the relationship between objects and the body, dance offers another way to express the ideas behind Afsaneh.

“I consider the heart of my work to be creating the envelope for human experience, and there is no more beautifully distilled expression of that experience than Martha’s language of movement,” he says.

The films will be released episodically beginning in fall 2026, allowing the collection’s narrative to unfold over time rather than through a single presentation.

Act V: Afsaneh premiered in New York in October 2026, with subsequent presentations planned for London later that month and Miami and Los Angeles in February 2027. Each city will receive its own interpretation of the collection, extending the idea of a story that evolves as it moves between places.

For Hendifar, Afsaneh is ultimately about the relationship between creation and experience. Its objects carry references to mythology, memory, and the natural world, but their meaning is not fixed. Like the fable that inspired them, they are intended to become part of the stories unfolding around them.

Though he’s on the cusp of a career breakthrough in the DC Universe’s body horror flick Clayface, he’s already got his sights set on the next thing.

A FEW DAYS before we speak, Tom Rhys Harries turned 36 years old—and by his own admission, he didn’t make too big a deal out of it. Rhys Harries prefers not to dwell on the past and, beyond that, only spends so much time thinking about the present; his focus is often on what comes next. Still, he’s facing down an October full of milestones, and with them a press cycle full of questions about the comic book character he’s brought to life. A few short weeks after his birthday, the biggest project of his acting career will hit theaters: Clayface, the most experimental outing yet in James Gunn’s quasi-rebooted DC Universe and Rhys Harries’s debut as a blockbuster leading man.

The film is the sort of project fans have been begging Marvel and DC alike to take on for ages: a lower-budget exploration of a character (in this case, one best known as a Batman villain) through a real genre lens. And make no mistake, Clayface commits to the horror genre wholeheartedly. It is a gnarly, hard-R-rated body horror joint that had the audience at our small early screening squirming (Rhys Harries quietly joined in the back, the first time he’d seen the film with a proper audience of any kind). At the center of a cast featuring Naomi Ackie, Max Minghella, and Eddie Marsan is Rhys Harries as Matt Hagen, an actor on the cusp of superstardom whose career is derailed by a vicious attack from Gotham City mobsters. An experimental treatment provided by Ackie’s character begins to restore balance to his life, only to spur on a further descent into mutation and madness.

There are certainly parallels between Matt’s budding stardom and the position Rhys Harries finds himself in now, one where any future restaurant birthday dinners might come with a side of craning necks and iPhone camera flashes. It’s the sort of perpetual perception that pushes Matt’s life further and further off course in the film.

But Rhys Harries seems, for his part, unfazed by all this. When we speak on a sunny Sunday morning in Los Angeles, he’s in his home getting ready for a day of press. In talking about the film and his craft, he’s straightforward and thoughtful, completely devoid of frivolity or sound bite answers. It is—and I mean this as a compliment—very difficult to imagine him answering a series of canned “This or That” questions for a TikToker or YouTube channel. He lights up when talking about literature, motorcycles, the work of other filmmakers, and seems hesitant to center himself even when talking about the film he’s in. It does, as you get to know Rhys Harries a bit better, make sense. He grew up aspiring not to the Oscars but to pro rugby, a sport of uninterrupted play. If you find yourself on the wrong end of a tackle, nobody’s stopping to check on you. The game keeps moving forward, bloody noses and all.

Ahead of the release of Clayface, Rhys Harries tapped in with Men’s Health to discuss his leading man debut, body horror, and why fixing motorcycles helps to keep his head on straight.

MEN’S HEALTH: Was the screening last week your first time seeing Clayface with an audience?

TOM RHYS HARRIES: Yes, it was. I’ve seen it before with just the director [James Watkins] and Naomi [Ackie]. But even with last week’s screening being kind of a small room, yeah, it was my first time.

MH: What was that like? I know a few of us were reacting pretty viscerally to it at times.

TRH: There’s something specific about watching a movie like this in that environment. It would be completely different in a full theater, but there’s something kind of exposing about being in a room with, like, six or seven people. You can’t necessarily feel like you have the license to be vocal, even if you can’t help it at times.

MH: In the last week you’ve celebrated a birthday, seen Clayface with an audience for the first time, and in just a couple of weeks it’s going to hit theaters and most likely change a lot of things for you. What’s the experience of all this happening at once been like?

TRH: I don’t really get too fussed on my birthday. The older I get, I’m like, All right, that’s that. I feel like that with work as well: I’m pretty pragmatic, and I want things to go well for everyone involved. But I kind of want it to be done and out of the way—I like forward motion.

MH: The Matt Hagen/Clayface character—in a film filled with graphic, violent content—is an interesting choice for your blockbuster debut, but it’s been far from an overnight success for you. You’ve worked steadily for the last several years.

TRH: No, but there’s an undeniable aspect of reach here with these studio films. That’s a real thing. Regardless of whether people engage with it and it resonates, you do have wider reach with something like this.

MH: It’s a compelling decision, though—your breakout role coming as an actor who just got his first Oscar nomination and is really starting to cement himself as a movie star. It puts the concept of a leading man and performance under a microscope. What compelled you about Matt as a character and the film’s point of view on performance?

TRH: It’s a pretty multifaceted role. I felt like I was at a point in my career and life where I could meet the requirements of the role and a project like this. I say that with the caveat of knowing that I don’t think I would have been able to handle it when I was younger. I started acting kind of late—I wasn’t a child actor or anything. The role didn’t feel like something that was naturally in my wheelhouse. I had to spend a long time trying to feel as if I understood just a semblance of what it was about. I’m fairly inquisitive and curious, so I have to scratch at something for a long time before I feel like I have a handle on it. Maybe that speaks to an insecurity in me, or maybe it pertains to everyone. But there’s an element of it within us all, that question of Am I qualified to be doing this? It makes you think about yourself and overlaps, in this case, with acting quite a bit. But when you’re in a position like this, there’s just nowhere to hide. I felt like in taking this on I was kind of asking myself, What kind of actor do I want to be? What interests me? You keep going back to that and reviewing it and trying to push yourself, and that’s what I did here. I never want to rest on my laurels, even if that can be pretty seductive.

MH: In exploring those questions, what answers did you find? What interests you as a performer?

TRH: I think about this quite a lot, and ultimately I just feel that being able to do this for a living is such a privilege. And so I always want to ask myself what value I’m bringing to the table. How can I make what I’m doing bigger than this one thing? It has to exist in service to other people. I want to work with actors and writers and directors on projects that have something to say, because my job is to facilitate that and aid that.

MH: That feels not too far removed from something Matt is navigating in the film—how to exist in this rarefied air. What was your first impression of Matt when you read the script, and how did your take on him change as shooting progressed?

TRH: It felt clear early on that the film is concerned with vanity. You’ve got someone who’s navigating the world he’s found himself in and is playing to his strengths. I think what Matt desperately wants is to be additive and good. I know that sounds fairly simple, but when you have these extraordinary worlds and circumstances of comic book and science fiction film, they always work best when they have a firm grasp on the metaphor they’re trying to convey and have a root in something human. We’re able to look at them from a distance and then, at the appropriate time, clock the relevance of what it means to be human in our own world.

MH: There’s a decision the film makes in its approach to Matt that is particularly compelling. In act one he’s the subject of a smear campaign involving allegations of inappropriate behavior with women. The film never makes it clear how much of it is made up vs. how much of it has some root in reality. It adds a particular charge to certain scenes involving Naomi Ackie’s character. Throughout filming, how conscious were you of how Matt would be perceived in this regard, and how much were you tilting the balance in either direction?

TRH: I’m not really a fan of spoon-feeding an audience. One of my favorite films is Prisoners with Jake Gyllenhaal. He plays a cop who’s kind of got the bit between his teeth, but he also has this intricate tattoo that just peeks out from his shirt collar. It’s never explained, but alludes to a past that’s not as straight-laced as his present. Anything like that, within the medium of storytelling, gives the audience license to take ownership and imagine circumstances and color things out however they feel they should be.

Then again, I don’t know how much my thoughts on the issue matter. When people watch the film, they’ll get a snapshot of a man who’s arrived at this pivotal point in his life. Everything he’s done and everything that’s happened to him will be informing the decisions he makes and how he moves through the world. As an artist, I just try to load it up with as much tension as possible.

MH: You are, and pardon me here, a really handsome guy who took on a role of a handsome guy who gets his face sliced up and then spends a whole lot of the movie under some prosthetic or another. It’s a bit of an unconventional way to spend your blockbuster comic book movie debut, playing around with heavy makeup and gore effects rather than posing with your jawline at the perfect angle. Was this something that drew you to the production, and was there some level of intention in taking on a role that subverts traditional leading man good looks?

TRH: Honestly? I don’t think so. I really struggle with the calculation of thinking about what my next move will be. What I do know is sometimes I’ll read something and be surprised by it, energized by it—I’ll have some sort of reaction and realize it’s something I want to engage with. It just boils down to the story and if there’s something in it that resonates with me for whatever reason.

MH: It’s clear that you have this workmanlike approach to acting. You mentioned earlier not growing up in the industry, and I know you spent most of your young adulthood wanting to be a rugby player. Did growing up around that influence this no-nonsense approach you have now?

TRH: I love doing manual things. Acting, as a profession, is so subjective and internal. There’s no right or wrong approach or solution to it. I haven’t done it in a beat because I’ve been traveling so much, but I ride motorbikes. I love tweaking and tinkering with them because there’s no ambiguity to whether a bike functions or not. It runs or it doesn’t run. If you’ve done an adjustment and something feels off, then you know it’s broken. I’m drawn to counterbalancing things in that way.

It’s easy to get too intellectual or heady and pontificate on the art. And I think ultimately you’re just looking for if something feels right or doesn’t. A lot of the best and brightest actors I’ve worked with are able to just vibe something out. They wouldn’t necessarily be able to tell you how they got there. It’s just trust and instinct and feeling. And I like to counterbalance that experience by, like, pressure-washing the patio, because either you’ve cleaned it or you haven’t. There’s nothing to pontificate on there.

MH: You mentioned the practicality of working on bikes and how they either work or they don’t. Is that a pull from Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance?

TRH: Absolutely, yeah. I’m really fortunate to have people who have sort of been mentors to me or are just a little older, and one of my go-to touchstone people gave me that book when I was about 21. I also—I don’t want to get into the weeds with this because it could sound so pretentious—but I came across a Nietzsche book around the same time because I was doing a play with Nietzsche in it. So it was another weird counterbalance of the sort of gentle pondering of Zen and then having this book of nihilistic philosophy in my life all at once. I don’t know what kind of damage that must have done to me, reading those at such a young age.

It kind of draws back to Clayface, because so much of this movie is about what’s hardwired into our systems and what surfaces when we’re exposed to certain things at certain ages. Matt was exposed to violence at a really young age, and I believe violence is taught. That really interested me—he gains this new set of powers and with them the capacity to express himself in ways he’s never had access to before. So, on some level, he’s reverting back to a 7-year-old child and the violence he was taught then.

TRH: I don’t have one currently, but I’ve had a bunch over the years. My favorite was this matte black Harley that I was kicking about on the outskirts of London. I was in my 20s and saw someone drive through the streets of London on one, and then eight or nine years later, I could finally afford one of my own. I’ve also got loads of vintage Harley shirts and merchandise, but I feel like a fraud, because I haven’t owned one for a hot second now. I feel a bit fraudulent at the moment! I do have a license and I have had a Harley though, I promise.

#747. Scientists May Have Severely Miscalculated How Many Humans Are on Earth—or Maybe the Maps Got It Wrong

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A disputed study raises a practical question. If population maps put people in the wrong places, could disaster plans underestimate how many need help?

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. Why Trust Us?

Josias Láng-Ritter found a way to check population maps by looking at places that had disappeared underwater.

A postdoctoral researcher in water and environmental engineering at Finland’s Aalto University, Láng-Ritter collected records of people across the globe who’d been relocated due to dam construction. He wanted to know whether population maps showing those places before they flooded accounted for the residents who had to move.

In a 2025 study in Nature Communications, Láng-Ritter and his colleagues compared records from 307 dam projects across 35 countries. They looked at population estimates for 1975 through 2010 and found far fewer people on the maps than in the resettlement records. Depending on the dataset, the maps underestimated the populations anywhere from 53 to 84 percent.

The finding inspired plenty of hand-wringing headlines, suggesting there might be billions more humans on the planet than anyone realized. But Láng-Ritter tells Popular Mechanics that his research “cannot support any revision to the global population total.”

The more unsettling possibility is that we could have a reasonably accurate count of a country’s people and still put them in the wrong places. And that could have disastrous consequences.

“Global population maps are widely used for planning and risk assessment,” Láng-Ritter says. They help estimate how many people live in flood zones and guide decisions about warning systems, shelters, and relief supplies. If rural communities appear smaller than they are, he says, they would likely receive “fewer resources, or lower priority, than towns and cities.” An inaccurate map could affect whether a village got a shelter at all. If the map shows fewer people at risk there than elsewhere, officials might give other communities priority when deciding where to put warning systems, shelters, and emergency supplies.

The problem, Láng-Ritter notes, is that a census and a population map answer very different questions. A census records how many people live in a particular province, while a map divides that total among smaller areas, estimating where all of those bodies actually reside. Too many might be assigned to a town, or too few to nearby villages, without changing the province’s total count. “While rural populations are underrepresented in these maps, urban populations might be overrepresented due to erroneous allocation of population counts to rural and urban areas,” Láng-Ritter says.

Some researchers disagree about whether rural populations are widely underestimated. In a critique posted to arXiv in February 2026, Till Koebe, a postdoctoral researcher at Germany’s Saarland University, and five colleagues argued that the large shortfalls in rural population numbers reflected problems with the comparisons and where historical maps placed people, rather than rural residents going uncounted per se.

Researchers from WorldPop and the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre raised a more specific objection in a separate response. Many population maps estimate where people live by detecting buildings in satellite imagery—but the imagery available for a reservoir site was often captured after the valley had already flooded. Where a village once stood, those images show only open water, so the map registers no settlement there. Even when such a map is meant to represent the population in the years before the dam was built, it may not show a village that was, at the time, still standing and full of residents.

The critics accuse Láng-Ritter’s study of confusing missing data with misplaced data. The people, they argue, are still in the maps, “just in slightly different locations.”

But a slightly different location can be the difference between the dry side of a flood line and the wet side. Put a village on the wrong side, and the officials tallying who needs help won’t count anyone in it.

Láng-Ritter doesn’t oversell his case. He calls the critique “a valuable contribution to the ongoing discussion,” cautions against reading his percentages “too literally,” and treats them as a signal of a problem worth investigating rather than a figure to plan around. He also distinguishes a plausible failure from a documented one: “I’m not aware of a documented case where a population map error was shown to cause an inadequate response.”

Such a case would be hard to prove. Too much else goes into an emergency response to pin a bad outcome on a bad map. And the error often never gets that far, because the people deciding what to send know the village is there; local officials, Láng-Ritter says, usually have “better information than global maps.” His worry is what happens when that knowledge runs out—when the map is the only thing in the room.

What he can’t say is whether the problem persists. His records stop in 2010. Closing that gap would take a fresh comparison: a survey of rural households, or resettlement records from a recent dam project, measured against what the maps claim for the same place and period. Until someone does that work, his advice is to trust local figures over global ones. And when a global map is all there is, assume it undercounts the countryside. Don’t just ask whether a shelter has enough room—plan for the night more people arrive than it has beds for.

The headlines promised billions of hidden humans, but Láng-Ritter’s study doesn’t really prove they’re there. The question it leaves behind is narrower and harder to shake: when officials say a village has enough shelter, how do they know how many people will need it? A national total won’t tell them. Only a count of the people who actually live there will—and it has to come while there’s still time to make room.

Friday’s A Closer Look is typically a judicial biography. This week we cover Justice John Catron, who served on the court from 1837 to 1865.

Justice John Catron was a man of contradictions – for example, a southern slaveholder, he also opposed the secession of Tennessee while on the court and sought to preserve the Union. At the same time, Catron remains to this day a somewhat “obscure figure,” with not one full-length biography to his name.

Catron’s birthplace and date are uncertain, with some sources putting it in Pennsylvania around 1786, and others in Virginia between 1781 and 1792. What is known is that he was born into a poor German family, had minimal schooling, married in 1807, and, by 1812, settled in Sparta, Tennessee. Not long after, Catron served as a private under then-General Andrew Jackson in the Alabama campaigns against the Creek Indians and the War of 1812, reportedly becoming so ill that he had to be brought back to Tennessee in a wagon. (Some sources say he also fought in the Battle of New Orleans.)

Catron was licensed to practice law in 1815 and then served as a prosecutor on a Tennessee “mountain circuit.” He relocated to Nashville in 1818, and soon had a thriving practice centered on land law and disputes. In 1824, the Tennessee Legislature elected Catron to the state’s Supreme Court of Errors and Appeals, then Tennessee’s highest court, where he served for seven years. His best-known ruling there was reportedly one in 1829 that disbarred a lawyer for fighting a duel in Kentucky. Catron rejected the argument that a duel amounted to an “honorable homicide” and wrote that the law treated it as “a wicked and wilful [sic] murder.” In 1831, he became the court’s first chief justice and served for three more years. As chief justice, Catron upheld the freeing of a group of enslaved people in an 1834 case, but only on the condition that they leave the United States. The next year, he upheld Tennessee’s extension of its criminal laws over Cherokee land inside the state.

Catron’s involvement in politics reportedly led to his chief justiceship ending; one scholar writes that then-presidential-candidate Hugh Lawson White’s allies forced Catron off the bench after he backed Martin Van Buren over White in the 1836 election, while another account says the legislature retaliated for his backing of Van Buren by abolishing the Court of Errors and Appeals altogether. Catron then went on to manage Van Buren’s Tennessee campaign.

Catron’s support for Van Buren and “align[ment] with Jacksonian democracy” led to Jackson nominating Catron to the Supreme Court on his last full day as president on March 3, 1837, the same day that Congress added two seats to the Supreme Court (bringing it to nine). Five days after Jackson’s nomination, with Van Buren now in the White House, the Senate confirmed Catron by a vote of 28 to 15.

Catron “never sought to take a leading part in the great public questions before the Supreme Court,” instead opting for opining on “complex technical matters” and the common law. Averaging 7.5 opinions per term (a lower rate than any Taney Court colleague except Justice James Wayne), Catron usually sided with Chief Justice Roger Taney and often took cases over Spanish land grant disputes. Catron did make clear some of his own views; in the 1853 case of Ohio Life Insurance & Trust Co. v. Debolt, he warned against the rapid growth of corporations, writing that “these numerous bodies of associated wealth” risked “stand[ing] above the state government creating them.”

Catron’s most significant moment on the court came in the 1857 case of Dred Scott v. Sandford, where he agreed in his concurrence that Congress lacked the power to prevent slavery in the western territories and that Scott remained enslaved. While the case was pending, he also wrote privately to then-president-elect James Buchanan, asking him to persuade Justice Robert Grier to join the majority (Grier ultimately did so).

Catron enslaved people throughout his life, and in 1827 he reportedly fathered a son whom he never acknowledged with Sally, an enslaved woman. However, Catron did not follow Tennessee out of the Union and in 1861 returned to Nashville to try to persuade the state’s leaders not to secede. After they declined, he left for Kentucky and his property in Tennessee was confiscated. He kept riding circuit in Kentucky and Missouri, and when the army locked up civilians who sympathized with the Confederacy, Catron turned away their requests for habeas corpus, effectively supporting the detentions. Once federal forces took control of Nashville, he went back and opened the U.S. district court there in June 1862. Catron dissented in the 1863 Prize Cases, arguing that President Abraham Lincoln could not blockade Southern ports without Congress’ approval.

Catron died in his Nashville home in May 1865, shortly after the Confederate surrender. Henry Stanbery was nominated by President Andrew Johnson to fill his seat, but the Senate did not act before Congress eliminated the seat in 1866 to keep Johnson from appointing justices.

#1135. What’s It Like to Shack Up With Elizabeth Holmes? Just Ask Lance Oppenheim

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The last few years of Lance Oppenheim’s life have been defined, in part, by two men: Nathan Fielder, Hollywood’s resident agent of chaos, and Robert Pattinson, its box office powerhouse. When Oppenheim and I spoke earlier this month, he was flanked by a life-sized poster of the Batman and Odyssey actor, who starred in (and produced) the director’s just-released debut feature, Primetime. That film was followed, three weeks later, by You Can See Everything, the momentous and at times unwieldy documentary Oppenheim co-directed with Fielder. “It’s all extremely dizzying,” says the 30-year-old filmmaker. “I kind of feel like I’m in a simulation or something…”

That’s sort of what it feels like to watch You Can See Everything, in which Oppenheim and Fielder embed themselves in the Del Mar, California home of Elizabeth Holmes, the infamous founder of Theranos, in the month leading up to her 11-year prison sentence. That experience, which unfolds over three invasive and inviting hours, was a far cry from the work he’d done on Primetime, a quasi-biopic of another outsized 21st century figure: To Catch a Predator host Chris Hansen. It wasn’t just that the movie was Oppenheim’s first scripted feature. The scale of the production also dwarfed the eight-person documentary crew he and Fielder brought into Holmes’s California rental. Until Primetime, he says, he hadn’t made a movie with a crew of more than a dozen people.

But regardless of their size or scope, Oppenheim’s movies are exercises in profound intimacy. In his 2020 documentary, Some Kind of Heaven, he explored the world’s largest retirement community and all its colorful characters; in 2024’s Ren Faire, 80-year-old George Coulam, the founder of the Texas Renaissance festival, tells Oppenheim of his struggles with erectile dysfunction medication and his affinity for sugar babies. It’s no wonder Elizabeth Holmes let Oppenheim get so close; people tend to open up to him, even against their better judgment.

Though Oppenheim prefers to let his films do the talking ("I can't do anything, unfortunately, except make films to express my emotions and feelings," he insists), he got candid with us earlier this month about filming with Pattinson, Fielder, Holmes, and her husband Billy Evans. From ingratiating himself with a convicted criminal to welcoming his first child just five days before shooting his debut feature, Oppenheim is having a moment, personally and professionally. #2573. A Pediatrician Breaks Down RSV, From Warning Signs To Prevention

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Dr. Mona Amin explains why RSV hits babies harder, which symptoms mean it’s time to call the doctor, and what to ask at your next pediatrician visit.

If you're a parent or soon-to-be one, you've undoubtedly heard of RSV. But if you're anything like me, you may still be a little fuzzy about what it really is, perhaps even filing it away in your brain as "just a bad cold." And for older kids and adults, that's often true. For babies, though, it can be a very different (and more serious) story.

The season for respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), which is common and extremely contagious, generally runs from fall to spring in the U.S. In other words, now is a great time to get informed. Scary Mommy spoke to board-certified pediatrician Dr. Mona Amin, who has partnered with Sanofi on its RSV awareness campaign, about why RSV is a big deal for infants and the warning signs every parent should know.

“In older individuals, it can look like what I’d describe as a really bad head cold,” Dr. Amin says. But for some, RSV can affect the lungs and breathing passages. This is true for people with underlying conditions like asthma, and it’s especially true with babies.

The virus causes significant inflammation in the airways, particularly in the lower airways. That can lead to conditions like bronchiolitis (inflammation of the small airways in the lungs) or pneumonia in young babies and children.

The really tricky part: There’s no reliable way to know which babies will get seriously sick. “It’s hard to predict, unfortunately, which babies may develop the serious symptoms,” Amin explains.

Before speaking with Amin, I was shocked to learn that two out of three babies get RSV by the time they turn one, and it’s a leading cause of hospitalization for babies under one. And of those babies who are hospitalized, more than 75% are healthy, with no preexisting conditions.

Of course, that doesn’t mean that every baby who gets RSV ends up in the hospital. But what it does mean is that parents who have healthy babies shouldn’t assume they’re in the clear.

“I don’t want parents to think, My baby was full-term and healthy, so RSV isn’t something I need to think about,” Amin says. “We just don’t know which baby is healthy enough or strong enough to be left unprotected.”

So, what’s a mom supposed to do? Well, one prevention option is Beyfortus, a monoclonal antibody for infants. It’s important to understand, though, that Beyfortus is not a vaccine, but a preventative antibody. “A vaccine teaches your immune system to make its own antibodies,” Amin explains. "With Beyfortus, we're giving the baby the antibodies directly."

Still not sure what that means? Fair. Essentially, it means your babe is getting protection during those early months when they're most vulnerable to falling seriously ill from RSV. Amin says Beyfortus is administered as a single dose, with no boosters, and it can cover babies for five to six months of an RSV season.

Amin is clear that Beyfortus won’t keep a baby from getting RSV. The goal is to reduce the risk of lower respiratory infections like bronchiolitis and pneumonia... and the hospitalizations and doctor visits that come with them.

In short: before RSV season, with a trusted pediatrician.

If you’re pregnant heading into RSV season, go ahead and talk to your OB-GYN about options. Then, once your baby arrives, Beyfortus can be given as early as the newborn period and is recommended for babies under eight months.

Her big TL;DR takeaway: Talk to your own child’s doctor about what’s right for your baby. Not sure what to say? Amin has a script for that: “I’ve heard about Beyfortus and RSV. When is my child eligible to get it, and what are your thoughts?”

The trouble with RSV is that, for many babies, it does initially present like a bad cold. And if your baby has a stuffy nose but is feeding well, has a fever that comes down with medication, and can be soothed, you’re likely in ride-it-out territory.

#2887. The Sneaky Reason Hunters Sometimes Miss Easy Shots on Big Game

Published Oct 23, 2026 12:51 PM EDT

“I couldn’t have missed; the crosshairs were right on.” How often have you heard this pitiful phrase, or, lamenting a missed deer, uttered it yourself?

It may come as a shock, but the reason why some shots mysteriously miss their targets can be explained by the fact that the shooter does indeed clearly recall seeing the crosshairs when he pulled the trigger. The memory of having seen the crosshair, or reticle, may very well have resulted from the shooter having had to look for the reticle in the scope’s field of view and then deliberately overlay it on the target. That means that the shooter’s primary attention had been focused on the reticle rather than the target.

Any time your concentration is not on the target, a miss is in the making.

Does this mean that the reticle in some scopes — perhaps the one you use — can be wrong for the kind of hunting and shooting that it is used for? Exactly. Of all the factors that have to work together when you aim your scope-sighted rifle, shotgun or handgun, one of the most important — the reticle in your scope — is one of the least understood. Also, for reasons that I can only guess at, discussions and even supposedly learned writings about telescopic sights seldom do more than pay lip service to reticles.

Before I sat down to write this column, I thumbed through the half-dozen good books on shooting that have been written during the past half-century and found that although the authors describe different reticles, usually with tidy illustrations, no really helpful suggestions or worthwhile conclusions are offered.

The standard advice being, “Here they are boys; use the one that tickles your fancy.”

I was even more distressed when, upon rereading the chapters on telescopic sights in a couple of books that I’d written, I discovered that although I had outdone myself in describing the mechanics and technical aspects of scopes and reticles, I had largely failed to take the subject off the pages and put it in a real shooting situation. I hadn’t explained why one type of reticle can offer a solid advantage over another.

Scope makers themselves have likewise been of little help, mainly catering to popular trends in reticles and promoting the optical qualities of their products while at the same time tending to ignore the simple fact that first and foremost a telescopic sight is a sight — an aiming device — and that we aim with the reticle, not the lenses. The optical qualities and magnification of the scope, whatever they may be, merely support the sight’s only purpose: aiming.

This sounds like a critical assessment, and it is intended to be, because when it comes to reticles, shooters aren’t being led astray so much as they are not being led at all. We get away with this because virtually any reticle, even a bad one, permits pretty good aiming, and as long as the scope can be sighted in and the bullets hit near where we aim on the practice range, we figure that we’re doing OK. What more can be asked?

But shooters and hunters are constantly asking for more, and I have a notion that once they become more acquainted with the relative advantages of different types of reticles, there will be as much campfire debate over crosshairs and dots and posts as there is over the .270 versus the .30-06.

I get a good bit of mail from readers asking for advice when they are planning on buying a new hunting rig. A typical letter reads something like:

“I’m thinking about buying a bolt-action rifle in .280 Remington caliber, and I want to mount a 3X-to-9X variable scope on it. Will this scope be a good all-around choice?”

Very seldom, however, is there an inquiry about the best choice in reticles for different kinds of hunting, even though reticle choice, in many instances, is at least as important as a scope’s magnification, field of view, brightness or any of the other criteria by which a scope is selected.

To get some perspective on the importance of a scope’s reticle, always keep in mind the simple fact that you aim with the reticle. So let’s consider how a reticle affects how you aim.

Quite a few years ago, when I first began doing a lot of varmint shooting, I was very much of the opinion that the best reticle was an extremely fine crosshair. My reasoning was that fine crosshairs allowed greater aiming accuracy because very small targets such as thumbtacks, or a woodchuck’s eye, could be divided into precise quadrants. For the most part, this was only wishful thinking because the precise bullet placement that I sought to achieve by such hairsplitting greatly exceeded the accuracy of my rifles and ammunition. Of course, I was also overly optimistic about my marksmanship. To make what I believed to be a good thing even better, I took the crosshairs out of some of my target scopes and replaced them with the thinnest filaments I could find. Youth and innocence require no apology.

More serious, however, was what these slender reticles might have been doing to both my shooting and aiming habits. To aim the fine crosshairs, it was necessary to divide my concentration between the reticle and the target, which, in any shooting situation, is the crooked road to a bad shot. Ideally, the reticle should be of a size and shape that insinuates itself on the target — the image you see in the scope — and transmits the point of aim to your brain without you having to look for it or think about it. That’s why when a reticle is right for the job, you will make a good shot without a clear recollection of having noticed the reticle at all. In other words, the right reticle lets you concentrate on where you want to hit. An apt comparison is a well-fitted shotgun that lets you give your full attention to the flight of the target without having to think about aligning the barrel.

Obviously, what is needed in the way of a reticle is one that is so distinct that it is instantly seen without looking for it and one that remains a distinct element of the sight picture even when you concentrate fully on the target. It would seem then that the ideal reticle would be a broad, very black crosshair, but this is somewhat of an oversimplification because different shapes, as well as sizes, of reticles may be best for different types of shooting.

In recent times, some scope makers have been making a big thing out of the brightness, or “light-gathering” qualities, of their scopes — the idea being that the oversize tubes and objective lenses transmit more light to the shooter’s eye and thereby permit better viewing and aiming during periods of low light, such as at dawn or dusk. This is fine as far as it goes, but without matching the reticle to the hunting conditions, there is little point in having an extra-bright scope.

A while back a scope importer sent me a new model to test that was claimed to be wonderful for shooting in low light. It had a 30mm tube (nearly 3/16 of an inch bigger than a standard one-inch scope tube) and a skillet-size objective (front) lens that transmitted a wonderfully bright field of view. Sadly, though, the scope would have been virtually worthless for hunting in low-light conditions because its standard crosshairs got lost in the evening shadows.

During the many excruciating hours that I’ve spent in a leopard blind, I’ve found over and over that when I’ve had to call it quits on account of darkness, it was not because of being unable to see the target zone, but rather because the reticle in my scope had faded into the gloom. This is why the size and shape of the reticle is more important than the brightness, or so-called “light-gathering” qualities, of a scope that will be used for shooting in dim light.

European hunters, who often shoot in darkness that would astonish their North American counterparts, have long been wise to the absolute necessity of a bold reticle for hunting in poor light. This is why German and Austrian scope makers have traditionally offered not only a wider variety of reticles than American scope makers, but also reticles of much bolder dimensions.

To give you an idea of the difference, the crosswires of a typical 4X American scope will subtend about one-half to three-quarters of a minute of angle at 100 yards. In layman’s terms, this means that the crosswires appear that wide at 100 yards, twice that wide at 200 yards, with progressive enlargements as the range increases. If the reticle is the popular dual-dimension crosshair, such as Leupold’s Duplex pattern, the thicker outer arms of the reticle will subtend about two minutes of angle, more or less.

By comparison, the center wires on a European scope will subtend a full minute of angle, with the thicker arms appearing to be as wide as six to eight inches at 100 yards! Not only that, but the thick arms may reach much closer to the center. These arms reach in so close in some models that even if the thinner center part of the reticle is lost in darkness, the thicker arms can still be used to reasonably aim a shot.

North American hunters, followed by American scope makers, have never cottoned to these bold, extra-thick reticles because they blot out a sizable portion of the field of view, as well as seem to make aiming somewhat imprecise. In truth, though, the broad outer arms of the European reticles don’t block out anything important, and the thick center crosswires, though not the best choice if you want to shoot quarter-inch groups, make it possible to aim a rifle in dim light that would render more delicate reticles useless. My favorite of the European-style reticles has only three heavy arms, with the top arm being omitted. This leaves the top half of the field of view unobstructed, but there is still plenty of reticle definition for aiming in poor light.

I’m not saying that a European-made scope is the only salvation for hunters who take to their deer stands during the darkest minutes of legal shooting hours. There are plenty of scopes made here and in Japan that can be had with low-light reticles. Leupold’s 6×42 scope, for example, comes with a choice of either standard or heavy reticles, with the heavy style having center crosshairs nearly an inch thick (as they appear at 100 yards) and more than 2 1/2 inches wide at the outer arms. Some scope catalogs include tables of reticle subtensions, which I don’t think are often read by consumers but can provide valuable information if you take the time to look at them.

Though the reticles I’ve been discussing may work best in conditions of poor light, there are other hunting and shooting situations where other types of reticles will work better. For example, if you hunt deer in timbered or brushy terrain, where the shooting distance is usually close with the quarry frequently moving or on the run, a heavy European-style crosswire reticle becomes a less than perfect choice. The reason is that when you shoot at a moving target, you want two things: maximum reticle definition and an unobstructed view of the target, especially on the horizontal plain where the target is moving. This latter criterion explains why a reticle with thick horizontal cross arms is a poor choice: As you swing ahead of the running target, the horizontal arms may hide or largely obscure what you are shooting at.

The old-fashioned flat-topped standing-post reticle, with or without visible crosswire, is hard to beat for hunting in brush or timber and for shots at moving game. The bold profile of the post can be easily seen against a brushy background, and the open field of view to either side of the post allows you a clear view of the target even when you are leading a running shot. Troy Lawton, a shooting friend who won the 50-meter running-target event at the U.S. International Shooting Championships a few months ago, tells me that he uses a post reticle for the difficult running-boar events in international competition. Presumably he does this for the reasons that I’ve just described, because what works best in the hunting field frequently works best on the target range as well.

Simple post reticles are not as common in American brands of scopes as they once were. The reason for this, I think, is that modern-day rifle and scope buyers have become overly obsessed with accuracy, and they fear that a post reticle won’t allow the precise aiming that is necessary to shoot bragging-worthy groups at the local club’s range. This is a fundamental but common mistake, because you don’t hunt big game on the practice range. When selecting a hunting reticle, try to imagine how it will look on a miserable, dark, rainy day in heavy brush. In such conditions, a big post can be mighty comforting. That’s why a big post is my first choice in a scope for a shotgun with rifled slugs, because the terrain, hunting conditions and distances at which rifled shotgun slugs are typically used are those where a post reticle is at its best. What with today’s improved slugs and slug-shooting guns, along with hunting regulations requiring their use in some states or regions, I wouldn’t be surprised if the post reticle was to make a comeback. Scope makers take note.

I’ve saved the dot reticle for last because more and more I’ve come to regard a dot as the best all-around reticle for most forms of rifle shooting — especially for any sort of shooting, such as target shooting, plinking and varmint hunting, where careful aiming is required and your eye is kept busy aiming many shots for extended periods. This represents quite a change of opinion, because there was time, long past, when I was of the notion that a dot, no matter how small, covered up too much of the target and thus restricted the accuracy of my aim. But as I mentioned earlier, those were innocent times. Also, a dot reticle nearly caused me a disaster on an Alaska bear hunt several years ago. This I’ll describe presently.

For decades past, the dot reticle has often been referred to as a Lee dot, named after T.K. “Tackhole” Lee, who installed dot reticles in scopes of all types. Lee was famous for his “floating” dot, which was suspended on a filament of spider web so fine as to be all but invisible, making the dot truly appear to float in space. Of course, such dots were a fascinating technical achievement, but in a more practical sense, they left a lot to be desired, as I learned on a cold, drizzly day on the Alaska Peninsula when I tried to draw a bead — or rather a dot — on a bear.

My scope was one of the old 2.5X Lyman Alaskans set in a low Griffin & Howe mount, the way it was done back in the 1950s, and altogether it made for a fine weatherproof rig for hunting bears along the blustery Alaska coast. I’d spotted a nice bear from a hill above and worked my way down to within a range of about 80 yards, remaining hidden in the brush while the brownie scavenged among sea-eroded boulders along the shore. When I raised the .375 Holland & Holland to fire, what I saw was not one dot, but dozens of dots of all sizes, caused by specks of rain on the scope’s eyepiece. By the time I sorted out the dot I wanted to aim with, the bear had disappeared behind a rock and then reappeared several yards down the beach. So I had to make a dash to catch up for another shot, at which point I again played pick the right dot, and the bear once more disappeared. After I had played this guessing game a few more times, the bear stopped long enough for me to get the right dot on target.

Right then I swore off dots, but only for a while. Nowadays I use dot reticles in most of the scopes that I use regularly, especially those that I use for target and varmint shooting, but they aren’t floating dots. Instead, I want the dot suspended on visible wires that instantly lead my eye to the dot. Not only that, but I’ve come to prefer what many shooters would consider oversize dots. The lone exception is the one-eighth of a minute dot in the 36X scope that I use for benchrest shooting. But even such a small dot causes less eye fatigue than crosshairs when a shooter is aiming at a target for long minutes at a time. The same is true with reticles for varmint shooting, only here I prefer a larger dot that can be easily seen against the varied backgrounds typical of varmint-infested pastures.

Where a dot is at its most helpful is in situations where the hunter or target shooter does not have a solid rest and must shoot offhand or from a relatively unsupported position. With the constant twitching of the sights typical of offhand shooting, especially when you’re out of breath or excited about a shot at a trophy deer, ordinary crosshairs can be especially difficult to see as they jiggle and skitter across the target. Trying to keep up with the crosshairs causes you to concentrate on the reticle rather than the target, and as described at the beginning of this column, a miss is in the making.

With a nice plump dot suspended on clearly seen wires, you don’t have to look for or even think about the reticle, and your full attention is focused on where you want the bullet to hit. Also, as you’ll discover, a big dot seems to jiggle less than a crosshair, thus reducing any tendency to jerk the trigger. This is why the dot reticle is an overwhelming favorite with silhouette shooters who fire only from the difficult offhand position. The preferred dot size for silhouette shooting is about a half-minute, which looks big and fat through the high-magnification scopes (16X or more) usually used in competition.

Shooters who use fine crosshairs in the belief that these reticles are an aid to precise aiming (as I thought as a youngster) are only kidding themselves. Savvy shooters, especially experienced target shooters, know that an undersize reticle causes eye fatigue and leads to reduced target concentration. That’s why the top shooters who pump shot after shot into a tiny bull’s-eye use surprisingly large dots or thick crosshairs.

For hunting, the size of the dot should be matched to the magnification of the scope, with lower-power scopes having progressively larger dots. A 4X scope, for example, should have a dot in the 2 1/2 to three-minute range, and a two-minute dot is about perfect for a 6X scope. With modern variable-power scopes, the size of the dot will diminish progressively as the magnification is increased. The dot reticle in Leupold’s 2X-to-7X variable scope, for example, subtends 4.3 minutes of angle at the lower setting and 1.61 minutes at full power, which is ideal.

Whatever reticle you use, be sure that it is perfectly focused for your shooting eye. All too often a scope doesn’t do all it can for a shooter simply because the shooter isn’t seeing the reticle as clearly as he should. Scopes are usually delivered with the eyepiece lens focused for normal vision. But don’t count on the lens being focused, especially when using secondhand scopes. You can easily bring your scope’s reticle into clear focus for your eye by screwing the eyepiece out until the reticle looks fuzzy. Then, while looking through the scope at a neutral background, such as the sky, screw the eyepiece forward (to the right) until the reticle is sharp. While focusing the eyepiece, it is a good idea to look away from the eyepiece every few seconds so that your eye doesn’t accommodate itself to the scope (false focus). Also, if you use prescription glasses when shooting or hunting, be sure to wear them when focusing the eyepiece. Likewise, if you don’t wear your corrective lenses when hunting, focus the scope without them. And if you wear bifocals, be sure to focus the scope while looking through the long-distance part of your glasses.

Remember, the reticle is what you aim with, and the better you see it, the better you’ll shoot.

#2579. The Promise & Peril Of Mom Friends

You may love them or hate them, but, for a huge chunk of time, you need them.

When my kids were younger, I had a few groups of women I considered my “mom friends” — the mothers of my children’s friends. You’ll see it in the first-hand reports of toxic celebrity-mom group chats and mega-hits like Big Little Lies, which features rich moms, working-class moms, and a murder. These friendships are a focal point of our cultural imagination, where society can project all our ideas and expectations of women raising children in these times. But far from the media and the memes, mom groups are complicated. A source of comfort and camaraderie, yes, but also awkwardness — and sometimes even distress.

The good stuff first: there’s a lot to love about friendships with other mothers. They’re a built-in support network, there when you need them. There is always someone available to pick up your kid when you’re stuck in traffic or give a cuddle when they’re scared at a sleep over. They have an extra pair of shin pads at the soccer tournament when you forgot to pack them, or a nut-free snack on the field trip for your highly-allergic toddler. Underneath the relief these encounters engender lies a deeper and implicit exchange of trust. It says: Please take care of my kid. Please make sure they are cared for, supported, seen, loved. When we’re at our best, we’re working towards a shared goal: helping to raise each other’s children. Building and sustaining the village. There’s joy in that; a bit of magic, too.

These friendships also provide a rare space where we can acknowledge the realities of motherhood, the impossible things asked of women raising children. Whatever it is that’s weighing you down — a partner who calls caring for his own children “babysitting,” unhelpful administrators for a kid struggling at school, a job that won’t give you paid leave — other mothers have undoubtedly been there. They can confirm for you the ways in which society pretends to revere mothers in theory but crushes them in practice, and can help you find laughter in the absurdity of it all.

And yet. These friendships, so tender and interdependent in some ways, can also be a source of unease. It is sometimes tricky to figure out just how much of ourselves to share, and how much we really want to know about the other women in our proverbial village. Some of what we find out inadvertently is funny, or endearing, or the kind of information that brings us closer. On the school group chat we’ll find another mom who has the same sarcastic sense of humor, or who also hates the same teacher that we do. We find out which mom is a Scientologist, which one brags incessantly about her kids. Occasionally we’ll find out intimate things when someone seeks advice about a breast lump or impending separation that she hasn’t told the kids about yet.

But there are other times when scratching below the surface of these friendships and seeing what’s underneath is more complicated. For me, that often involves learning things about other mothers that I wish I hadn’t known. Because knowing requires a decision: what then? What do I do? Or say? Or not say? And, on the flip side, how much do I want to reveal about myself and my own opinions?

My kids go to an international school with a very diverse group of families from all over the world, with wildly different priorities and values than my own. I’m not just talking about voting differently (though there’s that, too); there are moms at school who are anti-immigrant or make jokes about gay people or have blinding amounts of wealth. Some of them yell at their nannies in front of me. Some don’t make their kids wear seatbelts. They think and talk and parent differently than I do. And I’m sure some of them don’t like or agree with me, find me self-righteous or judgmental, with no sense of humor. (Fair on 1 and 2, wrong on 3!) I’m sure I’m not everyone’s cup of tea, either.

For years in situations like these, I struggled to figure out where to draw the line, if at all; if my kids were physically safe, was there anything else I truly needed to consider before I agreed to that playdate or sleepover? Were there questions to ask? And if I didn’t like what I learned, what was I supposed to do rearrange carpool? Encourage my kids to find other friends, so that I didn’t have to socialize and rely on people I didn’t agree with? Is that possible? Perhaps more importantly, is it worth it?

When one of my kids was little and starting a new school, I met a mom at back-to-school night. I mentioned my work with human rights for migrant workers; she went on what I can only describe as a diatribe, without irony, about how domestic workers (the poorest and least protected of the working poor) are entitled and demanding and need to be paid less. And then she promptly invited my kid to a birthday party. Should I have declined the invitation? Said no and argued all night about international labor standards? Said yes and argued about labor standards? Did I even want to engage? In that case, it turned out, I did not. I wanted to go to a birthday party and eat cupcakes and exchange Lego gifts and not have to always be my full self, not have to ponder whether to respond when someone makes an off-color comment, risking either conflict or complicity. I wanted to make small talk, to think about what we have in common (our kids and their friendship), not what separates us (everything else).

Now that my kids are older, the complications of mom friendships have lost some of their intensity. In part, it’s because I don’t rely on other mothers as much as I used to; the days of chaperones and carpools have passed. And my kids have grown, have developed their own opinions and worldviews, largely unaffected by their friends’ parents, despite my fears. Like most teenagers, they’re discerning; they’ve identified a few adults they admire and have written off the rest. And I’ve seen that one of the benefits of living in a community with people who are so different from us is that my family has had more explicit conversations about topics like wealth and race and privilege than we might have had in a social world where people’s behaviors and choices looked like ours.

I realized, too, that my judgements of other moms for the ways they vote or talk or act, or my concerns about their judgements of me — they didn’t matter that much. After all, we weren’t close enough to really change each other’s minds about anything of substance. We weren’t real friends, we were mom friends. I can recognize this category of relationships for what they were: intimate and interdependent, and somehow still distant. I can acknowledge that these mom friends lived and talked in ways that were troubling to me, and that I also appreciated the role they played in my kids’ lives. And I’m reminded, of course, that this is what having a proverbial village is all about; back when our foremothers raised kids in actual villages, they weren’t all best friends, they probably didn’t even like each other that much.

They just had to know that they were there.

Effective dimension is a scale-dependent measure of information complexity [1]. Originally developed in statistical information theory to quantify the number of truly active parameters in a model, it transforms the coordinate system to eliminate redundant parameter combinations based on the size of available data. It has since been adopted in classical and quantum machine learning to precisely evaluate a model's expressivity, trainability, and ability to generalize to new data.

Importantly, effective dimension is related to generalization. Under the assumptions of the framework, it can provide a complexity-dependent bound on generalization performance [2]. Intuitively, a model with fewer distinguishable parameter directions should be less effective at fitting arbitrary fluctuations in a finite dataset.

It can also help analyse trainability. Highly expressive models are not automatically easy to train: some parameter directions may have almost no detectable effect on the output distribution, while others may be strongly correlated. These effects can make optimization poorly conditioned or effectively flat [3]. Furthermore, such models may also be more susceptible to overfitting, where a model captures noise or idiosyncrasies in the training data rather than patterns that transfer to unseen data, leading to poor generalization.

Training and test losses are still essential diagnostics, but they depend on the optimization trajectory. A poor initialization, an unsuitable learning rate, or a weak optimizer can make a trainable architecture look untrainable in a particular run. Effective dimension instead characterizes the model’s parameter geometry relative to data, making it a complementary diagnostic rather than a replacement for empirical training curves [4].

Beyond these diagnostics, effective dimension matters because parameter count can misrepresent how complex a model really is. Every additional parameter has a cost: more circuit evaluations for gradients, more gates exposed to noise, and a larger search space to optimize. This cost is worthwhile only if the parameter adds a direction in which the model can actually change its behavior. Effective dimension estimates how many such directions exist, and so indicates whether a model's size is being used. When two models perform similarly, the one with fewer effective directions is usually the better choice, since it achieves the same result with less complexity. This is Occam's razor: prefer the simpler explanation when it accounts for the observations equally well. The aim is not to minimize complexity at all costs, but to find a model whose complexity suits the problem.

There are two useful ways to study the effective dimension of this family:

Global effective dimension, which considers the entire parameter space [1].

Local effective dimension, which considers a neighborhood around a particular parameter configuration, usually one reached during or after training [2].

The global effective dimension asks how many informational parameter directions the model has on average across the full parameter space Θ\ThetaΘ.

This makes it useful for architecture-level comparisons. For example, if we want to compare a variational quantum circuit with a classical neural network while controlling for other factors, global effective dimension can reveal whether one architecture has more distinguishable functional degrees of freedom than the other.

Because it averages over the entire parameter space, global effective dimension is not tied to one specific initialization or one optimization trajectory. This is valuable when making controlled, ceteris paribus comparisons between model classes.

However, global effective dimension should not be interpreted as completely independent of data. The Fisher information matrix depends on the model’s predictive distribution and, in supervised learning, typically on the distribution of input data. What global effective dimension removes is the dependence on a single trained solution or a particular region visited by one optimizer run.

That broad perspective is also its main limitation.

A model may contain highly informational regions of parameter space that are never reached in practice. Perhaps those regions are difficult to optimize, perhaps the initialization places training far away from them, or perhaps regularization prevents the model from entering them. In such cases, global effective dimension may describe the theoretical capacity of the model family without accurately reflecting the capacity used by a particular trained model.

This motivates the local effective dimension.

Instead of integrating over all of Θ\ThetaΘ, local effective dimension restricts the analysis to a neighborhood around a reference parameter vector θ∗\boldsymbol{\theta}^*θ∗, often the parameters obtained after training:

The local measure therefore asks a more practical question:

Near the solution that training actually found, how many parameter directions are distinguishable from the available data?

In this sense, local effective dimension can reflect the interaction between the architecture, the dataset, initialization, and optimization procedure. It is especially useful when we want to understand the complexity and generalization behavior of a trained model rather than the theoretical capacity of the entire architecture. Furthermore, local effective dimension can help with verifying if the trained model still has any unused learning capacity.

In the large-nnn limit, the effective dimension approaches the maximal rank of the Fisher information matrix under the assumptions of the construction.

The parameter nnn is often described as the dataset size. That is broadly correct, because the effective-dimension framework connects nnn to finite-sample generalization. But it is also helpful to interpret nnn as a resolution scale.

A larger dataset can distinguish smaller differences between two nearby predictive distributions. As nnn increases, directions that were previously too weak to observe may become informative.

Imagine examining an object at different magnifications. At a coarse scale, it may look like a simple two-dimensional surface. At a finer scale, you may discover that the surface is actually a narrow curve winding through space. At an even finer scale, that curve may reveal small oscillations or additional structure.

This is why it is good practice to evaluate effective dimension across a range of nnn values rather than reporting only one number. In a real application, the most relevant values are those near the size of the datasets on which the model will actually be trained.

Effective dimension is a complexity measure based on the geometry of the model’s statistical profile. It goes beyond raw parameter count by identifying how many parameter directions are actually distinguishable from data.

Its value comes from connecting several ideas that are often studied separately:

Parameter redundancy and identifiability.

Trainability and optimization landscapes.

Under the assumptions of the underlying theory, effective dimension can be used to derive bounds on generalization error. It should not be read as a direct prediction of test accuracy, but it provides a principled complexity quantity that can complement empirical training curves and validation results.

Importantly, effective dimension quantifies learning capacity through parameter space, a perspective that applies equally to classical and parameterized quantum models. In quantum machine learning, alternative frameworks characterize capacity directly over the Hilbert space of quantum states, which this metric does not explicitly address.
