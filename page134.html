Officials have a plan to whittle down the institution’s projected deficit to $3 million through expense shaving and revenue increases.

In addition to the workforce reductions, UMaine officials plan to centralize some operations and sunset others, decrease reliance on external vendors and seek outside funds to finance some job roles.

When and if all those efforts are realized, the challenge of closing UMaine’s remaining $3 million budget gap “will be the Cabinet’s main focus over the coming weeks,” the senior leaders said.

All that applies to only a portion of UMaine’s budget. In September, UMaine President Joan Ferrini-Mundy and Provost Gabriel Paquette said the institution faced a projected $19 million deficit for the next fiscal year. Of that, the university’s academic affairs unit committed to eliminating $10 million from base expenses.

The academic side planned to do so by cutting 20% of vacant positions within academic affairs in addition to reducing instruction budgets and consolidating course sections. In the longer term, UMaine might also increase course seat limits and close out some “underenrolled” programs, the leaders said.

But, even with all those measures, Ferrini-Mundy and Paquette warned that staff and faculty layoffs would ultimately be necessary to completely eliminate the university’s deficit while options to limit layoffs would be “pursued aggressively.”

At the time, the leaders cited “demographic and other enrollment pressures” that have weighed on revenue as expenses have grown.

Without budget cuts, UMaine leaders project its total deficit would grow from $19 million fiscal 2028 to $34.6 million in 2029 and $41.1 million by 2030, according to an October presentation.

Looking forward to fall 2027, administrators anticipate an enrollment decline of up to 2%, to 10,511 students, according to the presentation. But if UMaine can increase enrollment and retention, particularly among first-years and graduate students paying full tuition, it could staunch that decline to 1%.

At the Association of School Business Officials International’s 2026 Annual Conference & Expo, approximately 750 attendees — primarily K-12 finance experts — gathered in Pittsburgh last week to share tips on their best practices for navigating some of the growing challenges districts are experiencing nationwide.

One of those concerns discussed by school finance leaders included inadequate state funding that is being exacerbated by the expansion of school choice and the competition for students.

During an Oct. 14 panel discussion with top school business officials at the ASBO conference, Anya Randle, deputy assistant superintendent at the Louisiana Department of Education said that public school systems are struggling to secure proper funding through Louisiana's education funding formula.

“Our battle is fighting school choice, getting adequate funding,” and addressing a decline in public school enrollment, Randle said. “We can't hide behind the Excel spreadsheets or just give the auditors the documents,” Randle said. If district finance leaders don’t tell their own stories, she said, then “someone else may be telling our story for us, and it may not be an accurate story.”

During the ASBO conference sessions held between Oct. 14-16, school finance leaders also discussed how they’re innovating amid their district’s budget challenges and enrollment declines. Notable sessions included a Delaware district leader’s advice on developing a process to show district investments are improving student outcomes as well as an Indiana school business official’s tips for finding strategic ways to save millions in a K-12 system’s budget.

#2974. Bodies of Missing Japanese Climbers Found on Gangapurna West

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This morning, rescuers found the bodies of Japanese climbers Hidesuke Taneishi, 40, and Tetsu Hashimoto, 25, on Gangapurna West.

“Both bodies were found at around 6,300m on the south side of Gangapurna West,” the rescue team told The Tourism Times.

Hidesuke Taneishi (left) and Tetsu Hashimoto (right) with a support team member in Kathmandu earlier this week. Photo: Cosmo Adventure

The climbers were attempting the first ascent of the 7,140m mountain and lost contact on October 15 at approximately 7,050m on their summit push.

A search-and-rescue team has been looking for the pair since October 19. Unfortunately, no one knew where they were on the mountain, or even if they were on the mountain at all. Therefore, the search started at Manang village, with no success.

The team included helicopter pilot Sobit Gauchan, high-altitude photographer and climber Manish Maharjan, and IFMGA mountain guide Pansang Rinzee Sherpa. The helicopter dropped Maharjan and Rinzee at 5,750m, and from there they used a drone to search the upper slopes. They scouted the northeast and southwest sides of the mountain, and finally found the remains of the Japanese climbers’ higher camp. Today, they found the bodies on the south side.

As for the cause of the accident, Rinzee told ExplorersWeb that their observations of the terrain pointed to a snow slab collapse.

Several teams have attempted Gangapurna West, located next to the Annapurna massif in Manang district, since it opened to foreign teams in 2014. However, none have summited. The mountain is also known as Asapurna or Lachenal Peak.

Team leader Hidesuke Taneishi was nominated for a Piolet d’Or last year after his first ascent of 6,652m Pholesobi Peak, also in Nepal, via a direct route up its vertiginous north face with Hiroki Yamamoto.

#1647. Building Glassdoor Recruiter: The story and technology behind smarter job matching

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Lauren Rosenthal, Director of Product | Oct 23, 2026

It's a tough time to be looking for a job. And increasingly, it's a tough time to hire, too.

We recently posted an open role on my team at Glassdoor. Within just days, we received hundreds of applications — a symptom of today's "spray and pray" job search fatigue, where candidates feel forced to apply everywhere just to get noticed. After sharing the opening with my network, I received a number of direct messages almost immediately. This was a big contrast to just a few years ago, when I was hiring for another product manager position. Back then, I spent weeks scouring my network, asking for introductions, and actively recruiting candidates.

I share that story to emphasize how much the dynamics have shifted. The latest jobs report shows that roughly 27% of unemployed Americans have been looking for work for six months or longer. Friends who are job searching tell me they're applying to dozens of roles each week, often without hearing back. Meanwhile, employers are inundated with applications, making it harder to identify candidates who are genuinely a good fit.

At Glassdoor, we call this the “double needle-in-a-haystack problem.” Job seekers and employers are searching through a noisy marketplace, and both are frustrated by the experience.

This year, we officially launched Glassdoor Recruiter, the curated job search platform that transforms job seeking from high-effort scrolling into quality-first matching.

For years, job searching has largely been about volume. Job boards give you thousands of opportunities, and it's up to you to sort through them. But we thought, what if we flipped that model? What if, instead of searching through hundreds of jobs, you could review just a handful of incredible, attainable matches each week, with a clear explanation of why each one might be right for you? That's what our job matching team of product, UX design, engineering and machine learning experts ‌set out to solve, combining Glassdoor's workplace insights with Indeed's extensive job inventory and new AI technology.

Large language models (LLMs) can interpret complex information and understand meaning beyond keyword matches. Our team has combined traditional machine learning with LLMs to build more accurate job matching technology to provide users with high-quality matches and a job match score. The score provides quick fit signals around skills, compensation, culture and work settings, factoring in Glassdoor's deep knowledge of companies to determine which opportunities are worth a closer look. This is especially powerful for complex roles, like Software Engineers, where candidates with similar job titles may have very different skills, specializations, and career goals.

Our goal isn't just to recommend jobs that match a resume. It's to identify jobs that are both appealing to job seekers and realistic given their qualifications, working preferences, what matters most in their next role, plus the employer's needs.

AI has a reputation for being a black box. But at Glassdoor, transparency is a big part of who we are, and we don't want to lose that just because we're using AI. We wanted job seekers to understand why a role might be right for them, using the authentic experiences and millions of insights people have shared on Glassdoor for years.

Here's how it works: During onboarding, job seekers share their preferences, deal breakers, and career aspirations. Glassdoor Recruiter then evaluates thousands of jobs, scoring and ranking opportunities based on job seeker preferences and employer requirements.

For top matches, we provide a detailed job-fit breakdown across four dimensions: Skills & Experience, Compensation, Wellbeing & Culture, and Location & Work Setting. We draw on what job seekers have come to rely on Glassdoor for, like detailed company ratings from real employees (including the attributes that go into Glassdoor’s Best Places to Work rankings, like Career Growth and Work-life Balance). Collectively, these factors help job seekers evaluate whether an opportunity is actually right for them.

One of my favorite parts is that Glassdoor Recruiter gets smarter over time. After receiving recommendations, job seekers can save, apply to, or pass on matches. When someone passes, we ask why, then incorporate that feedback into future recommendations. The idea is that you put in a little work upfront, and Glassdoor Recruiter does the bulk of the work from there, so you don't have to spend hours searching through jobs that aren't right for you.

I've been refining my own profile lately, and noticed more Principal Product Manager roles than Director of Product roles. AI is changing expectations for job seekers, and increasingly we're being asked to roll up our sleeves and build, on top of managing. But AI also gives us an opportunity to make technology work for us.

Our vision is a deeply personalized recruiting experience that truly knows you, one that understands what you've done and what you want to do next. As Glassdoor Recruiter learns from your preferences and the marketplace evolves, we want it to take the work out of finding a job. Not more jobs for the sake of more jobs, but fewer, better quality opportunities worth your time.

Finding a job shouldn't feel like searching for a needle in a haystack. Neither should finding the right person to hire. We're working to make both sides a lot easier, and doing it the Glassdoor way.

Tell Glassdoor Recruiter what you’re looking for, and let it work for you.

She brings more than 15 years of product management expertise spanning gaming, e-commerce, and product consulting. #912. Scientists discover a hidden messaging system deep inside the brain

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Deep within the brainstem is a tiny group of nerve cells that plays an outsized role in how the brain functions. This region, called the locus coeruleus (LC), gets its name from the Latin words for "blue place." Although it contains only a small fraction of the brain's neurons, it produces norepinephrine (NE), a chemical messenger that helps regulate attention, learning, heart rate, and the body's reactions to stress and excitement.

Scientists have long assumed that the LC operates like a loudspeaker, sending essentially the same norepinephrine signal across the brain. However, a new study published in Nature challenges that understanding. Using brain imaging, recordings of neural activity, genetic techniques, and behavioral experiments in mice, researchers at the Allen Institute discovered that the LC communicates through several distinct pathways. Rather than broadcasting one general message, it delivers signals to specific destinations, with different groups of neurons responding to different experiences.

A Hidden Brain Network That Sends Precise Messages

By tracing the connections of LC neurons, researchers found that different groups of cells communicate with specific regions of the nervous system. These pathways determine where signals trigger the release of norepinephrine, creating a much more organized communication network than previously recognized.

Neurons in the upper (dorsal) portion of the LC primarily connect to the cerebral cortex and forebrain. Meanwhile, cells in the lower (ventral) portion send their signals mainly to the brainstem and spinal cord. This arrangement allows different parts of the LC to influence separate brain functions.

"The findings suggest the brain's norepinephrine system is far more like a targeted postal network than a foghorn," said Karel Svoboda, director of Neural Dynamics at the Allen Institute and study co-author.

Why the Discovery Could Matter for Brain Disorders

Understanding how this signaling network is organized could eventually change how certain neurological and mental health conditions are treated.

Norepinephrine plays a central role in regulating attention and mood, making it an important target for medications used to treat depression, anxiety, and ADHD. Neurons in the LC are also among the earliest brain cells to deteriorate in Alzheimer's disease, suggesting that the new findings could have implications for understanding and treating these conditions.

Current treatments that influence norepinephrine can affect multiple brain circuits at once, sometimes producing beneficial effects in one region while causing unwanted effects elsewhere. A clearer map of the LC's specialized pathways could help scientists develop therapies that act more selectively on the circuits involved in particular symptoms.

The research received support from the National Institutes of Health's Brain Research Through Advancing Innovative Neurotechnologies® Initiative, also known as The BRAIN Initiative®.

Scientists Identify Brain Cells Involved in Learning

The researchers also uncovered an important distinction between the functions of different LC neurons.

Cells in the dorsal LC that connect to the cortex were particularly active during learning. When mice performed decision-making tasks and adjusted their choices after an unfavorable result, these neurons increased their activity.

Neurons in the ventral LC behaved differently. Their activity rose shortly before mice ignored signals indicating that a reward might be available. This suggests that these cells help regulate whether animals respond to opportunities in their surroundings.

"What emerged was a clear map: neurons in the dorsal LC that send signals upward to the cortex are involved in learning, whereas neurons in the ventral region projecting downward to the brainstem and spinal cord govern whether animals engage with their environment at all," said Svoboda. "We also showed that these anatomical differences are mirrored by distinct gene expression patterns."

These findings indicate that the differences between the two groups extend beyond their locations and connections. They also have distinct patterns of gene activity that reflect their specialized roles.

Researchers Discover a Record-Breaking Neuron

The study involved an extensive examination of the mouse brain. Scientists used whole-brain imaging to study nearly 35,000 neurons, analyzed the genetic profiles of almost 400,000 cells, and reconstructed the complete structures of several exceptionally complex nerve cells.

During this work, they encountered another remarkable finding: LC neurons possess extraordinarily long axons. These projections carry signals from nerve cells to other cells, allowing information to travel across the nervous system.

The researchers found that LC axons measured approximately 35 centimeters long on average. One particularly extraordinary axon had a total reconstructed length of 70.32 centimeters, making it part of the longest single neuron ever measured in a mouse.

"This neuron, like many others that we studied, supplies NE to a very large volume of the cerebral cortex. For the brain, this is highly unusual. Most neurons are more specific in their targets," said Jeremiah Cohen, scientist at the Allen Institute and study co-author. "But this neuron doesn't release NE everywhere. It ignores the cerebellum, brainstem, and spinal cord."

Despite its remarkable length and extensive reach, the neuron does not distribute norepinephrine throughout the entire brain. Instead, its connections remain selective, reinforcing the discovery that the LC operates through carefully organized signaling pathways.

A New Understanding of How the Brain Learns

The researchers also identified intriguing similarities between the norepinephrine system and the brain's dopamine signaling system.

Dopamine is another major chemical messenger that sends signals to the basal ganglia, a brain region involved in forming habits. Scientists suspect that dopamine and norepinephrine work together to support learning, potentially allowing the brain to acquire several complex and abstract concepts at the same time.

By revealing the intricate connections, specialized functions, and unusual physical structure of neurons in the locus coeruleus, the research provides a more detailed picture of how the animal brain communicates and learns.

Although the findings come from experiments in mice, they could eventually help scientists better understand human brain function and design treatments that target specific neural circuits rather than affecting entire signaling systems.

#3755. Galerie Patrick Seguin shows demountable Jean Prouvé house with Japanese tea room interior at Design Miami.Paris

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Photography by Galerie Patrick Seguin

Galerie Patrick Seguin in Paris has brought a 1944 house by architect Jean Prouvé to Design Miami.Paris, where it was reinterpreted as a Japanese pavilion.

The 6×6 Demountable House was designed in the 1940s to be assembled at speed and function as housing for those who had been made homeless by war.

It is currently for sale via Galerie Patrick Seguin and on show in the gardens of the Hôtel de Maisons during Design Miami.Paris.

“This house was designed at the end of the second world war to provide temporary accommodation for displaced families whilst their homes were being rebuilt,” the gallery’s founder Patrick Seguin told Dezeen.

“Its exposed structure showcases all of Prouvé’s ingenuity; he was able to respond to the urgency of post-war reconstruction with a design that could be assembled in a single day,” Seguin continued.

For Design Miami.Paris, the gallery reimagined its interior as a contemporary chashitsu – a traditional Japanese pavilion used for tea ceremonies.

“This new reading creates a dialogue between Prouvé’s architecture and Japanese aesthetics and reflects the close ties he maintained throughout his life with Japan, where he was invited to give lectures on several occasions,” Seguin said.

“Japanese architect Reiko Hayama was also one of his closest collaborators, joining his atelier in the early 1970s.”

Because it had been designed to be demountable, taking the wooden house to Paris for the show was easy, according to the gallery.

“Prouvé designed the houses to be easily assembled and dismantled; that’s the ingenuity of these structures,” Seguin said. “The 6×6 house only requires a single day to be assembled by three people.”

After the end of Design Miami.Paris, the Prouvé house “will no doubt be moved to its new owner’s home,” the gallerist concluded.

This year’s edition of Design Miami.Paris also saw Apple unveil its Designers of Tomorrow.

The Jean Prouvé house is on show at Design Miami.Paris 2026 from 21 to 25 October 2026 at L’hôtel de Maisons, 51 Rue de l’Université, 75007 Paris, France. See Dezeen Events Guide for more design events and exhibitions around the world.

#771. Scientists propose a way to detect dangerous “mirror life” before it exists

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Scientists have proposed what they believe is the first way to detect "mirror life," a hypothetical form of life built from molecules that are reversed versions of those found in living organisms on Earth.

Researchers from the University of Strathclyde and Durham University have developed a proposed detection technique that uses molecules capable of emitting light with distinct left and right-handed properties. By analyzing these light signals, scientists may eventually be able to distinguish conventional organisms from their mirror-image counterparts.

Although mirror life does not currently exist, some experts consider its potential creation a serious biological safety concern. These artificial organisms could behave in unpredictable ways when interacting with Earth's ecosystems and might evade certain natural defenses that protect people, animals, and plants.

The likelihood of mirror life emerging is considered low, but its possible consequences could be severe enough to justify developing detection methods in advance.

Why Mirror Life Could Pose a Biological Threat

Concerns about mirror life have prompted scientists to recommend restrictions on research intended to produce organisms capable of reproducing using mirror-image biological molecules.

The potential dangers have also attracted attention from governments and international organizations, including the UK Government Office for Science and the United Nations Secretary General's Scientific Advisory Board.

The underlying science involves a property of molecules known as chirality, often described as molecular "handedness." Just as a person's left and right hands are mirror images that cannot be perfectly aligned, some molecules can exist in two forms that have identical chemical components but different three-dimensional arrangements.

These subtle structural differences can have major biological consequences. Two mirror-image versions of a molecule contain the same atoms connected in the same order, yet the human body may respond to them very differently. For example, one form of a medication could successfully treat a disease, while its mirror-image counterpart might be ineffective or even harmful.

Scientists are concerned that organisms assembled from reversed biological building blocks could interact with existing life in unexpected ways.

Importantly, the researchers emphasize that mirror life remains entirely hypothetical. Their proposed detection method is intended as a precaution, not a response to an existing biological threat.

The proposed approach relies on a phenomenon called circularly polarized luminescence (CPL), which can reveal the molecular handedness, or chirality, of certain substances and biological structures.

Light can exhibit different polarization patterns, including forms that rotate in opposite directions. By studying these patterns, researchers can obtain information about the three-dimensional arrangement of molecules.

The underlying concept has a surprisingly familiar application: the polarized light technology used in modern 3D movie glasses to create the illusion of depth.

The research, published in Royal Society Open Science under the title 'Circularly Polarized Luminescence (CPL) as a Contingency Assay for "Mirror Life" Bio-Hazards', was led by Dr. Lewis MacKenzie of the University of Strathclyde's Department of Pure and Applied Chemistry, working with Professor Robert Pal of Durham University.

Dr. MacKenzie said: "We don't expect mirror life to appear tomorrow, but if it did arise, the consequences could be severe, and it makes sense to develop detection methods before they are needed. Professor Pal and I had been working on CPL technologies before recent concerns became prominent, and guarding against mirror life was a clear fit for our research."

A Molecular Fingerprint for Detecting Mirror Life

To identify a possible mirror organism, the researchers suggest using specialized fluorescent probes that attach to particular structures inside cells.

Once bound to their targets, these probes would generate characteristic CPL signals. Scientists could analyze the resulting patterns to determine whether a biological sample contains the molecular handedness associated with ordinary terrestrial life or its reversed equivalent.

In effect, the light signals could serve as a molecular fingerprint, revealing differences that might otherwise be difficult to identify.

However, relying on a single fluorescent probe could produce misleading results. To improve reliability and reduce the risk of false readings, the team proposes combining several probes designed to recognize different cellular structures.

By examining multiple signals together, researchers hope to develop a more dependable way to distinguish conventional organisms from hypothetical mirror life.

Testing the Technology Without Creating Mirror Organisms

One important advantage of the proposed technique is that scientists could begin investigating and refining it using ordinary biological samples. There would be no need to manufacture mirror organisms simply to explore whether the underlying detection principles work.

The researchers also believe the method could eventually be incorporated into several types of scientific equipment, potentially allowing mirror-life screening in different environments.

Professor Pal has already demonstrated laboratory-scale CPL detection technologies involving microscopes, cameras, and biological cell plate readers, which are instruments commonly used to examine large numbers of cell samples.

However, the complete detection method specifically designed to identify mirror life has not yet been experimentally demonstrated. Further research will be necessary to determine whether the proposed approach can reliably distinguish between different forms of molecular handedness in biological samples.

Professor Pal added: "CPL detection relies on the same fundamental concept that allows modern 3D cinema glasses to create a stereoscopic image.

"This CPL detection approach can be adapted to many types of systems for analysis of cells, including biological plate readers, microscopes, and cameras. This means our mirror life assay approach could be generalized to many environments depending on end user needs."
