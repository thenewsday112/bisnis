As the legal dispute over the paintings’ ownership continues, no one seems to be asking: What were their names? Who may still be grieving their deaths? What decision would best honor them?

The decades-long ownership dispute between the family of late Holocaust survivor Dina Gottliebova Babbitt and the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum over seven watercolors depicting Roma prisoners is back in the news. That's because the dispute culminated in a California lawsuit initiated by her daughters 17 years after their mother's death.

Nicknamed the “Angel of Death,” Dr. Josef Mengele ordered Babbitt to portray the traits of Roma prisoners as part of his pseudo-scientific experiments to prove what he perceived as their racial inferiority. Under these extreme life-and-death circumstances, Babbitt created the watercolors to save her life as well as her mother's. All the Roma people forced to pose for these portraits were later executed.

The news coverage and conversations around the ownership dispute have long missed an essential frame through which to view this tragedy: the rights and dignity of the Roma people who were depicted, killed, and forgotten. What decision would best honor them? How do we stop the harmful experiment's long-term impact from further stigmatizing Roma identity?

#3954. How Schools Might Become Places of Vibrancy and Connection

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

“I spent a year as a high school teacher. I was shocked to discover the cleverness and ingenuity of the dumb boys.” These words come from Redford (pseudonym), a remarkable young man I interviewed in September. The “dumb boys,” of whom his math classroom was chiefly composed, were those who had disengaged from their studies years earlier and no longer applied themselves with any real effort. In his classroom, he maintained, they came alive.

It is no secret that by high school, many boys (and some girls too, of course) feel little self-assurance in the classroom. They may be confident elsewhere, such as on a sports team, yet, toward schoolwork, they have become indifferent or dismissive. They find instruction an imposition and become unreceptive to it. Many conclude their teachers dislike them and have even given up on them.

Many factors contribute to these attitudes, and I’ve written earlier about the debilitating effects of early education and the felt absence of compelling reasons to invest or work hard. But whatever the reason, for many boys, school is boring, conflictual, alienating.

Even for the estranged, however, as Redford’s experience illustrates, classrooms can become places of vibrancy and connection. In terms of what inspires and invigorates students, it is fair to say the actual curriculum plays only a secondary role. The real factors are resonant interactions, both among the students themselves as well as with the teacher.1 There is no formula for this, but Redford’s unorthodox experiment suggests three approaches that can promote engagement.

Teachers often recognize that the curriculum and especially its rigid enforcement can get in the way of learning. They lament how the obsessive focus on student achievement metrics has led to a high-surveillance, low-trust, and intensely programmed school day. Under this regime, education can get reduced to little more than the transmission of facts and skills. It isn’t rewarding for educators and “for a lot of students,” said one teacher in a National Education Association publication, “It’s like boot camp.”2

Redford’s school required a weekly plan “that accounted for every minute of classroom instruction time.” While he prepared basic instructional outlines, he allowed considerable spontaneity in class to take advantage of opportune moments when something different, something life-giving, was clearly needed. With some frequency, he “tossed the curriculum aside for important things, such as ad hoc debates, arm-wrestling competitions, and walks outside.” If it was “just a beautiful day,” he said, “we’d go outside and do something there.”

The educational research literature documents the general tendency of girls to try harder and be more compliant in school than boys.3 While acknowledging that pattern, Redfield saw an even bigger differentiator in the “physicality thing” for boys. Unlike girls, boys expend a lot of energy just to contain themselves, to stay seated, keep their bodies still, and remain quiet. So, Redfield loosened the strictures. Students could get up, and at times, he said, as much as half of his class would be standing in the back of the room, where, generally, they were not disruptive and followed instructions—with exceptions, “obviously.”

Another significant boy-girl difference is in attitudes toward competition. School administrators strongly discourage open competition, seeking to keep achievement individual and private so that no one “feels bad for losing.” While girls had their own ways of distinguishing themselves, Redford observed, they were “not really interested in finding out who was better than who at various tasks.” The boys, on the other hand, craved competition even when they were “not very good at the thing they were trying to compete at.”

To make a place for this need, Redfield held various contests, such as in-class “arithmetic competitions.” He also assigned students to teams, with an incentive to collectively score the highest on each test. “Usually the prize would be, like, a pizza or something.” The kids responded and encouraged one another not to be a “straggler” and “drag down” the total.

Team competition enhanced motivation and prompted the boys to cooperate as a group. There is, Redfield thought, “a kind of internal politics that emerges among boys that are trying to compete in a group setting.” Educational researchers have noted that “boys’ [disruptive] conduct is particularly responsive to negative peer effects.”4 But peers working together can also engender creative interactions and greater self-assurance.

It is one thing when students are only answerable to teachers and parents, another when they feel responsible to one another. Boys, Redfield argued, may not care about pleasing the teacher or about grades or even the subject matter, but they don’t want to “disappoint their comrades” and “can hold each other accountable in ways that he [the teacher] can never do.” Then, the outcome is not just about them as individuals. For the sake of the team, “you have to start performing,” Redfield observed, “you have to pull your weight.” And “that’s a very valuable skill to learn.”

Cooperation built community. To track students’ whereabouts, Redford’s school had an AI-powered surveillance system. Since the cameras kept account of how long students were gone from the classroom, and Redfield did not enforce all the rules on attendance, he invited his seniors to see if they could “fool the system.” As one sign of their cleverness and ingenuity, they invented all sorts of “crazy” and inventive ways to move about undetected.

The boys also used their cleverness and ingenuity to devise elaborate schemes to cheat on tests. Initial efforts failed, and they suffered the penalties. Undeterred, they kept trying. As they got better, it turned into “an implicit game” with the teacher. Could they outwit him?

Redfield put into place what he called “abnormally high testing security.” It didn’t work. After one class, in particular, when the scores came in “abnormally high,” Redfield was confused. The boys must be communicating, he concluded. But how? Suspicious, and being a longtime radio hobbyist, he came to the next test equipped with a “spectrum analyzer,” a device that can detect radio signals. Sure enough, although he could not specifically identify which boys were involved, some were operating a network of Morse code transmitters.

Rather than being disappointed or angry, Redfield was impressed with their cooperation and cunning. For many boys, he said, there would be no “catching up on algebra via dutiful studying at the kitchen table.” But some, at least, “had to know the answers and transmit them to other students.” And, frankly, “under what other circumstances would a teenage boy who checked out of math class 5 years ago learn Morse code?” Together, faced with risk and uncertainty, they found a way to a good grade via collective action, even if resorting to illicit means.

Redfield’s experience suggests several recommendations, especially if implemented early, before boys have become alienated and withdrawn: that team competition and “stakes beyond the individual be invited back into education,” that room be made for “spontaneity and male passion,” and that schools recognize more fully that, as Plutarch said, “children are not vessels to be filled, but torches to be lit.”

Unfortunately, creating a more vibrant classroom in this way tends to run counter to schools’ regulations and focus on narrow performance metrics. A cooperative administration is also required. According to Redfield, his relationship with the principal was “chummy,” and he was friendly with the other teachers, at least some of whom liked what he was doing. But his methods were a professional bridge too far. His contract, too predictably, was not renewed.

SLIDING INTO A pair of slip-on walking shoes might be the easiest thing you’ll do all day. This footwear was built for speed and convenience, making it an accessory that deserves valuable real estate by your door.

Slip-on walking shoes have a rocky history—older versions had a looser fit, raising the risk of instability and friction, especially with higher mileage. But newer designs use no-tie laces, sock liners, pull tabs, and collapsible heels to create a custom fit that rivals what you’ll get with traditional laces.

These shoes are an obvious choice if you have dexterity issues, but they're also just convenient for everyone looking for an easy pair of walking shoes. Slip-on walking shoes also offer more structure than other laceless shoe designs, which has earned them a fanbase with podiatrists. “They are typically a much better option than flip-flops or slides because they provide more stability and keep the foot properly secured,” says Melissa Lockwood, DPM, a podiatrist at Heartland Foot and Ankle Associates in Bloomington, Illinois.

Nicole Graf, DPM, founder of Lexington Podiatry and associate professor at the University of Kentucky, also calls slip-on walking shoes a “huge step up” from flip-flops and slides.

Slip-on walking shoes aren’t for everyone. The fit can still throw some people off, and some simply prefer the tighter feel that laces deliver. But if you want travel-friendly shoes you can toss on in a second, these are a solid choice. As people who value quality, supportive footwear, Men’s Health editors have put in plenty of miles in slip-on walking shoes. The below check all the boxes.

The On Cloud 6 is an excellent example of how good a slip-on shoe can be. You can get in and out of this sneaker with no issue thanks to its wide opening, but the shoe’s elastic “speed” laces and sockliner create a snug fit once you’re in. (Those elastic laces can be adjusted to find your perfect fit.)

The sneaker uses Zero-Gravity CloudTec, which is On’s signature lightweight foam with hollow pods below for a cushioned, yet stable, feel underfoot. (Just be aware that those pods are prone to trapping small pebbles and debris.) A built-in flexible plate, called a Speedboard, helps push you forward with each step. This shoe also comes in a waterproof option, if you’re into rainy strolls. With a wide range of color options and an unobnoxious design, you can pair these with just about anything.

It’s tough to find a quality shoe under $100 right now, let alone one from a big brand. But the Adidas Cloudfoam Cuxxion Sock Shoes have some colorways under $50, making them an ideal choice for budget shoppers who don’t want to mess with laces.

This shoe uses Adidas’ signature Cloudfoam technology at the midsole for a plush, cushioned feel. Its Adiwear outsole is durable and grippy enough for most needs, too. But the real standout in this shoe is its elastic sock upper. It molds to your foot to create a surprisingly snug feel, even for narrow feet. The elastic sock collar supports that secure feel, although it may be too tight for people with wider ankles. The engineered knit upper is highly breathable, lowering the odds you’ll end your walk with sweaty feet. We appreciate the oversized pull tabs in front and back, making the process of getting these on and off seamless.

#547. A New Chemistry Breakthrough Could Change How Scientists Build Quantum Computers

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

By modeling diamond defects as molecules, scientists can predict their behavior faster and identify promising building blocks for quantum computers and sensors.

Most materials being considered for quantum technology can hold a stable quantum state only when chilled to extreme temperatures. Diamond-based materials can work at room temperature, but their usefulness depends on defects in the crystal that have been difficult and computationally demanding to simulate.

At the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE) Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory (PPPL), staff research scientist Mark Martirez has developed a far faster way to calculate how those defects respond to light and microwave pulses. The approach has identified promising boron- and oxygen-based defects that could serve as qubits, the basic information-processing units of quantum computers and sensors. The research was recently published in ACS Central Science and has already attracted attention from other experts in the field.

Pure diamond consists entirely of carbon atoms, but storing and processing quantum information requires a disruption in that structure. A carbon atom might be missing from its usual position, an atom of another element might take its place, or both changes might occur together. The cluster of atoms closest to the defect forms the qubit.

Scientists use laser light and microwave pulses to read information stored in these qubits. Most quantum diamond research has focused on the nitrogen-vacancy defect, in which a nitrogen atom replaces a carbon atom and a nearby position normally occupied by another carbon atom is empty. When illuminated by a laser, this qubit emits red light if it is in a particular quantum state. Calculating how other defects respond to light and microwaves helps researchers assess whether they, too, could be useful.

Martirez simplifies those calculations by computationally “carving out” a cluster of just 16 to 30 atoms around a defect and considering it in isolation. This makes highly accurate tools that chemists have used for decades to study simple compounds available for studying defects inside a solid.

“Because we’re treating the qubits as molecules, the cost of calculating these defects is equivalent to the cost of just modeling a simple molecule,” Martirez said. “This method really opened the floodgates” in terms of how qubits can be assessed.

The calculations identified oxygen-based defects that shine blue and boron-based defects that emit ultraviolet light. These defects, known as color centers, could potentially serve different roles within a single quantum device.

“One could imagine, then, combining different qubits of different responses, so you can basically probe one qubit but not the other,” Martirez said.

Understanding the chemistry could also change how researchers conceive of a qubit’s structure. Diamond-based qubits are often treated as a physics problem, but Martirez’s work shows that chemistry offers another way to understand them. A fuller chemical understanding could let scientists design and build qubits for specific purposes, much as chemists design custom molecules, such as a new medication.

Martirez encouraged other chemists to bring their expertise into solid-state physics. “At this moment in time, there’s a great emphasis on building the first state-of-the-art qubit computer,” he said. “But it’s not just physicists who should be participating in this research. Chemists have decades of developed intuition about chemical systems that could be easily translated into understanding defects in solids as well.”

Practical quantum devices could eventually support sensors with greater sensitivity and accuracy than those currently available, along with computers capable of tackling highly complex problems far faster than today’s conventional supercomputers. Developing these future technologies is among the strategic national priorities PPPL works to advance.

For quantum chemistry, that progress could create a useful cycle. The field could help develop tools that allow it to “enrich itself” in the decades ahead, Martirez said. He expects a practical system containing multiple qubits to greatly improve the accuracy of quantum chemistry calculations.

“We could describe larger molecules, biomolecules, proteins, and everything like that in a more accurate way, and that could improve all the chemical-adjacent industries, such as pharmaceutical science and materials science. Even our fundamental understanding of molecular systems could be enriched.”

Reference: “A Chemist’s Guide to Solid-State Qubits: Diamond Color Centers as Embedded Vacancy-Centered Molecules” by John Mark P. Martirez, July 27, 2026, ACS Central Science. DOI: 10.1021/acscentsci.6c00548

“Through high school and college, running was just so fun; I had other things going on at all times: extracurriculars, stuff with friends on the weekends, football games, and clubs,” she says. “With professional running, I was so excited to go all in, but I realized very quickly I’m not one-dimensional. It’s really hard for me not to have other buckets to pour myself into.”

Back in the 2010s, pros were mostly all about running, and not as much about side hustles and other hobbies. “This is the way it has to be if I want to go all in: I have to live, train, breathe, eat, and sleep running,” McClain says. That all-or-nothing mindset led to repeated injuries and burnout, which forced McClain to part ways with her sponsor in 2018 and, at 26, move back to Phoenix with her parents.

Over the next few years, McClain took a full-time marketing job and got married. “It was a good reset for me,” McClain says. “It gave me a lot of perspective and helped me detach my identity from running. I’d been running competitively since I was 12, so I really didn’t know who I was or what I wanted outside of that. I needed self-discovery time to find out who Jess is when she’s not racing.”

Just when she started considering a return to running, the pandemic shut down racing. But then, in 2022, right before her 30th birthday, McClain raced the Mesa Marathon, her first 26.2 miles, on a lark, training only six weeks before the big day, and finishing in 2:33:34. She qualified for the 2024 Olympic Marathon Trials, where she placed fourth. At the 2024 New York City Marathon and 2025 Boston Marathon, she scooped up top 10 placements. And at the 2026 Boston Marathon, McClain broke the American course record with a time of 2:20:49.

She returned to her sponsor, Brooks. This go-round feels different, she says. “I’ve just had so much fun,” she says. “It took that reset and step back to rediscover why I love racing and getting the best out of myself, but I’m able to do everything else in life to some extent, too. It took a minute to find my recipe, but I did.”

Part of reclaiming her love of running came through music, something that’s been with her since her early days of running. “I remember having the clunky MP3 player and the old headphones that if you moved them a certain way, the music would scratch,” she says, laughing. “Now I am somebody who likes music and podcasts, definitely on my easy runs or solo runs.” Sometimes she’ll put her phone in the back pocket of her sports bra and play music aloud during track workouts for an extra boost.

“I’m a Swiftie for sure—I grew up with her, and we’re the same age, so it’s a nostalgic thing,” she says, “but I love the teenage angst in ‘90s alternative punk rock.” She’s recently been listening to Taking Back Sunday, My Chemical Romance, Matchbox Twenty, and Papa Roach as well.

Runner’s World asked McClain to guest-DJ a running playlist, and she’s put together quite the treat. Her playlist, of course, includes plenty of Taylor Swift, but also some blink-182, Hoodie Allen, and The Veronicas to give it a bit of edge. It may just be the boost you need as well!

#4349. Anthropic now lets Claude users jump out of plane without parachute

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Meet CLAUDE_CODE_EVEN_MORE_DANGEROUSLY_SKIP_PERMISSIONS

Anthropic, prone to pronouncements about model safety, has given developers using Claude Code the opportunity to throw its guardrails out the window. The company's latest Claude Code build (v2.1.311) supports an environmental variable designed to allow the AI agent to run the rm command, which deletes files, without asking for approval.

The designated environmental variable (a value developers set in their shell) is called:

CLAUDE_CODE_EVEN_MORE_DANGEROUSLY_SKIP_PERMISSIONS.

Daisy Hollman, a member of technical staff at Anthropic, announced the change in a social media post.

"Fun fact: --dangerously-skip-permissions doesn't actually approve EVERYTHING – we still block some obvious things like rm -rf ~," she wrote. "But today's release will let you disable this if you really want, by setting a new env var CLAUDE_CODE_EVEN_MORE_DANGEROUSLY_SKIP_PERMISSIONS=1"

Not everyone believed Hollman, perhaps due to the absurdity of the variable name or because the change isn't listed in the v2.1.311 change log. But she offered reassurance that it's real.

"Not a joke!" she responded. "I hope this helps, and I hope the variable name is memorable."

The name is easy to remember because it is very much like the aforementioned Claude Code flag --dangerously-skip-permissions. That command line flag enables bypassPermissions mode, which first surfaced in Claude Code v1.0.18, on June 9, 2025.

Claude Code has several permission modes: default, acceptEdits, plan, auto, dontAsk, and bypassPermissions. Auto mode, which nominally allows everything but runs background safety checks, became the default in August; bypassPermissions also nominally allows everything, without the safety checks.

But there are still exceptions that do not get automatically approved and require a permission prompt, such as rm and rmdir.

The new CLAUDE_CODE_EVEN_MORE_DANGEROUSLY_SKIP_PERMISSIONS environmental variable removes this last agentic restraint, though other things like managed settings may impose their own limitations.

It's perhaps worth noting that there are numerous accounts of AI agents issuing commands like rm -rf ~ that wipe hard drives or take other action that results in data loss. But as they say, you only live once. ®

#391. NASA Invites Media to Crew Completion of Yearlong Mars Simulation

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The four volunteers who lived and worked in a simulated NASA Mars mission for more than a year will exit their habitat on Saturday, Oct. 31, after 378 days of isolation in a facility at the agency’s Johnson Space Center in Houston. Health and performance data collected from the simulation can help NASA develop methods to counter human spaceflight risks for future Artemis and Moon Base missions.

Beginning at 3 p.m. EDT, NASA will provide live coverage of the volunteers’ departure from the Crew Health and Performance Exploration Analog (CHAPEA, pronounced cha-pea’-ah) mission habitat. The exit ceremony will be streamed on NASA Johnson’s YouTube and social accounts.

Johnson Center Director Vanessa Wyche and NASA astronaut Jack Hathaway will provide remarks. The CHAPEA crew members Ross Elder, Ellen Ellis, James Spicer, and Matthew Montgomery also will provide remarks.

Media are invited to watch the CHAPEA Mission 2 ceremony live at NASA Johnson on a screen outside the hangar holding the CHAPEA facility, after which a subject matter expert will be available in person to answer questions. Media will have the opportunity to capture the moment the crew exits the building, stepping outside for the first time in more than a year.

U.S. media interested in attending the activities in person, or media interested in speaking virtually with the CHAPEA Mission 2 crew members in the days following completion of their mission, must contact the NASA Johnson newsroom no later than 2 p.m., Friday, Oct. 30, at jsc-newsroom@mail.nasa.gov.

The second CHAPEA mission began on Oct. 19, 2025. Four crew members have lived and worked inside a 3D-printed 1,700-square-foot facility, simulating Mars mission operations, conducting simulated spacewalks, exercising, performing habitat maintenance, growing plants, and more, while operating under stressors that future deep space crews will likely experience. These included communication delays, resource limitations, and isolation.

Data from the mission can aid preparation for NASA’s lunar missions by optimizing food system design, efficient use of crew time, bandwidth on NASA’s Deep Space Network, and other aspects of living in deep space. In addition, data can help flight doctors and psychologists strengthen spaceflight medical care and behavioral support practices to ensure crew readiness and performance.
