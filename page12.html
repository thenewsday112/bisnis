#800. How green is Europe's urban public transport? A data-driven reality check

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Greener buses and cleaner fleets are reshaping public transport across Europe. But are cities moving fast enough to meet their zero-emission goals?

An electric plug icon on their windows makes them easy to spot in cities like Brussels and Madrid. Quietly navigating the streets, electric buses have become an increasingly common sight on Europe's streets in recent years.

Michela Le Pira, assistant professor of transportation at Italy's University of Catania, says removing tailpipe emissions — the gases and particles generated when a combustion vehicle is running — might be "the easiest strategy to improve public transport."

Most Europeans live in urban settings, and road transport harms air quality. In 2023, almost 300,000 deaths across the EU were caused by air pollution levels exceeding the World Health Organization's (WHO) recommended thresholds.

Of all fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentrations in European urban areas, about 14% come from road transport, including public, private and freight transport. Additionally, towns and cities account for nearly a quarter of the European Union's transport greenhouse gas emissions (GHG).

DW and the European Data Journalism Network evaluated more than a decade's worth of data from more than 100 reports and sources on public transit and the emissions it causes, and identified 148 sustainability commitments by local authorities and transport operators in 33 cities across Europe. The analysis focuses on motorized mass transit, leaving out bikes and pedestrians as well as taxis, shared vehicles and e-scooters.

The EU wants to become the first climate-neutral continent by 2050. A key part of that plan is cutting transport-related GHG emissions by 90% from 1990 levels.

As DW's analysis shows, Copenhagen is the only EU capital that has almost electrified its entire bus fleet — its vehicles do not have combustion engines and therefore do not produce tailpipe emissions. In cities like Rome, Lisbon and Nicosia, more than 90% of buses still run on diesel, and metros and trams account for less than 10% of their public transport fleet.

Although cars still dominate European streets, "electric buses are one way to reduce local air pollution," said Martin Baierl, data analyst with Transport & Environment, an advocacy non-profit for clean transport.

EU funds enabled many cities to increase their electric and alternative fuel bus fleets from 2021 on.

Electric buses comprise 20% of the total fleet in EU capitals and selected cities. Their number increased by 55% between 2021 and 2025 — equivalent to 1,234 more such vehicles on the road. The number of diesel-only buses has been declining since then, although they still outnumber those running on alternative fuel.

In addition to decarbonizing the bus fleet and helping to reduce air pollution, embracing electric models can help to lower noise and public transport operating costs. All the more so if the city produces its own electricity, explains Baierl. However, the outlay for e-buses is higher than for diesel models. In 20 out of the 31 cities with data to analyze, over 50% of the bus fleet is made up of traditional vehicles.

Brussels, for example, was an early adopter of moving away from traditional diesel-powered buses, switching to hybrids instead. Now the Belgian capital is in the process of electrifying its fleet, but at a slower pace. "It's not easy to know what the right technology will be in the end," Baierl said.

Another example is hydrogen-powered buses, which also produce zero emissions but are more costly to operate than their electric counterparts.

Transportation is the only major sector where greenhouse gas emissions remain above 1990 levels, despite the EU's ambition of a 90% reduction by 2050.

On average, public transport — including buses, trams and metros — is used for more than a quarter of trips in the 10 largest EU capitals, according to recent data from the non-profit group CDP (formerly the Carbon Disclosure Project). In Stockholm, the share is 49%, while in the smaller Slovenian capital of Ljubljana, it's 11%. DW analysis of transport sustainability pledges reveals that only four cities have explicitly committed to achieving zero emissions in buses or all public transport.

Yannis Papadimitriou lives 85 kilometers (about 53 miles) away from Athens, the EU capital with the highest air pollution mortality risk. He travels to work by commuter rail, a means of transport he'd like to see promoted more.

"For me it is a very conscious choice to use electric public transport, especially trains, which are the safest, most environmentally friendly and quickest way of transport."

Though trains, trams and metros run on electricity, there are more buses within city boundaries, and in many urban areas the majority still run on diesel.

In Northern Europe, 15 kilometers away from Copenhagen, Poul Kattler also used to commute to work. He is now retired and takes the bus more frequently with his grandchildren. Despite Copenhagen having an almost completely fossil-fuel-free public transport system (only one bus runs on biomethane), the metro expansion, which is responding to the city's growth, comes with its own set of concerns.

"The whole construction phase [of the new metro line] is extremely environmentally demanding," Kattler said. If the construction emissions are considered, "the CO2 balance would be very poor for the metro now."

But in the long run, the benefits of a new metro line in reducing CO2 emissions outweigh its construction-related emissions, Le Pira said.

Combustion or exhaust emissions are not the only way transport pollutes the air and the environment. Indirect emissions, like those coming from electricity generation or fuel production, add to transport's CO2 footprint. Brakes and wheels release non-exhaust particle matter when wagons or vehicles slow down.

Since 1992, buses and any vehicle running on fossil fuels in the EU have been subject to stricter emissions standards. Fine particle emissions from buses and heavy-duty vehicles had dropped by 92% in 2022 according to the European Environment Agency. Yet overall road transport non-exhaust emissions from tire wear have increased 58% in two decades, as cars have become heavier.

With a few exceptions, EU member states are not close to cutting greenhouse gas transport emissions by 90%, as envisioned by the EU's 2050 target.

Zero-emission public transport is one step toward decarbonizing transport in cities and meeting GHG reduction goals. The next challenge is to get people out of their cars.

"If we talk about sustainable mobility in general and not only clean public transport, the main problem is the modal shift from private cars to public transport," Le Pira said.

On average, private automobiles account for more than a third of transport in the largest EU capitals, according to CDP data.

Cheaper ticket prices for groups, like students, and disincentives for car use, such as low-emission zones, are two complementary ways of attracting more passengers while reducing private transport.

In her research work, Le Pira investigates how public transport can be more efficient and appealing for city dwellers, for example, by combining transport modes where people step off their bikes and onto a train. This kind of solution could be particularly relevant for small cities "where cars are still the main transport mode."

According to Baierl, places with good public transport draw people in. "Cities where not everybody has to use a car are just more livable," he said.

#790. Germany's women: Ena Mahmutovic makes her case to become Germany's next No.1 goalkeeper

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Germany's win against Australia saw Ena Mahmutovic excel in goal. The 22-year-old Bayern Munich goalkeeper is considered the successor to Ann-Katrin Berger and is making a name for herself.

With determination, pace, and four beautifully crafted goals, Germany's women won its friendly against Australia, thrilling not only the fans but also head coach Christian Wück.

"You can see how fired up the girls are, how well they're already playing together, and that we have an incredible amount of talent on the field and on the bench," said a delighted Wück after his team's well-deserved 4-2 victory.

Since there were several notable absences, including captain Giulia Gwinn and top scorer Lea Schüller, others had to step up and fill the gaps. And they did so with flying colors. Melissa Kössler made a strong case for herself as a striker by scoring her first two international goals. Center back Camilla Küver and Sjoeke Nüsken were also on the scoresheet.

Much like it was with the men's team during Jürgen Klopp's first international break as Germany head coach, the question as to who will emerge as the next number one goalkeeper for the women was also a hot topic.

The difference is that unlike Manuel Neuer, who has retired from international football, Ann-Katrin Berger is sticking around for at least one more tournament cycle.

However, with just three matches to go before the start of the playoffs in the National Women's Soccer League (NWSL), where she plays for Gotham FC, Berger is giving these two Germany friendlies a miss.

This means 22-year-old Bayern Munich goalkeeper Ena Mahmutovic, who is seen as a potential successor to Berger, took her her chance.

"Ena deserves to play the next match," Wück said ahead of the game. "We must not forget that she already kept a clean sheet in Slovenia (Germany's last World Cup qualifier). And I hope she can repeat that tomorrow."

Some will draw parallels to Jonas Urbig, who many see as a future number one for Germany's men. Both he and Mahmutovic were born in 2003 in the western state of North Rhine-Westphalia, and both are seen as up-and-coming stars for their club and country.

The difference is that while Urbig remains an understudy to Neuer at Bayern, Mahmutovic is already the first-choice keeper at her club, having won the starting job over Maria Luisa Grohs prior to the start of the season.

For Germany, though, Mahmutovic still has to bide her time, while there are already strong indications that Klopp intends to make Urbig his number one.

"I'm obviously delighted," Mahmutovic said ahead of the games.

"It's always something special, of course – especially playing against a team from another continent. I think testing ourselves against them is very helpful as we look ahead to the World Cup."

Bayern director Bianca Rech recently described Mahmutovic as "the future of the national team," calling on Wück not only to give her a callup, but also regular playing time.

Wück still sees Berger as slightly better than Mahmutovic, but he is also full of praise for the young keeper.

"Ena's performances have been very good," he said. "She will get her playing time."

The match against Slovenia was just Mahmutovic's second appearances for Germany. Her first, which came almost two years ago, was memorable for the wrong reason. In the dying minutes of a friendly in December 2024, she lost control of the ball after a back pass, allowing an Italian forward to score the winner in a 2-1 defeat for Germany. Still, Mahmutovic was included in Germany's squad for the 2025 Euros.

Had it been up to her father – who is from Montenegro – Mahmutovic would never have become a goalkeeper.

"He still says I would have been better as an outfield player because I'm good with both feet," she said.

That's a quality that evidently wasn't lost on Thomas Gerstner, when her coach at her then-club Duisburg, gave her her Women's Bundesliga debut in May 2020. With the team coping with several absences, the then-16-year-old, who had been on the bench as backup goalkeeper, came on as an outfield player.

"The coach brought me on during stoppage time," Mahmutovic recalled. "I got to make two sprints – that was my debut."

That, however, was a one-off, and nobody questions Mahmutovic's strengths as a goalkeeper.

"She is one of the most talented goalkeepers in the world," said Bayern coach Jose Barcala, noting that she is the kind of player who can decide a match.

Mahmutovic will be hoping she gets many more opportunities to do just that for Germany.

#783. Pope lung nodule: Could it be cancer?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The case of Pope Leo shows how small lung nodules are often found during routine health checks. Quick treatment is generally advised before nodules can grow to become larger life-threatening masses.

It often takes a chest X-ray or CT scan to spot small lung (or pulmonary) nodules, especially when a patient is asymptomatic.

At less than 3 centimeters (1.1 inches), lung nodules are less likely to press against your airway and cause any chest pain or shortness of breath or wheezing.

In the case of Pope Leo XIV, who told a gathering at the Vatican on October 11, 2026, doctors had found a nodule on his lung, it is unclear exactly how large that nodule is.

All we know (at time of writing on October 12, 2026) is that it was "detected at a very early stage," and that it was "well localized."

That suggests it has not spread or metastasized, although its full status is not publicly known. Doctors advised the pontiff to have his lung nodule removed.

Whether his was a chance finding — or whether the pope had presented with symptoms — we do not know.

But "incidental findings of lung nodule(s) in asymptomatic individuals" is considered an "increasingly common clinical dilemma," according to a 2019 paper published in the Annals of Thoracic Medicine.

This "dilemma" rests in part on the fact that biopsies — removing a small sample of tissue from the spot or nodule to determine whether it's malignant (cancerous) — are difficult to perform on small nodules.

The risks involved in a surgical diagnosis are considered "excessive compared to the relatively low prevalence of malignancy in the small nodules."

That's according to a 2017 paper published in the European Respiratory Review. And that may also be the dilemma that Pope Leo's doctors have faced.

Fortunately many — if not most — early-stage lung nodules are benign. It is also possible to have more than one nodule at a time, and they can all be benign.

It's said the pope will need a week to recover after his operation to remove the lung nodule. He is due to travel to Uruguay, Argentina and Peru between November 6-17.

Lung nodules are generally defined as a white spot or lesion on the lung. They have a rounded or irregular shape.

Radiologists refer to their "opacity" because, compared to the surrounding air-filled lung tissue, lung nodules appear as white or gray spots on a CT scan — that is, they have a certain density.

Lung nodules can be caused by old lung infections, foreign particles that are inhaled and get lodged in the lung, inflammation, scar tissue and indeed the onset of cancer.

At more than 3 centimeters, the nodule is defined as a mass. The bigger it gets, the greater the chance that it will obstruct your airway and the lung's ability to function.

Symptoms may start with chest pain, shortness of breath and wheezing. They may then progress to a chronic cough or coughing up of blood, fatigue, hoarseness, loss of appetite, unexplained weight loss and recurring respiratory infections like bronchitis or pneumonia.

It is hard to determine when lung nodules become life-threatening cancer. But one indicator of a lung nodule's malignancy is its size.

At 2 milimeters or smaller, the risk of cancer is very small and remains so up to a nodule size of about 6-7 milimeters.

The risk of cancer becomes 9%-10% at about 8 to 10 milimeters. And at larger than 1 centimeter, the risk of cancer is about 15%.

The density of a lung nodule is also a vital indicator of malignancy.

There are two main categories of lung nodule density: solid and subsolid. Subsolid nodules are further divided into pure ground-glass (nonsolid) and part-solid nodules.

According to a 2026 review article published in the New England Journal of Medicine, subsolid nodules are less prevalent than solid nodules, but they have "a higher likelihood of being malignant."

It is said that radiologists see lung nodules in about 30% of chest CT scans. But that number may be rising.

A Dutch study published in 2023 cited an increase in lung nodule detections from 38% of scans in 2008 to 50% in 2019. The same study said "the proportion of patients in whom significant new nodules (≥ 5 mm) were reported increased from 9% in 2010 to 17% in 2017."

However, this may also be a result of the development of better scanning technology in recent years and the use of artificial intelligence to spot tumors.

Bearing in mind that most lung nodules are benign, lung cancer does tend to start with a lesion or nodule. And lung cancer remains the most common type of cancer and the most common cause of cancer death in the world.

The World Health Organization's International Agency for Research on Cancer says that in 2024 almost 2.65 million people were diagnosed with lung cancer and more than 1.8 million people died from the disease.

#775. Germany: Labor Minister's resignation puts a spotlight on cracks within the government

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Following the sudden resignation of Bärbel Bas as Labor Minister and co-chair of the Social Democratic Party, accusations are flying in Chancellor Merz's coalition government.

The week kicked off with a bang: On Monday morning, Bärbel Bas announced her resignation as Labor Minister and as Co-Chair of the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD). "Following the death of my stepdaughter, I realized that I need time to come to terms with my grief," Bärbel Bas wrote in a personal statement to party members.

However, she also cited "the public attacks against me — unfortunately, including some from within the ranks of our coalition partner" as reasons for her resignation. In fact, the pressure on Bas increased significantly in recent days. The centrist coalition government is urgently awaiting key legislative proposals from the Ministry of Labor.

Before the summer recess in July, a commission had already presented comprehensive proposals for reforming Germany's statutory pension system. The coalition partners of SPD and the center-right Christian Democratic Union and Christian Social Union (CDU/CSU) wanted these proposals to be quickly turned into legislation. To date, however, no finalized drafts have been presented.

"The minister is no longer tenable. For months, she has been blocking the reforms our country so urgently needs," railed Christian von Stetten, head of the CDU/CSU parliamentary group's caucus representing medium-sized enterprises, said in reference to Bas in September.

In early October, such criticism was echoed by CDU Economy Minister Katherina Reiche, who demanded that the pension reform be passed by the end of the year. Bas had to submit the bill "promptly," Reiche said, although it was known at the time what a difficult personal situation her cabinet colleague was in at the time.

SPD co-leader, Finance Minister and Vice-Chancellor Lars Klingbeil reacted sharply on Monday, saying the political attacks against Bas had been "disgraceful."  Following a meeting of the SPD leadership, he told journalists in Berlin: "This poisons the political discourse, and — in the interest of preserving our political culture — I hope that everyone will take a step back and reflect on what has actually happened over the past few weeks." He called on all centrist politicians to be careful "that we don't destroy the good people in politics by attacking one another."

Such accusations carry significant weight at a time when the coalition is already mired in a deep crisis.

Adding to this is the new vacancy at the top of the SPD. Without Bas, Klingbeil will lead the SPD alone. A party convention will be scheduled for December or January to elect new leadership for the Social Democrats. It is not certain whether Klingbeil will run again. He received a very poor result during his last reelection as party leader.

Alexander Schweitzer is to succeed Bärbel Bas as Federal Minister of Labor. The former state premier of the state of Rhineland-Palatinate had previously served as that state's Minister of Labor and Social Affairs. Chancellor Friedrich Merz expressed confidence that Schweitzer's experience would ensure " good cooperation within the federal government," adding that he hoped that the pending legislative proposals "will now be quickly approved by the cabinet and can be introduced into the parliamentary process."

The chancellor refuted the accusations leveled by Bas, saying that he had never discussed personal matters with her. Friedrich Merz said he had explicitly advised Bas to take the time she needed to grieve.

Mistrust between the coalition partners has increased significantly, with SPD lawmakers speaking of an increasingly toxic political climate.

For months, the CDU and SPD have been grappling with low poll numbers, disappointing election results, and disputes over key reform projects. Plans for changes to the electricity tax, the introduction of a sugar tax, the pension and the labor market reform — hardly any initiative has been able to proceed without public controversy.

Adding to this is the crisis within the chancellor's party, the CDU, which saw a historic election defeat in the eastern state of Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania in September, when it even lost representation in a state parliament for the first time in history.

On Sunday and Monday, the party leadership met for a closed-door session to discuss the way forward.

They produced a four-page document which indicated a shift in priorities, putting more emphasis on social issues: affordable housing, lower electricity prices, better health care, and greater respect for citizens' lifetime achievements are mentioned in the paper. This is seen as a response to criticism that the CDU had been focusing too much on economic and regulatory issues.

The chancellor admitted that voters feel that politicians are not taking their hardships and fears of social decline seriously enough. "We have to work our way out of this slump — and get out of a crisis of confidence," Merz said. "You can't just reinvent a party overnight over a weekend, but we want to show that we're ready for change... and we have to be, given the election results."

The chancellor announced that he would lower the electricity tax for everyone — a promise he had made before the general election in 2025 but had not kept.

With the closed-door meeting paper, the CDU is also sending a signal to the SPD. Instead of a programmatic shift further to the right, it is emphasizing its traditional social values — a stance that resonates well within the Social Democrats.

The chancellor made an effort to emphasize his intention to continue with the current coalition government: "The personnel changes will not call into question the basis of the coalition agreement." He then repeated what he had recently said during a visit to Ukraine: "We need a stable federal government right now in order to remain capable of acting on the international stage."

Both CDU/CSU and SPD know that calling early elections could primarily benefit the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD), which is leading in the polls nationwide. So despite the deep rifts, Klingbeil and Merz currently see no alternative to their coalition.

#766. 125 years of Thomas Mann's 'Buddenbrooks'

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This over 1,000-page masterpiece offers a panoramic view of society in the 19th century and exposes the collapse of a merchant family: unrecognized sons, disillusioned daughters and the loss of middle-class ideals.

Who would have imagined it in the beginning? A 26-year-old man without a high school diploma, but greatly ambitious, writes a two-pound book with a melancholy undertone. "I'm not exactly delighted," his publisher initially responded. But he ended up publishing the novel anyway, more out of goodwill than conviction. And the rest is history.

For many, Thomas Mann's Buddenbrooks is considered THE German social novel. It is indeed a classic, and one that garnered the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929. Global fame, an international best-seller, and required reading for many German students. Anyone who mentions Thomas Mann thinks of Buddenbrooks in the same breath. Why is that?

Does the explanation lie in the autobiographical connections between the work and the author?

Thomas Mann was born in Lübeck to a merchant family, but his artistic aspirations did not fit in well in the middle-class mainstream. This struggle inspired Mann's first work. The inability of this sensitive bohemian to carry on the family's time-honored business after the death of his father was sniffed at and derided.

Reflecting the situation of his novel's character Hanno, Mann had a privileged life in a wealthy family, but one that was at risk of collapse. The aspiring novelist also had to withstand disappointing the expectations of others. He was forced to defend his dream of an artistic life. Yet as much as he was inspired by real life, he forcefully altered these biographical details to place his unique literary mark on his writing.

But does that really matter in light of people who choose not to make the distinction between an author's life and his work? Even though the name "Lübeck" does not appear in the novel, citizens there felt markedly insulted after the publication of Buddenbrooks in 1901.

That likely lies in the fact that the image the author portrayed was not one of an idyllic northern German city, but one of a place that had difficulty blending tradition with modernity.

This contradiction is also seen in each of the figures in the novel. Nothing is like it appears. Mann lays down this foundation of this skepticism from the very beginning, in a scene taking place on a very ordinary Thursday in October 1835. The Buddenbrook family comes together for their ordinary late lunch.

"There they all sat, on heavy, high-backed chairs, consuming good heavy food from good silver plates, drinking full-bodied wines and expressing their views freely on all subjects. When they began to talk shop, they slipped unconsciously more and more into dialect, and used the clumsy but comfortable idioms that seemed to embody to them the business efficiency and the easy well-being of their community. Sometimes they even used an over-drawn pronunciation by way of making fun of themselves and each other, and relished their clipped phrases and exaggerated vowels with the same heartiness they did their food."

There sits the leader of the family company, Johann Buddenbrook the Elder, and his wife, his son and counsel Jean, along with his family. The grandchildren are also there: Tony the dreamer, the quarrelsome rebel Christian, and Thomas, the eldest grandchild.

It is Thomas who is supposed to later take on the responsibility of continuing the family tradition, regardless of the cost. Mann weaves his narrative work starting with this main family, and then across several generations.

But appearances in the family are deceptive. The reader senses it's all headed toward a downfall. The new house is taken over from a family that was once as illustrious as the Buddenbrooks, but faced financial ruin and had to leave.

Johann and his family aim to make a better go of it. They make decisions on what is demanded economically, but fail — financially, interpersonally, and mentally.

Tony and Thomas end up falling in love with people not befitting their social standing, but do not have the courage to stand up to their parents. They enter marriages of convenience, yet it is these partnerships that paradoxically set disaster in motion.

From an early age, Thomas Mann was influenced by Schopenhauer's pessimistic notion of the will being a blind force at work in the world. Thus, there is likewise no escape for the Buddenbrooks. They speak of emotions and morality, yet it is money and economics that steer the ship. Failure, decline and even death seem to be the only viable outcome.

Mann also punctuates this hopelessness in a stylistic manner, by consistently repeating entire passages word for word, or evoking motifs such as the family registry or the estate in all their various forms.

The extent of this demise appears linguistically molded and exaggerated. The Buddenbrooks, this proud north German family of refinement, actually speaks the vernacular studded with empty French expressions. "Je, den Düwel ook, c'est la question, ma très chère demoiselle!"

The lowly servants, the courtesans, and the demi-monde: all of them speak the language of the street, raw and unpolished. In a virtuoso manner, Thomas Mann orchestrated this mass of voices into a panorama of society in the 19th century. The snobbishness on the part of the Buddenbrooks thus appears as a relic of a long lost time. It's a symbol of their inability to adapt to change and place their faith in progress.

Yet the fact that this extensive work spanning several generations does not get weighed down by aesthetic excess is purely due to Mann's brilliance as an author. He is able to bring every one of his numerous characters to life in words. Life that is full of complexity, contradictions and ambiguities. Some of these characters accompany the reader page for page; others appear only briefly.

And yet one begins to contemplate how someone like Grünlich could become a dodgy, unscrupulous husband and catches oneself wanting to chase the fearful Hanno out of the music conservatory and yell to him: "Go, throw yourself into life! You are not a failure." It is precisely this empathy that spurs one on to read through the night and prompts one to chuckle at all the intricate sentences and philosophical digressions.

#716. Do non-German nationals commit more crimes than Germans?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

People without German passports appear more frequently in German police crime statistics than German nationals. A new study explains why.

Every year, when the Federal Criminal Police Office publishes the police crime statistics, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) — a party that is, in part, right-wing extremist — pays particularly close attention. They are looking for crimes committed by refugees, migrants and foreigners living in Germany.

But studies by media scholars show that 95% of the AfD's press releases focus on non-German suspects, while crimes involving German suspects are almost completely ignored.

However, the common narrative of the AfD — that non-Germans are inherently much more violent — is simply not true.

In a new research paper, Susann Prätor, a sociologist, criminologist and professor at the Lower Saxony Police Academy, has taken a very close look at crime data in Germany, together with her colleagues from the University of Zurich and the Lower Saxony Criminological Research Institute.

She looked not only at officially reported crime — that is, all offenses that appeared in police crime statistics — but also at unreported crime: These are all offenses that are not reported or detected, and therefore do not appear in any official statistics but are identified through representative surveys.

"According to police crime statistics, people who do not have German citizenship commit crimes more frequently than German citizens. However, studies into unreported cases show that non-German nationals are twice as likely to be reported. Differences between the two groups are also evident in crimes that go unreported, but they are significantly less pronounced," Prätor told DW.

Crimes committed by foreign nationals are therefore twice as likely to appear in police crime statistics as crimes committed by Germans.

These statistics record all reported cases and do not specify whether the allegations prove to be true.

When the statistics are adjusted for the bias in the reporting of crimes, foreigners are no longer 2.6 times more likely to commit crimes than Germans, but only 1.7 times more likely. This figure is also consistent with the results of studies on unreported juvenile violent crime. According to these studies, foreign youths are about 1.6 times as likely to commit violent crimes as German youths.

Other factors affecting crime include age and gender. Young men make up a larger proportion of the foreign-born population, and young men are the group most prone to criminal behavior across all societies.

According to Prätor, "categories such as being young, male, poorly educated and having a history of violence are factors that increase the likelihood of violent behavior. A certain concept of masculinity also plays a role, wherever that may come from. But we do not see culture as a factor, contrary to the prejudice that masculinity and violence play a greater role in other cultures."

Prätor points to a survey on attitudes toward violence. According to the survey, 5% of German youths view violence positively. Among those without a German passport, the figure is 10%.

The criminologist has identified a crucial factor: "Family upbringing plays an important role in shaping attitudes toward violence, because these attitudes are formed in the home. If I experience violence in my own home, I will have a different relationship to violence than if I hadn't experienced it," said Prätor.

Germany is, however, on the right track, says Prätor, even if it doesn't always feel that way. "Fifty or sixty years ago, parental violence was much more common in child-rearing than it is today. Acts of violence committed by young people are also far less frequent than they were in the 1990s or the early 2000s."

Prätor emphasizes that it is important to bring objectivity to the debate. "Of every 100 German citizens, 0.2% commit violent crimes; among non-German nationals, the figure is 0.6%."

However, Prätor points out that "more than 99% of the people living here in Germany — whether of German or non-German origin — the overwhelming majority in this country do not commit any serious violent crimes at all."

#703. Nord Stream: Germany puts suspected Ukrainian pipeline saboteur on trial

A Ukrainian citizen faces court in Hamburg, accused of attacking the Nord Stream gas pipelines in 2022. It raises questions about Kyiv's possible role in the explosions. German support for Ukraine is also under scrutiny.

The aerial photos made headlines worldwide: On September 26, 2022, massive quantities of methane gas bubbled on the surface of the Baltic Sea near the island of Bornholm. Unknown people had blown up three of the four sections of the Nord Stream 1 and 2 gas pipelines from Russia to Germany.

The attack, which occurred seven months after Moscow launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine, meant the end of a mega energy project between Germany and Russia in which both sides had invested billions, and which secured affordable Russian gas for Germany. However, the flow of gas via Nord Stream 1 had been restricted or stopped several times by Russia since the Ukraine war began; Nord Stream 2 was not yet operational.

From October 14, Ukrainian citizen Serhii K. is facing trial before a court in the northern German city of Hamburg. He was arrested in Italy in August 2025 on a European arrest warrant and later extradited to Germany. This week he must answer for the attacks before the Hanseatic Higher Regional Court. He is accused of being an accomplice to a war crime by attacking civil energy infrastructure, disruption of public services, causing an explosion and destroying structures.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has repeatedly said that Ukraine was not involved in the 2022 attack on the Nord Stream underwater gas pipelines.

"Ukraine has never officially taken part in any operation related to the blowing up of Nord Stream," Zelenskyy told reporters again in August in response to a question about the ongoing investigation.

The fact that the accused is Ukrainian is especially politically sensitive. Germany continues to support the country after it was attacked by Russia: militarily, financially, with humanitarian aid and by taking in about 1.3 million refugees.

Critics of Germany's support for Ukraine, especially the right-wing extremist Alternative for Germany (AfD) and the left-wing populist Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) want to improve relations with Moscow, resume energy deliveries from Russia – including via a repaired Nord Stream pipeline — and stop supporting Ukraine. Sahra Wagenknecht, former leader of the BSW, told news agency Reuters in 2025 following Serhii K.'s arrest: "It is totally absurd that Germany spends many billions on aid for Ukraine but never demands an explanation from [President Volodymyr] Zelenskyy. Questions should also be raised about possible compensation."

Alice Weidel, co-leader of the AfD, has shared similar sentiments. In early 2026, she asserted that the German government must seek financial repayment from Ukraine. If the AfD came into power, she would demand Kyiv compensate Germany for blowing up the pipeline and pay for its repair. According to her, a country that does such things is "no friend of Germany." She has, however, failed to provide proof that Ukraine was behind the attack.

In an interview with public broadcaster Deutschlandfunk in 2023, German naval commander Göran Swistek pointed out that, given the technical, financial, and organizational effort involved, “there is likely a state-run structure behind it.” In his opinion, a group of amateurs could not have carried out the act.

However, he considered it unlikely that this state could be Ukraine, since Ukraine, after all, would not want to jeopardize German support. At the time, Swistek thought a false lead pointing to Russia was possible.

The German-Russian Nord Stream project had many opponents from the beginning, including Poland, the US, the Baltic countries and — especially since Russia's full-scale invasion — Ukraine. That is why investigators always ask: Who benefits from the explosion?

"By halting supplies or using the threat to do so as leverage, Russia exerted immense pressure on Germany at the time, and gas markets went haywire," he told DW. "The explosion thwarted Russian attempts to extort Germany, prices for natural gas sank. The AfD gained significant support in three state parliament elections held in eastern Germany during September. The party could potentially supply the state premier in Saxony-Anhalt. The AfD has also led nationwide polls for months, far ahead of the conservative CDU of Chancellor Friedrich Merz.

Wagenknech told a campaign event before the state parliament elections that the federal government was "Sinking billions upon billions into the black hole of Ukraine, while in Germany, in our schools, the children are no longer properly learning reading, writing and arithmetic." Kretschmer's position, however, contradicts the federal CDU party line and European Union sanctions policies.

I can only advise everyone, including people from the CDU, to keep their distance from such calls, because they are against German interests."

In any case, the outcome of the trial in Hamburg will undoubtedly influence the public debate about further support for Ukraine.

#693. Trump's diesel deal with Russia is unlikely to significantly lower global fuel prices despite expectations of rapid relief

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As the world's largest diesel exporter, the US benefits from high prices. At home, however, consumers are feeling the strain of rising living costs. Why Donald Trump's deal with Russia is unlikely to change that.

With or without a deal with Russia, diesel prices on global markets are unlikely to fall significantly anytime soon.

That is the assessment of experts interviewed by DW regarding the latest fuel-supply agreement between US President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin.

"The main reason for high diesel prices is the war in Iran and the simultaneous disruption of shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandeb Strait," said Samina Sultan, a foreign trade expert at the German Economic Institute (IW) in Cologne.

The situation has led to production outages and made fuel shipments "more expensive, less secure and more difficult," she said.

Trump's deal with Russia also has an additional geopolitical dimension, Sultan said: "It causes the wars in Ukraine and Iran to become completely intertwined."

Things are looking up, though. Roughly 13.5 million barrels of crude oil are once again flowing through the Strait of Hormuz a day, according to shipping and commodities analytics firm Kpler.

But for refined products such as diesel, volumes remain far lower at around 677,000 barrels per day, compared with 3.6 million barrels before the war.

Carsten Fritsch, a commodities analyst at Commerzbank, also attributes the price increases to the wars.

"If nothing changes in Ukraine and the Middle East, I see very few ways to resolve these supply bottlenecks," he said.

In a social media post on October 9, Trump announced that he would temporarily suspend some sanctions on Russia and begin importing Russian diesel.

He wrote "diesel prices for Americans and, indeed, the world, will be COMING DOWN, IN RECORD NUMBERS, AND FAST!" in a mix of lowercase and all caps.

Russia confirmed the arrangement. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov told Russian state news agency TASS on October 13 that supplying diesel fuel was "in the interests of Russia, the United States and global energy markets."

Diesel prices in the US and many EU countries have risen sharply over the past 12 months.

According to the US Energy Information Administration (EIA), prices in the US have climbed by about 60% and now stand at $6.30 per gallon, equivalent to $1.66 (or €1.49) per liter of diesel.

But even if Russia were able to flood global markets with cheap diesel, prices would not fall nearly as much as they recently rose, something all of the experts contacted by DW agree on.

Many refineries around the Gulf region have suffered disruptions or damage, while transporting fuel via shadow fleets and securing insurance remains costly, cumbersome and risky.

Another key shift is that Russia is no longer the world's largest diesel exporter. In fact, it is facing its own fuel shortages at home.

The US is now the world's biggest diesel exporter. In 2025, it exported an average of 1.26 million barrels of diesel per day, accounting for roughly 15.4% of global diesel exports.

At the same time, Russia fell to second place with a 9.5% share, and that was before major Ukrainian drone attacks on its refineries.

Besides the new US-Russia diesel agreement, Trump also recently floated the idea of US diesel export restrictions. But the idea was abandoned because it could lead to even higher diesel prices in the country, according to Samina Sultan.

"There simply is not enough diesel storage capacity in the United States," Sultan said. "Production would have to be reduced, and then prices would rise again."

If the US were to impose export restrictions on diesel, buyers in Latin America and the European Union would be hit particularly hard. Since Russia invaded Ukraine, EU countries have increasingly imported diesel from the US instead of Russia because of sanctions.

"An export ban merely redistributes the shortage; it does not solve it," said ING chief economist Carsten Brzeski.

"Global prices would continue to rise, while refineries would scale back production as margins shrink," he added.

Even if all of the diesel volumes announced by Trump were combined, totaling 35.8 million barrels, they would only cover about nine days of US domestic demand.

"I CANNOT OVERSTATE HOW MUCH OF A NOTHING BURGER THIS IS," Canadian oil market analyst Rory Johnston wrote in an all-caps post on X shortly after the announcement.

Russia would normally export almost 10 times that volume if its refinery fleet was not being targeted by Ukrainian drone attacks, he added.

Canada also supplies diesel to the United States. Roughly 80% of all US diesel imports come from its northern neighbor.

Given Trump's verbal attacks on Canada and the ongoing trade dispute, analysts view the prospect of Canada supplying significantly more diesel to the US as highly unlikely.
