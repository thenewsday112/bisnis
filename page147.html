#3358. Garmin’s New Screen-Free Fitness Band Takes Aim at WHOOP and Oura With No Subscription Required

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The CIRQA band tracks a broad sweep of daily health and fitness metrics, giving Garmin users a fuller view of life on and off the bike.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

Most fitness trackers come with a second price tag: a monthly fee for seeing the data they collect. Garmin’s $200 CIRQA Smart Band needs no subscription for core health, sleep, and activity tracking. That sets it apart from WHOOP’s and Oura’s paid membership models. Garmin’s pitch is straightforward: buy the band, wear it around the clock, and check the data in Garmin Connect without another recurring bill. It’s a refreshing proposition in a world lousy with subscriptions.

Very basically, the CIRQA is a screen-free fabric band designed for all-day wear on the wrist or upper arm; the concept is similar to the Whoop band. It tracks heart rate, sleep, HRV, stress, skin temperature, Pulse Ox, and Garmin’s Body Battery estimate. Garmin claims up to 10 days of battery life. The band supports more than 80 activity types, uses your phone for GPS, and can broadcast heart rate to a cycling computer. Garmin pitches the lack of a screen as a feature for riders who want the data but don’t want to wear a smartwatch.

The single button handles one favorite activity, which you set in Garmin Connect. Press it once to start recording and again to stop; the band vibrates and flashes an LED to confirm. You can start other activities in the app, and CIRQA can automatically detect some sessions from movement and exertion data. That keeps the band clean and simple, with setup and seeing the details handled through the phone.

I’ve tracked rides with cycling computers since the days of the fabled Garmin Edge 500. Over the years, I’ve sent those files through Strava, TrainingPeaks, and a rotating cast of other platforms. Plenty of them can tell me what I did on the bike. Garmin’s appeal, and maybe its best selling point, is the Garmin Connect app.

With a Garmin cycling computer, your ride data already lives in Garmin Connect. Add the CIRQA, or another Garmin wearable, and the app puts that training alongside sleep, stress, HRV, and other health metrics. The CIRQA’s unique appeal isn’t any one metric the band collects. It’s that those metrics feed into the same ecosystem as your ride data, giving you a more complete picture of your training in the full context of your day.

That can be useful if you’re trying to make better training decisions yourself, or if you work with a coach who wants more context than your power files and weekly mileage can provide. A hard workout looks different after a great night’s sleep than it does after a week of poor sleep and high stress. Putting that data in one place makes it easier to see when a planned session fits or when it's time to adjust the plan.

Wearable device options are more plentiful than ever, and the CIRQA won’t be the right pick for everyone. Its clearest audience is the rider who already uses Garmin Connect and wants health and recovery data alongside cycling workouts. If your bike computer already uploads rides there, the CIRQA adds context about what happens between them. That connection is the main reason to choose it.

It should also appeal to people who want to track sleep, stress, and activity without taking on another subscription. You don’t need a paid membership for CIRQA’s core tracking, which makes it a good option for riders who want to monitor their daily data without another recurring bill.

The other clear fit is someone who wants less screen time on their wrist. The CIRQA has no display or smartwatch notifications. Maybe you wear an analog watch and want to keep it that way; maybe you’re tired of your smartwatch and want a wearable that quietly collects data for you to check later. If you want live metrics and notifications or even the time at a glance, the CIRQA probably isn’t for you.

Sleep tracking and HRV status worked seamlessly for me. I don’t find much value in comparing one wearable’s numbers directly with another’s. It’s more important for the numbers to be consistent and CIRQA’s were. Its readings tracked with how I felt from one day to the next, and also mirrored the trend lines of numbers from other smartwatches I’ve been testing, which made them useful as context rather than scores I had to chase.

Having sleep, HRV, and stress inside Garmin Connect alongside my ride data made those numbers actionable for me. I’m self-coached, so I’m deciding every day whether to do the workout on my calendar, go easier, or take a day off. Garmin Connect’s ability to bring my on-bike training and the rest of my day together in one place felt like the most useful thing about the CIRQA band.

The band has a single button, an underrated feature, and maybe a Band-Aid, too. I liked being able to start and stop an activity manually instead of hoping the band’s auto-detection caught it, especially for commutes, hikes, and runs. In general, auto-detection did a good job with walks, runs, hikes, gym workouts, and swims. My twice-a-day e-bike daycare trip was a different story: CIRQA picked it up about half the time. If it were more consistent on those rides, the button would matter less. But even if automatic activity tracking worked perfectly, having a manual stop/start button feels useful.

As someone trying to avoid another subscription, I see CIRQA as Garmin’s best alternative to WHOOP. There’s meaningful overlap in the day-to-day metrics, like sleep and HRV. WHOOP packages those metrics more neatly into an easy-to-read Strain/Recovery dashboard. CIRQA’s advantage is the Garmin Connect app, which combines ride data with all the metrics CIRQA tracks, which makes it an easy sell for existing Garmin users. But its lack of a subscription makes it easy to recommend to everyone else looking for a wearable device.

#1523. Inside the Lives of Lady Preppers

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Women's Health may earn commission from the links on this page, but we only feature products we believe in. Why Trust Us?

They’re stockpiling food, packing bugout bags, and learning survival skills—but not necessarily because they think the world is ending.

THERE’S A WELL-KNOWN maxim among preppers—particularly the small but growing subset of female preppers—that you prep for Tuesday, not doomsday. On the day I interview Krystal C., a 37-year-old military vet turned prepping influencer in Utah, it is a Monday, and I am not prepared for that day or the one after it. That morning, I left my phone at home as I dropped my daughter off at daycare, and I haven’t seen my keys—both car and home—in weeks. (I’ve been stealing my husband’s set or simply going without.) Unlike me, Krystal has been prepping since childhood.

She learned prepping from her grandparents, although they didn’t use that term. “We called it being responsible and deliberate living,” Krystal says. It’s a sentiment she shares with Lisa Bedford, author of the Survival Mom book and blog and de facto founding mother of the female prepper movement. “Even to this day, 17 years later, I don’t really think of it as prepping,” the 50-something content creator says. “I really think of it as just taking care of my family and being proactive.”

Both women started seriously prepping around 2009, roughly two years before the term prepper entered mainstream consciousness. In 2011, Doomsday Preppers debuted on the National Geographic channel, and that image of preppers—predominantly white men in paramilitary garb, determined to protect their heavily armed bunkers at all costs—has endured. I spoke with 10 women for this story who all told me that while that type of prepper does exist, a more practical, family-focused approach to prepping has emerged in the 15 years since, and it’s a movement led by everyday women—often the mom next door.

Even though prepping is in Krystal’s DNA, she, like many others, got more serious about it after some kind of disaster. For her and Lisa, it was the Great Recession in 2008. For Zani Sunshine, a 45-year-old homesteading prepper—and one of the few highly visible Black female influencers in the prepping community—it was Hurricane Katrina and then the 2016 presidential election. Marcy R.,* another Black female prepper, started cramming her New York apartment with extra food and water after witnessing 9/11. Sue M., a 66-year-old entertainment paralegal in Los Angeles, started prepping due to the COVID-19 pandemic. For other women, like Ambrosia Luzius, a 41-year-old emergency management professional, it was not a once-in-a-lifetime disaster but the ordinary miracle of motherhood that pushed her into prepping.

Amy Edelman, 67, does not consider herself a prepper, but she did coauthor The Emergency Playbook, a how-to guide to everyday preparedness, and has a “zombie closet in the basement” filled with canned goods and vegetarian MREs (short for “meals, ready-to-eat,” originally developed by the U.S. military). When Superstorm Sandy knocked out the power in Amy’s house for a week, she decided to get a few more flashlights and stock some water in her basement. When inflation spiked the price of tomatoes, she went to her local garden center and bought what she needed to grow her own. She has a bugout bag (the colloquial term for the pack of essentials you prepare in order to flee your home) and knows how much it should weigh based on her body weight (15 to 20 percent). But she hasn’t road-tested it by walking three times around the park yet, which is something she recommends in her book.

Despite Amy’s aversion to the “prepper” title, she shares the same goal as pretty much all the other women I interviewed: “to change the reputation around prepping to something that’s more lifestyle-y.” She wants readers, particularly women, to realize that “you can do all this without losing your sense of normalcy.”

But here’s the thing: Female preppers already know that. They reject the notion that they are doing anything unusual. Instead, they see themselves as guided by ancestral common sense, rediscovering one lost art at a time, and their lives are richer—in some cases, literally—for it. The more I talked to these women, the more I realized…they may be right.

Prepping is defined as “the management of stockpiled household items in anticipation of market disruption,” according to a study published in the journal Sociology. In other words, it’s on us: Women are the primary shoppers for the vast majority (78 percent) of U.S. households, per Numerator, a consumer data and market research firm; and moms, in particular, do 80 percent of grocery shopping in households where a spouse or partner is present, according to Pew Research Center. They also take on more of the mental load of household management.

Women tend to be the organizers of diaper bags, the meal preppers, and the schedulers of doctor appointments, Krystal says: “This is all very natural. Women have always been caregivers and nurturers, and that sometimes gets swept to the side.” In fact, it’s the traits least associated with the “typical prepper” lifestyle that Krystal and other female preppers say make women arguably better suited to the task of prepping than camo-clad men doing Red Dawn cosplay. Male preppers “don’t have the same pressures and responsibilities of caring for everyone on a day-to-day micro level,” Zani, the homesteading prepper, says. “They may be the breadwinners, but they’re not usually the ones doing the family management.”

Historically, male preppers have been more visible for their focus on tactical preparedness, like combat and survivalist skills, but Lisa has been an early adopter of what’s now known as “practical preparedness.” This is essentially an extended version of household management that revolves around water and long-term food storage (which includes gardening, canning, and, on occasion, freeze-drying), first-aid supplies, and “everyday” activities, like practicing situational awareness. Lisa started out the way most women preppers do: buying extra shelf-stable groceries for her family. Her husband was in the construction industry during the housing crisis, and she wanted to make sure that if his business went under, their kids would still be able to eat.

The threat of food insecurity also drove Ambrosia to start prepping as a new mom. She’d just finished grad school but, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, had difficulty finding a job. So she and her family had to go on WIC for a few months. Wanting to take back control of caring for her family, she told her husband that she wanted to use 20 percent of their weekly food budget for long-term food storage. “When we did that, it changed so much for us,” she says. “Here we were a family that was struggling. We were going to food pantries, and now I’m giving away food to our neighbors.”

This approach continues to serve Ambrosia and her family well as they’ve been less affected by the supply chain issues, rising inflation, and food shortages of recent years. “If I buy flour today, it is X price. There’s no arguing that tomorrow is X plus one, and next year is X plus two. So if I buy it today and my brain is thinking long-term, I’m saving myself money today, but I’m saving future-me money, too,” Ambrosia says. “That’s the way I’ve told people like, ‘Hey, I’m not crazy.’ I’m not preparing for a shit-hits-the-fan [SHTF] situation. I’m just preparing for the regular future with hiccups.”

Black female preppers, however, are not necessarily prepping only for the occasional layoff. They’re focused on societal collapse, too, Zani says. Prepping is new to her majority Black, female, and urban audience (she currently has more than half a million Instagram followers) because “there’s a lot of things that we have to deal with and think about that white people do not.” When you’re just trying to survive amid the systemic racism and oppression of today, it’s difficult to think about, much less plan for, tomorrow. Still, Zani’s advice to her followers sits squarely in the practical preparedness realm. “I try to press upon them that this is not just for the end of the world,” she says, but that prepping for things like job loss and natural disasters can be helpful for anyone no matter how they feel about what’s going on. “If you do lose your job and you have food in your house, that’s going to help you get through it better.”

Long associated with Second Amendment–sponsored protection (Krystal refers to ammunition as “Freedom Seeds” in one Prepping 101 video), prepping—and most preppers—have been assumed to be politically conservative. Indeed, many are, but over the past 10 years Zani has seen a political shift among preppers. In fact, all the women I spoke to either identified as being on the liberal side of the spectrum or were careful not to share their political views. (For all of the prepping content creators, aside from Zani, it’s bad for business.)

Sue even sees prepping as a way to reach across the aisle. “There’s so much division and hate right now in the world, it’s nice to find common ground with people,” she says. “A lot of these prepper people, I suspect, have very different political views than I do, but that’s not what we talk about.”

Incidentally, the right to bear arms was the one political view shared by all of the women I spoke with, except for Amy, who actually advocates against gun ownership in her book. Another quality preppers tend to have in common is a general distrust of the government, Krystal says. They don’t believe that the government is coming to save them when disaster strikes—or worse, they’re worried that government agencies will seize their preps. “I’ve been doing this on social media for so long, I’ll see people that are like, ‘I’m prepping because Biden ran.’ ‘I’m prepping because Trump ran,’ ” she says. “I remain very apolitical.… If you’re worrying about a certain scenario, prep for it.”

Similarly, Des, a 30-something prepping content creator and single mom in the Northern California suburbs, doesn’t “share too much” of her political views online because the way she sees it, a power outage or flash flood doesn’t care who you voted for. Disaster doesn’t discriminate; it impacts everyone.

But a SHTF situation wouldn’t impact everyone equally. On average, Black Americans receive less Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) relief funding than white Americans, and their communities take longer to recover in the wake of natural disasters. That’s why Zani isn’t afraid to ruffle a few (a lot of) feathers with her political views; the stakes are too high. “As a Black woman, I can’t stay silent, especially since I’m just waking up my audience and this is something that might heavily affect them,” she says. Zani notes that her audience has not had the privilege to prepare because they’re forced to spend the majority of their time, energy, and money simply trying to survive systemic racism and oppression. “That’s why [Black] people aren’t thinking about job loss, natural disasters. It’s just, ‘Where is this country going?’ ”

The urge to prep stems from uncertainty about the future. For many women, including myself, that uncertainty only amplifies when they become moms. Morgan Rogue, 42, a prepping content creator and mom of three in the Texas suburbs, sums it up perfectly: “I’m going to be prepared for anything so they don’t have to go through anything.”

But while many moms are interested in prepping (and have the Costco receipts to prove it), Krystal often hears from these followers that they don’t know how to get started. She tells them, “Your diaper bag is already better than some guys’ bugout bags.” Then she encourages them to take that approach and apply it to other areas in their lives. That, in essence, is practical prepping and why women, particularly mothers, are natural-born preppers.

Morgan has involved her children with prepping “since birth” simply by doing everyday prepper activities, such as packing bugout bags, around them. Now that her kids are older, she gamifies preparedness drills, like seeing who can get out of the house with their bugout bag the fastest.

Des also uses games to pass down preparedness skills (like situational awareness) to her 7-year-old daughter. Her goal is to inspire and empower her daughter, not scare her, so she incorporates things like a prepper version of “I Spy” where she asks her daughter to spot anything that might look out of place when they’re in public. Other times, she encourages her daughter to help her figure out the safest place to park. The goal is to encourage kids to be more vigilant in everyday situations, Des says.

Ambrosia also weaves in preparedness lessons and uses her garden to help teach her 5-year-old daughter where food comes from, how to grow it, and how to prepare it to last longer. She also dresses her daughter in boys’ clothes because they’re more “durable” and “thicker” and makes sure she’s always wearing practical shoes. She teaches her children the importance of having an exit plan in place and a predetermined meeting spot to reconvene if there’s an emergency.

Former New York City resident Marcy now lives “on a family compound” in South Carolina with her four adult children, their spouses, and her grandchildren. Being a mother has always impacted how she’s prepped, but becoming a grandmother has recently changed her approach because she makes sure to stock extra diapers, baby formula, and infant medication. “I tried to get my children to join in, but they said I have enough food for everybody,” she says. “Sometimes, it’s in one ear, out the other.”

Meanwhile, Sue’s 31-year-old son is “not on board at all” with her prepping. “He doesn’t have the worrywart mentality,” she says, “so my son thinks I’m being ridiculous.” Even though he doesn’t live with his parents, Sue and her husband (who has gotten on board) make sure to prepare with him in mind.

“Most preppers feel lonely and isolated in their prepping because our families want nothing to do with it,” says Zani. For these preppers, community—either in person or online—can be a lifeline.

When Des started prepping in 2017, she looked for advice and, she hoped, community on social media. What she found left her feeling “a bit discouraged.” At the time, Des says, the online prepping community was “extremely male-dominated” and often tinged by that trademark “gloom and doom.” But the landscape is changing.

In 2022, a female-focused subreddit r/TwoXPreppers branched off from r/preppers, an ostensibly coed prepping platform where the majority of posts were nonetheless driven by male-centric concerns. Despite its biologically based name, the forum provides a trans-inclusive and pro-LGBTQIA “place where women can talk about prepping for their specific needs.” A month after its creation, it had 7,500 members while the original preppers subreddit boasted 292,000. Now, r/TwoXPreppers has grown in influence—up to 33,000 weekly visitors—while r/preppers has actually shrunk to 137,000.

But prepping platforms aren’t limited to Reddit. In April 2026, a small group of female preppers created the “Women Only Survival Preppers” group on Facebook. In just a few short months, more than 13,000 women (including myself) have joined. “We absolutely need a safe space, and I really think that this group is fostering that,” says Storm, a 57-year-old prepper in Wabash Valley, Indiana, who joined in May.

Given Ambrosia’s background in emergency management, she’d also joined prepper Facebook groups over the years. But she was especially drawn to the women-only group because it reminded her of her grandmother, who’d tried to teach her preparedness skills as a child. Ambrosia now admits she didn’t really listen. “I’m trying to gather all that knowledge, and that’s why I started networking with other women in prepping,” she says. She sees herself as part of this “underbelly movement” of women rediscovering—and, just as important, sharing—forgotten knowledge passed down by the women who came before them. “We’re kind of just relearning it together,” she says. “Women are unique [in that we] don’t learn something to keep it and to knowledge-hoard. I’m learning something, and that day or the next day, I’m teaching another woman.”

Sue joined the group for similar reasons: “I just thought I would really like to get to know other women who are doing this,” she says, adding that she admires how “self-sufficient” some of the other women are. When we speak, she’s been a member of the Facebook group for less than two weeks but has already learned a lot from other women’s posts, particularly about gardening and raising chickens.

Lest you think “Women Only Survival Preppers” is a virtual Lilith Fair at the end of the world, I should remind you that it is, after all, still a Facebook group, where the ordinary pitfalls of human interaction still apply. There have been controversies around LGBTQ issues, debates around abortion and vaccines; in July, one woman was even banned for posting the address of another (a.k.a. doxxing). Still, all the women I interviewed agreed that it was better than the alternative: dealing with men.

For Marcy, the women in the female prepper Facebook groups are more mature and confident and have more common sense than men in similar groups who seem to focus on only one thing. “Everything was about weapons, weapons, weapons,” she says. While firearms are part of prepping for Marcy, having a stable food supply, first-aid skills, and a strong community dynamic is more her focus.

In fact, Marcy left three coed prepper groups “because the men were out of control,” she says. She often read “very degrading and disgusting” comments, and even though they weren’t directed at her, she still felt attacked. Storm and Sue confirm that they’ve also seen no shortage of misogynistic comments in other coed online prepper spaces. Krystal doesn’t even use her last name due to threats she’s received online, and other content creators also talk about pushback from male followers who say that female preppers don’t know what they’re talking about or that they’re not “real” preppers.

The women-only groups also provide a safe space for female preppers to discuss one of the biggest threats to them in SHTF scenarios: sexual violence. Storm doesn’t mince words when she talks about the threat men pose to women. “Forget when shit hits the fan. Women are raped by men every day,” she says. “Do I think it’s going to get worse? Absolutely.” The threat of men is why Sue, after “being very anti-gun for many years,” recently bought a pistol and started taking shooting lessons. “I still don’t think anyone needs a semiautomatic weapon, but…I also think, unfortunately, that’s a skill [gun familiarity] you should have,” Sue says. “[I] hope I never have to use it, but I don’t want to be in a position where I wished I had learned.”

While all of the women I spoke with found their positive prepping community online, Ambrosia is one of a select few with an in-person prepper group, and she’s also encouraged all of her friends to prep. Krystal has friends who come over with the food they’ve grown, and they’ll can together, and also run different drills. “Community is so important because if you want to be completely self-reliant, you’re going to end up relying on others,” says Krystal. “Lone wolves do not survive long.”

Morgan has also held preparedness classes in her community, although it hasn’t been the best place to meet fellow female preppers since the majority of attendees have been men. She’s thinking about holding a women-only event because, she hypothesizes, women may be more comfortable around other women.

The preppers report that prepping benefits their mental health. They used words like safe, secure, grounded, optimistic, and peace of mind to describe the impact it’s had on their lives. Zani, who has had depression since she was 13 (for which she currently takes medication), says she used to have “extreme anxiety all of the time” when she was working a traditional full-time job and living in a city. She was burnt out from trying to balance work responsibilities, a household budget, and child-rearing duties. Prepping and homesteading have allowed her to depend less on money, so she “can have a freer lifestyle,” she says. “I very, very rarely have any anxiety whatsoever now.”

Prepping may be one of the only actions that both comes from and alleviates anxiety. “Fear leads us to do some pretty productive things,” says Lisa, likening prepping to getting an annual mammogram. “Not many women like going in for a breast exam—having those smooshed between metal plates doesn’t feel good—but there’s that little, tiny bit of risk, that little, tiny bit of fear that makes me think, Maybe I should do that.”

That was the case for Morgan, who says prepping cured her doomscrolling habit. Now, she uses that time to learn an off-grid cooking method, tend her garden, or simply go for a walk. It’s made a difference for Amy too: “All I read these days is how stressed-out people are,” she says. “But if you do something every day to prepare, you don’t have to worry about it.” In other words, if you think of prepping as a last-ditch effort to stave off the apocalypse, you’re going to live your life like that. If you think of it as taking everyday action to adapt to a changing world, you’re going to live your life like that.

Still, a perspective change doesn’t promise an instant fix. “I am incredibly depressed about what’s happening in this country,” Sue says. “I don’t drink; I don’t do drugs. As a millennial, she’s witnessed so many “once-in-a-lifetime” events—terrorist attacks, multiple wars, a Great Recession, a global pandemic, and dozens of climate change–related natural disasters—that they’ve become normal. “When people say, ‘You’re doing all of this, and nothing’s going to happen,’ I’m like, ‘Whoa, where have you been the last 30 years?’ ” Krystal says. “It’s been a rough go.” As a fellow millennial, I have to agree.

To customize my approach to prepping, Lisa told me to begin tracking what I was already eating and how many everyday supplies (toilet paper, laundry detergent, etc.) I used for two to three weeks. I’m nowhere near close to having years’ worth of prepped food like some women preppers, or even having a casual zombie apocalypse closet like Amy, but my meager preps have already come in handy. One weekday night, I noticed my husband opening up a can of black beans from my small but mighty prepping pantry. (I knew it was one of these cans because I’d taken to writing the expiration date on the top with a black Sharpie.) “Did you take this from my stash?” I asked him.

“Yeah, I forgot to get black beans at the store,” he admitted reluctantly. “So I’m really glad we had this extra can, in case of an emergency.” He was mocking me, but still, I think I made my point.

And as the women preppers promised, I even started to feel a small yet significant shift in my mental health. The situation was scary, especially with a young child at home, but I felt—for lack of a better word—prepared.

And Krystal was right about diaper bags being underrated proof of women’s everyday preparedness. My car keys were in there after all.

For most of our relationship, nobody paid us a second look. Now, a few years into August’s new life, holding hands is a political statement. I wouldn’t have it any other way.

A few weeks ago, after we got home from work and the Atlanta weather had cooled off, my husband, August, and I were out for a walk, as couples have done since time immemorial. As each of us absentmindedly reached out for the other’s hand, we shared a subtle nod with two well-dressed men who passed us on the opposite side of the path. I thought it was nothing more than an unspoken, gentlemanly, “How d’you do?” But August interpreted it as something else.

“It’s weird that for the first 15 years of our relationship, nobody paid any attention to whether or not we were holding hands,” August said. “Now it’s a political statement.”

What had changed? Three or four years ago—it’s hard to pin down the exact date—August wasn’t my husband. They were my wife.

August and I first met in Asheville, North Carolina, in 2006, when they were a 22-year-old, female-presenting barista. I was a few years older, working as a dishwasher. One of my earliest memories of August, before I even knew their name, occurred at the coffee shop we both frequented. I had a mohawk and mutton chops at the time and wore a heavy, utilitarian kilt. August was petite with a cute smile and a mane of curly brown hair. We were hanging out with mutual friends, but when August came to sit down at our table, all the seats were occupied. I very gentlemanly offered them my chair, but to my surprise, they simply took a seat on my lap. That, as they say, was that.

About a year later, we announced that we were running off to San Jose so August could attend grad school for library science. We should’ve known the plan would fail for a few reasons: Neither of us had ever been to San Jose, August had never even been on a plane before, and the Great Recession had just begun a few months prior. We couch-surfed with friends in Berkeley until the companies we worked for went bankrupt within a few weeks of each other. Still, we powered through to San Jose, moving into what could be best described as a hovel with a sink and a shower. We filled out applications for weeks before we realized there were no jobs to be had.

Every couple should start their relationship with an utter failure to make sure they’re both in it for the long haul. We had previously purchased plane tickets to visit August’s parents in Tennessee and asked ourselves, What if we just go and don’t come back to California? If we could survive this disaster, we thought, then we can survive anything. Maybe we needed something to look forward to, but around this time, we started planning our wedding. Neither of us ever popped the question. We both just knew.

Moving in with our parents or back to Asheville, however, felt like too much of a setback. August’s brother helped us find jobs and a place to stay where he lived, in Greensboro, North Carolina. Sure enough, a month later, we were living with a roommate and working service-industry jobs.

We got married in 2008 at my parent’s farmhouse in middle-of-nowhere Kentucky. The wedding theme was “garden party at which we get married.” My father had an Amish butcher roast a goat for the occasion, and my cousins made a vat of our secret-family-recipe German potato salad. We had cupcakes instead of wedding cake, before that idea was cool and then not cool. There was so much homemade wine and moonshine that two people passed out in the living room before all was said and done.

Less than a year later, August was accepted to another graduate program, this time in Iowa. Thankfully, it worked out a lot better. August finally began their career as an academic librarian after getting a job at the University of Georgia and later Georgia Tech.

I don’t remember the first time they mentioned wanting to transition. Like our decision to marry, it seemed like something that was going to happen eventually. Back when we lived in Asheville, August would do this thing they called Mustache Thursday. Every week, on that day, they would wear an obviously fake handlebar mustache to work. People did that sort of thing in mid-aughts Asheville, but for August, it hinted at what they were feeling inside themself.

In 2023, we settled into a comfortable middle-class existence in Atlanta. August realized they wanted to talk to the doctor about microdosing testosterone. What I remember most about their first shot is the immediate pep in their step and the way they described the feeling: “It was like when an old radio dial is stuck between two stations. You tap it, and one comes in clear.” They almost immediately went from a microdose to a regular dose.

August has a big family, so they sent out a group email to inform everyone. We knew my sister would be cool, so we just called her. I told my parents in person, since I was going up for a visit anyway. I was probably a bit dramatic with them; I don’t have a lot of experience delivering Big News. They were sitting on the couch watching Jeopardy! while I was doing something in the kitchen. I asked them to turn off the television because I had something important to share.

Everyone was cool or at least feigned coolness; I expected some muttering behind the scenes. But we made it pretty clear that we weren’t interested in feedback. The Big News was not phrased as a question. There was no “What do you think?” or “How do you feel?” Only: “This is what’s happening.” We didn’t want to create the impression that anyone else got a vote on our relationship or August’s gender.

The top surgery was a different story—not that people were opposed. But no one, myself included, was thrilled about the idea of August getting major surgery. If anyone in our families announced they were having an elective procedure with a long recovery time, we’d all probably have about the same supportive but nervous reaction.

To prepare, August and I watched a lot of the plastic-surgery show Botched, read up on top-surgery recovery, and talked to friends who’d had it done. My day job is at a lactation clinic, so I already knew a lot more about breast and chest surgery than most men. One thing I didn’t know about top surgery, however, was that they ask you to bring in images of what you want your chest to look like. August went with Bill Skarsgård; we agreed that Alexander would have been a bit much.

My mom is a retired RN with a Pride-flag tattoo, so of course she came down to help out the week after surgery. One afternoon, while August was having a nap, my mom asked me a question on a drive to the grocery store: “So, given everything that’s been going on, do I need to prepare myself for an amicable divorce between you two?”

It was, I suppose, a fair question, but honestly the thought had never occurred to me. We’d been through so much together. If the California debacle and multiple grad schools didn’t split us up, why would a little thing like transitioning?

I don’t begrudge people who end a relationship after their partner changes genders. If you’ve always considered both yourself and your marriage to be straight, breaking up seems like a fairly reasonable reaction. My situation is not exactly unique, but it’s certainly rare. We know a few male couples in which one is trans and the other cis, but the former had almost always transitioned before meeting their current partner.

As much as I loved August pre-transition—their cute voice, their big hair—I love their deeper voice and broader chest and shoulders. What I love most, though, is their newfound self-assurance. I always used to tell them, “I wish you knew how great you are.” Whether it’s because of testosterone’s known impact on confidence or because now they just feel more comfortable with who they are as a person, I feel like now they finally do.

Recently, August and I were again walking through our neighborhood when we had another interaction with a passerby. An older man by himself saw us, two men nonchalantly holding hands. He said nothing but dramatically placed his hand on his heart and smiled at us.

Others may disagree, but I don’t think I necessarily come off as gay. That being said, people don’t look too surprised when I say, “My husband…” This affords me the privilege of being largely anonymous and occasionally invisible. I’m also a pretty big guy, so I never feel as though anybody is going to target me even if they see me only as a gay man. The only time I’ve ever been called a faggot is when the speaker was already in a car, driving away from me.

There’s a comfort in that invisibility that I didn’t recognize until it was gone. Now, a solid three years into August’s transition, even if people don’t clock August as trans, we appear as middle-aged gay guys. Although we live in an area that could fairly be referred to as Gay Country, it’s slightly unnerving for random bystanders to have an opinion on our sexualities, even if it’s only visible through a look or a pointed lack thereof.

Historically, this is the best time and probably one of the best places to be gay or trans. But that didn’t happen by magic, and we still have a lot of work to do. Part of that work is just making ourselves visible. Because when the two of us, one trans, one cis, walk down the street and hold hands, we’re making a political statement—whether we mean to or not.

#4361. Could On-Policy Self-Distillation Revolutionize Post-Training?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Publish AI, ML & data-science insights to a global community of data professionals.

Teaching foundation models new skills without breaking them is hard. A new reinforcement learning-based approach might have the power to change that.

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD)(1) has taken the world of machine learning research by storm. Although OPSD frameworks rely heavily on older, more established approaches, the first papers on the topic were only submitted in late January 2026, just under nine months ago at the time of writing this article [1, 2]. Several dozen papers have followed within that brief timespan [3], covering everything from extensions [3] to limitations [4, 5].

In this article, we'll explore the literature on OPSD, mainly through the lens of the Self-Distillation Fine-Tuning (SDFT) framework introduced by Shenfeld et al. in Self-distillation enables continual learning [1]. As we'll see, the term "continual learning" in the paper's title refers to a model's ability to effectively and dynamically learn from a datastream. We'll start by examining the problem OPSD frameworks set out to solve and the approaches they build on.

After that, we'll look at how SDFT works and the theoretical explanations for its success. These foundations motivate the experiments described in the paper, with their promising results lending credence to the prospect of OPSD being widely applicable. Finally, we'll touch on two central concerns that have surfaced regarding OPSD.

As ever more powerful foundation models have hit the market, the sheer number of tasks they can perform well out of the box has skyrocketed. These models are pre-trained on enormous amounts of data using an incredible amount of computational resources. Pre-training is probably the first thing that comes to mind for most people when talking about "training" machine learning models in general. It is the stage during which a model attains its fundamental capabilities and knowledge.

Post-training complements pre-training by teaching a model to channel what it has learned during pre-training for a certain purpose, to behave in desirable ways, or to learn new skills. Post-training goals could be anything from better instruction following to improved performance on difficult math problems. Foundation models also undergo extensive post-training before being released for use.

For many highly complex or customized tasks, however, further training remains necessary. Pre-training a custom model from scratch would be prohibitively expensive and impractical. Instead, we carry out additional post-training on foundation models. Fine-tuning is a particular form of post-training. Conducting it a single time could work if we want to specialize the model for a particular domain. But what if we want to build on a vision-language-action (VLA) model to enable a robot to carry out a variety of intricate tasks? Or to post-train an LLM for better cross-domain performance in a low- to mid-resource language(2)?

The key problem that arises in such cases is catastrophic forgetting: models forgetting how to handle older tasks as a result of learning new ones. When catastrophic forgetting happens during post-training, it often happens rapidly. Naturally, as we train a model on the new tasks we want to teach it, we influence its weights. This is the mechanism by which learning happens. We gradually "pull" the weights away from those of the base model.

However, if we throw too much at the model, be it too much new data or even a small amount of new data that is too radically out-of-distribution compared to what it has seen before, we risk pulling so hard that we interfere with its ability to carry out the tasks it was previously trained to conduct. What constitutes "too much" in this case is often much less than what you would expect from a human.

Continual learning, as the name implies, is the ability of a model to learn continually from a potentially endless and highly variable stream of data while avoiding catastrophic forgetting. Continual learning, done properly, yields models that are capable of acquiring an arbitrary number of new skills without meaningfully sacrificing prior abilities.

Interest in continual learning has spiked over the past year (see Figure 1). Real headway has been made in that time. The unquestionable improvement in available open-weight foundation models, paired with new post-training methods, has ushered in an era where true continual learning seems more applicable to a wider range of real-world problems than ever.

A key family of methods introduced in that time, as mentioned above, is OPSD, with a particular member of that family being SDFT. Having understood the problem these approaches aim to solve, we can review the more traditional approaches SDFT builds upon and the theoretical and empirical results thanks to which OPSD has drawn so much attention since the beginning of 2026.

Knowledge distillation is a well-established machine learning technique. It has garnered significant attention over the past year, much of it negative and centering on accusations of distillation attacks [6]. However, there's nothing inherently bad about distillation. It is a highly effective and widely used technique. The idea at its core is simple: use a bigger, more complex model as a teacher to train a student model to replicate its outputs.

Self-distillation puts a twist on the classic approach. Rather than using a different, more powerful model in the role of the teacher, both the teacher and the student are copies of the exact same model. The only things that distinguish the teacher from the student are that the teacher is given access to privileged information and that its parameters are updated slightly differently. As we'll see below, this can be beneficial in reducing catastrophic forgetting.

OPSD methods in general, and SDFT in particular, employ self-distillation. (That's what the "SD" in both of them stands for.) In SDFT, the teacher is conditioned on expert demonstrations for each query (see Figure 2). Theoretically, these expert demonstrations could include anything that might give the teacher an edge. They could be brief justifications of correct answers, instructions on how to reach such correct answers, or even entire reasoning traces where thinking models are concerned.

In SDFT in particular, for multiple choice questions drawn from a chemistry subset of the SciKnowEval benchmark [7], the teacher model is given the correct answer and a brief explanation on how to derive it (e.g., the process needed to determine the molar mass of some particular molecule). The student and teacher are both instantiations of (non-thinking) Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct. The chemistry expert demonstrations are generated by the much stronger GPT-4o [1].

For each token generated by the student while answering a query, the student and the teacher each produce a next-token distribution, denoted PPP and QQQ respectively in Figure 2. The difference between PPP and QQQ is that QQQ is informed by the privileged demonstrations to which only the teacher has access. At each step, the training objective is to minimize the reverse(3) KL divergence between PPP and QQQ. Computing the divergence is the only thing QQQ is used for, as the teacher never actually generates any tokens.

Training directly updates student parameters at each step. Teacher parameters are initially identical to student parameters. There are three ways teacher parameters can be updated throughout training.

No updates: The teacher weights are frozen in their initial state. Shenfeld et al. [1] report that frozen teacher weights prevent the teacher from improving alongside the student during training, leading to suboptimal performance.

Student-identical updates: I.e., directly using the continuously updated student model as the teacher throughout training. According to Shenfeld et al. [1], this lowers the stability of training. An overly similar student-teacher pair develops an echo chamber in which variations can send training off the rails.

Averaged updates: Due to the flaws of the aforementioned approaches, Shenfeld et al. [1] opt for a happy medium. In SDFT (and most other OPSD frameworks), teacher weights are updated as an exponential moving average (EMA) of student parameters. This means that although the parameters of the two start off exactly the same, they diverge during training.

Self-distillation might seem somewhat counterintuitive at first. Wouldn't using a more expressive model as the teacher yield better results than just a slightly improved copy of the student model? Alternatively, why not just feed the expert demonstrations straight into the student rather than pass them through the teacher? This is where catastrophic forgetting comes back into play. While a stronger teacher may be better at helping the student learn the new task, in doing so, it can cause the student's performance on tasks it was pre-trained on to deteriorate.

The idea behind why self-distillation seems to mitigate catastrophic forgetting is illustrated in Figure 3. By using the same model as both the student and the teacher, the teacher diverges much less from the student than a bigger model would. To reuse our earlier analogy, the self-distillation teacher pulls on the student's weights in a much gentler and more targeted way, but still teaches it what it needs to learn.

Shenfeld et al. provide empirical evidence from several experiments that SDFT yields excellent post-training outcomes while avoiding catastrophic forgetting almost entirely. Before exploring the results, though, we need to cover the second important aspect of OPSD: the fact that it is on-policy.

When training language models, the models themselves act as what are called policies in the context of reinforcement learning (RL). There are two types of policy-based RL methods: on-policy and off-policy.

As Cameron R. Wolfe puts it in the Deep (Learning) Focus article "Online versus Offline RL for LLMs"(4):

By “on-policy” sampling, we mean that completions [model outputs] used to train our LLM in the core RL training loop are generated in real-time by the LLM itself—the completions are not generated by another model or stored in an offline, pre-computed dataset. [9]

Standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is inherently off-policy precisely because the model being fine-tuned learns from data that is pre-generated and stored offline.

Both off-policy and on-policy RL methods have been experimented with extensively for post-training LLMs. In past work, however, Shenfeld et al. have claimed that on-policy RL is uniquely suited to post-training [10]. In particular, they demonstrated that models trained using on-policy RL, when compared to those trained using standard SFT, can achieve similar performance on new tasks while forgetting less [10].

The reason for this reduced forgetting is the apparent innate tendency of on-policy RL to converge to policies with minimal KL divergence from the base policy implemented by the model before fine-tuning (see Figure 4). Intuitively, because the model is only trained on outputs it itself generates, on-policy RL post-training leads the model to reweight outputs it would already have been reasonably likely to generate moreso than to assign probability to outputs straying significantly from the base policy. This differs from standard SFT, where the external training set may cause the model to imitate patterns the base policy wouldn't. There could be infinitely many policies that perform well on some new task we want to train the base model to succeed at, but on-policy RL seems to consistently pick ones that are closer in KL to the base policy [10].

Notice the similarity between the argument for on-policy RL and the one for self-distillation. In tandem, they act as force multipliers, with their respective mechanisms for mitigating catastrophic forgetting compounding to yield SDFT.

The breakneck speed at which papers on variants of OPSD have appeared since January 2026 is a strong indicator of its potential, but what matters much more is how it performs in practice. We've seen that the theory behind it is sound. Beyond just making sense on paper, though, the symbiosis between on-policy RL and self-distillation is borne out by numerous experiments in the literature. We'll turn our attention to some examples conducted by Shenfeld et al. [1] and by Zhao et al. [2].

In Self-distillation enables continual learning [1], Shenfeld et al. train a model on three separate, very distinct tasks, one after another, once using SFT and once using their SDFT framework. They compare per-task performance throughout training for the model under each training method.

SFT exhibits textbook catastrophic forgetting. Accuracy on the tasks other than the one being trained for at any given time degrades to well below the pre-trained model's baseline. Any improvement achieved while focusing on a particular task is quickly undone upon moving on to the next one [1].

SDFT, on the other hand, not only maintains baseline performance before the targeted training for each task, but consistently preserves task-specific improvement, with no notable drop in performance (i.e., forgetting) on the first task even after hundreds of training steps dedicated to the other two. Interference between tasks aside, SDFT's task-specific improvement even yields higher accuracy on each novel task than SFT does [1].

The experiments by Zhao et al. [2] focus on new-task performance and token efficiency, training models using their OPSD framework, the well-established GRPO [11], and SFT, and comparing them. In OPSD, the teacher is given expert rollouts as privileged information. In SFT, the model is trained with the expert rollouts as reference solutions. OPSD and SFT are therefore roughly comparable in terms of the information they receive. GRPO, however, receives much less information. Using GRPO, the model never actually uses the expert rollouts except for a binary comparison of final answers, so the comparison between the training methods is confounded.

Nevertheless, Zhao et al. show that OPSD significantly improves over GRPO on token efficiency during training, requiring far fewer tokens over 100 training steps while markedly outperforming GRPO on the AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25 benchmarks after those 100 steps [2]. This can probably be attributed to OPSD taking advantage of the expert demonstrations in their entirety, whereas GRPO only uses the final answers. There is a significant information asymmetry at play.

The performance edge OPSD has over GRPO becomes much more modest when each is run for 500 training steps rather than 100, especially on the larger models tested, Qwen-3-8B and Qwen-3-4B. GRPO even beats OPSD on HMMT25 with Qwen-3-8B. However, the gap in favor of OPSD remains pronounced with the smaller Qwen-3-1.7B. It also handily beats SFT across the board [2].

While OPSD appears to be promising in enabling LLMs to learn new capabilities without forgetting old ones, it is not without its limitations. A central one raised by Nicolicioiu et al. [4] is loss of diversity in trained student outputs under OPSD regimes where demonstrations are sampled from LLM outputs, dubbed Self-Distillation with Sampled Demonstrations (SDSD).

They describe and measure two forms of diversity: functional and semantic. The former is the extent to which additional attempts by a model to solve a problem help it actually do so. A measure of the functional diversity of a model is the slope of its pass@k curve (i.e., given k attempts to solve each problem in the test set, what percentage of the problems does the model sucessfully solve?) Semantic diversity is the degree to which a model's outputs differ in terms of broad strategy. The authors measure this using a task specifically constructed to facilitate multiple distinct solution approaches [4].

Their results indicate that both functional and semantic diversity are lower in models trained using SDSD than in those trained with GRPO. The pass@k curves of SDSD-trained models tend to be almost entirely flat across several different benchmarks. This means that an SDSD-trained model essentially tries to solve a problem the same way every time, meaning that if it doesn't manage to solve it the first time, there's little chance it'll manage to do so the second or even the 64th. SDSD-trained models also fare significantly worse on the authors' semantic diversity task [4]. Practically, this means that these models may be inherently worse for use cases like simple agentic tasks or mathematical theorem proving, where it is common practice to generate many rollouts and select the best of them using LLM judge evaluation.

Nicolicioiu et al. attribute this loss of diversity to the model's biases compounding during training. They point out the role of the general mode-seeking tendency of on-policy RL in this process. "Mode-seeking" means that on-policy RL concentrates the model's probability mass at the distribution's peaks, leading to "likely-get-likelier" behavior, as the authors dub it. This behavior is exacerbated by the teacher having an outsized preference for solutions that resemble the sampled demonstration that constitutes its privileged information. It rewards sampled student rollouts that resemble the demonstration and punishes ones that do not, even if they are equally correct [4].

Another important question is whether the results attested in the early papers by Shenfeld et al. [1] and Zhao et al. [2] can be replicated with thinking models. The aforementioned experiments are all conducted using instruct-tuned (non-thinking) models. However, thinking models have proven uniquely adept at challenging reasoning tasks, universally topping benchmark performance leaderboards. If OPSD could be used to post-train them, too, that would be highly valuable.

Unfortunately, Kaur et al. [5] show that OPSD doesn't generalize to thinking models, at least not directly. They run experiments with five different thinking models on the AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25 benchmarks. Their central finding is that OPSD degrades avg@16 (average accuracy across 16 trained-student rollouts) for every single model on every single benchmark, often by more than five points, and sometimes by almost ten. They hypothesize that this is due to the signal given by the teacher model at inflection points in the student's thinking trace, potentially closing the door to student self-correction before committing to a flawed solution path [5].

After describing what continual learning is and what makes it a difficult goal to achieve, we broke down On-Policy Self-Distillation approaches into their two key components: self-distillation and on-policy reinforcement learning, focusing on the SDFT framework introduced by Shenfeld et al. [1]. We considered each of these aspects in its respective wider machine learning context, understanding how self-distillation differs from classic supervised fine-tuning, how on-policy RL differs from off-policy RL, and how each individually contributes to the mitigation of catastrophic forgetting.

We followed up on the theory by looking into the encouraging experimental results on OPSD in the literature, before finally touching on some of the core limitations OPSD has out of the box, namely, loss of output diversity through compounding biases, as well as the apparent degradation of thinking models.

#4206. How to deal with anxious-ambivalent attachment in a relationship

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

Have you ever texted your partner only to spiral into oblivion waiting for them to respond? You wonder if they’re upset with you, if you did something wrong, or if they’re quietly pulling away. When their response finally comes through, you exhale the biggest sigh of relief. And then you start second-guessing why you were so worried in the first place.

If this sounds familiar, you may be dabbling in the world of ambivalent attachment, which is one of many attachment styles formed in adolescence and carried into adulthood.

Ambivalent attachment is a relationship pattern where you want to feel close to your partner but can’t always get there. It can show up as craving reassurance but doubting it, swinging between feeling connected to your partner and fearing they’ll leave, or analyzing small interactions for a hidden meaning that often isn’t there. While it may feel overwhelming, this attachment style isn’t a flaw in your personality, but rather a pattern shaped by early experiences in your life.

Let’s unpack what ambivalent attachment means, how to spot the signs in adulthood, and what to do if you (or your partner) lean toward this attachment style. Plus, we’ll dive into how to create steadier, more supportive relationships that feel good for all involved.

Anxious-ambivalent attachment—also called ambivalent attachment—is a relationship pattern where you crave reassurance yet struggle to feel settled once it’s given. It’s like wanting to lean on someone but worrying they might step away just as you do.

In adult relationships, ambivalent attachment may show up as feeling unsure about your partner’s commitment to you, cycling between connection and doubt, or getting caught in emotional highs and lows depending on how available your partner feels to you.

While it can feel overwhelming, this isn’t a flaw. It’s a protective strategy learned early in life that can be reshaped over time.

Ambivalent attachment often develops in childhood when caregiving is inconsistent. Basically, sometimes you were nurtured, and sometimes you weren’t. This unpredictability creates uncertainty in children, as they learn that comfort may arrive (but not reliably), so they cling tightly when it does appear. Over time, this wires the nervous system to stay on high alert for signs of loss or disconnection.

While it may seem easy to blame the caregivers involved, that’s not always the best answer or solution. Many people provide inconsistent care because of stress, lack of support, or their own attachment struggles. The good news is that even if this style formed early, it can shift in adulthood through consistent experiences of safety and connection.

Ambivalent attachment doesn’t always look dramatic from the outside. It often shows up in subtle, everyday ways that leave both partners feeling drained or confused. While everyone feels insecure at times, people with an ambivalent attachment style experience these patterns more intensely and more often.

1. Constant need for reassurance: You might ask for confirmation of your partner’s love or commitment regularly through words, texts, or gestures. The tricky part for people with an ambivalent attachment style is that reassurance often doesn’t stick. Even after hearing “I love you,” doubt can creep back quickly, fueling the cycle for reassurance again.

2. Fear of abandonment: Small shifts in a partner’s behavior—like if they take longer than usual to reply to a message or are quieter than usual—can trigger unnecessary worry. The fear isn’t just about being left by your partner, it’s about being forgotten or no longer valued. And for someone with this attachment style, those fears can be dire.

3. Hyper-attunement to signals: People with ambivalent attachment often read between the lines of every interaction, scanning tone, body language, or text punctuation for hidden meaning. A short text that simply says, “K,” might feel like rejection rather than just brevity.

4. Emotional highs and lows: Relationships can feel like a rollercoaster. You may feel ecstatic when things feel close and connected, then anxious when any distance appears. The intensity of these swings can feel overwhelming for both partners.

5. Difficulty trusting stability: Even when the relationship is going well, there’s a lingering sense that it could change at any moment. This makes it hard to relax into intimacy, because safety doesn’t feel fully reliable.

6. Feeling “too much” or “not enough”: Self-doubt often runs in the background, accompanied by persistent worrying that your needs are excessive or fearing that you’re somehow lacking. This inner tug-of-war can make it difficult to feel secure in how you show up in a relationship.

Working with ambivalent attachment is about finding steadier ground, both within yourself and your relationships. These strategies combine self-regulation, clearer communication, and practical agreements that build trust over time.

1. Recognize and name the pattern: The first step is simply noticing when old fears are coming up. Take time to put a name to the feeling, and remind yourself that it’s a learned response and not proof that something is wrong with you or your relationship. Taking a beat to give your feelings a name gives you the chance to pause before you react impulsively.

2. Soothe your body before your thoughts: Ambivalent attachment often activates the nervous system, which can make worries feel louder than they are. Simple grounding tools, such as intentional breathing, a mindful walk, or even running cold water over your wrists (which may help calm your nervous system) can bring your body back to baseline. Once you’re calmer, you’ll have more perspective on what’s really happening.

3. Ask directly instead of protesting: Protest behaviors (testing someone, withdrawing your affection, or sending a string of texts from the top of your mind) can backfire and push your partner away instead of bringing them closer. Practice naming your needs clearly instead. Try saying something like, “I’m feeling anxious right now and could use reassurance that we’re okay.” It might feel vulnerable to be so honest and open, but being direct usually leads to better connection.

4. Build a personal reassurance toolkit: Instead of depending only on your partner for reassurance, create a set of resources you can turn to yourself. This might include rereading kind messages, journaling, doing grounding exercises, or calling a supportive friend. Having options beyond your partner reduces the sense of desperation when anxiety spikes.

5. Practice small steps toward trust: Shifting attachment styles takes time, but small experiments help. Try waiting a little longer than you typically would before sending a check-in text, and see how that feels. You can also remind yourself of past experiences where closeness stood the test of time or survived a disagreement. Each successful moment builds evidence that security is possible, even if your old fears insist otherwise.

6. Offer steady reassurance with boundaries: Consistency may help calm anxiety. Simple habits like a morning check-in or saying when you’ll call back create predictability. But it’s also important to pair reassurance with realistic boundaries. You might say, “I can’t text during work, but I’ll check in at lunch.”

7. Respond to the need, not just the words: Underneath protests or big reactions is usually a fear of being abandoned. Instead of focusing only on the tone or delivery of something your partner says, try reflecting the underlying need. You can say, “I hear that you’re worried I’m pulling away, but I want you to know I’m here.” This validation often matters more than a perfect solution.

8. Set clear repair practices: When tensions rise, agree in advance on how to pause and return to the conversation when you’re level-headed. Try saying, “If one of us feels overwhelmed, we’ll take a 20-minute break and come back at an agreed time.” Returning as promised helps rebuild trust, strengthens your relationship, and makes conflicts feel less threatening.

9. Create shared routines for connection: Structure helps balance anxiety. A weekly check-in, an agreed-upon text during the workday, or even a daily hug ritual can anchor the relationship. These small but predictable moments reduce the need for constant reassurance.

Ambivalent attachment usually begins in early childhood. When a caregiver is sometimes warm and attentive but other times distracted, stressed, or unavailable, a child learns that love is unpredictable. This inconsistency creates anxiety about whether comfort will be there when it’s needed most.

Over time, the child develops strategies to cling tightly to connection, like seeking closeness while bracing for it to disappear. It’s not always about neglect. Often, caregivers themselves are overwhelmed or carrying their own attachment wounds. But the result is the same, and it’s that love is both essential and unreliable.

You may notice repeating patterns in your relationships, like craving reassurance but rarely feeling settled when you receive it. Small shifts in your partner’s tone, attention, or texting habits may trigger outsized worry about abandonment.

Overthinking, analyzing, or swinging between feeling deeply connected and then uncertain are also common. If these patterns show up across multiple relationships, that can be a strong sign of ambivalent attachment.

Attachment styles can change. While your early experiences lay the foundation, your attachment style can shift as you build new experiences of safety and stability. Supportive relationships, therapy, and personal work on self-regulation all help strengthen secure attachment.

The process is gradual and often happens through repeated moments of repair and reassurance. Over time, these experiences retrain your nervous system to trust that closeness can last.

Both ambivalent and avoidant attachment come from early inconsistencies in caregiving, but they lead to opposite coping strategies. People with ambivalent attachment often lean in, seeking closeness, reassurance, and connection. Sometimes, these manifest in ways that feel intense or overwhelming to their partner.

Patience and predictability are key. Your partner may need more reassurance than you thought, so offering it consistently and directly can ease their anxiety. For example, sending a quick “thinking of you” text before a long meeting or confirming plans in advance can make a big difference.

#2963. A Mysterious Sea Monster Was Vexing Fishermen. We Set Out to Catch It

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 10:37 AM EDT

The hottest fishing news, tips, and tactics

AS THE GUNNER II nosed into the 3½-knot current of the Gulf Stream, the double line on the Fathometer ran straight across the chart, telling us that, 800 feet below, the bottom was smooth.

“The top line is mud,” Capt. Jack Carpenter explained. “The lower line is the solid rock of the bottom. That means there’s about 48 feet of mud down there.”

Suddenly the lines began to climb, almost straight up for 100, 200, 300 feet. Then, just as abruptly, they leveled off again at about 288 feet down.

“This is it,” said Al. “We’re over The Hump.”

“I’ll drop a flag marker,” said Captain Jack. “Don’t want to lose this place after all the trouble we’ve had finding it. It’s only 400 feet long and half that wide, and that’s not big, out in the middle of this ocean.”

Al Pflueger, owner of the Gunner II, Capt. Jack Carpenter, Harold Graves, who heads up a stereoscopic instruments company in Portland, Oregon, and I were about 18 miles offshore from Islamorada, Florida, on the Florida Keys, and The Hump we’d been seeking is a mount that rears up from the floor of the Atlantic Ocean. The mount is supposed to be fairly easy to find, for with almost any kind of a tide running, choppy seas make up and a whitecapped rip fingers The Hump. But this day was so calm and the tide so slow that no rip was forming, and finding that small mount on the wide expanse of ocean had been like trying to find the hook eye of a tiny trout fly at dusk.

The Hump has a history. Time after time boats have come into the docks at Islamorada with stories of great battering rams of fish hooked out there, fish that popped the most powerful lines, froze the most expertly machined reels, tore tackle to bits.

“Swordfish,” guessed some anglers. Yet giant swordfish have been hooked and boated on lighter gear than they were using.

“Huge marlin,” said others. But again, great hulks of marlin have been taken quite often on regular sport tackle.

“They’re not ‘running fish’, whatever they are,” still others said. “They’re not fish that take the bait and swim off, pulling laterally to get away. They’re something that goes down and just breaks the tackle by pure brute force. Giant squid, perhaps.”

“Maybe even a coelacanth,” another fisherman suggested, naming the strange, prehistoric creature caught some years ago off the South African coast, which had been believed extinct for millions of years.

And so the bets went on, ranging from the best known and most likely swimmers of Florida coastal waters to strange and unknown denizens of the unexplored depths. Inquisitive and frustrated anglers went on trying every method they knew to bring up one of those monsters, methods varying from primitive but efficient handlines, which were inevitably snatched away from scorched gloves, to regular trolling tackle, which usually resulted in a cut-off.

One angler went out equipped with some barrels, tying his line to them. When he had a hit on his handline, he struck hard to set the hook, felt the heft of the fish, then dropped the line over, thinking to tire his catch in this way and eventually get it up. The barrels went off, skidding along the surface, then disappeared. A couple of them never reappeared; the others came bobbing to the top some time later, the lines scraped, torn, and cut from being pulled across rocks.

So the game went on. Time and again average fish were boated—tilefish, groupers, snappers, a shark—but nothing over 100 pounds. What was smashing up tackle out there over The Hump had to be bigger than that.

Al Pflueger had long been curious about the tackle-busters on The Hump. Al operates one of the largest taxidermy businesses in the United States, at Miami, Florida. His staff can make lifelike mounts of anything that swims, and the skins of great fish are sent to him from all over the world by gamefishermen and ichthyologists. Al can identify and give you the scientific name of almost any fish known, and is recognized as one of the nation’s top authorities. He seems to have been born with a natural curiosity about fish, and he spends most of his time studying them.

For some years he has been particularly interested in deep-fathom fishing from the scientific viewpoint, and has had the Gunner II especially rigged to find out what is down in the depths. He couldn’t have chosen a better teammate than Capt. Jack Carpenter. Over the years, Jack has guided the great names in sport fishing to most of the hotspots of the seas. Strangely, in all those years, he picked up a rod to fish for himself only twice. On those two occasions, two days apart in 1941, he took an 825-pound and an 880-pound bluefin tuna, the latter still standing as the world record in the 80-pound-line test class.

Harold and I felt we were in good company, with these two, in our jaunt to see what we could find out about The Hump.

“Deep-fathom fishing is fascinating,” Al said. “Last year, for instance, we caught a shark that had lights in back of its eyes. It’s called the cow shark, a very deep dweller and a strange one, too. It has a deep-emerald color behind the pupil of its eye. It even shines in the daytime. The first one we caught was terrific. I looked over the side and saw this green light coming up through the water, growing more vivid all the time. Then I saw it was the eye of this shark.”

“Do other deep fish have it, too?” I asked.

“As far as I know, only the cow shark, the oilfish, and the dog shark,” he said. “Nobody seems to know why.”

“The Japanese landed a shark that had a bill like a sawfish,” Jack Carpenter broke in, “and once I saw a dead squid whose two longest tentacles measured six feet. Its head was a foot and a half long.”

“Max Lerner caught a five and then a 6-foot squid on hook and line, off Cabo Blanco,” Al said.

“In Bermuda,” I contributed, “Louis Mowbray used a modified version of the Japanese long-line rig to catch a lancet fish, the first of that species in perfect condition ever taken in the Atlantic.”

Al had evolved his apparatus from first a long handline to a hand-operated winch, and finally into a slick, electrically operated machine with a slightly Rube Goldberg look, two of which were now mounted on the gunwales, one on either side of the cockpit.

The four-foot-long metal bars used as rods, and the entire frameworks, are so solid as to be almost unbreakable. Wound on the huge spools was 5,000 feet of 500-pound-test stainless-steel line, with seven-strand twisted cable for the leaders, testing out at the same 500-pound breaking strength as the line. For fishing in 300 feet of water, where there is no current, the outfits call for five-pound sinkers, but as the water gets deeper or the current increases, the weight of the sinkers is upped to as much as 30 pounds to get the bait down to the desired depth.

Hooks are from sizes 6/0 to 12/0 and are of triple strength.

The big reels run on 32-volt current, but can be operated exactly as game-fishing reels with star drags. They can be thrown into free spool, the drags can be tightened or loosened, and all the while the electric motors keep ticking away, ready to pull fish up as they tire.

“We’ll drift,” said Jack, when he had got the marker overboard. “Then when the tide gets stronger, I’ll run one engine real slow and head the boat into the tide. That way we can hold her in one spot.”

The first baits that we used were four-pound jack crevallé, one to each of the rigs.

“Let’s go,” said Al, and he and Jack each pushed a button to put the reels in free spool.

We leaned over to watch the baits sink. Down they went, down and down, and when at last the heavy weights hit bottom, Jack and Al pressed buttons on their rigs to bring the weights up about two feet. We waited.

Almost immediately Al’s rod shook violently.

“Got ‘im,” Al shouted as he thumbed the button to start the line up and set the hook.

The stiff rod began beating up and down, and at the same moment, “Strike!” yelled Jack, and his rod also went into a dizzy downbeat.

This was something to see. Both those “rods,” really solid iron bars, were dancing up and down like bamboo, line paying out protestingly over the tips, reels squeaking as they lost line, groaning as they gained. And those two men, who have fished the waters of the world for gamefish, were glued to that deep-fathom gear with as much enthusiasm as if they were battling those monsters on the lightest of tackle.

Staring through the clear water, I saw a small, silvery spot far below. It turned brownish, grew rapidly, and then suddenly, 25 feet away from the boat, a fish bounced out of the water in a froth of bursting bubbles.

“It’s a coney grouper,” Al said. “Not very large, though,” he added as the electric winch pulled the fish over to the boat and then swung its 30 pounds over the side.

At the same time, Jack was boating his fish, this one a long, pale-gray tilefish that weighed about 20 pounds. Tilefish are most commonly found along the 100-fathom curve, and there are three members of the family—the true tilefish, the gray, which we caught, and the sand. The true tile is also found in Florida waters, but the gray is there in greater numbers.

“That’s 20 pounds of good eating,” said Jack. “Both of these fish are delicacies.”

“They’ve been down there feeding on crabs till they’re fit to burst,” said Al, “and that’s what makes fish flesh worth eating.”

The next drop brought a strike to Al’s bait before it hit the bottom, and the fish fought back so hard I thought it was going to tear the rig apart. Then everything went slack. Al brought the line up.

The leader was cut clean through just above the hooks. “That was a big shark,” Jack commented.

After that, for a long time, there wasn’t a strike.

“No wonder things are quiet,” said Al suddenly. “We’ve drifted off The Hump. The marker is 200 feet back.” We pulled the lines in, ran back, and dropped the baits over again.

Al’s rod went into all kinds of bobs and bends, swipes and swoops, and the feet of the rig strained at their holdfasts. This was a big one, all right. The reel protested violently and the motor croaked, then the big reel gave line, took some, and held even. But that fish wanted out and he went out. He took line, faster and faster, and then there was a loud Ping and everything went slack.

When Al brought his line in, we saw that the hook was broken off at the bend.

“Either a bad hook,” Jack said, “or a mighty sharp-toothed fish.”

Al put on new terminal tackle, baited the hook, dropped it down until it hit bottom, then buttoned it up for a couple of feet. There was an immediate strike. He pushed the button again, and the fight between monster and machine was on. This fish was even bigger than the last one. It fought harder and longer, and for a while it looked as if it was going to win, for once more everything went slack.

“Guess the hook must have come out,” I said.

“There he is, back there,” said Harold Graves. “He’s back there on the surface.” A big, bloated-looking creature lay on the top, about 40 feet from the boat.

“It’s a big Warsaw grouper,” said Al. “We brought him up so fast his air bladder blew up and his stomach turned inside out, so he can’t get down again. If such a fish is returned to the water headfirst and with great force, it will often retract its stomach and go on down. But if it’s left floating helplessly on top like that it would soon fall prey to a shark or be killed from above by sea birds.”

Within limits, fish have the ability to adapt to depth changes. Most are not called upon to do so to any great extent because everything they need to sustain life is usually found where they normally live. The more primitive fishes have the air bladder openly connected to the esophagus, but the higher-evolved, spiney-rayed fishes, such as we were dealing with, have no such connection. They can control the air in the bladder as they change depths, by secretion. But if they are brought up rapidly—as on rod and reel and as with the tackle we were using—the air expands beyond the point of control. When that happens, the fish “fall” upward to the surface, bloated, ruptured, and even exploded. Their stomachs turn inside out and their eyes protrude out of their sockets.

We went over and pulled the big grouper aboard. He was even bigger than he had looked.

“He’ll go 125 pounds,” said Al. “We’re getting into the big ones now.”

Once more Jack ran the boat back to the flag and sent his bait on its way. He had a strike on the way down.

This fish fought like the other only harder, but finally it came up and much closer to the boat. I saw it coming, and at first I thought it was a big turtle. But when it hit the top and the bursting bubbles subsided, it looked like a brother of the first one.

“A bit bigger,” said Al. “He’ll go a good 150.”

This was not my first experience with deep-fathom fishing. Eight years earlier I had spent a day with Thorvald Sanchez, Cuban sportsman, on his Siboney II. Thorvald wasn’t interested so much in big fish at great depths as in finding new areas where either shrimps or snappers congregated in sufficient quantity to provide commercial fishing. He had rigged an outfit which was a sort of pygmy cousin of the one Al uses. An automobile spring served as the rod, a pair of bicycle wheels, hand-manipulated, ran the big spool which held 1,500 feet of 100-pound-test Monel wire line, and the 250-pound-test leaders made of piano wire carried two big hooks which were baited with chunks of dead fish. A seven-pound lead weight took the baits down to the 1,200-foot-deep bottom off Punta del Este on the Isle of Pines.

With that rig, and at that depth, our biggest fish had been a 22-pound snapper and one lonely, 50-pound sand shark, and the number of small fish was an eye-opener to me, particularly the snappers. We took several only five and six inches long, ample proof that fish of this species must spawn and live a great deal of their lives way down there in the depths.

“I was surprised sharks didn’t bother us more, that time,” I told Al. “Maybe they don’t like to go so deep.”

“I’ve seen photographs of an unnamed six-foot shark at about 4,000 meters,” he said, “and in the Gulf of Aden one was taken at 1,800 meters. There are even records of sharks in the deep, abyssal regions, the netherlands of the seas, water below 6,540 feet or 1,090 fathoms. But in general, you can pretty well name the fish you’ll find at certain depths. At 100 fathoms you can bet on gray tilefish, Warsaw grouper, silk and blackfin snappers, all the shallow-water sharks, vermillion snappers, and the great mako sharks; and at night oilfish move up to that depth temporarily.”

“That sounds like a good fishing depth,” I said.

He went on to say that at 300 fathoms and more, oilfish may be found in the daytime, along with spiny dogfish, cow sharks, queen snappers, and Aprion snappers running to 500 pounds.

“We’ve fished for oilfish in Bermuda, with long lines,” I said. “Those things really tear up tackle.”

Oilfish are so named because of the purgative effect they produce when eaten. They are also known as tapioca fish because their flesh, just under the skin, looks like tapioca. They are rare, grow as large as 100 pounds, and are terribly strong. Their eyes are two inches across, with a one-inch black center, and they have thin, very sharp teeth. Usually they are taken only by commercial fishermen using extremely long handlines.

“With heavy gear and real luck you might be able to land a 100-pounder,” said Captain Carpenter. “But if they grew any bigger, you’d never land one.”

“Maybe that’s the scoundrel that’s breaking up the tackle on The Hump,” I suggested.

They both dropped their baits overboard again and put thumbs to the buttons to run them down. We found right then that those 100 and 150-pounders we’d seen so far were just hors d’oeuvres. Jack’s next fish was a Warsaw grouper that went 200 pounds. Al landed a 250-pounder, and he no sooner got his bait overboard again than he was into something that outfought all the others put together. But finally the sturdy gear pulled him to the surface. It was another Warsaw grouper—300 pounds.

“They get bigger and bigger,” I said. Al and Jack were too busy to answer, and also too breathless. Though the machines did the work, it still took the three of us, giving our best efforts, to haul those brutes over the gunwale.

Next time the baits went over they had simultaneous hits. The two rigs strained harder than ever, both reels losing line. Jack and Al tightened the drags. And, as if the lines were tied together, both went slack the same instant. When the lines were reeled in, two of the big hooks, one on each outfit, were straightened out.

“We must have had twins on,” grinned Jack as he put on a new hook, baited it, and let it down. In seconds the rod started bobbing up and down, with heavy emphasis on the down. Then it plunged almost to the surface, gave a tremendous shudder, jumped back with a thud, and vibrated like a piano wire for a few seconds.

“Sure busted something that time,” said Jack.

“I’ve got one,” shouted Al as his rod went into the same kind of dance.

It ended the same way, too, with that hard down pull, shudder, and whanging vibrations. This time, when they brought the lines in, both hooks had been broken off at the bend.

“Now we’re really into heavy stuff,” said Al. “This calls for bigger hooks.” They rigged up with triple-strength 10/0 hooks.

Al got to the bottom first and brought the hooks up a couple of feet. The moment it stopped there was a strike.

What had happened before was child’s play. This fish took off on a 100-foot run against the heaviest drag Al had yet placed on the reel. But it finally pulled the fish to a stop, though it kept yanking and tugging, and Al got back some line. Then it went off again, showing all the strength of a hippo, slowly but steadily. Again the drag stopped him and pulled him upward and toward the boat.

“He’s the biggest one yet by 100 pounds,” Al said.

“He’s coming now,” said Harold. “I can see him down there.”

I looked into the water at what appeared to be a distorted blimp that changed, as it came higher, to the proportions of a fish, moving up fast. It hit the surface surrounded by bubbles bursting so hard you could hear them. Lying out there on his side, his air bladder inside out, this Warsaw grouper bulged a good foot above the surface. Jack and Al grabbed the leader and tried to lift.

“Can’t make it,” Jack said. “You fellows help.”

Harold and I jumped to the line. We heaved, got the head of the fish up, heaved some more, and had him coming. There was a nervous moment when he balanced on the gunwale, but we hauled again and skidded him into the boat.

“He’s a good 500 pounds,” puffed Al. “Look what’s coming out of his mouth.” An orange-colored crab with long, spidery legs had popped out of the fish’s mouth. Then another, and still more, poured from his inside-out stomach. We took 36 crabs out of that fish.

“Look at this,” said Jack. He reached down and pulled a two-foot long, whitish eel, twice as thick around as a pencil, from the lining of the stomach. “It’s petrified. Never saw anything like that before.”

Minutes later Jack hooked another Warsaw, again a 500-pounder.

Then something different picked up his bait. This fish, instead of playing the customary bottom-hugging tactics, moved forward through the water. “Must be a shark,” said Jack. “There he is.”

About 100 feet away something was thrashing the surface into whitecaps, and in the midst of spray we saw the tail of a shark flailing away. The motor drew him slowly our way and we soon saw that it was a sand shark, going about 300 pounds. A couple of 00 shotgun blasts took care of it.

By the time both rigs had hooked another 500 pounder each, daylight was running out, and we were all good and tired from heaving those big creatures aboard.

Al’s line got down first, and right off he had a strike that rocked the boat. This fish had the bit in its teeth. It made a 500-foot run, dodged, twisted, and yanked, turned over on the leader, brushed the wire against rocky outjuttings, and all but tore the machine from its moorings. That fish just didn’t want to come up. After half an hour, however, Al started to regain a bit of line.

“When he reaches a certain point where his bladder can’t take it,” Al said, “he’ll start his fall upstairs. Then we’ll have him.”

Up and up he came, then we saw him deep down, a twisted, whitish form that seemed as big as a submarine. He wasn’t atom-powered, but he was the next thing to it. Then he blew up.

We saw crabs come shooting out as he emptied his stomach, and an eel, and then we fell back because he came so fast it looked as if someone had thrown him at us. As I watched, an extra big Warsaw swam close to that great fish, opened its mouth and inhaled, and then the swordfish was gone. Only the tip of its tail stuck out the Warsaw’s mouth.

It rocked me, that sight. I jumped up in bed.

“Warsaw grouper!” I shouted. “They are the ones that break things up.”

“Go back to sleep,” came drowsily from Al. “Of course it’s the Warsaws. Today we found out.”

#3929. Scientists discover a surprising secret behind the human brain’s wrinkles

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Most primates have relatively large brains covered in distinctive folds and grooves. However, common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus) are an unusual exception. These small monkeys have brains that are almost entirely smooth, with very little folding. Scientists at the German Primate Center (DPZ), the Leibniz Institute for Primate Research in Göttingen, have now identified several cellular processes that help explain this difference.

Using miniature brain structures known as organoids, the researchers discovered that certain neural progenitor cells, which produce neurons, develop differently in marmosets than in humans. These differences involve the cells' structure, the speed at which they divide, and the timing of their development. Together, these changes reduce the number of neurons produced, contributing to the smaller size and smoother surface of the marmoset brain. Published in Science Advances, the findings provide new insights into primate brain evolution and could eventually improve our understanding of human brain development disorders.

Why Some Primate Brains Are Smooth Instead of Folded

Brain size and the extent of surface folding are important features that distinguish different primate species. The human brain, for example, has a highly folded surface resembling the ridges and grooves of a walnut. This arrangement greatly expands the brain's surface area, creating room for billions of neurons that support complex thinking and other advanced mental abilities.

Scientists believe that the common ancestor of modern primates already possessed a moderately sized brain with at least some folding. Yet not all primates living today retain these characteristics.

Common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus), small monkeys native to South America that are becoming increasingly important in biomedical research, have brains with almost no visible folds. Researchers at the German Primate Center set out to determine why their brains develop so differently from those of humans and other primates.

"We wanted to understand which changes at the cellular level cause the marmoset brain to grow less and form fewer folds," explains Lidiia Tynianskaia, one of the study's two first authors and a PhD student in the Junior Research Group Brain Development and Evolution at the DPZ. "At the beginning of development, the common marmoset brain exhibits the typical structure and composition of a large, folded primate brain. As development progresses, processes must therefore occur that effectively slow down the production of nerve cells."

Scientists Discover What Slows Brain Growth

To investigate these developmental differences, the team compared brain organoids grown from human and common marmoset cells. Their attention centered on neural progenitor cells, the cells responsible for generating neurons during brain development.

These progenitor cells play a major role in determining the eventual size and shape of the brain. The more neurons they produce, the greater the potential for the cerebral cortex to expand and develop folds.

The researchers found that the marmoset's smaller, smoother cerebral cortex is not the result of a single developmental change. Instead, several differences involving distinct types of progenitor cells work together to limit neuron production and brain folding.

"Our investigations have shown that certain progenitor cells in the common marmoset divide significantly more slowly than in humans," explains César Mateo Bastidas Betancourt, also a first author of the study and a PhD student in the Junior Research Group. "Other progenitor cells have a simpler structure than their human counterparts, with fewer processes, and are therefore less proliferative. Both of these factors ultimately result in fewer nerve cells, which contributes to a smaller size and less folding of the cerebral cortex in marmosets."

The scientists also identified important differences in when certain progenitor cell characteristics and behaviors emerge during development. Compared with humans, marmosets have a shorter overall period during which these cells can multiply rapidly.

Together, the slower rate of cell division, simpler cell structures, and shorter window for rapid growth help explain why the marmoset brain produces fewer neurons and develops far fewer folds.

Miniature Brains Reveal Important Developmental Clues

Much of the research relied on brain organoids, small, three-dimensional structures grown from cells that reproduce important features of early brain development. These miniature models allowed scientists to investigate developmental changes across multiple samples and compare the timing of key processes.

"The study combines the advantages of in vivo and in vitro methods," says Michael Heide, head of the Junior Research Group Brain Development and Evolution. "Organoids are well-suited for obtaining statistically robust results because such sample sizes are not feasible in primates. We subsequently repeated some key experiments in fetal brain tissue to confirm the results from the organoids."

One particularly important finding involved the relationship between development in organoids and development in actual marmoset brains. The researchers determined that a marmoset brain organoid grown for 50 days closely matches the developmental stage of a natural marmoset brain at day 90.

Establishing this relationship was essential because brain organoids and actual brains do not necessarily develop at the same rate. By identifying corresponding developmental stages, the scientists could make meaningful comparisons and interpret their experimental findings more reliably.

What Smooth Monkey Brains Reveal About Human Brain Evolution

The findings help explain how relatively small differences in the behavior and developmental timing of brain-building cells can contribute to major differences in brain size and folding among primates.

They also offer a potential explanation for how the wide variety of primate brain shapes and sizes emerged over evolutionary time, including why some species developed relatively smooth brains while others possess much larger, heavily folded ones.

Beyond their evolutionary significance, these discoveries provide a foundation for investigating how the human brain develops and what happens when those processes are disrupted. A better understanding of the cellular mechanisms that control neuron production and brain growth could ultimately help researchers study developmental disorders affecting the human brain.

Climate change is making Earth progressively rounder, new research finds.

The findings are a signal of human-caused climate change, however, and tracking the shape of the Earth can help reveal just how much impact people are having on its ice caps.

Our planet is not a perfect sphere. Because of its rotation, it is a bit flatter at the poles and bulges in the middle. But the new study reveals that Earth is getting rounder at an accelerated rate, largely due to shifts caused by the melting polar ice caps.

This finding applies only to the solid parts of the planet; the ocean continues to bulge outward at the equator.

The study, published Sept. 9 in the journal JGR Solid Earth, used direct measurements from the global navigation satellite system (GNSS) to track the planet's shape-shifting over time. This system provides direct measurements, but there are limits in how it can be used. For example, there are few GNSS stations near the North and South poles and few in remote oceans because they must be deployed on buoys.

Christoforos Kotsakis, a professor of geodesy at the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki in Greece, developed a new method for analyzing GNSS data to better capture the changes in the planet's surface. He found that the poles are rising and the equator's bulge has been shrinking, each by about 0.04 inch (1 millimeter) per year between 2011 to 2015. That's a small number, considering Earth's diameter at the equator is about 7,926 miles (12,756 kilometers). But that change is bigger than between 1997-2000, when Earth was becoming about 0.02 inch (0.5 mm) rounder per year. This means Earth's shape is rounding at 0.016 inch (0.4 mm) more per year with each decade that passes.

The shift is being driven by polar ice melt, which takes pressure off the Earth's crust and allows it to rise slowly, similar to how rising bread dough springs back after you press a finger into it, Kotsakis reported. Meanwhile, increased mass toward the equator puts pressure on the crust there, reducing its bulge. However, there's a catch: When considering the solid Earth and the ocean together, the planet is getting flatter by about 0.03 inches (0.8 mm) a year because the ocean is getting bulgier at the equator as water shifts away from the poles. This 'water weight' overwhelms the rounding happening in the rock below.

Struggling to create Facebook, Instagram, or TikTok ads that people remember long enough to buy from you? Wondering how consumer psychology can tell you why your winning ads work and how to repeat that success?

In this article, you’ll discover how to use behavioral science and consumer psychology to create social ads by deciding how to deliver a message, who to aim it at, and where it fits in the customer journey.

Sarah Levinger says the biggest misconception about ads is that every ad is supposed to sell. Conversion matters, especially in e-commerce, but for most brands, the core job of advertising is to build recall. A customer who doesn’t remember a brand exists has nowhere to convert to and ends up buying whatever shows up in their feed. Sarah recommends designing ads to target both recall and conversion simultaneously.

​Behavioral science makes that possible because it explains why a piece of creative works. When marketers can identify the psychological reason an ad or organic post resonated, they can replicate that success rather than guessing. Sarah sees the results reflected in lower-cost customers, longer-lasting ads, access to audiences a brand has never reached before, and less decision fatigue about what to create next.

​The way ads get distributed raises the stakes. TikTok built its feed on interest graphs, and the approach now extends across the major platforms. An interest graph serves content based on whether the platform predicts someone will be interested in it, rather than on whether they follow the creator or have searched for a term. The algorithm learns from behavior such as watching, liking, commenting, sharing, and saving, and it updates in real-time as moods shift. Sarah’s own feed serves intense content during anxious weeks and cooking and sewing tutorials on relaxed Saturdays. Because marketers can’t dictate who sees their content, every concept, organic or paid, has to be built for interest and taste rather than topics and keywords.

​Sarah’s process for building that kind of creative follows three decisions in order: how the message is delivered, who it targets, and where it fits in the customer journey. All three start from a defined core message. If a brand like Nike has a core message of pushing through and doing it, the three steps determine how that message gets expressed in each ad.

The first decision is whether the message should be positive or negative and how intense it should feel. Sarah makes this call using the valence and intensity model, also called the valence and arousal model.

​Valence is the emotional range a message activates, from positive (happy, comforting) to negative (sad, frustrating, disappointing). It’s a range, not a binary. Intensity is how strongly the message is delivered, and it affects how the message’s valence is interpreted and how important it feels.

A reminder to take out the trash can be positive and low-intensity (“I’m so sorry, I need your help with this, I completely forgot”) or negative and high-intensity (“You’ve missed the trash five times. I need you to step it up”). Each version prompts a different response depending on the listener’s mood.

These choices matter because emotional messages are encoded in the brain more strongly than logical, factual ones. The chemicals the brain produces in response to an emotional experience, positive or negative, tell it what to remember and what to ignore. That’s why emotion drives recall.

Sarah sets valence based on category psychology: what the product is and what’s being sold. Some categories respond best to negative messaging. A carbon monoxide detector or fire alarm ad that promises “protect your family, and everything’s going to be great” won’t land. The negative version is far more salient: there’s a silent, invisible danger in the home, and without a detector, people risk harming their own family by accident.

Intensity is a brand decision rather than a category decision. Brands whose positioning is supportive, essentially “we’re not trying to trigger you; we just want to help you feel better,” should stay in low intensity all year. Sarah says those brands rarely change intensity, and it works because that’s how the brand is interpreted.

​Pro Tip: Single-word swaps can shift valence and intensity enough to move revenue. For about 18 months, the watch brand Original Grain ran ads telling customers they “deserved” to treat themselves to a watch. Sarah’s research showed customers didn’t feel they deserved anything; they felt they’d earned it. Changing “deserve” to “earned” across all of the brand’s messaging was a six-figure change. “Deserve” is passive. “Earned” is active.

The second decision is who the message targets. Sarah doesn’t use customer avatars. She calls them an overused crutch: a fabricated customer who doesn’t exist, used to make large business decisions. An e-commerce brand might sell to an 18-year-old and a 57-year-old, people of every gender and political affiliation, and people who own five dogs alongside people who hate dogs. Instead, Sarah targets a version of the self. Her model is based on self-discrepancy theory, which holds that every person has three versions of themselves and switches between them throughout the day. Each one is tied to a different time period.

​Ideal Self: The future-focused, aspirational self. Messaging about a better version of the customer, doing better tomorrow, or changing their life targets the ideal self.

​Actual Self: The present-day self, which is biased and wants its existing beliefs validated. Actual-self messaging doesn’t ask anyone to change; it affirms who they already are. Sarah often sees eco-friendly and environmentally focused brands activate the actual self.

​Instead, she maps creative to the Messy Middle, a Google study of how people shop in digital ecosystems. It identified four behaviors people move through when they purchase: trigger, exploration, evaluation, and purchase. Every piece of content fits into one of the four.

The problem is either acute (sudden, unexpected, and irritating, like a burst pipe) or chronic (something they’ve avoided for a long time). Trigger content should speak to one of those two problem types and address the person’s experience at a high level rather than the product.

Point-of-view content is one of the best formats here (“Your house just flooded because a pipe burst. What are you going to do? You’re going to call Hank,” followed by the pitch), and day-in-the-life content also works (a stay-at-home mom trying to get the plumbing fixed before school pickup at 3 p.m.). Both rely on storytelling about the trigger moment.

​Exploration Content: In this stage, people explore the industry, not the brand. They’re asking what creatine is, how protein works, or whether hydroxyapatite matters in toothpaste. Across the more than 150 brands Sarah has worked with, the exploration content that performs best covers what to avoid (“don’t believe the hype about this product”) or things people don’t know.

Long-form, documentary-style content in the vein of How It’s Made fits here. Deep researchers can stay in this stage for a long time, so skipping exploration content overlooks a large portion of the potential audience. This content should deliver facts, proof, stats, numbers, ingredients, and how those ingredients work, all in service of the brand’s unique selling proposition (USP). A brand that doesn’t clearly differ from competitors has to win on personality by being interesting, fun, or likable.

Duolingo competes with many language apps and wins evaluations largely through content built around its characters and mascot. Sarah expects AI to change this stage, since shoppers who use AI to evaluate products may compare more than two options.

​Purchase Content: The shopper is waiting for an excuse to buy from a specific brand. Purchase-stage ads give them that excuse.

Kushner first revealed the project in 2024, and in May this year, Ivanka Trump excitedly discussed the project in a podcast interview, stating it is “almost daunting in its size”.

No architect is currently officially connected to the project. Berlin studio Laboratory for Visionary Architecture (LAVA) previously developed a proposal for the peninsula site, but is no longer working on the project, while US website Architect’s Newspaper ascertained that architecture studios BIG, Kengo Kuma & Associates, Ateliers Jean Nouvel, K-studio and Emre Arolat Architecture had previously been commissioned to develop a framework. BIG has since said it is no longer involved.

The Albanian government is under fire for supporting the contentious project and for passing a law allowing so-called “strategic investors” to build in the protected landscapes. The European Parliament applied pressure on the Albanian Government to repeal this law in June.

Dutch politician Strik has since accused Rama of “trying to help realise the megalomaniacal luxury resort plans of Ivanka Trump and Jared Kushner” and claimed he is fast-tracking the development to avoid EU environmental rules before the country, which is an EU candidate seeking membership, is bound to them.

Albania is expected to join the EU by 2030. Strik suggested its actions could compromise the country’s EU accession process.

“I accuse Rama of serving his own interests and the interests of Kushner and not the interests of the environment and the Albanian people,” MEP Strik told the Guardian.

“The government clearly intends to finalise the procedures before it is legally bound by EU standards,” she continued.

The development is the focus of the Flamingo Revolution

“Such sneaky and disloyal practices should be strongly criticised by the EU. If Albania seriously wants to be a reliable candidate member state, it should respect EU law and not try to circumvent it,” added Strik.

The MEP’s accusations come amid broader anti-government protests in the capital city, Tirana, which are an escalation of months of peaceful demonstrations linked to the Flamingo Revolution.

The Flamingo Revolution is an environmental movement that began in opposition to the high-profile resort in June after groundworks commenced. It is named after the protected bird species found within the landscapes earmarked for development.

“If the Albanian government had taken the Flamingo Revolution seriously, we wouldn’t be here,” Strik said.

The Guardian reported that Kushner has claimed the goal is “to build to the highest design and environmental standards and leave this extraordinary part of Albania greener, more biodiverse and environmentally stronger”.

However, an ecologist at the BirdLife Albania organisation who is among those expressing concerns about the project told the BBC that it “would completely destroy that wild region”.

In an exclusive interview with Dezeen, Rama spoke of his belief in the power of architecture to make positive change in Albania.

“I am a big believer in architecture and the power of architecture to influence the way we live,” Rama said.

“I think that for a country like Albania, good architecture is an uplifting presence – it’s a source of extra energy for the society and for the way we envisage our future.”
