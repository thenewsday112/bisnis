How underwater exploration can help prepare astronauts for the challenges of spacewalks.

I was breathing just fine through my life support systems, but I kept feeling an urge to double check that everything was ok with my "spacesuit" before heading out on this "spacewalk." I have worn many different types of spacesuits on Earth during my career, but for the first time in my life, I knew that I couldn’t just stop to breathe the air outside if there were an emergency. That's because I was underwater.

On this excursion, I was leading a simulated space mission underwater called SEA STAR. My "spacesuit" was actually scuba diving gear, and the simulated spacewalks, or EVAs (Extra-Vehicular Activities), included outings where we explored underwater environments. Being underwater, if we encountered a major problem with our gear, we would have had to treat it as seriously as if we were astronauts on the International Space Station. There was no immediate "out." This was serious.

Before this underwater mission, I had worked as the commander of nearly 40 analog missions that simulated crewed missions to the moon and Mars. The goal of these types of missions is to test what living and working in lunar or Martian environments might be like. We study everything from the psychology of living in extreme conditions, or in isolation, to the technology that astronauts might use to complete those missions successfully.

Testing spacesuit technology is also an important part of these analog space missions. These suits are usually a much simpler version of the ones astronauts use in space, which can weigh around 310 pounds (141 kilograms) when fully equipped with life support systems.

Analog spacesuits usually weigh a maximum of 30 pounds (14 kg). While there are many differences between suits designed for use in space and these suits designed for testing on Earth, the biggest one is that, on an analog mission, crew members can open their helmets and breathe the outside air if necessary.

Astronauts in space don’t have that advantage. But neither do aquanauts, those performing a simulated space mission underwater.

Aquanauts spend at least 24 hours underwater in a so-called state of saturation. That’s a physiological condition achieved by a person who breathes underwater long enough for the body’s tissues to absorb the maximum possible amount of inert gas (like nitrogen), for a specific depth and pressure.

Water leaking into our suits or into our underwater habitat can pose serious risks for the crew, as well as health problems related to the higher pressures underwater, amongst other challenges unique to this environment.

This is what pushed me to be extra thorough, and perhaps even a bit paranoid, when preparing for my scuba diving "spacewalks." While scuba diving, the dangers of your equipment failing or decompression sickness can be as lethal as some of the difficulties astronauts face during their spacewalks. That’s why we follow strict protocols when preparing to go on underwater EVAs and during the outings themselves.

As an experienced scuba diver, I wasn’t concerned with the scuba diving aspect of the mission itself. In many ways, it was much easier than some dives in pitch black water I performed in the open ocean or cave dives. But for these EVAs, I was more on edge because I was the Commander of the SEA STAR crew living 22 feet (7 meters) underwater in the station, called the Jules’ Undersea Lab, in Florida. Being responsible for other people’s lives made me extra cautious and also more worried than usual when diving.

Despite the many challenges of these underwater EVAs, these experiences and lessons learned can teach us a lot about what we still need to improve for future long-duration space missions.

Our aquanaut "spacesuits" were essentially the same as scuba gear, with the exception of two important accessories that aren’t always necessary for normal dives. Those were gloves and flashlights. We needed the gloves as protection from a number of factors in the environment around us, like barnacles and other sharp natural formations. They also made using cameras and vacuums for environmental cleaning underwater more challenging, which was a good approximation for how gloves limit astronauts’ dexterity during spacewalks.

The underwater environment around us had very low visibility, which is why the flashlights were a mandatory component of our EVAs. They helped us to navigate through the darkness and find each other. Even still, with just the small flash of light we could only see a couple of feet ahead of us. That is why it was also vital that we worked together and moved side-by-side.

Good communication skills were another crucial component of our underwater simulated space mission. Before each EVA, we prepared a plan, talked about backup plans and went over emergency procedures. During the EVA, we used standard scuba diving sign language to communicate, and we created some new mission-specific signals as well. This visual communication is very useful during simulated space missions on land, too, especially in situations when our communications technologies have not worked properly.

No matter how much we prepared, though, the fear of the unknown and of getting lost can still get to a crew. A common safety protocol when diving is to safely return to the surface if you get lost or separated from your team and cannot find them. However, during the SEA STAR mission, we planned on staying underwater for the full five-day duration of our mission. That meant that such an option was out of the question, except of course in emergency scenarios.

Personally, I enjoyed the challenge of the low visibility in new terrain. It allowed me to hone my orientation and navigation skills, as well as my abilities as a leader in challenging environments. Though I was more vigilant than I perhaps needed to be, once I dove out of our submerged station, I went straight into my favorite mode: exploration. Despite not being able to see very much ahead of me, and all while keeping a watchful eye on my crewmembers, I finally truly felt like an astronaut on a spacewalk. Darkness was all around me, but my determination and curiosity propelled me toward exploring the unknown.

#4391. A WNBA championship before lunch? Fans call out Game 6 scheduling.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Golden State Valkyries could win their first WNBA championship on Thursday morning. Watching them do it might require an awkward conversation with your boss: The WNBA announced Monday that Game 6 of the Finals against the Atlanta Dream will tip off at 12:30 p.m. ET on Oct. 29 — 9:30 a.m. in the Bay Area, where the Valkyries are based.

Valkyries guard Veronica Burton has a suggestion. "Call in sick. I recommend it," she said after Golden State’s Monday-night win, according to reporter Annie Costabile. That victory gave the team a 3–2 series lead and a chance to clinch a title in the franchise’s second season — provided its fans can clear their work calendars to watch.

The issue comes down to two teams needing the same arena. The Dream's usual home, Gateway Center Arena, holds approximately 3,500 spectators. Atlanta hosted Finals Games 3 and 4 at the roughly 17,000-seat State Farm Arena instead, selling out both games. Keeping Game 6 there, however, meant working around the Atlanta Hawks, who were already scheduled to host the Cleveland Cavaliers on Thursday evening.

The solution? A doubleheader. Move the WNBA game from 8 p.m. to 12:30 p.m. and delay the Hawks’ tipoff by an hour, from 7 p.m. to 8 p.m. Commissioner Cathy Engelbert thanked the teams, leagues, arena, and broadcaster "for the collaboration and flexibility that made this move possible."

Fans were less enthusiastic about the flexibility now required of them.

"How are we supposed to watch this game in the middle of the workday?? This is so strange to me," one commenter asked on Reddit's r/wnba. Another Atlanta-based commenter said they had planned to attend but now couldn't even watch live. "I’d rather them play at Gateway so at least folks could watch it in real time," they wrote.

Flavor Flav offered fans some help getting the afternoon off, posting a signed permission slip to give their bosses. "Consider it your contribution to gender equality," he wrote in the document.

Commentators across sports offered their own (rather heated) opinions. To Richard Deitsch, host of the Sports Media Podcast, the timing threatened to undercut the moment the season had been building toward. "The prospect of crowning a champion on a Thursday afternoon is just brutal optics (and will be brutal television ratings.)," he wrote on X.

The Ringer founder Bill Simmons sounded similarly exasperated with league leadership, writing, "People want to like this league!"

Their frustration comes after a season of record audience growth for the WNBA. According to the league, 51 regular-season telecasts averaged at least one million viewers, up from 19 last year. Its social accounts generated four billion views, with engagement up 24 percent from 2025. The demand is there, which helps explain why making a potential championship game harder to watch has struck such a nerve.

For some critics, the decision also sends a message about how the players are valued. Former NFL player and commentator Emmanuel Acho called it "high-key disrespectful" to the sport and its athletes.

In the Reddit discussion, some fans also questioned why a women's Finals game had to work around a men's regular-season matchup in the first place, with many finding the whole ordeal wildly "mysoginistic" and exposed a blaring double standard. "If this were a men’s game, this time would never even be considered," one Reddit commenter said. Another commenter in r/WNBATalks wrote, "If society was less misogynistic, it wouldn’t be an issue."

Women's sports creator and podcast host Coach Jackie also questioned the decision in a video posted to Instagram. "Somebody could become a WNBA champion over brunch? During lunch? While you’re in a Teams meeting? Did you think it through?" she asked.

The players, meanwhile, have been more measured. Dream guard Jordin Canada welcomed the chance to play in front of another State Farm Arena crowd, while teammate Allisha Gray kept her focus on what was at stake.

"It’s the Finals, you gotta do what you gotta do," Gray told the San Francisco Chronicle. "We can play at 8 a.m., 10 a.m., midnight, you have a championship to win."

#1731. LinkedIn launches data insights hub

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The platform’s Economic Graph has been renamed LinkedIn Pulse and reimagined as a channel for global labor market analysis.

LinkedIn has announced the relaunch of its Economic Graph insights element, which will now be called LinkedIn Pulse.

As explained by LinkedIn: “Opportunity is changing. Understanding where it’s moving has never mattered more. That’s why today, the LinkedIn Economic Graph becomes LinkedIn Pulse. LinkedIn Pulse brings together data and insights from across LinkedIn’s 1.4 billion members, 71 million companies and 42,000 skills to help businesses, governments and people understand how work is changing right now, where opportunity is emerging, and what to do next.”

LinkedIn CMO Jessica Jensen said the updated Pulse hub provides a new place to help people make sense of how work is changing, where opportunity is emerging and what that means for them.

As per Jensen:  “Our economists have a real-time view into the global labor market, helping companies, policymakers, governments, and the media understand where work is headed. And we’re bringing that insight to all of you with Pulse, to help you make smarter decisions about your career, team or business.”

LinkedIn is also expanding its content and analysis by partnering with journalists, including Scalable founder Kaya Yurieff, to break down specific elements of economic opportunity, such as the impact of artificial intelligence on the workforce.

The new Pulse resource could provide valuable data notes and trend insights, though the name is a little confusing because LinkedIn Pulse was previously the name of LinkedIn’s creator publishing platform.

When LinkedIn initially launched its long-form content creation tools in 2015under the moniker LinkedIn Pulse, creators were very keen to publish their content in the app, with the hopes of getting viral traction when selected by the Pulse algorithm.

But changes to content distribution made that idea less appealing over time, and the name “Pulse” seemingly fell by the wayside.

But now it’s back, in a new form, with LinkedIn using the title to bring new energy to its internal insights.

On Monday, OpenAI announced impressive breakthroughs in mathematics, computer science, and physics, along with what the company says is an evolving approach to communicating its findings. But despite its apparent course correction, researchers who spoke to The Verge are growing increasingly tired of the company’s antics.

It has been a turbulent few months between OpenAI and mathematicians, who are still grappling with the hundreds of preprints unceremoniously dumped on them at the start of October. This time, OpenAI says it is working with expert “shepherds” to decide how best to share its latest breakthroughs.

All three results concern some of the most consequential unsolved problems in mathematics, computer science, and physics. Some extend OpenAI’s previous work and others break new ground. Following advice from OpenAI’s “shepherds,” each has a different release strategy. For the mathematics result, OpenAI released the AI-generated output, “leaving researchers free to organize their own efforts to explain the result.” For its computer science finding, OpenAI will also assemble an external group of researchers “to explain the result.” Physics, meanwhile, has not been released at all, and OpenAI said it will “defer” to an external group of experts about how to release the materials.

Given the varying levels of disclosure and the complexity of evaluating such work, it is difficult for researchers to fully judge the company’s findings. Even with all of the information, doing so would take time. But experts who spoke to The Verge said there are good reasons to take the results seriously, variously describing them as “huge,” “major breakthroughs,” and “significant.”

“It doesn’t make any sense that first only a few distinguished scientists can look at them.”

But researchers’ excitement about the science was tempered by exasperation with OpenAI’s conduct, a familiar feeling after months of what they describe as an exhausting barrage of AI-generated findings. Many were eager for the wider community to get on with the arduous task of scrutinizing the AI lab’s findings and questioned why OpenAI was putting up additional barriers by handpicking experts to oversee the process.

Most experts The Verge spoke to welcomed OpenAI’s decision to consult outside researchers, but argued that publicly announcing results should also mean making that work available for anyone to scrutinize. “I am fine with them releasing the problems as they did today,” said Constantin Kogler, a researcher at the Institute for Advanced Study, welcoming the more manageable batch of three results compared with the hundreds released previously. But he questioned the decision to restrict access to experts of OpenAI’s choosing. “It doesn’t make any sense that first only a few distinguished scientists can look at them,” he said.

“The fact that they handpick the group is a disaster,” Andreas Karch, a physics professor at the University of Texas at Austin, told The Verge. “The large labs have enough power as it is. They should not be picking winners and losers.” Karch initially suggested selecting researchers through a “competitive process,” though he acknowledged it would likely produce a similar group, which he described as “first rate.” Later, he questioned why OpenAI “can’t release the proof now,” in addition to appointing its team of experts. “This would democratize access while still showing that they care about making the results meaningful and understandable,” he said.

“My sense is that for any approach, there is going to be someone who is upset.”

It is exactly the kind of gatekeeping the Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence (AGMAI), a group of experts OpenAI had previously pledged to consult on how to share its findings, warned against just a day before the announcement. “We recommend that AI companies do not adopt this practice,” they said, arguing that selecting certain people to receive the output first is “unnecessary,” borrows the reputation of selected researchers, and risks unequal access, biased selection, and delays, among other issues. Instead, they recommended that AI labs release output on scholarly repositories, with formalized proofs and information on how the problems were chosen, and provide funding and other support for people working on understanding the results.

Following the publication of the latest results, AGMAI urged OpenAI to “make public without delay the natural-language and Lean outputs behind its announced” results in physics:

”While these are central questions in physics, they are also important questions in mathematics. The existence of quantum Yang–Mills theory is part of a Millennium Prize problem and the construction of interacting quantum field theories in four dimensions has been a goal of mathematical physics for decades.

We have great respect for the scientists asked by OpenAI to lead this work. But as we explained in our recent statement, understanding should develop in the open, with everyone given the same access. OpenAI have said they delayed release until the Lean formalization was complete. By their own account this is now complete, so there is no scientific reason for delaying the release any further.”

Making the findings openly available could also lead to better science and encourage wider adoption of new tools like AI, Hassett said. “Having many groups around the world wrestle with these results supports rapid adoption of new tools by those that would like to use them,” he said.

”Fundamental results are being solved so quickly without human contribution ... the papers are difficult to read and verify.”

Finding a way to communicate with researchers in the fields OpenAI is increasingly encroaching on that satisfies everyone may be impossible. The company says it is experimenting with different approaches and acknowledges that each has tradeoffs. “My sense is that for any approach, there is going to be someone who is upset,” said Stanford mathematician Jared Duker Lichtman. “So it’s just a question of finding the least offensive option.”

Xiaoyu He, a mathematics professor at the Georgia Institute of Technology, thinks the problem is deeper than how OpenAI chooses to announce its findings. ”The results are very impressive,” he said, “but I think they are making a show of trying to be responsible and thoughtful about how they release results while not being able to change the main issues: that fundamental results are being solved so quickly without human contribution, and that the papers are difficult to read and verify.”

#941. We Can’t Get Enough of All of the Art on the Spring 2027 Runways

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

“Is fashion art?” is the perennial question in this industry. Designers are inherently creative people, who, both out of natural habit and in perpetual search for inspiration, are constantly engaging with different mediums and the cool communities that surround them. What is changing is the public prominence of these conversations, exemplified by the creators with which they partner, such as Amelia, a 32-year-old TikTok art historian and Columbia student better known as Meelz on Art, whose analysis has gotten her seated at shows by Loewe and Dior.

“I think art is probably the largest, most influential cultural movement on fashion designers, on designers of all kinds,” Dr. Elizabeth Way, curator of Art X Fashion at the Museum at FIT, tells Vogue. For proof, look no further than brands like Louis Vuitton and Dior who flexed their cultural prowess with cruise shows at The Frick and LACMA, respectively, earlier this year. But you don’t need a museum to make a connection. Vogue’s Laird Borrelli-Persson combed through over 400 of our spring 2027 reviews for explicit art callouts. Naturally wide-ranging—some Dadaism here, some 18th-century-inspired contemporary art there—the list provides a fun window into designers’ brains.

Some polar opposite sources can produce strikingly complementary results. Lii’s love for Alex Katz’s graphic flair is miles away from Loewe’s references to 17th century Spanish painters Francisco de Zurbarán and Diego Velázquez (the former of which is now on view at the Louvre), and yet both spring 2027 collections were filled with unique visions of futuristic sportswear. “Loewe was especially intriguing because it was so paradoxical,” says Amelia. “It was at once extremely contemporary and simultaneously steeped in tradition. Many of the pieces were bright and saturated and exhibited severe, geometric purity. This showed up in dramatic drapery a la Zurbarán and courtly embellishment, like the clothes in a Velázquez portrait.”

At Dior things followed in a more traditional vein. “The most explicit art historical connection in his recent show came in the form of Jonathan Anderson’s replication of an 18th-century wooden chair from his personal collection which served as seats for some of the guests,” Amelia says. That said, the airy refinement of the clothing took on the precise motion blur of Gerhard Richter’s paintings. For further classical tradition, Bora Aksu drew inspiration from 17th-century portrait artist Mary Beale, while Zimmermann’s Nicky Zimmermann looked to the 19th-century natural history illustrators Harriet and Helena Scott.

Alexander Calder at the Tate Gallery, London.

In contrast, the whimsical Alexander Calder popped up as inspiration on multiple occasions, both at Givenchy, where Sarah Burton drew reference from Cirque Calder, and at Michael Kors, who zeroed in on the more mod aspects of the sculptor’s shapes. Rachel Comey’s 25th anniversary show was an ode to the visual artist and novelist Rosalyn Drexler, and Kiko Kostadinov took on an experimental edge with the works of Italian artists Agostino Bonalumi and Nanda Vigo.

Meanwhile, Prada’s 63 skirts made a subtle nod to one of its own traveling exhibitions from the early 2000s, titled “Waist Down.” “We used the skirt as a framework for different ideas, different approaches, like a raw material to express concepts,” said Miuccia Prada in the show notes. “The limitation of its simple shape, almost like a canvas, can force ideas, make you reinvent the form within its strict confines.” The skirts’ gradient dyed leather was rather reminiscent of the emotionally charged works of Mark Rothko.

“Art teaches clothing how to transcend its necessity,” says Anne Higonnet, art historian and professor at Barnard. “Every day, people out there are using color, shape, texture, composition, and rhythm to go beyond covering our vulnerable, knobby, oozing, mobile anatomies, beyond even expressing our position in society.” If you’re going to wear it, you might as well know the reference, too.

#3406. How to Make Certain Your Body is Prepared for This Common Yet Intense Backbend

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

In Yoga Journal’s Archives series, we share a curated collection of articles originally published in past issues beginning in 1975. This article about how to do Upward-Facing Dog Pose first appeared in the May-June 2003 issue of Yoga Journal. You can find more of our Archives here.

Before doing Urdhva Mukha Svanasana, it is wise to prepare your body for the intense spinal bending this pose requires. One way to prepare for the intensity of the backbend in is to first progress through somewhat easier versions of backbends. In each of the following poses, you must engage your quadriceps to straighten the legs, which energizes the pelvis. To lengthen and protect the back of the lumbar spine in these poses, you must also draw your tailbone away from the lower spine and lift the pit of your abdomen toward your sternum. (The pit of the abdomen is at the bottom of the belly, just above the pubic bone.) And you must draw your kidney energy through the body into your upper chest to create a lift and an opening in your lungs, ribs, and diaphragm.

In such poses, the lumbar spine becomes crunched because we fail to use the buttocks and the pit of the abdomen correctly. Although contraction of the buttocks pulls the back side of the pelvis away from the lumbar spine, helping to prevent crunching or collapsing in the lower back, contracting the buttocks also compresses the sacroiliac joints and causes external rotation in the thighs, which are undesirable actions in Bhujangasana and Urdhva Mukha Svanasana. What to do?

My solution is first to press the outer edge of the feet into the floor and only then, maintaining this action, to contract the buttocks. Doing these actions in this order creates the pelvic tilt required to prevent compression in the lumbar spine, without compressing the sacroiliac joints or causing external rotation in the thighs.

Lie face down, feet 6-8 inches apart. Place your hands slightly in front of your shoulders, fingers spread and index fingers pointing straight ahead. Root your thighs, insteps, and the little toe sides of your feet, then contract your buttocks. Keep your feet six to eight inches apart, toes pointing straight back. Inhale and press your hands into the floor while pulling your spine forward and opening your chest. Straighten your elbows as much as possible. Pull the top of your head up and slightly back while drawing your spine toward the front of your body in Bhujangasana 1 (Cobra). Create a powerful pull from your perineum toward your heart center and from the bottom of your belly toward your sternum.

Also try to press your pubis and thighs fully into the floor. Your weight should be borne by your legs and hands, not your belly. Keep your elbows slightly bent while you press your shoulder blades down. Contract your quadriceps strongly, and create the lift of the perineum called mula bandha (root lock) by pulling energy from your pelvic floor toward your diaphragm. Lift your chin toward the ceiling unless you have neck problems; if that is the case, keep your neck in line with your spine. Firmly straighten your elbows (Figure 1).

You can also practice a variation of Cobra in which you simply lift your head and chest without putting weight on your hands. This prepares your back muscles for the deeper work ahead.

From Bhujangasana I (above), bend your elbows and move them away from each other. Then powerfully draw the sides of your waist forward and upward so that your armpits slide forward and are exposed. Hug your chest with your upper arms, squeeze your elbows in, and then slowly straighten your arms and pull your chest forward. Draw energy from your kidneys toward your upper chest and press your shoulder blades down to lift your spine. Lift your sternum as far as it will go, moving its energy into your chin. #1117. Watch <i><em>9-1-1 </em></i>for the Volcano Eruptions and Bee-nadoes. Stay for the Heart.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The first responder drama understands grief and the power of found families better than any other series on TV.

We all have our guilty pleasures: TV shows that we would never admit enjoying, binged in darkened rooms in the privacy of our homes. For over a decade, mine has been 9-1-1. The procedural drama by Ryan Murphy and the creators of American Horror Story centers around Los Angeles first responders including firemen, paramedics, and 9-1-1 operators. The calls they respond to are always insane, ranging from rescuing a baby flushed down the toilet to a highway shark attack. As 9-1-1 actor Peter Krause recently told Esquire, “I’m racing up ladders, jumping on fire escapes, going by exploding balls of fire, and diving underwater.” It’s campy action at its finest.

But 9-1-1 is also a tough show to stay up to date with—there are over 140 episodes across 10 seasons, not including the spin-offs. Critics call it trashy but, really, it’s absurd excellence. Me and the 9 million average viewers of the most recent season can’t be wrong. 9-1-1 is epic, must watch television. So, after a brief time away, I returned to 9-1-1 because I heard that something crazy occurred, even by 9-1-1’s standards. And no, I’m not talking about the volcano eruption.

Midway through season 8, 9-1-1 killed off a major character. For seven years on TV, Peter Krause played the soul of the fire station, Captain Bobby Nash. Then, in season 8, episode 15, he contracts a deadly fever during a chemical lab outbreak and decides to quarantine himself inside. Before he dies, he hands the sole antidote to his appointed successor, Howard “Chimney” Han (Kenneth Choi). Major character deaths are not new to TV. Game of Thrones famously dispatched characters like the series was pulling petals off daisies while playing, “He loves me, he loves me not.” But after eight seasons spent with Krause’s character basically serving as the paternal guiding hand of the series, it was an emotional blow.

Around the same time, I received news that my grandfather had terminal late-stage cancer. Due to distance—I live in New York City, while he lives in China—I realized that I would be unable to travel to say goodbye at his side. It was devastating, and I didn’t know how I would manage roughly 7,000 miles away from my family. We watch TV to be entertained and be comforted from the stress of our daily lives. We also tend to create parasocial relationships with our favorite TV characters. So, following my grandfather’s diagnosis, mourning the loss of someone so central to your family like the fictional Captain Nash helped me to grieve.

I originally avoided the show the best I could once images leaked of the cast carrying a casket through the streets of Los Angeles. Usually, 9-1-1’s promotion is enough to draw you back in. You’ve most likely seen the viral ads for 9-1-1 containing bee-nadoes, tsunamis on Santa Monica, or giant inflatable runaway pumpkins. But despite the wacky premises, 9-1-1 was something that I was relegated to watching alone—mostly because my boyfriend would groan and exit the room every time I had it on our TV. Plus, when I bring it up amongst friends and family, I’m usually met with raised brows and derision. But I’m tired of pretending that 9-1-1 isn’t amazing TV. It isn’t solely the ridiculous emergencies that keeps me watching: It’s the genuine portrayal of the first responders and fire station’s found family.

Following Captain Nash’s death, the most recent season focused on the aftermath among those he left behind. The tremendous loss affected every character on the show, including the tough but vulnerable lead, police officer Athena Grant (Angela Bassett). As Grant, Bassett faces an array of life-or-death situations with compassion and ferocity every episode. Together with Krause’s Captain Nash, who played her husband, they faced a lot of fires and even one cruise ship invaded by pirates. They got through it by sticking together.

Aside from the leads, 9-1-1 also features the strong and smart Henrietta Wilson (Aisha Hinds). Jennifer Love Hewitt plays Maddie Buckley, a 9-1-1 operator and domestic abuse survivor. Her husband, Han, steps up as the new captain of the firehouse. But the character I find most compelling is Evan “Buck” Buckley (Oliver Stark), the impulsive, adrenaline chasing firefighter.

Buck is the beating heart of the show, starting out as a brash and immature member of the firehouse before growing into a more level-headed and responsible emergency responder. His close friendship with the single-father firefighter, Edmundo “Eddie” Diaz (Ryan Guzman), is constantly shipped by fans of the show in a fervor that would make Spock and Kirk stans blush. (The passion from fans rooting for the two to get together has only increased since Buck came out as bisexual in season seven.) But the show wants you to root for them, often spotlighting how the two characters would literally die in a blaze of glory just to save the other.

Throughout my years watching 9-1-1, these characters became something of my found family as well. Seeing every character struggle with the loss of Captain Nash in their own ways—like reconnecting with religion, numbing themselves with substance abuse, or facing their fears—helped me accept that my grief might manifest differently than I expected. It was cathartic to see all the ways that the pain would be survivable with the help of my loved ones.

The tenth season of 9-1-1, which premiered on ABC on October 15, teases an erupting volcano in the middle of Los Angeles. That’s just good TV, folks. But as the season carries on, the show spreads a message of optimism for humanity—proving every episode that moving with compassion and relying on community helps us build resiliency no matter what absurd tragedies we may face. I don’t care if the volcano takes Los Angeles back to the prehistoric era, I know that firehouse 118 will get through it with the power of their friendship and their boundless determination to do good. Amidst our constant news cycle dominated by bad actors, we could all do with a little dose of hope.

#4518. YouTuber mods NES into a retro PC that can run Doom and play movies… sorta

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A YouTuber has turned Nintendo’s iconic NES into a fully functional retro PC, complete with a keyboard, mouse, internet browser, DVD player, and even the ability to run Doom.

Originally released in North America in 1985, the Nintendo Entertainment System wasn’t exactly designed for desktop computing. However, that didn’t stop YouTuber Throaty Mumbo from giving the legendary console a serious upgrade.

In a 55-minute YouTube video, the creator joked that he was getting “kind of sick of video games” and wanted to turn his NES into something resembling a 1990s computer.

Considering Nintendo’s original Japanese console was called the Famicom, short for Family Computer, the project brings things full circle.

The project, dubbed NESPC, didn’t get off to the smoothest start. Mumbo initially experimented with a bootleg Windows 98 program that barely functioned, followed by an incredibly slow Linux-based emulator.

Eventually, he developed his own Windows-inspired desktop using an EverDrive N8 Pro cartridge and its onboard FPGA, which handles the demanding x86 processing rather than relying on the NES’s original CPU.

The finished product supports a proper desktop environment with a Start menu, file manager, trash bin, and even Minesweeper.

Mumbo also created a custom USB adapter to support a keyboard and mouse, along with a MIDI keyboard for making music.

Using his previously developed NESCD accessory, Mumbo managed to get the console playing CDs and DVDs. He even demonstrated an episode of Aqua Teen Hunger Force running in glorious, extremely crunchy black and white.

“Very strange and impractical, but definitely very neat,” he admitted.

The NES can also connect to Wi-Fi and browse websites, including Wikipedia and YouTube. Videos aren’t exactly HD, but watching YouTube on a 40-year-old Nintendo console is an achievement in itself.

For good measure, he added customizable desktop backgrounds, flying toaster screensavers, and the ability to play Game Boy games like Pokemon.

The visuals are glitchy, thanks to the NES’s limited graphics capabilities, but the game runs with help from the cartridge’s additional hardware.

Of course, Doom has been popping up everywhere lately. In August 2026, another developer managed to get the shooter running on a Canon DSLR camera.

The project also follows another recent retro computing experiment in which a YouTuber rebuilt his childhood gaming PC, only to discover 30-year-old homework and Doom 2 cheats on an old hard drive.

Mumbo, meanwhile, has made the NESPC project available on GitHub for anyone brave enough to attempt their own build.

Between watching YouTube, playing Pokemon, and shooting demons in Doom, Nintendo’s oldest home console is proving there’s still plenty of life left in it.
