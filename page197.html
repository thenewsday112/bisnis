#3465. Weekend Warm-Up: Cycling 54,000Km Around the World

📅 Oct 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In this film, Morgan Monchaud and friends Brian Mathe and Siphay Vera embark on a three-year cycling journey across the globe. Bertrand Dolci, the only team member with significant cycling experience, occasionally joins them. With no budget for accommodation, they sleep on couches, in barns, and by roadsides, across deserts, jungles, mountains, and cities.

Cycling across the salt flats of Patagonia. Photo: Screenshot

We find our cyclists in Patagonia, already eight months out of France. Proceeding northwards at a frenetic pace, they enter the Amazon. With so much ground to cover, the film doesn’t linger on any one location or incident. A dreamlike procession through shifting landscapes is interrupted by personal or practical asides, such as bike repairs and local TV appearances.

In the Amazon, they struggle to push their bikes along muddy roads in stifling heat, following a hand-drawn map. After 40 days in the so-called Green Inferno, a very Biblical number of days to struggle in such an environment, they emerge.

While the trio rents a boat to Panama, the film flashes back to their earlier detour. Following in the footprints of Monchaud’s father, who sailed to Antarctica with friends in 1980, they sail into the Drake Passage. Their storm-tossed boat survives the challenge, gliding swan-like among the white masses of the Antarctic coast.

Monchaud’s father tags along for this leg. Photo: Screenshot

Back in Colombia, they join the crew of a retired Frenchman headed to Panama. Monchaud feels these brief encounters are the beauty of travel, and why they conducted the trip on a shoestring budget.

Montage-ing up through Central America and Mexico, they cross the border into Arizona. They head to Phoenix instead of my hometown of Tucson, which is a huge mistake in my opinion, though it does allow them to pick up Etienne Houles, their missing fourth Musketeer. Tales of their adventures have finally inspired him to quit his job and, wisely, leave Phoenix.

Then it’s up the West Coast and into Canada. Recalling the stories of Jack London, they hit on a new side-quest: build a raft and paddle down the Yukon River.

A bad fall sends Houles back to France, saddened but undaunted. The remaining three build their raft. Photo: Screenshot

Joined by Dolci, their DIY, GPS-less raft floats down the river, looking for all the world like a Canadian remake of Aguirre, the Wrath of God. Nevertheless, they survive the journey to Alaska.

Oceans being generally unbikeable, they hop a flight to the Oceania stage and reunite with a recovered Houles.

“Seeking adventure is only a pretext to meet people,” Monchaud explains, as they help clear fallen trees for their hosts, an elderly New Zealand couple. Monchaud is always happy to talk to a friendly local, but is rarely moved by their safety advice. When an Australian cop advises them not to bike into the outback, where the next water station is 250km away, they do so anyway.

Their final leg takes them through Southeast Asia from Bangkok to Laos, where Vera’s family is from. They navigate blindly through the tropical forest, then up into the mountains to a family reunion.

Despite visa issues in China, they make it to Kazakhstan. After a final side-quest to learn foraging skills with a shepherd family, the trio re-enters Europe via Istanbul. Their return to France marks three years and 54,000km.

Lou Bodenhemier holds an MA in History from the University of Limerick and a BA in Creative Writing from the University of Arizona. He’s interested in maritime and disaster history as well as criminal history, and his dissertation focused on the werewolf trials of early modern Europe. At the present moment he can most likely be found perusing records of shipboard crime and punishment during the Age of Sail, or failing that, writing historical fiction horror stories. He lives in Dublin and hates the sun.

#3473. Japanese Open New Route in the Karakoram

📅 Oct 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A month ago, a Japanese team went quietly into the Karakoram and opened a difficult new route.

Takaaki Nagato, Ryo Masumoto, and Hiroki Yamamoto climbed the south face of 6,677m Koz Sar in the Batura Muztagh range. They climbed for 14 days in capsule style to complete their new 1,400m line. The team needed six portaledge camps before summiting on August 29. It took them two further days to descend.

Team member Yamamoto was nominated for a Piolet d’Or last year after the first ascent of 6,652m Pholesobi Peak in Nepal, via a direct route up its vertiginous north face with Hidesuke Taneishi. Taneishi is currently on his way to attempt the first ascent of Gangapurna West, also in Nepal.

#3567. I Took Marijuana Edibles to Find Out If Weed Makes You Faster—Or If It Just Feels That Way

📅 Oct 02, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The ride felt easier and more immersive—find out if the data told a different story.

I still remember my first time in Colorado after weed was legalized. I walked into a dispensary and my mind was blown by the very chill and knowledgeable manager at the shop walking me through all the strains available. He pointed out the anticipated vibes around each one and which were the best options for activities like rides and trail runs.

Marijuana use in outdoor sports is not new, but efforts to study its effect on performance have been gaining steam. Everyone from the University of Colorado Boulder to the Gatorade Sports Science Institute is putting resources into how this substance impacts endurance sports.

More states have legalized recreational marijuana and many cyclists I’ve talked to have asked whether there are performance benefits or risks. While there are several helpful studies on the effects of marijuana on endurance athletes, none focus on cycling.

With few specific answers available, I wanted to put together my own test. Since I live in New Jersey, a state where recreational weed is legal, I bought a pack of edibles from a local dispensary and gathered all of the sensors I have available. Then I put together a simple study. In the safety of my own home, I would jump on Zwift, following the same route before and after taking edibles. The location was important. While research on marijuana’s impact on cycling performance is rare, multiple studies have shown that THC can impair coordination and slow reaction times, two factors that can increase the risk of cycling, especially outside the relatively controlled environment of my home trainer setup.

To keep outside variables in check, I rode the trainer through Zwift’s Loopin Lava route. I chose this route because I wanted something less than 60 minutes (including warmup and cooldown) that had sustained climbing sections as well as some flat and rolling sections to get a bit of variety. In all, it has about 690 feet of climbing over 11.5 miles.

I aimed to average about 185 watts (roughly zone two) on both tests—keeping my power output the same on both rides, I hoped, would reveal something about weed’s effects on my workout.

My home lab felt like it was made for this moment. As a health and fitness editor at Bicycling, and longtime fitness industry professional, I’ve been growing my collection of sensors for years—I was using a Garmin heart-rate sensor, a power meter built into the Wahoo trainer, a CORE temperature sensor, a Nix sweat sensor, and I used a pulse oximeter to check my oxygen saturation levels in 10-minute intervals. These sensors gave me insight into things I was curious about for the test—I even used my Coros Pace 4 as a backup. Does heart rate change? How does sweat rate and sweat content change after some gummies? Does internal body temperature or oxygen saturation rise or fall after a little weed? I was about to find out.

I did my baseline ride first, then took about a 90-minute break to let my internal temperature reset and let my body reset. Once I finished my first ride I took 40 milligrams of marijuana edibles and gave them some time to kick in (yes, that’s a relatively large dose; I wanted to make sure I experienced the full effects of the edibles on my riding in this not-too-scientific test).

I pulled all the data through Strava, the CORE app, the Nix app, and then pooled it into two columns of an Excel spreadsheet. This was the easiest way to get all the data into one place for analysis.

I opted for edibles after reading several peer-reviewed articles that found that smoking in general can cause changes in heart rate. Edibles would remove that variable, as well as make it easy to consume a target dose. With edibles the math was simple—4 gummies contained 40 milligrams. I had some idea what I was getting into. A 2023 study in the journal Sports Medicine, looked at the effects of marijuana on runners jogging on a treadmill.

One of its findings was that weed could help athletes get a jump on tapping into the runner’s high. “It is possible that exogenous cannabinoids like THC or CBD might activate the endocannabinoid system in a way that mimics the runner’s high,” said Laurel Gibson, PhD, a researcher on the project. It was time for me to find out.

I wasn't "in Barstow on the edge of the desert when the drugs began to kick in," as Hunter S. Thompson wrote in Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas—I was in my basement getting ready to get back on the trainer. I queued up a chill electronic playlist to tap into the beats with my pedal stroke. I did the same 15-minute warmup I did prior to the first ride while I got all my sensors dialed in. Toward the end of my warmup I noticed something—I was getting sucked into the ride. If you’ve ever been on a trainer ride, then you know that the monotony of riding indoors can make you want to hit the eject button as soon as possible. On my first ride I was watching the clock to see when I’d be done, but on this warmup I quickly felt myself getting more hooked on my cadence.

Once the test ride started, I felt like I was more immersed in the virtual world compared to the baseline ride—I was the avatar. As I spun up and down hills, I focused on my breathing and cadence. I felt locked in, in a way that couldn’t have been more different from the first ride. I just wanted to keep going.

Even though it felt harder to push the same watts on this ride, I didn’t want to stop once the route was done. I had to remind myself that I was doing this as an experiment and it was time to get off the bike—I could have kept pedaling around that virtual world for hours. Again—not surprising. The Sports Medicine study found that marijuana increases enjoyment, enhances recovery, and heightens motivation during workouts. In these ways, at least, it seems marijuana’s effects on cyclists are similar to the runners in the study.

It turns out weed is not a performance-enhancing drug for me. Comparing the two rides showed there was a noticeable difference in heart rate as well as body temperature—two traditional indicators of performance. During the roughly 32-minute effort, my average heart rate went up 28 beats per minute (bpm) while my max internal body temperature went up almost a full degree after taking the 40mg dose. That suggests the THC and CBD in the gummies had a negative effect on my performance. While I wasn’t too surprised by this, I was surprised by what I felt versus what the numbers showed.

For an expert opinion, I shared my results with Angela D. Bryan, PhD, adjunct professor, Division of Medical Oncology at the University of Colorado School of Medicine, editor in chief of Annals of Behavioral Medicine, and a researcher on the 2023 Sports Medicine study. She quickly zeroed in on my 40mg dose and the rise in heart rate. “That’s a huge dose, actually, and THC itself causes an acute increase in heart rate. So that’s why you saw that,” she said. Her note on heart rate is important because if THC raises heart rate, that in and of itself can impact performance.

I didn’t have a drop in my ability to sustain power, and I didn’t notice an inability to recover when I tried to put in a dig on the climb. The biggest performance takeaway I had is this: THC and CBD can raise heart rate while riding, which can impact performance. But oddly, in the moment I didn’t feel that I was working harder or that my heart was pounding faster. This is a different and potentially positive effect of marijuana.

Not everyone is training for high performance—a lot of us just want to ride more. During my edible test I didn’t dread getting on the trainer, and once I was on it, I didn’t want to get off. I might have had a higher heart rate, but I enjoyed the experience significantly more. I could see this fitting in on trainer rides in general and zone one, recovery, or zone two rides more specifically. Of course, more research needs to be done on the long-term effects of THC and CBD on athletes.

This was just one trial, at a relatively high dosage. Overall, I found the experience pleasant, and the impact on performance modest—and the idea of experiencing the “runner’s high” sooner is appealing—especially on trainer rides.

#814. 'True Winner' Pumpkin Chili and More Recipes We Made This Week

📅 Oct 02, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

It’s no secret that Bon Appétit editors cook a lot for work. So it should come as no surprise that we cook a lot during our off hours too. Here are the recipes we’re whipping up this month to get dinner on the table, entertain our friends, satisfy a sweet tooth, use up leftovers, and everything in between. For even more staff favorites, click here.

Though I am a vegetarian chili gal myself, I just made a batch of pumpkin chili for a care package for a meat loving loved one. It has ground beef, two types of beans (I threw in lentils too), and instead of the typical tomato backdrop, a can of pumpkin purée for a glowing hue and squashy depth. I almost always double a soup recipe—more yield, barely more work—and I’m glad I did. Now I have a few extra containers in the freezer that’ll come in handy for a cold night down the road. —Emma Laperruque, cooking director

Also for the care package, I made a giant batch of Chickpea Saag. With frozen spinach and canned chickpeas, this recipe makes the most of ingredients I almost always have around. With rice, Greek yogurt, and lime wedges, it’s a cozy meal in its own right. But I like to sprinkle some feta on top just to gild the lily. —E.L.

I love hosting, but at the end of a long week, I need to maximize flavor and minimize dishes. That’s why I love this Spiced Red Rice Chicken recipe from Eden Eats. It allowed me to have everything prepped before guests arrived, so I could enjoy the hang and not fuss about timing. After seasoning chicken thighs generously with salt and letting them rest for 30 minutes, I seared them, set aside, and sautéed onions, garlic in all the flavor left over (I skipped the red pepper for one of my guests). I added additional spices and tomato paste and toasted the rice. I added chicken broth, placed the chicken back in skin side up, and tossed in some knobs of butter so the rice would crisp up nicely. Into the oven at 425 for 30 minutes, and it was yummy and convenient, not to mention a definite crowd-pleaser—and my dishwasher was happy! —Abi Lieff, assistant to the editor in chief

I’m not much of a pasta person. (I know, gasp!) But when fellow Test Kitchen buddy Rebecca Firkser first developed her Smashed Broccoli Pasta—a recipe that combines over a pound of broccoli with less than one pound of pasta—my heart opened up to the idea of making it for dinner. Her technique is genius, smashing broccoli into verdant rubble that burrows its way into tubular pasta. With not much more (just red pepper, garlic, and anchovies if you’re feeling frisky), you get a veggie-loaded, fiber-packed pasta in no time. Rebecca, thank you. Perhaps I will become a pasta convert. —Jesse Szewczyk, Senior Test Kitchen Editor

A fried fish sandwich has no season. Also, I’m currently in Tennessee, where it’s hit 90° this week, so it’s basically still summer. I used my colleague Shilpa Uskokovic’s excellent Fritto Misto batter to coat some flounder. After frying, I slid a couple small fillets into top-split hot dog buns with juicy slices of tomato and a quick tartar sauce. It was pure summer satisfaction, even this deep into October. —Joe Sevier, senior editor, SEO and cooking

The days when my husband and I both commute are, to put it lightly, b-a-n-a-n-a-s. That’s when my deep freezer fulfills its destiny. Case in point: this superlative basil pesto, thawed overnight, tossed with hot rigatoni, plus frozen peas thrown in with the pasta for the last few minutes of boiling. It is nourishing and feels purposeful, even if it took less than 10 minutes of effort. —Emma Laperruque, cooking director

Andy Baraghani’s Honey-Chipotle Shrimp Tacos are one of my most-made recipes of all time. Last week I decided to change things up and eat the shrimp over some homemade Mexican rice and beans instead of wrapping them in a tortilla. The shrimp are so easy to make (just toss in a two-ingredient marinade and pan-fry) that I ended up cooking fresh batches for lunch on the following days to pair with the leftover rice and beans. The canned chipotles in adobo lend a smoky complexity, and I love how the honey helps the shrimp take on just enough char. A luxurious WFH lunch if there ever was one! —Alaina Chou, commerce writer

Soup on a weeknight? It’s a foreign concept to me. If I’m making soup, I’m committing hours to the cause. But this week I couldn’t quell my craving for a sippable bowl, so I scoured the archive for a quick hit and landed on this Curried Lentil, Tomato, and Coconut Soup. Red lentils break down and create a substantial body, while golden Japanese S&B curry powder adds a mellow earthy kick. After mixing in crushed tomatoes and coconut milk, I went rogue and followed up with a couple dashes of fish sauce for extra umami. —Nina Moskowitz, associate editor, cooking

The ever adaptable rotisserie chicken is one of my favorite emergency dinners. When crunched for time, I’ll grab one on my way home and transform the bird into something unrecognizable. Most recently, I used it to make Test Kitchen editor Rebecca Firkser’s Nuoc Cham Chicken Salad. The pulled meat is tossed with the quickest mayo-lime dressing, then stuffed into lettuce leaves. I like to finish the cups with sliced cucumber, fried shallots, and a flurry of fresh herbs. Don’t bother bringing out plates. I eat this with my hands straight from the platter like nachos. —Carly Westerfield, manager, audience strategy

Whenever I want something more for breakfast, but I don’t want to work for it, it’s a Dutch baby all the way. Heat a skillet with butter, dump a couple of always-on-hand ingredients—eggs, flour, milk, sugar—into a blender, and 20 minutes later, it isn’t breakfast: It’s brunch. Last weekend I tossed the reserved browned butter from Epi’s Take It Easy Dutch Baby into a bowl of sliced Italian plums. I added a glug of maple syrup and a pinch of salt, then let the fruit soak in the flavors while I fiddled over my coffee. Puffed bébé out of the oven, the plums and all their juices went on top, and I sat down to enjoy my morning. —Joe Sevier, senior editor, cooking and SEO

I make lentil soup pretty frequently once the “feels like” temperature dips below 50 degrees. And I was just about to mince up my mirepoix when I stumbled upon senior Test Kitchen editor Shilpa Uskokovic’s Soy-Braised Black Lentils. The promise of salty, tender-but-toothsome legumes on a bed of rice was good enough to shake up my routine. I hauled out my rice cooker, set it to steam, and started a dead-simple simmer of a pack of shiny black Beluga lentils with glugs of soy sauce and thin slices of garlic. Once the liquid was mostly gone, I stirred in glossy agave nectar plus added sugar until they formed a loose, syrupy glaze. We ate half the pot tucked into sheets of toasted seaweed snack along with generous spoonfuls of rice. The next morning, the rest was eaten with crusty bread and fried eggs. —Kelsey Jane Youngman, senior cooking editor

Ever since I started working at BA, I’ve seen this single recipe for Bolognese top the charts time and time again. An oldie but a baddie, it’s received over 500 comments that would taunt me on a daily basis, with one overall glaring thesis: Make this! Now! So I decided to hunker down, put on a killer album (Once in a Lifetime), and get comfortable in the kitchen. Yes, this Bolognese takes time—cough cough, three hours—but so do all good things. For those who need immediate gratification, your kitchen will be awash in the savory, primal smells of browned beef and pancetta in just 10 minutes. After adding a good amount of liquid from wine, stock, and milk (in stages), then stirring, dancing, and stirring, it was finally time to serve my meaty sauce tangled with fat pappardelle and a charitable shower of Parmesan. —Nina Moskowitz, associate editor, cooking

When I saw a bunch of bright-eyed leeks at the farmers market from my favorite vendor, I knew just what to do with them: my colleague Rebecca Firkser’s cheesy baked pasta. It was one of those love-at-first-sight recipes for me in the Test Kitchen—somehow minimalist and dramatic at the same time. Save this one for a chilly night when you’re having friends over. The sauce uses a cup of white wine, so there’s most of a bottle left to enjoy with dinner. —Emma Laperruque, cooking director

I’ve been making a lot of solo dinners lately, and nine times out of ten that means I’m cooking an omelet. My version with fried shallot, apple, and Fontina cheese is a go-to—I wrote all about it for our series, Me, Myself, and Dinner—but if I haven’t gone grocery shopping, I’ll just cobble together fun stuff from my fridge. Some recent wins: chopped kimchi and yellow cheddar; spinach, feta, and dill; black beans, avocado, and salsa macha. —Rebecca Firkser, Test Kitchen editor

This week I did something I haven’t done in years. I roasted a chicken. And it was revelatory. Not fussy. Easy! Rewarding. Inspired by the method in Kitty Cowles’s cookbook, I sprinkled the bird with salt and let it hang out in the fridge for a few hours. Then I rubbed it with olive oil, threw some lemon and garlic in the cavity, and roasted it as high as my oven would go, convection on. While the chicken was resting, its skin a golden crackle, I deglazed the pan juices with white wine, and let those simmer into a tangy gravy. Served with a salad, it was the coziest supper, even romantic if you ignore my toddler cooing. The next day, the carcass simmered in a pot of water with onion and ginger for a broth I’ll sip for days. The surplus meat has morphed into a rice bowl, towering sandwich, day care lunch box, 3 p.m. snack. And the joy from it all? Incalculable. —E.L.

I’m always on the hunt for a protein-rich, not-too-finicky dinner that I can pull together in a blip. It’s gotta taste good because I’ll probably cook it for five consecutive days, at which point I will tire of it and resume my hunt anew. Like sands through the hourglass, these are the recipes of our lives. This week I settled on customizable Soy-Glazed Salmon Bowls. Cook the salmon in that salty-sweet soy and honey sauce, throw it atop rice, and add whatever vegetables you have. This time, I went with shreddy carrots and cukes, plus some of those crispy sheets of nori that I used to scoop up bite after bite. —Sam Stone, senior editor

My very talented colleague Shilpa Uskokovic forever changed the way I cook ground chicken. She mixes the chicken with baking soda—yes, baking soda—for clusters of bouncy nubbins. Now, anytime a recipe calls for ground meat, I use this hack. This week’s victim: Spicy Chicken Lettuce Wraps. It has a sweet, fiery sauce made from sriracha, brown sugar, soy, and fish sauce that deliberately clings to the juicy meat. I piled the chicken into butter lettuce with a squeeze of lime, and a handful of torn mint for freshness. —Nina Moskowitz, associate editor, cooking
