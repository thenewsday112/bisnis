The wellness program moves between inside and outside, with a pool, a hot tub, an outdoor shower, and a home gym designed to open to the landscape year-round.

#4033. Wearable Vagus Nerve Devices Rewire Depressive Brains

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A comprehensive review published in Med-X outlines how emerging wearable bioelectronics are transforming vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) from an invasive surgical procedure into accessible, home-based depression therapy. Non-invasive wearable VNS (wnVNS) stimulates peripheral vagal fibers via the ear or neck to enhance neuroplasticity, boost monoamines, and suppress systemic inflammation. Coupled with multimodal biometric sensing and artificial intelligence, wearable closed-loop systems could soon deliver personalized, real-time neuromodulation for treatment-resistant depression.

Major depressive disorder affects more than 280 million individuals globally, remaining a primary driver of disability and emotional suffering. While first-line pharmacotherapies and cognitive psychotherapies provide relief for many, approximately one-third of clinical patients experience treatment-resistant depression—failing to achieve remission despite multiple therapeutic regimens.

To overcome treatment resistance, clinical neuroscientists turned to neuromodulation, identifying vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) as a viable tool.

Historically, VNS required surgical implantation of a pulse generator beneath the clavicle with lead wires tunneled into the neck to wrap around the left cervical vagus nerve. While effective, the surgical risks, potential vocal cord side effects, and high medical expenses restricted the therapy to severe, intractable cases.

Now, a comprehensive review published in the journal Med-X provides a systematic evaluation of how rapid innovations in wearable bioelectronics, flexible materials, and artificial intelligence are transitioning VNS into a non-invasive, accessible, at-home reality.

The vagus nerve (cranial nerve X) is the body’s primary parasympathetic superhighway, bidirectional communication linking visceral organs directly to the brainstem’s nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS). From this relay hub, signals radiate to the locus coeruleus, dorsal raphe nucleus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex.

The authors emphasize that therapeutic efficacy depends strictly on bioelectronic engineering parameters. Pulse width, frequency, current amplitude, duty cycle, and the impedance of the skin-electrode interface dictate whether electrical pulses penetrate cutaneous barriers to recruit deep A\beta myelinated vagal afferents without causing local skin irritation or pain.

Clinical trials investigating taVNS and tcVNS in depressive cohorts report meaningful reductions in Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) scores, accompanied by improvements in autonomic metrics such as heart rate variability (HRV) and normalized cortical oscillations.

However, the authors caution that the field faces substantial translational challenges. The existing clinical literature remains fragmented by small sample sizes, heterogeneous stimulation protocols, and divergent outcome measures. Determining standardized stimulation windows, establishing dose-response curves, and tracking long-term maintenance outcomes represent urgent clinical priorities.

The review highlights that the future of wearable neuromodulation lies in transitioning away from static, “one-size-fits-all” electrical doses toward dynamic, closed-loop bioelectronics.

By embedding multimodal biosensors, measuring continuous electrocardiography, galvanic skin responses, and peripheral temperature, into wearable vagal devices, machine-learning algorithms could decode an individual’s real-time autonomic tone and depressive stress biomarkers.

When the system detects markers of autonomic dysregulation or escalating depressive withdrawal, it can automatically trigger or modulate stimulation pulses, delivering personalized, adaptive neuromodulation.

Connecting biological circuits, flexible bioelectronics, and adaptive AI algorithms, wearable vagus nerve stimulation is poised to dismantle the barriers of surgical neuromodulation, offering a scalable, home-based therapeutic paradigm for depression.

Wearable vagus nerve stimulation for depression: mechanisms, devices, and the road toward personalized neuromodulation

Major depressive disorder remains a leading cause of global disability, with a substantial fraction of patients exhibiting inadequate response to conventional pharmacological and psychotherapeutic treatments. Vagus nerve stimulation has emerged as an effective neuromodulation strategy for treatment-resistant depression, yet implantable systems are limited by surgical risk, cost, and restricted scalability.

Recent advances in non-invasive, wearable vagus nerve stimulation technologies, particularly transcutaneous auricular and cervical approaches, offer a promising pathway toward accessible, home-based neuromodulation. This Review synthesizes the anatomical and physiological rationale underlying wearable non-invasive VNS and critically evaluates mechanistic evidence spanning monoaminergic modulation, neuroplasticity, neuroimmune regulation, autonomic control, and large-scale brain network reorganization.

We further summarize clinical findings from implantable and non-invasive trials and discuss key engineering considerations, including electrode–skin interfaces, stimulation parameters, wearability, and safety. #4055. How Voting Can Help Those Struggling With Mental Illness

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

On Tuesday, November 3rd, Americans will return to the polls for the 2026 midterm elections, allowing voters to consider how we treat those who are most vulnerable among us. This includes the millions of Americans living with serious mental illness and/or substance use disorders, and the families who struggle every day to help them access the care they need.

These challenges are not simply private matters. They are public policy issues, and the people we elect have a significant role in shaping the laws, funding priorities and systems of care that determine what help is available.

This Election Day, voters have an opportunity to make the needs of people living with serious mental health and related challenges a larger part of the conversation, and a more significant priority.

In recent years, mental health has received greater public attention. We hear more about the importance of reducing stigma, taking time for self-care, and seeking help. These are important, but awareness by itself does not ensure that someone with a complex mental health diagnosis receives timely, appropriate and ongoing care. Being proactive is crucial.

Families know the difference between acknowledging a problem and addressing it. They know what it means to encounter long waiting lists, limited treatment options, and uncertainty about what to do when systems don’t recognize individuals’ inability to recognize their own symptoms. This reality is harder than most people will ever understand, but it is also needlessly too hard because we haven’t summoned the will to uphold a real social safety net.

Voting for representatives who will better fund community treatment programs, appropriate alternatives to incarceration, and, crucially, supportive housing, will go a long way toward helping these individuals and families, while at the same time providing tangible societal benefits.

In addition to funding, legislators can tighten and further enforce existing laws designed to ensure that insurers treat mental health and substance use issues with the same seriousness as physical illnesses, providing meaningful coverage for the cost of treatment. This is essential given the recent movement at the federal level to walk away from past parity promises, and the rising costs patients face regardless of what kind of care they need.

When systems fail to provide appropriate support, the consequences can extend far beyond the individual. Families become exhausted trying to navigate a largely broken system. Those who might achieve stability instead cycle through emergency departments, jails and the streets. Wasted potential and wasted resources take a toll at the community, state and national levels. Society risks normalizing a terrible status quo and becoming numb to others’ pain.

This is not only morally questionable; it is individually unwise. The issue of mental illness and substance use spans geographies, demographics and political affiliation. These diagnoses do not discriminate. What might be someone else’s problem today could be a neighbor’s, friend’s or family member’s tomorrow.

As a mental health attorney who counsels families of individuals with these diagnoses, I believe it is imperative to start thinking of these issues as part of a larger problem, for which we need a larger and more comprehensive solution. That requires each of us to show up on Election Day and vote for those who will bring us closer to that reality, where mental health issues become part of the conversation and are addressed through meaningful action.

#4063. The Relationship Mistake We Don't Notice Until It's Too Late

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

We know that relationships matter. Yet we consistently behave as though they can wait.

In the workplace, strong relationships can make the difference between a project's success, a secured promotion, or a closed deal. However, in our high-pressure, time-poor world, we tend to focus on what we’re trying to achieve now, doing the hard graft ourselves if need be.

We can address our longer-term goals another day.

But of course, that day soon comes, and we’re back to square one. Failing to invest in our relationships earlier makes the support or advocacy we need now harder to secure. Delaying nonurgent relationship-building activity carries real consequences, just ones we are not aware of until it’s too late.

It’s easy to assume that relationships will always be in place when needed. But trust, just like physical fitness or financial security, is built over time and only becomes apparent when we need it the most.

It’s natural to prioritise what is urgent, visible, and measurable. Particularly when we are held accountable for short-term targets. Nobody wants to explain why they have attended a conference or arranged a coffee meeting when the pressure is on.

Psychologists and behavioural economists describe this as present bias. We place greater value on what delivers results now than on benefits that may emerge later. Under pressure to meet targets, another hour at our desk feels productive. A conversation with someone who might be able to help us in six months feels like a luxury.

Relationship-building can be seen as a "soft" activity, a nice-to-have but nonessential way to spend our time. It doesn’t feel productive when we can’t demonstrate an immediate payoff, so we focus on activities we can count today.

Without relationships in place to guide, support, and advocate for us, however, it’s much harder to achieve those short-term goals. The insights and ideas other people can offer us can prevent us from repeating other people’s mistakes, while influential people in our network can open doors that would previously have remained blocked.

It’s a false economy to discard relationship-building for sweat and toil; if we put in the work up front to nurture the support we need, we can overcome challenges more easily. What feels like a distraction from achieving our goals may actually be an investment in achieving them more easily.

Failing to think strategically about relationships creates blind spots. Apart from the strongest relationships, which have almost become personal rather than purely professional, or those with people who work in immediate proximity, people fall off our radar if they are not immediately relevant to what we’re focusing on. It’s not necessarily that we wilfully neglect people when we don’t need them, but many people assume goodwill established earlier will remain available if we shelve the relationship for a while.

How many times have you been on the receiving end of a LinkedIn connection request from someone you haven’t been in touch with for a while, only to find it quickly followed up by a message asking for your help because they are looking for a new job? How did that make you feel?

Goodwill doesn't necessarily disappear when contact stops, but we shouldn't assume it remains unchanged. Regular, low-pressure interactions help maintain the trust and familiarity that make asking for support feel natural.

When we only reappear because we need something, our intentions may be genuine, but the approach can feel transactional. We are asking the relationship to deliver more than we have recently invested in it.

We can work with someone every day, collaborate on projects, and attend countless meetings without ever developing the trust that encourages them to challenge us, advocate for us, or offer support when things become difficult.

Regular connection is not enough on its own. Familiarity is not the same as trust. We need to find a balance between two key types of interaction:

If most of your conversations with a colleague are transactional, you may not be creating genuine relational depth and connection. We need curiosity, reciprocity, and the human touch. We need to find the space to understand the person behind the function.

This can be even harder when we are based in another office, or even another country, and most of our conversations happen remotely. But we need to proactively seek those points of connection, away from our respective agendas, if we are going to invest meaningfully in developing rapport and connection.

We need to think more strategically about our professional relationships and be willing to invest in them before we need to. But this doesn’t mean we approach other people to manipulate or trick them. This is about setting aside time, even during our busiest periods, to invest in meaningful and durable human relationships.

By doing this, we can ensure that support is there when we need it. The real mistake is not that people care too much or too little about relationships. It’s that they care too late.

A proposed shift in recommendations for hepatitis B vaccination in the U.S. could leave millions of young adults unprotected, a new modeling study suggests.

A proposed change to decades-old guidance on hepatitis B vaccination would lead to a dramatic increase in chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infections among young people, a new modeling study predicts.

Previously, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommended that all newborns be vaccinated against HBV within hours of birth ‪—‬ a strategy known as "universal birth-dose vaccination." But in December 2025, the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) advised ending this recommendation, and the CDC accepted that advice.

The new guidance would recommend birth doses only to babies whose mothers tested positive for HBV or whose HBV status was unknown. The guidance suggests that mothers who test negative should instead ask their doctors about whether their child needs a birth dose — an approach the CDC calls "shared clinical decision-making." The new recommendation states that babies who forgo the birth dose should be at least 2 months old before starting the HBV vaccine series.

Health experts widely criticized the proposed change when it was announced, and for the moment, its implementation has been put on pause by legal action. The modeling study, published Oct. 19 in the journal Annals of Internal Medicine, predicts what would happen if it came to pass.

"Overall, this modeling study has many strengths and I think presents an accurate projection of how the vaccine recommendations may impact future chronic HBV in the U.S.," Dr. Norah Terrault, a transplant hepatologist at the University of Southern California Keck School of Medicine who wasn't involved in the study, told Live Science in an email.

When caught in infancy or early childhood, over 90% of HBV infections become chronic. About 15% to 25% of people with chronic HBV go on to develop liver scarring (cirrhosis), a form of liver cancer called hepatocellular carcinoma, or another chronic liver disease. So, an uptick in chronic HBV infections would translate to an uptick in these harmful liver conditions.

"Not every pregnant person is tested for hepatitis B," said study co-author Dr. Amir Mohareb, an infectious-diseases physician-investigator at Massachusetts General Hospital and Harvard Medical School. For those who are tested, there's a chance their results are inaccurate or that they're not provided the right care after testing positive. "Something can be missed at any step in the care process, so the vaccine dose at birth provides protection," he said.

Mohareb and his colleagues built a simulation model using published data, including the current prevalence of HBV and the screening rate among pregnant people. The team also factored in the HBV test's sensitivity, the share of children who are fully vaccinated by age 2, and the risk of developing chronic HBV infection in different age groups. They made estimates of how often children are exposed to the virus through contact with infected household or community members.

HBV is "very, very transmissible," Mohareb said. It can spread from mother to child at birth or through exposure to tiny amounts of bodily fluids, like blood from a scratch, after birth. So, even if a baby does not get HBV from their mother, if they are unvaccinated, they can still get from another family member or from other children.

Infants and young children have a greater risk of their HBV infections becoming chronic than people infected in adulthood.

With this data, the researchers tested what would happen if vaccination were postponed to 2 months of age for babies whose mothers tested negative. Among babies born in 2026, 19% more would have chronic HBV infections by age 20, compared with universal birth-dose vaccination, they found.

In this scenario, the babies would still start the HBV vaccine series, albeit two months later than usual. However, the CDC's proposed recommendation offers more wiggle room; it says that parents should consult with their children's doctors to "decide when or if their child will begin the hepatitis B vaccine series" at all.

The researchers ran a stimulation to compare these two scenarios: one where kids get their first shot two months late and one intended to capture this looser "shared clinical decision-making" scenario.

"Other vaccines whose recommendations employ similar language had uptake as low as 25%," Mohareb noted. This aspect of their modeling has some uncertainty, he emphasized, since it's unknown exactly how the public will respond to such loosening of the guidance.

The researchers assumed that about 87% of kids would complete the vaccine series by age 2 under the deferred strategy, while only 50% would under the shared decision-making strategy. That 50% coverage, in turn, would increase chronic HBV infections acquired by age 20 by 83%, and about 50% and 70% of those born under that guidance would be susceptible to HBV infection at age 20.

That's about 1.8 million people in this age group per year, compared with fewer than 250,000 under universal birth-dose vaccination. "This represents a population that is at risk for acquiring HBV via sex, drug use etc — behaviors more prevalent among young adults," Terrault said.

To test the robustness of the findings, Mohareb's team varied the most uncertain inputs across wide ranges and ran the model 1,000 times. In every simulation, the birth-dose strategy led to fewer chronic infections than either alternative.

These conclusions seem "robust" because "they are the same conclusions no matter how we change the numbers of the model," Mohereb noted. Though of course "the specific magnitude of change depends on how many people defer vaccination," he said.

The new study does not specifically show how many additional cases of liver disease or cancer would result from the uptick in HBV infections. But Terrault said that the risks of liver failure, cirrhosis and liver cancer "would be anticipated to increase due to the missed opportunity to protect against infection."

Mohareb stressed that he is in no way opposed to the principle of shared decision-making between doctors and patients. "They should always make the decision together," he said. "But we rely on our public health agencies not to tell us that we should be making decisions with the patient, but rather to tell us what policy would make us healthier as a country."

Childhood HBV infections in the U.S. dropped from more than 25,000 cases a year in 1985 to fewer than 100 reported cases in 2024. This steep decline is credited to HBV screening during pregnancy, antibody treatment for babies born to HBV-positive mothers and universal birth-dose vaccination.

"Certainly without following a universal dose vaccination recommendation there would be a lot more hep B infections early in life," Dr. Brian McMahon, a hepatologist and the director of the Liver Disease and Hepatitis Program at the Alaska Native Medical Center, told Live Science in an email.
