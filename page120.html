Many utilities will send a specialist to find where your home leaks heated and cooled air, for free or at a discount. Search your utility’s name plus “home energy assessment” and book one before winter. You’ll leave with a to-do list and the rebates you qualify for.

A tax break won’t cover the assessment anymore. The federal credit worth up to $150 toward a home energy audit ended with 2025, which leaves utility and state programs as the main way to get one discounted.

A professional assessment goes well beyond a look around. The specialist works through the house room by room. The Energy Department notes assessors may use tools such as blower doors and infrared cameras to find where energy escapes. The report then spells out what to fix, and in some programs the findings decide which rebates you can get.

Massachusetts shows what a strong program looks like. Mass Save, sponsored by utilities including Eversource and National Grid, offers no-cost virtual or in-person assessments. Its follow-up incentives include 75% to 100% off approved insulation and air sealing, plus 0% financing for eligible upgrades. Renters in one- to four-unit homes qualify too, so check even if you don’t own.

When a search turns up nothing, check the rebates or energy efficiency page on your utility’s website, or call the number on your bill. If your household income is low, also contact your state’s weatherization agency about the federal Weatherization Assistance Program, which makes eligible homes more energy efficient at no cost to the household. Before the visit, list your drafty rooms and pull a year of energy bills, since both point the assessor to the problems.

Then work through the report’s fixes, cheapest first. Seal air leaks and handle the other low-cost repairs before you price rebated upgrades like insulation. ENERGY STAR puts the payoff from sealing leaks and adding insulation at up to 10% of your annual energy bills.

Book the visit before you buy a single tube of caulk, so every dollar goes to a leak the assessor found.

When you can’t keep up with your debts, work through the ways to repay them in full before you consider debt settlement or bankruptcy. Those two cut what you owe but can stay on your credit reports for seven years or longer, so use them only for debt you couldn’t repay within about five years.

Which move fits depends on how far behind you are and on what’s left of your take-home pay once housing, food, utilities, and transportation are paid. Missed payments narrow your choices, since lenders that see them on your credit reports are less likely to offer you a low rate. If you’re keeping up and just want to be out of debt sooner, the ways to pay off credit cards faster fit better.

Settlement and bankruptcy have costs beyond the years they can stay on your credit reports. The Federal Trade Commission warns that settlement programs often have you stop paying your creditors while you save, so late fees can grow and you can be sued. Bankruptcy brings a court filing fee, plus a lawyer’s fee if you hire one. And the IRS can tax whatever a settlement forgives, while debt erased in bankruptcy isn’t taxed.

Start here this month, before you call anyone. Knowing which bills you can’t let slip tells you how much you can offer the creditors you do call.

When you can’t cover every bill this month, pay in order of what you’d lose. Keep your housing, utilities, and car current first, since an eviction, shutoff, or repossession is far harder to undo than a late card payment. Then call each creditor you can’t pay in full before its due date.

This suits a setback you expect to end within months, like a job search or a medical leave. Asking costs nothing, and every other option on this list stays open.

If a layoff, medical bill, or pay cut means you can’t make a loan or credit card payment, call your lender before the payment is late and ask for its hardship team. You don’t need to be behind to ask, and calling first can keep a late payment off your credit report.

Read more: See a Missed Payment Coming? Call Your Lender for a Hardship Plan Now

For a cardholder who is stretched thin but hasn’t missed a payment yet, a lower rate cuts the interest that keeps the balance from shrinking.

If you carry a credit card balance, call your card issuer and ask it to cut your interest rate. Issuers grant most of these requests, and each percentage point off saves about $10 a year for every $1,000 you owe. Lead with your on-time payment record and a better offer from another card.

Read more: Your Credit Card APR Is Negotiable - Most Cardholders Who Ask Get a Cut

While your credit still qualifies you for a lower rate than your cards charge, a loan can turn several card payments into one payment with an end date.

A debt consolidation loan saves you money only when its APR, the yearly rate with the origination fee counted, is lower than your cards’ rates. Stretching the term adds interest that can outweigh what the lower rate saves, so prequalify with several lenders and choose the shortest term with a payment close to what you pay now.

Read more: Debt Consolidation Loans - A Lower Rate Can Still Cost More Than Your Cards

Even after missed payments have put a low-rate loan out of reach, this way to lower your rates can still work, since a counselor’s repayment plan isn’t a loan.

If card rates above 20% keep your balances from shrinking, ask a nonprofit credit counseling agency about a debt management plan. The agency asks your card issuers to lower those rates, often to single digits, and you pay off the cards with one monthly payment instead of a new loan.

Read more: Nonprofit Debt Management Plans Can Cut Card Rates to Single Digits - No Loan Needed

Unlike a new loan or a new plan, this frees cash by cutting a bill. It fits when the car’s payment, insurance, and upkeep are what leave too little for the rest of your debts.

Selling your car can free cash for your other debts when the sale clears the auto loan and whatever replaces the car costs less each month than the car does now. Check both before you list it, then send every freed dollar to your highest-rate debt.

Read more: Selling Your Car to Pay Off Debt - Only If Getting Around Costs Less

You don’t need a company for this. Once you’ve saved the cash, you can offer it to the creditor or collector directly, and a deal you strike yourself carries no fee.

Debt settlement can shrink what you owe, but it costs months of missed payments, a fee that typically runs 15% to 25% of the debt you enroll, and often income tax on what’s forgiven. Use it only when your accounts are already in collections and you can’t repay them even on a lower-rate plan.

Read more: Debt Settlement Is for Debts You Can't Repay - Count the Fees and Taxes First

The day you file, a rule called the automatic stay halts most collection calls, lawsuits, and wage garnishments, where a creditor takes money straight from your paycheck. Settlement offers no such pause while you save.

Filing for bankruptcy makes sense when your credit card and medical debt couldn’t be repaid within five years, even on a credit counseling plan. Multiply what you can spare each month by 60. If that falls far short of what you owe, stop cashing out your 401(k) to keep up and talk to a bankruptcy attorney.

Read more: The Five-Year Test - When Bankruptcy Beats Trying to Repay Your Debt

Any option here that lets you repay in full leaves your credit and your choices in better shape than settling or filing does. Rank your bills this month, then call each lender you can’t pay while your account is still current.

#3409. How Many Credit Cards You Need – Usually Two or Three, Each With a Job

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Two or three credit cards cover most people: an everyday card, one that pays more where you spend the most, and maybe a backup or travel card. Before you open another, make sure it would earn meaningfully more than your current cards on what you actually buy, after any annual fee, or fill a gap they leave.

Your credit score doesn’t set the number. InCharge Debt Solutions, a nonprofit credit counselor, says no formula can, so the count comes down to what each card does for you.

More cards can help in two ways. Each one adds its limit to your total available credit, so the same balance takes up a smaller share of it. That share, called utilization, counts heavily in your score. A $1,500 balance uses 30% of $5,000 in limits but only 15% of $10,000. A second card also gives you a way to pay when your main card is lost or frozen for suspected fraud.

Extra cards cost you, too. Each application brings a hard inquiry, a lender’s check of your credit report. FICO says one inquiry takes fewer than five points off most people’s scores, and it stops counting after a year. A new account lowers the average age of your accounts, another factor in your score. And every card adds a due date you could miss. Payment history makes up 35% of a FICO score, more than any other factor.

So test a new card before you apply. Use your last three months of statements to estimate what it would earn in a year, subtract any annual fee, and compare that with what your current cards earn on the same purchases. The gain is often smaller than the offer suggests. If a no-fee card pays 3% on groceries where yours pays 2%, moving $500 a month of groceries to it adds $60 a year.

If you want another card for rewards, pairing a category card with a flat-rate card usually pays best. Filling a real gap counts too, like getting a card with no foreign transaction fee before you travel abroad.

None of this means closing cards to get down to two or three. An old no-fee card usually does more good open than closed, because its limit keeps your utilization down.

When the next card offer arrives, name the job it would do that your current cards don’t. Skip any card with no new job to do.

SpaceX launched its newest batch of Starlink broadband internet satellites today (Oct. 23) from the California coast.

The spacecraft, Starlink Group 15-15, were stacked 27 high inside the payload fairing of a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket, which lifted off from Pad 4E at Vandenberg Space Force Base at 6:48 p.m. EDT (2248 GMT; 3:48 p.m. local time in California).

The rocket's first stage booster, designated B1063, executed its main engine cutoff and stage separation about 2.5 minutes into flight, sending the second stage to continue onward to low Earth orbit (LEO). The vehicle's two-piece protective payload fairing was jettisoned just before T+3 minutes.

The booster, meanwhile, reoriented for its descent back to Earth. As the mission clock passed 8 minutes, B1063 began its landing burn, and it touched down about 20 seconds later on SpaceX's droneship "Of Course I Still Love You." This was the 36th launch and landing for B1063, which notably launched NASA's DART asteroid-smashing mission in November 2021.

The Falcon 9's upper stage completed its first engine burn shortly after B1063 landed and will coast for about 45 minutes before initiating a second and final orbital insertion burn. Following that burn's execution, deployment of the vehicle's 27 satellites into LEO is scheduled to begin 62 minutes after launch.

Today's Starlink liftoff adds to SpaceX's growing constellation of more than 11,000 active spacecraft. But Starlinks aren't launching quite as often as they used to, as SpaceX shifts its focus from the Falcon 9 to its bigger and more powerful Starship rocket.

Starship is designed for full reusability and is equipped with a payload bay outfitted to accommodate SpaceX's upgraded Version 3 Starlink satellites, which are too large for the workhorse Falcon 9. Starship is still under development, but once the rocket begins operational flights (and that day is getting closer), SpaceX plans to phase out the Falcon 9 and its Falcon Heavy variant almost entirely.

Falcon 9's 100th launch of 2026 was the Starlink 10-49 group from SpaceX's pad at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, which was later revealed to be the final Falcon 9 Starlink mission that would fly from Florida's Space Coast. The same might soon be true for Starlink launches from the West Coast as well.

The reduction in missions lifting off from Florida has slowed Falcon 9's Starlink launch cadence considerably. At their peak, these missions were flying every two to three days. But the most recent one before today lifted off 11 days ago, and that launch itself was the first Starlink mission in more than three weeks.

Today's launch was the 116th Falcon 9 flight so far in 2026, with about three-quarters of those being dedicated to Starlink. The reduced cadence will likely solidify 2025 as the Falcon 9's busiest year ever, with 165 launches completed. At its current rate, the Falcon 9 is expected to fly just under 150 missions by the end of this year.

NASA just gave us our best look yet at its Artemis III moon-lander test flight.

The agency laid out a detailed concept of operations for Artemis III today (Oct. 23) during a press conference at Kennedy Space Center, stressing how important the flight is to its moon plans and how challenging it will be to make everything go right.

"We're launching three of the most powerful rockets in the world, and undertaking what is likely the most complicated launch campaign ever undertaken," Jeremy Parsons, NASA’s Artemis program manager, said during today's event, which lasted more than two hours.

Those three rockets are NASA's Space Launch System (SLS), SpaceX's Starship and Blue Origin's New Glenn, all of which will fly from Florida's Space Coast.

If all goes according to plan, New Glenn will lift off first, carrying Blue Origin's Blue Moon lunar lander to low Earth orbit (LEO). SLS will go next, launching the Artemis III astronauts — NASA's Randy Bresnik, Andre Douglas and Frank Rubio, and the European Space Agency's Luca Parmitano — aboard NASA's Orion spacecraft.

SLS will deploy Orion into a highly elliptical orbit, one that gets as close to Earth as 18 miles (29 kilometers) and as far away as 1,122 miles (1,806 km), according to Parsons. The capsule will then maneuver itself into an orbit with an altitude of about 345 miles (555 km).

"That's really about getting thermal stability," Parsons said. "Orion was built as a deep-space vehicle, and so we're going to have to really manage the thermal environments, which are higher in a low Earth orbit."

The Artemis III crew will spend two days checking out Orion and its systems in LEO before meeting up with Blue Moon, which will take place on Flight Day 3 at an altitude of 273 miles (439 km).

"Over the next two days, the crew will conduct operations docked with Blue Moon," Parsons said. "They're going to open up the hatch. They're going to enter the lander. They're going to evaluate the environmental controls, the life support systems. We'll confirm that the spacecraft can maintain the right atmospheric pressure, and we're also going to test emergency scenarios, dual vehicle control, and we're going to put our Mission Control teams through their paces."

After those operations are complete — likely on Flight Day 6 or thereabouts — Starship will launch, with a specially built docking adapter on its nose.

The SpaceX vehicle — the 171-foot-tall (52 meters) upper stage of its fully reusable Starship megarocket — will meet up with Orion in LEO on Flight Day 8, at an altitude of about 237 miles (381 km). But this will be a very different encounter than the rendezvous with Blue Moon.

"The demonstration with Starship is shorter, about two hours," Parsons said. "The crew will not enter the Starship during the mission, but it gives us critical data and allows us to really understand the forces of docking, how we're going to push, how we're going to work together."

Artemis III will continue for another six to nine days after Orion undocks from Starship before finally heading back down to Earth for a parachute-aided ocean splashdown. (The nominal mission length is 14 days, Parsons said, but it could last as long as 17 days.)

During that time, the crew will evaluate their survival suits — something that didn't get done during the 10-day Artemis II flight around the moon this past April — and do a variety of other work as well, from detailed health assessments to scientific experiments and technology demonstrations. The main goal is to keep everything on track for Artemis IV, which will land astronauts near the moon's south pole in 2028, if all goes to plan.

"Everything we do on Artemis III is designed to reduce risk for our future lunar landing missions," Parsons said. "We're teaching these teams how to work together, how to make critical real-time mission decisions, all in an environment that is low Earth orbit, where if something goes wrong, we can come home much more quickly."

It's unclear at the moment which of these two privately developed landers — Blue Moon or Starship — will fly on Artemis IV, pulling off the first crewed lunar touchdown since Apollo 17 in 1972.

NASA is still preserving optionality, "to give both of our lunar landing partners the greatest opportunity for readiness in advance of astronauts landing on the moon in 2028," NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said during today's event.

"We have no intention of surrendering a viable path to the surface before the data and schedule compels us to do so," he added.

No matter what decision NASA makes for Artemis IV, both Blue Moon and Starship should hit the gray dirt relatively soon, according to Parsons. "Regardless of who goes first, both providers are planning for lunar landing missions in 2028," he said.

And those missions will be quite different from each other. For example, on Blue Origin touchdowns, Orion will meet up with Blue Moon in lunar orbit. On SpaceX landings, however, Starship will likely dock with the NASA capsule in Earth orbit and propel it to the moon, Parsons said — the "push" he referred to above.

It remains unclear when exactly Artemis III will launch. NASA is targeting the summer of next year but may find it tough to meet that timeline: Last month, in an internal memo, Isaacman told agency employees that Artemis III was about 90 days behind schedule.

Some of the biggest challenges have involved the supply chain for the mission's lunar landers, Parsons said during today's event. But he and Isaacman both stressed that NASA is confident in its ability to work through such issues.

"We're moving with real speed and purpose right now," Isaacman said.

Lazuli will have three instruments: a wide-field optical imager, an integral field spectrograph, and the ExtraSolar Coronagraph (ESC).

New research proposes a list of targets for the ESC. It's titled "The first selection of optimal targets for the Lazuli ExtraSolar Coronagraphâs reflected starlight exoplanet campaign," and is available at arxiv.org. The authors are Logan Pearce from the Department of Astronomy at the University of Michigan, and Ewan Douglas from the Steward Observatory at the University of Arizona.

"Direct detection and characterization of exoplanet atmospheres via reflected starlight enables probes of composition of mature exoplanet systems (since flux is decoupled from age) at closer separations than via thermal emission (due to observations in visible wavelengths), and even probes of biosignatures," the authors write.

An illustration of the Lazuli Space Telescope. It will have a 3-meter mirror and three instruments. One of them is the ExtraSolar Coronagraph (ESC). The ESC will allow observations of exoplanets and their atmosphere with reflected starlight, rather than by their thermal emissions. Image Credit: Schmidt Sciences

The telescope's ESC will be especially important in this context. Characterizing exoplanet atmospheres in mature exoplanet systems with reflected starlight means the observations will not depend on an exoplanet's leftover heat from formation. Since older planets have aged, their leftover heat makes them dimmer over time. By using reflected starlight, the ESC will bypass that effect. It will also allow observations at closer separations than an infrared telescope, meaning it can observe planets closer to their stars, because planets closer to their stars reflect more light. (That's a simplified picture that ignores some details.)

"To date all exoplanets have been imaged through their self-luminous thermal emission, but direct, spatially resolved imaging of planets via reflected starlight is on the near horizon," the authors write. "Reflected light imaging enables detection and characterization of planets decoupled from age (since reflected starlight luminosity does not decrease with age), and the smaller resolution limits provided by imaging shorter wavelengths (â¼0.5â2Î¼m), where the star luminosity peaks, allows access to closer-in planets compared to thermal emission."

The researchers present a list of 27 targets for observations with the ESC, and all of them are giant exoplanets. "Our target list spans ages and temperatures of planets not probed before by direct imaging," the authors write, adding that some of them will also be accessible to the ELT's METIS thermal imager and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope's coronagraphic instrument. The targets will benefit from synergies created by multiple observations, and that will "provide comprehensive atmosphere characterization."

#1866. Consistency is key: A YouTube Shorts bonus program rewards creators who upload at regular intervals

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

YouTube is preparing to raise the entry threshold for its Partner Program, but at the same time, it is testing new ways for creators to make money. The platform quietly announced the launch of a Bonus Program that will deliver additional earnings to select Partner Program enrollees who consistently upload short-form content.

As with many new YouTube features, the YouTube Shorts Bonus Program was revealed in a Creator Insider video hosted by Rene Ritchie, YouTube's current Creator Liaison. "What if you could get paid a bonus just for keeping your Shorts schedule consistent?" Ritchie asked. He added that creators who are part of the test can take home "something extra based on your viewership."
