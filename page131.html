#3788. Artist duo David Brognon and Stéphanie Rollin win the Prix Marcel Duchamp

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Paris-and Luxembourg-based duo David Brognon and Stéphanie Rollin have won the Prix Marcel Duchamp, France’s biggest art prize worth €90,000 in total prize funds, including €35,000 for the winner. The other names shortlisted were the Malagasy artist Joël Andrianomearisoa, the French photographer Laura Henno and the French multidisciplinary artist Josèfa Ntjam.

The shortlisted artists typically receive an exhibition at the Centre Pompidou. However, as the space is closed for the next four years, the award exhibition has launched at the Musée d’Art Moderne de Paris (until 7 February 2027).

Brognon and Rollin present sound installations, time-based work, and sculptures exploring themes of “confinement, expectation, and control”, says the website for the Institute of Contemporary Art in Los Angeles which hosted a show of their work in 2019.

At the Musée d’Art Moderne de Paris, their installation entitled 5,376 years, 1,892 months, 1,110 days, 1,201 hours comprises fairground coin-pusher machines filled with "sobriety tokens" awarded by support groups such as Alcoholics Anonymous to mark abstinence milestones.

“With Brognon Rollin, the Prix Marcel Duchamp recognises a rigorous body of work that seeks to give form to what typically eludes our gaze: the experience of addiction, and the nature of private, inner resistance. The piece they conceived for the museum—which presents fairground objects as part of a subtle arrangement—radically shifts our perspective,” says Fabrice Hergott, the director of the Musée d’Art Moderne de Paris, in a statement.

The judging process is a lengthy affair, as is required by the prize founders, the Association for the International Diffusion of French Art (ADIAF). Judges this year include the artist Kader Attia, who won the prize in 2016, the artist Berlinde de Bruyckere and Karin Hindsbo, the interim director of Tate Modern.

The prize, launched in 2000, is awarded annually to a visual artist of French nationality or residence. Past winners include Gaëlle Choisne (2024), Cyprien Gaillard (2010) and Tatiana Trouvé (2007). Last year the Chinese painter Xie Lei was awarded the prize.

In every life, there comes a time when we are razed to the bone of our resilience by losses beyond our control — lacerations of the heart that feel barely bearable, that leave us bereft of solid ground. What then?

“In art,” Kafka assured his teenage walking companion, “one must throw one’s life away in order to gain it.” As in art, so in life — so suggests the American Tibetan Buddhist nun and teacher Pema Chödrön. In When Things Fall Apart: Heart Advice for Difficult Times (public library), she draws on her own confrontation with personal crisis and on the ancient teachings of Tibetan Buddhism to offer gentle and incisive guidance to the enormity we stand to gain during those times when all seems to be lost. Half a century after Albert Camus asserted that “there is no love of life without despair of life,” Chödrön reframes those moments of acute despair as opportunities for befriending life by befriending ourselves in the deepest sense.

Writing in that Buddhist way of wrapping in simple language the difficult and beautiful truths of existence, Chödrön examines the most elemental human response to the uncharted territory that comes with loss or any other species of unforeseen change:

Fear is a universal experience. Even the smallest insect feels it. We wade in the tidal pools and put our finger near the soft, open bodies of sea anemones and they close up. Everything spontaneously does that. It’s not a terrible thing that we feel fear when faced with the unknown. It is part of being alive, something we all share. We react against the possibility of loneliness, of death, of not having anything to hold on to. Fear is a natural reaction to moving closer to the truth.

If we commit ourselves to staying right where we are, then our experience becomes very vivid. Things become very clear when there is nowhere to escape.

This clarity, Chödrön argues, is a matter of becoming intimate with fear and rather than treating it as a problem to be solved, using it as a tool with which to dismantle all of our familiar structures of being, “a complete undoing of old ways of seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, and thinking.” Noting that bravery is not the absence of fear but the intimacy with fear, she writes:

When we really begin to do this, we’re going to be continually humbled. There’s not going to be much room for the arrogance that holding on to ideals can bring. The arrogance that inevitably does arise is going to be continually shot down by our own courage to step forward a little further. The kinds of discoveries that are made through practice have nothing to do with believing in anything. They have much more to do with having the courage to die, the courage to die continually.

In essence, this is the hard work of befriending ourselves, which is our only mechanism for befriending life in its completeness. Out of that, Chödrön argues, arises our deepest strength:

Only to the extent that we expose ourselves over and over to annihilation can that which is indestructible be found in us.

Things falling apart is a kind of testing and also a kind of healing. We think that the point is to pass the test or to overcome the problem, but the truth is that things don’t really get solved. They come together and they fall apart. Then they come together again and fall apart again. It’s just like that. The healing comes from letting there be room for all of this to happen: room for grief, for relief, for misery, for joy.

Decades after Rollo May made his case for the constructiveness of despair, Chödrön considers the fundamental choice we have in facing our unsettlement — whether with aggressive aversion or with generative openness to possibility:

Life is a good teacher and a good friend. Things are always in transition, if we could only realize it. Nothing ever sums itself up in the way that we like to dream about. The off-center, in-between state is an ideal situation, a situation in which we don’t get caught and we can open our hearts and minds beyond limit. It’s a very tender, nonaggressive, open-ended state of affairs.

To stay with that shakiness — to stay with a broken heart, with a rumbling stomach, with the feeling of hopelessness and wanting to get revenge — that is the path of true awakening. Sticking with that uncertainty, getting the knack of relaxing in the midst of chaos, learning not to panic — this is the spiritual path. Getting the knack of catching ourselves, of gently and compassionately catching ourselves, is the path of the warrior. We catch ourselves one zillion times as once again, whether we like it or not, we harden into resentment, bitterness, righteous indignation — harden in any way, even into a sense of relief, a sense of inspiration.

Half a century after Alan Watts began introducing Eastern teachings into the West with his clarion call for presence as the antidote to anxiety, Chödrön points to the present moment — however uncertain, however difficult — as the sole seedbed of wakefulness to all of life:

This very moment is the perfect teacher, and it’s always with us.

We can be with what’s happening and not dissociate. Awakeness is found in our pleasure and our pain, our confusion and our wisdom, available in each moment of our weird, unfathomable, ordinary everyday lives.

Remaining present and intimate with the moment, she argues, requires mastering maitri — the Buddhist practice of loving-kindness toward oneself, that most difficult art of self-compassion. She contrasts maitri with the typical Western therapy and self-help method of handling crises:

What makes maitri such a different approach is that we are not trying to solve a problem. We are not striving to make pain go away or to become a better person. In fact, we are giving up control altogether and letting concepts and ideals fall apart. This starts with realizing that whatever occurs is neither the beginning nor the end. It is just the same kind of normal human experience that’s been happening to everyday people from the beginning of time. Thoughts, emotions, moods, and memories come and they go, and basic nowness is always here.

In the midst of all the heavy dialogue with ourselves, open space is always there.

Another Buddhist concept at odds with our Western coping mechanisms is the Tibetan expression ye tang che. Chödrön explains its connotations, evocative of Camus’s insistence on the vitalizing power of despair:

The ye part means “totally, completely,” and the rest of it means “exhausted.” Altogether, ye tang che means totally tired out. We might say “totally fed up.” It describes an experience of complete hopelessness, of completely giving up hope. This is an important point. This is the beginning of the beginning. Without giving up hope — that there’s somewhere better to be, that there’s someone better to be — we will never relax with where we are or who we are.

Suffering begins to dissolve when we can question the belief or the hope that there’s anywhere to hide.

Decades after Simone de Beauvoir’s proclamation about atheism and the ultimate frontier of hope, Chödrön points out that at the heart of Buddhism’s approach is not the escapism of religion but the realism of secular philosophy. And yet these crude demarcations fail to capture the subtlety of these teachings. She clarifies:

The difference between theism and nontheism is not whether one does or does not believe in God… Theism is a deep-seated conviction that there’s some hand to hold: if we just do the right things, someone will appreciate us and take care of us. It means thinking there’s always going to be a babysitter available when we need one. We all are inclined to abdicate our responsibilities and delegate our authority to something outside ourselves. Nontheism is relaxing with the ambiguity and uncertainty of the present moment without reaching for anything to protect ourselves.

Hopelessness is the basic ground. Otherwise, we’re going to make the journey with the hope of getting security… Begin the journey without hope of getting ground under your feet. Begin with hopelessness.

When inspiration has become hidden, when we feel ready to give up, this is the time when healing can be found in the tenderness of pain itself… In the midst of loneliness, in the midst of fear, in the middle of feeling misunderstood and rejected is the heartbeat of all things.

Only through such active self-compassion to our own darkness, Chödrön suggests, can we begin to offer authentic light to anybody else, to become a force of radiance in the world. She writes:

We don’t set out to save the world; we set out to wonder how other people are doing and to reflect on how our actions affect other people’s hearts.

#3344. How Yin Yoga Bananasana Can Transform Your Swimming

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Swimmers spend plenty of time working on stroke mechanics and building upper-body strength, but mobility of the side body, particularly the intercostal muscles between the ribs, can affect how freely you can breathe, especially as fatigue sets in.

Bananasana, a gentle yin yoga pose, stretches the side body. A supported side-lying pose, with blocks under the ribs and head, creates a more targeted stretch that can help expand your ribs for three-dimensional breathing.

While Bananasana offers a relatively subtle lateral stretch of the obliques, intercostal muscles, and IT band, a supported side-lying variation increases the sensation and may help ease fascial tightness.

Fascia is a network of connective tissue that surrounds and connects muscles, nerves, bones, and organs. When fascia’s layers glide well, the body moves freely. However, training load, injury, prolonged postures, and other factors can contribute to stiffness or reduced mobility. Restriction in the rib cage and side body can make a full, expansive breath feel harder to achieve.

Give your shoulders and lats attention, but don’t overlook your ribs and side body. Add these stretches to your regular mobility routine to build breath awareness and find more ease and efficiency in the water.

The study, published in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition, suggests that creatine could help adults to maintain lean tissue and strength, even without regular exercise. Of course, maintaining adequate levels of muscle mass is particularly important as we age because muscle loss, also known as sarcopenia, can begin as early as 35 years, and typically sees lean mass decline at a rate of 1 to 2 percent, per year, for the average person. In fact, those who fail to engage with regular resistance training could lose as much as 4 to 6 pounds of muscle per decade.

For 12 weeks, men and women between the ages of 45 and 65 were provided with creatine or a maltodextrin placebo, with each group being split into those who wanted to participate in a supervised, structured exercise program, and those who didn’t want to commit to the organized sessions. #1465. Athletes From Every Sport Are Talking About ‘RED-S.’ Is Workout Hustle Culture to Blame?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

YOU NEED TO fuel your body properly for peak performance—that's not news. But hitting the right balance of fuel to energy output can be tough, especially if you're trying to lose weight in the process, are on a GLP-1 medication, and/or recently switched up your workouts. That's where you fall into danger of RED-S, a scary sounding condition where your body reaches a dangerous stage of energy depletion

Also known as “Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport,” RED-S can do everything from slow down your progress to mess with your body on a cellular level. "The simplest explanation is it's an energy mismatch," says Albert Matheny, RD, CSCS, co-founder of SoHo Strength Lab.

RED-S has always been a potential issue with athletes, but the rise of GLP-1s and a NO DAYS OFF workout hustle culture has pushed the condition into the fore.

Trainers say every active person should know the symptoms of RED-S—and what might cause them to show up.

RED-S HAPPENS WHEN you're not fueling your body properly. "It essentially means running your body's engine on empty," explains Dani Singer, CPT, founder of Fit2Go Personal Training. "It's a mismatch between the energy you take in through nutrition and the energy you expend through exercise and daily living."

While a slight calorie deficit is needed to lose weight, RED-S is more severe than cutting a few calories. It's chronically under-fueling while also trying to train at a high level. "You are demanding a level of output from your body that you aren't giving it the fuel to sustain," Singer says.

A FEW THINGS can go down. "Deep, lingering fatigue is usually the first red flag," Singer says. "You'll feel like you're moving through molasses." At the same time, because your body isn't dedicating energy to recovery, Singer says you become "highly susceptible" to overuse injuries and stress fractures due to decreased bone mineral density.

"You'll also likely see thermoregulation issues—always feeling cold—compromised immunity, hormonal imbalances, and ironically, a complete plateau in your fitness progress," Singer says. "You can't build muscle or acquire new physical skills if your body doesn't have the building blocks to do so."

With RED-S, your body does what it can with reduce energy it has. "It really prioritizes key metabolic functions and the things that require your body to function [in daily life]," Matheny says. He compares RED-S to being in low power mode—your body is going to try to support basic functions, while forgetting about things like muscle growth and fitness performance.

"We are constantly taught that 'eat less, move more' is the holy grail of fitness, but your body needs energy not just to exercise, but to simply keep the lights on for things like breathing, hormone production, and cellular repair," Singer says. "When you severely under-fuel while keeping your physical output high, your body goes into triage mode."

THERE ARE LIKELY a few things behind this. "For the general fitness population, it often comes from a toxic combination of diet culture and high-intensity workout trends demanding maximum output on minimum calories," Singer says.

And then there are GLP-1s. "These medications are incredibly effective at blunting the natural hunger cue," Singer explains. "A user might only be taking in 800 to 1,000 calories a day simply because they feel completely full." If someone on a GLP-1 decides to pair their weight loss journey with a rigorous new workout routine, they may not realize how little fuel is in the tank, Singer says.

BECAUSE EVERY PERSON’S energy needs are different, avoiding or finding a solution to RED-S can be complex. "The threshold in literature is 30 calories per kilogram of body weight—that's the lowest level," Matheny says. (That means a 180-pound man should be taking in 2,448 calories per day at a minimum.)

"A good routine should improve how you move and feel in daily life, not break you down," he says.

Aspergillus westerdijkiae shouldn't be here at all. But we might have a way to stop it.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. Why Trust Us?

Fungus has been indispensable to cheese ever since the moldy milk that sounds dreadful but tastes delicious was accidentally discovered around 7,000 years ago. In the millennia that followed, different species of fungi and bacteria have been purposely used to create different cheeses, such as Penicillium roqueforti, which gives Roquefort its blue veins and pungent aroma, or Penicillium camemberti, the reason why Camembert overflows with gooey indulgence. Unfortunately, not all fungi crawling in cheese enhance the sensory experience. Some of them can be poisonous.

When a panicked cheesemaker approached Tufts University microbiologist Benjamin Wolfe, the cheese in question was rotting instead of ripening, its rind pocked with patches that were visibly disintegrating. The timing was no coincidence. Wolfe and his research team had already been investigating an outbreak among artisan cheeses in the northeastern US, one that left flaking black patches on goat cheese and other varieties aging in cheese caves. Analysis of the samples revealed the culprit: Aspergillus westerdijkiae, a mold that was hardly new to science, but had never before made itself at home in cheese. As it feeds, the fungus secretes dangerous toxins—and a species that had never troubled cheesemakers before has suddenly become their bane.

“Over the past decade, disruptive contamination events of Aspergillus spoilage molds have occurred at cheese production facilities in Massachusetts, Connecticut, and Vermont in the United States, causing aesthetic, flavor, and potential safety issues,” Wolfe said in a study recently published in Applied and Environmental Microbiology.

Aged cheeses owe their distinct flavors to an entire microbiome thriving on their surfaces, and which microbes take up residence depends on the variety the cheese is destined to become. Now, however, those beneficial bacteria and fungi are facing an unwelcome intruder. Spoilage molds can muscle out the communities that belong on the rind, leaving behind cheese that looks unappetizing—and, worse, may be unsafe to eat. Aspergillus species in the Circumdati group are known to produce ochratoxins and other harmful mycotoxins. Ochratoxins, in particular, can damage the kidneys. A. westerdijkiae compounds the problem by secreting antimicrobial metabolites such as penicillic acid, which keep beneficial bacteria and fungi from colonizing the rind in the first place.

Until now, though, the genomes of these molds—and how they interact with their surroundings—had gone unexamined, and that gap is what Wolfe and his team set out to close. What little was known pointed in a strange direction. Spoilage Aspergillus species tolerate higher temperatures and thrive in warmer conditions, and while A. westerdijkiae had already been linked to the spoilage of foods other than cheese, the fungus had drawn little scientific scrutiny. It also doesn’t occur naturally in the region it’s now plaguing, which led Wolfe to suspect it had arrived from somewhere else—possibly hitching a ride on imported ingredients like salt. To find out, he isolated Aspergillus strains from contaminated rinds across multiple facilities. The damage, he found, looks different on every cheese: black flakes on goat cheese, fuzzy white and dark gray splotches or dusty black patches on block cheddars.

Comparing those genomes turned up a surprise: A. westerdijkiae wasn’t working alone. A second species, Aspergillus ostianus, was infesting rinds alongside it, and each species was represented by several distinct strains across cheeses aged in different locations. Sometimes they overlapped. One sample harbored two strains of A. ostianus plus a strain of A. westerdijkiae. The genomes also revealed that strains within a species leaned on the same molecular machinery—metabolism, along with core cellular processes like DNA replication and repair. More than a thousand of those genes ramped up once A. westerdijkiae was placed on cheese, a sign that they’re what allow the fungus to take hold on a rind specifically. A. westerdijkiae also carried genes for a wider range of mycotoxins than A. ostianus did.

Where it came from is still an open question. When Wolfe searched the GlobalFungi database, A. westerdijkiae turned up nowhere in the northeastern U.S. That leaves two possibilities: either it was imported, or it’s been there all along and simply went unidentified. The one thing every affected facility had in common was salt. While Wolfe keeps hunting for a natural reservoir in the region, he has a practical fix for cheesemakers in the meantime: hold cheese caves at or below 50°F, a temperature at which the fungus slows dramatically. It works. Cheesemakers who brought their caves into that range told him the rot stopped. The salt, though, still has his attention.

“We are unaware of studies demonstrating the presence of A. westerdijkiae in sea salts,” he said, “but this may be a fruitful area of future research for understanding how these spoilage molds may spread around the world and into food production environments.”

Black holes more massive than entire galaxies are not a major component of the mysterious dark matter that's thought to make up most of the matter in the cosmos, a new study finds.

For decades, to explain why galaxies do not spin apart at the speeds at which they whirl, scientists have suggested the existence of an unseen substance known as dark matter, whose gravitational pull helps keep galaxies together. Dark matter is thought to make up about five-sixths of all matter in the universe.

Much remains unknown about dark matter, however — including whether or not it actually exists. Black holes make good potential contenders for dark matter, since they are extremely massive and are difficult to spot in the dark depths of space.

In the new study, researchers investigated whether dark matter could be made of "stupendously large black holes," ones with masses greater than 100 billion times the mass of the sun. Stupendously large black holes greater than 1 million billion to 10 million billion suns in mass would be heavier than entire galaxies.

So far, astronomers have not detected any stupendously large black holes — currently, the largest black hole known may weigh about 36 billion suns. Since researchers have not seen any even after looking at many galaxies, the new study noted they cannot make up all of dark matter — but team members wondered if the exotic objects still might constitute a significant portion of the puzzling substance.

Often, dark matter is thought of "as a fine dust spread everywhere," study co-author Christopher Gerlach, a theoretical physicist at the Mainz Institute for Theoretical Physics in Germany, told Space.com. However, if dark matter is packed into a handful of gigantic objects such as stupendously large black holes, "the universe becomes grainy on very large scales. And we can check that."

Specifically, the scientists analyzed the light from the dawn of the universe, study co-author Yann Gouttenoire, a theoretical physicist at the Mainz Institute for Theoretical Physics, told Space.com.

For the first 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the cosmos was filled with a hot plasma in which light was trapped. Eventually, the expansion of the universe let the cosmos cool enough for the plasma to largely dissipate and form atoms. This in turn let light shine freely, "and that light is still arriving today," Gerlach explained. "We call it the cosmic microwave background, a snapshot of the infant universe covering the whole sky."

The temperature of the cosmic microwave background "varies from place to place by about one part in 100,000," study co-author Antonio Iovino, a theoretical physicist at New York University, Abu Dhabi, in the United Arab Emirates, told Space.com. "That pattern tells us how matter was spread back then."

Analyzing the cosmic microwave background revealed it is not grainy the way stupendously black holes would have made it. "So these black holes can't be a significant part of the dark matter," Nicholas Leister, a theoretical physicist at the Mainz Institute for Theoretical Physics, told Space.com.

The researchers cautioned that the new findings do not suggest that stupendously large black holes cannot exist, only that they do not make up a major fraction of dark matter. They added that another candidate for dark matter — relatively tiny black holes the size of asteroids — might potentially exist. They are now investigating whether they can rule out such itty-bitty black holes as a major constituent of dark matter.

The scientists detail their findings in a study that will be published Oct. 26 in the journal Physical Review Letters.

#2194. Mexico: Delay in Legal Reform Harms People With Disabilities

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image A dog and a woman with a disability outside the Supreme Court of Justice of the Nation in Mexico City, Mexico, March 24, 2026. © 2026 Gerardo Vieyra/NurPhoto via AP Photo

(Mexico City) – Mexico’s Senate should amend legislation approved by the Chamber of Deputies on October 20, 2026, to ensure that people with disabilities can appoint supporters to make legal decisions by April 1, 2027, as originally planned, Human Rights Watch said today. The current legislation would postpone the deadline for implementing the National Code of Civil and Family Procedures, which requires these appointments, to July 1, 2031.

The code, adopted in June 2023, introduced reforms to improve access to justice and guarantee the right of people with disabilities throughout Mexico to make legally recognized decisions. However, implementation has lagged. Mexico City is the only jurisdiction to have declared the code partially in force, including provisions allowing adults to designate supporters to help them exercise their legal capacity.

“If the Senate fails to amend the legislation to allow the original deadline for appointing supporters to take effect, local judges will face a legal vacuum,” said Carlos Ríos Espinosa, associate disability rights director at Human Rights Watch. “While judges can no longer bar someone from making autonomous decisions, the procedure to help people make those decisions has not yet entered into force. This could leave people with disabilities at risk of losing their property or suffering harm to their health and physical integrity.”

The 2023 code abolished interdiction, a discriminatory guardianship procedure that allowed courts to deprive adults, particularly people with intellectual or psychosocial disabilities, of their right to make legally recognized decisions. Under this system, guardians could make decisions concerning a person’s property, finances, and personal affairs, often without respecting the person’s will and preferences.

Interdiction was abolished on June 8, 2023. Mexico’s Supreme Court has recognized that these procedures are incompatible with international human rights standards and that their repeal does not depend on the gradual implementation of the new code.

However, abolishing interdiction without providing effective alternatives can leave some people without essential legal protections.

The new code establishes an extraordinary judicial procedure for appointing supporters when a person’s will and preferences cannot be determined despite considerable efforts. This mechanism allows a judge to appoint someone to facilitate the exercise of legal capacity, subject to strict safeguards against abuse and requirements to ensure the best possible interpretation of the person’s will and preferences.

Without this procedure, people may face serious consequences. Consider, for example, someone who falls into a coma without having left any advance directives concerning their property, health care, or family affairs. If urgent decisions are required, the absence of a legal mechanism to appoint supporters could put their property at risk or jeopardize their health and physical integrity.

Unlike interdiction, the extraordinary appointment of supporters does not authorize courts to declare a person legally incapable or place them under a general guardianship regime that substitutes another person’s decisions for their own.

International human rights law recognizes that everyone has the right to make decisions about their own lives and requires governments to ensure that people with disabilities have access to the support they require to do so.

Organizations of people with disabilities, older people’s rights groups, professional associations, and human rights organizations have called for the extraordinary procedure for appointing supporters to be exempted from postponement.

The Senate still has an opportunity to amend the legislation approved by the Chamber of Deputies and to introduce an exception allowing this procedure to be implemented as originally planned.

“The government may reasonably require additional time and resources to implement a reform of this magnitude, but delaying a critical safeguard for people with disabilities for nearly five more years is unjustifiable,” Ríos Espinosa said. “The Senate still has an opportunity to correct this situation and prevent people from being exposed to abuse and serious violations of their rights.”

The front of the Centre Pompidou Francilien, with an exterior created using Douglas fir, overlooks a lake and a park

The Centre Pompidou has unveiled a new, large site in a southern suburb of Paris focused on conservation, restoration and storage. Centre Pompidou Francilien – fabrique de l’art in Massy, which will also host exhibitions and residencies, has been open for three “Prélude!” weekends this month ahead of its inauguration in March.

The three-storey rectangular building spans 30,000 sq m. Most of the site is devoted to storage (13,000 sq m) and to conservation and restoration ateliers (4,000 sq m), which are inaccessible to the public. Around 3,000 sq m is open to visitors, including 2,200 sq m of exhibition space.

The impetus for the creation of the institution stemmed from a wish to have a single facility in which to store works from its expansive collection, when they are not on display at its main site, Centre Pompidou, or on loan. This importance of this function has been heightened in light of the Centre Pompidou closing for renovations until 2030.

Centre Pompidou’s holdings comprise some 150,000 Modern and contemporary works of art—more than any other institution in Europe and second globally to New York’s Museum of Modern Art. The works are owned by the French State and held by the Musée national d’art modern, which is located at the Centre Pompidou site.

Over the next two years, 645 trucks will transport the collection, currently dispersed across eight rented spaces, to Centre Pompidou Francilien. Once unloaded, each work will be examined, treated and decontaminated, if necessary, in the studios before being placed in the reserves. There are 20 large storage areas in total; large-scale sculptures and installations will be kept on the ground floor, lighter works on the first and second floor.

The site will also serve as a centre for the dispersal of the around 6,000 works that Centre Pompidou loans each year, an operation that will be facilitated by the site’s proximity to Orly airport.

Speaking to The Art Newspaper, Alexia Fabre, the director of the Centre Pompidou Francilien, emphasises the importance of the workshop facilities. “It is a revolution for the expertise of the Centre Pompidou; it means all our teams working on restoration, framing and conservation will be in the same place,” she says.

An external escalator pays tribute to the well-known outdoor escalator at the Centre Pompidou.
