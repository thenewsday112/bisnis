The XPPen Magic Pro 13 is an Android 16 tablet going on sale today at an early-bird price of $899 / £899, with digital art-focused specs that compete with both Wacom and iPad.

The original XPPen Magic Drawing Pad opened my eyes and made me question whether an iPad really was the only sensible choice for mobile digital art. It had a drawing-focused screen and a stylus built for artists, without the glossy glass that makes an iPad feel like sketching on a glass table. I use an iPad Pro but a Rock Paper Pencil screen protector to get that toothy drawing-tablet feel.

Since XPPen's first Android tablet, Wacom has launched the high-spec OLED MovinkPad Pro 14, and Huion has gone for a budget spec with the Kamvas Pad 12. But the new XPPen Magic Pro 13 sits at the higher end, alongside Wacom and Apple. I've been using the Magic Pro 13 for about three weeks, and it's an impressive tablet for artists looking for a strong iPad alternative.

The new Magic Pro 13 features a 120Hz, 3K display with a nano-etched finish and Android 16. Its 5080 LPI resolution and 16K-level pressure stylus make it accurate, while the MediaTek Dimensity 8300 lets it handle everything an Android tablet needs, including 3D art and games.

The big idea is Drawing Display Mode: you can connect the Magic Pro 13 to a compatible PC with a single full-featured USB-C cable, and it becomes a pen display running at up to 3K at 120Hz. The stylus keeps its 16K pressure levels, tilt detection, hover preview and customisable barrel buttons, and XPPen promises ultra-low-latency input.

XPPen's pitch is that it makes this connectivity easier. In practice, that means you can sketch on the sofa or the train in your favourite Android apps, then plug in at your desk to keep going and use the tablet like a standard drawing tablet. It could save you buying a standalone tablet and a separate pen display.

XPPen’s Brian Huang, Marketing Director, says the Magic Pro 13 is “an industry-first 2-in-1 device” that works as a standalone drawing pad and a professional drawing display. “Artists need creative tools that can adapt to the way they work, wherever they create,” he adds.

Wacom's MovinkPad Pro 14 can do something similar, but it's not as simple as plug-and-play; you need to enable Developer Mode and USB Debugging in the Android settings (though it also offers WiFi connectivity).

The Magic Pro 13's sleek, tactile 3K display lets you choose 60Hz, 90Hz, or 120Hz, and its nano-etched AG+AF glass feels papery and more natural than drawing on an iPad. The display offers a bright 650 nits peak brightness, higher than the iPad and iPad Air but below the OLEDs of the MovinkPad Pro 14 and iPad Pro, plus 100% sRGB and 96% DCI-P3 coverage, with system-level switching between sRGB, Adobe RGB, and DCI-P3. There’s also TÜV SÜD Low Blue Light certification.

The new X4 Smart Chip Stylus has a bullet-shaped nib, 16K pressure levels, 60-degree tilt and a claimed 2g activation force. In use, I've found the low threshold helps with light marks and delicate shading and is a huge step up from the original Magic Drawing Pad. Slow diagonals and gradual line-weight changes tell me more than the pressure count alone, especially against Wacom’s Pro Pen 3 and Huion’s PenTech 4.0, and the XPPen feels good (my full review is coming).

The Magic Pro 13 is 6.65mm thick and weighs 655g, with a 10,200mAh battery and 45W PD fast charging on both USB-C ports. It has an 8MP front camera and a 13MP rear camera for capturing references and textures on the go.

XPPen has added its own software, a must and a sign the brand is finally catching up with Wacom in software and driver support. The Shelf app lets you tag, annotate, and search reference images, and Canvas lets you make quick sketches. You also get Quick Draw with Screen Off, Freeform Windowing and Screenshot Doodle.

That’s a lot for an Android drawing tablet, and Huion’s Kamvas Pad 12 is the cheaper option at $499, but it has a less powerful processor and a lower-spec display. With XPPen's new Magic Pro 13, you’re paying for the higher-end tech you expect from Wacom and Apple: a high-refresh 3K screen, 120Hz, the MediaTek Dimensity 8300 processor to handle 3D as well as standard apps, and an easy-to-use PC drawing mode. Whether the pen and the desktop connection justify the money is something I explore further in my full review.

#2256. Ninth Circuit Rules Trump Illegally Federalized National Guard in Los Angeles

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

The ruling is an important decision on judicial review of executive invocation of emergency powers to use the military for domestic law enforcement.

On October 23, in the case of Newsom v. Trump, the US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit ruled Donald Trump's June 2025 federalization of the National Guard in Los Angeles was likely illegal. This decision has little immediate effect, because the National Guard deployment ended months ago, after the Supreme Court ruled against Trump in a related Illinois case. But it has important implications for judicial review of executive invocation of emergency powers to use the military domestically. Among other things, it emphasizes that such actions are reviewable, and the president should get only limited deference. It also adopts a properly restrictive definition of "rebellion" (which is one of the conditions that might justify federalization of the National Guard).

Trump federalized the Guard in response to anti-ICE protests. The district court ruled his action was illegal and issued an injunction blocking it. But the Ninth Circuit lifted the preliminary injunction, in a decision I criticized at the time.

The relevant statute 10 U.S.C. Section 12406, can only be used to federalize state forces in one of the following situations:

1) the United States, or any of the Commonwealths or possessions, is invaded or is in danger of invasion by a foreign nation;

(2) there is a rebellion or danger of a rebellion against the authority of the Government of the United States; or

(3) the President is unable with the regular forces to execute the laws of the United States

Last year, the Ninth Circuit ruled (incorrectly, I think) that Trump's action might have been permissible under Subsection 3: a situation where "the President is unable with the regular forces to execute the laws of the United States." As the Ninth Circuit acknowledges in their most recent decision, this basis for Trump's actions was undermined by the Supreme Court's December ruling in the related case of Trump v. Illinois, which held that Subsection 3 can only be invoked when the president is authorized to use regular federal military forces to enforce the law, and those forces are inadequate. These circumstances do not apply to the anti-ICE protests in Los Angeles and Chicago.

No one claims that the situation in Los Angeles amounted to an "invasion" by a "foreign nation." Thus, in its most recent decision the Ninth Circuit considered whether Trump's actions were authorized by Subsection 2, which allows federalization of the guard when "there is a rebellion or danger of a rebellion against the authority of the Government of the United States."

The majority opinion by Judge Jennifer Sung holds that the determination that there is a "rebellion" is subject to judicial review and can be invalidated " if it "did not reflect a colorable assessment within a range of honest judgment." I think this is overly deferential. For reasons I outlined in an article published last year, there should be little or no deference when the executive invokes extraordinary emergency powers. Still, the Ninth Circuit standard is far from a blank check, and does allow invalidation of executive actions where the claim of rebellion is blatantly wrong - as it was here.

Following the district court, the Ninth Circuit defined  "rebellion" as "a violent, armed, organized, open and avowed uprising against the government as a whole." This differentiates "rebellion" from smaller-scale violence or other violations of law. I think that definition is largely correct. The court summarizes a wide range of historical evidence supporting it.

The Ninth Circuit also concluded that events in Los Angeles did not even come close to meeting that standard. There were some violations of law by protestors, and some small-scale violence. But no systematic effort to overthrow the government or anything close to it. If anything, both this issue and the definition of "rebellion" are easier calls than the court suggests.

Judge Sung, author of the majority opinion, is a liberal Biden appointee. But her opinion was joined by Judge Eric Miller, a conservative Trump appointee and onetime clerk for Supreme Court Justice Clarence Thomas. This suggests the issue here cuts across ideological and jurisprudential lines. Judge Mark Bennett, another Trump appointee, dissented, but mostly on procedural grounds.

Judge Bennett did also take issue with the majority's conclusion that if Trump's federalization of the National Guard exceeded his statutory authority, it also violated the Tenth Amendment, which reserves to the states or to "the people," those "powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution." He argues that the power to call forth the National Guard is delegated to Congress by the Calling Forth Clause of the Constitution, which gives Congress power "[t]o provide for calling forth the Militia to execute the Laws of the Union, suppress Insurrections and repel Invasions." This is true enough. But in situations where Congress has not exercised that authority, states retain total control over their militia (of which the National Guard is the principal modern example), and that control is protected by the Tenth Amendment. Indeed, such control over state military forces is a central element of state sovereignty. Overriding it without proper constitutional authority qualifies as unconstitutional "commandeering" of state government forces. As the majority explains:

The federal government—whether the President or Congress—violates the Tenth Amendment by commandeering state personnel to enforce or administer federal law. Printz v. United States, 521 U.S. 898, 925 (1997) (Under the Tenth Amendment, "the Federal Government may not compel the States to implement, by legislation or executive action, federal regulatory programs."). If, for example, the President commandeered state police to help federal officers enforce federal immigration laws, that would violate the Tenth Amendment….

California National Guard members are state personnel, unless properly federalized… The President purported to convert the Guard into federal personnel by issuing federalization orders. If those federalization orders had been valid, then the President would have deployed federal officers in Los Angeles, and no commandeering of state personnel would have occurred. But because the President lacked authority to federalize the California National Guard, the federalization orders were invalid, and the deployed Guard members remained state personnel….

In other words, because the deployed members of the California National Guard were not properly federalized, they remained the legal equivalent (for Tenth Amendment purposes) of the state police. And because the challenged executive orders compelled those state officers to help federal officers enforce federal law, the orders violated the Tenth Amendment….

The Tenth Amendment protects States against overreach by the "Federal Government," whether "by legislation or executive action." Printz, 521 U.S. at 925, 935. The President cannot command state officials to enforce federal law even if a federal statute would authorize him, under some other circumstances, to call those officials into federal service. If the statute did not authorize the President to federalize the California Guard members here…, then the federalization orders were invalid, the Guard members remained state personnel, and Defendants violated the Tenth Amendment by commandeering those state personnel to administer federal immigration laws.

Exactly so. The majority and dissent  consider a number of procedural issues, which I will not try to address. The majority also parses a variety of relevant precedents in detail. I may have more to say about some of them in future writings.

For the moment, this decision has little immediate effect. But it sets an important precedent in favor of judicial review of executive invocation of emergency powers, and on the meaning of "rebellion."

#1817. Instagram chases YouTube’s TV watch time with 90-minute uploads. Will creators get paid, though?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Heads up, Netflix and Disney: Instagram wants a piece of YouTube‘s TV watch time, too.

“We’ve been hearing for a long time that people love to watch Reels together in their living rooms with their friends, so we’ve been working on a TV version of Reels,” Tessa Lyons, Instagram’s VP of Product, said in an announcement video alongside chief Adam Mosseri. “And we know to do that well, we need more than just short-form video.”

As a result of this insight, Instagram is testing a creatively named new feature, Features, that will let creators populate its platform with horizontal videos up to 90 minutes long.

Per The Hollywood Reporter, Instagram hopes Features will appeal to long-form content creators, podcasters, and comedians. It has already tested Features with some long-form creators in Brazil and plans to expand to “tens of thousands of creators” in the U.S. over the coming months.

Creators using Features will also get access to a tool YouTubers (and anyone else who’s been clued in to the clipping scene) will find familiar: Clips as Reels, which lets creators excerpt short segments from their Features videos and post them as standalone shorts on Instagram’s TikTok copycat.

The clipped Reels will automatically link out to the long-form video from which they were taken.

“What you’re now going to be able to do is, you see a clip of a podcast that you love, you can just click and continue watching or watch the full video, and you’ll be able to dive in and see the whole thing because creators are going to be able to link these Reels to their overall feature,” Lyons said.

THR reports Instagram plans to recommend posts made with Clips as Reels on its Explore page and in other areas around the platform.

But he thinks the secret sauce is the Clips as Reels system, which is “trying to make it so that the short-form and the long-form videos can be symbiotic.”

“We’re going to try to support these different types of formats, both in the mobile app, but also on the TV app, which […] is going to take some time to get it right,” he added. “But I’m actually very excited about it, and we’re very committed to it long-term.”

For us, the elephant in the room is ad revenue. YouTube, at the core of its business model, is set up to pass ad revenue on to creators. That’s why creators benefit from the prevalence of long-form content being watched on desktops, mobile, and (especially) on TVs.

More watch time in a premium locale like the living room TV means more command of high-value audience attention, which means more attention from brands, higher CPMs–and, if all goes well, more money in creators’ pockets.

Instagram is owned by Meta, one of the biggest digital ad businesses in the world. It would stand to reason that if Insta (which, like everyone else, has struggled to effectively monetize short-form video) is embracing longer content and also gunning for TV watch time, Meta will run ads against creators’ long-form uploads.

If that happens, how does Instagram plan to reward creators for making all that revenue-earning content?

“It’s too early to share a long-term monetization model, but we’re exploring ways for creators to earn from brands, fans, and Instagram, including performance-based incentives,” a Meta spokesperson told TechCrunch. “Over time, we think there will be compounding benefits for creators to tell new stories, reach new audiences, and find new partners, which should lead to many monetization opportunities.”

Is that the corporate equivalent of your parents saying We’ll see? Maybe. For now at least, it seems creators won’t earn extra money from uploading Features.

So will all this work out? Also maybe. Instagram tried doing TV in 2018, with little success–RIP, IGTV–but we’re in a wildly different era now, and 90 minutes is Instagram’s girthiest content limit to date. It might be able to follow the footprints YouTube left years ago.

#561. Mayo Clinic discovers new ways to fight the “zombie” cells that drive aging

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Seven studies from Mayo Clinic are revealing new details about senescent cells, often called "zombie" cells because they stop dividing but remain active in the body. These aging cells release inflammatory substances that can contribute to diseases associated with growing older.

The research, published in Nature journals, was conducted by scientists at Mayo Clinic's Robert and Arlene Kogod Center on Aging. Rather than simply confirming that senescent cells play a role in aging, the studies investigate how to recognize different types of these cells, why they produce harmful inflammation and how their effects might be reduced.

The findings also contribute to a nationwide effort to create detailed maps of senescent cells throughout the human body. Known as the Cellular Senescence Network (SenNet) Consortium, the project brings together researchers across the United States and receives funding from the National Institutes of Health Common Fund. Its goal is to document where senescent cells occur in different tissues and how they change throughout a person's lifetime.

"This body of work is moving the aging field forward to precisely identify, understand and target the specific senescent cells that contribute to age-related dysfunction, which is the necessary groundwork to develop new therapies that support healthy aging," says Marissa Schafer, Ph.D., a Mayo Clinic scientist whose laboratory directed several of the investigations.

Scientists Discover That Zombie Cells Are Not All Alike

Identifying senescent cells reliably has long been a major obstacle for scientists studying aging. The new research shows that these cells are much more varied than previously understood. Their characteristics differ depending on where they are located in the body and which biological processes caused them to become senescent.

To help address this challenge, researchers developed a tool called the SenMayo gene set. It uses patterns of gene activity to identify senescent cells more consistently across tissues and experimental systems.

Scientists tested the approach using bone biopsies from older adults. They also found that the gene signature became less prominent after senescent cells were removed in preclinical experiments. Since its development, SenMayo has become a widely used resource for researchers investigating aging and related diseases.

Mayo Clinic endocrinologist Sundeep Khosla, M.D., served as senior author of the study, "A new gene set identifies senescent cells and predicts senescence-associated pathways across tissues." Other Mayo Clinic researchers who contributed to the paper include Nathan LeBrasseur, Ph.D., Diana Jurk, Ph.D., João Passos, Ph.D., and LaTonya Hickson, M.D..

A Hidden Connection Between Mitochondria and Chronic Inflammation

Other studies explored one of the most damaging features of zombie cells: their tendency to generate ongoing inflammation.

The researchers discovered additional biological processes that explain why senescent cells continue producing inflammatory signals. Several of these processes involve malfunctioning mitochondria, the tiny structures responsible for producing much of a cell's energy.

In one pathway, DNA and RNA originating from mitochondria activate immune responses that contribute to inflammation. Another involves changes in mitochondrial metabolism that influence inflammatory gene activity through epigenetic mechanisms. These mechanisms affect how genes are switched on or off without changing the underlying DNA sequence.

The discoveries suggest that researchers may eventually be able to interfere with the processes responsible for chronic inflammation while leaving the senescent cells themselves intact. This would offer another approach to treatment beyond attempting to destroy every harmful aging cell.

Experimental Treatments Show Promise Against Aging Cells

The Mayo Clinic team also investigated possible ways to reduce the number of senescent cells or limit the damage they cause.

The approaches included senolytic medications, which are designed to eliminate senescent cells, a natural compound present in tomatoes, and genetic techniques intended to target particular populations of aging cells.

When tested in older mice, these interventions produced encouraging results. Depending on the approach, researchers observed reductions in inflammatory measures and improvements in several aging-related outcomes, including physical frailty, inflammation in the brain and performance on cognitive tasks.

Another investigation uncovered a possible way to measure aspects of biological aging through blood testing. Researchers identified IL-23R as a potential biomarker that might eventually help scientists evaluate aging-related changes and determine whether future treatments are working.

These results remain preliminary in terms of medical applications. More research will be necessary to establish whether the approaches are safe and effective in people. Nevertheless, the experiments provide valuable clues for developing treatments that more precisely address the cellular processes involved in age-related diseases.

A New Direction for Healthy Aging Research

Together, the seven studies represent an important shift in how researchers approach cellular aging. Scientists are moving beyond the broad observation that senescent cells contribute to age-related decline and toward identifying which populations cause the greatest problems, what makes them harmful and how those specific effects could be controlled.

The findings also challenge the idea that eliminating senescent cells must always be the primary treatment strategy. By understanding the molecular pathways that trigger persistent inflammation, researchers may be able to develop therapies that block damaging signals without removing the cells responsible for producing them.

Mayo Clinic researcher Darren Baker, Ph.D., served as senior author of the paper "Senotypes define the diverse landscape of senescent cells," which examines the varied characteristics of these aging cell populations.

The work also supports Mayo Clinic's broader Precure Research initiative. This program aims to equip physicians with better ways to identify and intervene in harmful biological changes before they develop into disease or advance into conditions that are difficult to treat.

By revealing how zombie cells differ, why they promote inflammation and which processes might be targeted, the research offers a more detailed roadmap for future treatments designed to support healthier aging.

The New York Times published more excerpts from Bob Woodward's new book. It seems that Woodward has material from Justices Breyer and Kagan:

Mr. Woodward also writes about encounters with more recent justices. He reports that his wife, Elsa Walsh, a former journalist for The Post and The New Yorker, sat next to former Justice Stephen G. Breyer at a dinner and he told her that he was "forced out" to make way for someone younger on the court.

He also recalls running into Justice Elena Kagan at a Washington party in 2023. Justice Kagan expressed admiration for "The Brethren" and Mr. Woodward noted that the main point was how the ideological center of the court in that era dominated. "Yes, I prefer it that way," Justice Kagan said. "That's exactly what it should be."

When he asked her if she was happy on the court, Justice Kagan said no, she was not, given the situation.

I don't think Justice Breyer gets to complain to a reporter's wife about being pushed out. It was his choice. No one forced him to step aside for Justice Jackson. But he folded. By contrast, Justices Thomas and Alito resisted all of the pressure.

As for Justice Kagan, does she really want the Court to be governed by the "ideological center"? Would the center of the Court have been Merrick Garland? I don't doubt that she is unhappy.
