Candidates are holding on to the offers they get, which makes every interview precious and each of these common interview questions worth practicing. Job offer decline rates fell to 21.4% for interviews that began between January 1 and April 15, 2026, according to Glassdoor Economic Research¹. That's down 5.1 percentage points from the same period last year.

Daniel Zhao, Glassdoor's chief economist, told CNBC Make It that "there's no time like the present" to practice your interviewing skills. One of the best ways to get ready for a virtual job interview is to practice your responses to the most common interview questions.

We've sifted through tens of thousands of interview reviews to find out some of the most common interview questions candidates get asked during recent interviews. If you have a job interview lined up, practice in front of a mirror or ask a friend or family member to listen to your answers to the following questions, so you’ll be ready to put your best foot forward. General advice: Keep your responses concise and purposeful (under 90 seconds). Use the "Present-Past-Future" framework: describe your current role, briefly explain relevant experience, and connect your goals to this specific opportunity. When discussing what you'd accomplish, show you've researched the role and understand immediate priorities. Avoid generic answers — specificity demonstrates preparation and genuine interest.

Daniel Zhao's advice in CNBC Make It: show hiring managers you're "one of the top 10 candidates that they have seen out of potentially 100."

How do I find interview questions for a specific company or role?

Search the company's interview reviews on Glassdoor to see the questions recent candidates reported. Reviews also show how candidates rated the difficulty and their overall experience.

What should I do if I get a question that isn't on this list?

Pause, ask a clarifying question if you need one, and adapt one of your prepared stories to the new prompt. Then connect your answer back to the skills the role requires.

Can I use artificial intelligence (AI) to practice common interview questions?

Yes, use AI tools to generate practice questions and pressure-test your drafts, then say each answer out loud in your own words. Over-scripted answers sound rehearsed, so treat AI output as raw material for your own phrasing.

#1710. 9 must-have words to use in a resume

📅 Oct 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Glassdoor Team | Author & Career Expert at Glassdoor | Oct 7, 2026

Before you call yourself a "rockstar" or pitch your "synergy" one more time, think twice. Clichés don't show what you did.

The right words to use in a resume show your impact before anyone picks up the phone. Proofread, too, since typos distract from your strengths.

"The words used show what level the candidates are at in their career," says job search expert Susan Joyce. "If I picked up a resume for a C-suite candidate who chose vague descriptives, they would immediately be discredited."

Based on expert research, here are nine words key words to include on your resume.

Job coach Angela Copeland says, "Rather than stating that you're an 'excellent digital marketer,' prove it." Her example: "Grew online sales and revenue by 200% in one year."

A recruiter on Glassdoor Community flagged one resume as "lacking context - achievements, outcomes, scope, size, budget of all projects and teams you've been on." Add those details to your work experience section.

Life and career coach Jenn DeWall favors action verbs that show leadership and transformation. "'Redesign' demonstrates problem-solving skills as well as the ability to think big picture and reduce process inefficiencies."

If clichés are a no-no, simple yet powerful words are your go-tos. Simple, practical words that show responsibility carry real weight: launched, solved, transformed, and optimized.

According to master resume writer Jacqui Barrett-Poindexter, modernized, along with "conceived and metamorphosed," is "like a switch, powering up the candidate's story." They show "how they've improved, envisioned and transformed their work environments."

"These words show that the candidate is thinking about their own activities in terms of how they'll improve the business," says author Josh Doody. "Terms like 'hard-working' don't do this because working hard doesn't necessarily produce better results."

"Anyone can say they 'led' a team," insists Joyce. "… Consider the verb 'orchestrated' and how it shows, versus just telling, the hiring manager what was accomplished."

Show you owned the work, says executive coach Kate O'Sullivan. She looks for "candidates who have had a role in shaping something from idea all the way through execution."

A resume is not the time to be shy, says Copeland. "If you were instrumental in a project, replace the word 'helped' with the word 'spearheaded.'"

It shows dedication, start to finish, says DeWall. "Managers can gauge aptitude, readiness, and even your leadership skills from paper."

These five habits help your resume wording land.

Skip the latest buzzword and get specific, Joyce says. "It is important to use verbs that really pinpoint what was accomplished, i.e., influenced, improved, achieved, etc."

O'Sullivan says "it's very hard to convince a resume reader that you possess various soft skills… just by listing these." For teamwork, cite "onboarding new hires or developing a cross-training program."

"Demonstrate those skills in the examples you list for each job/role/title don't list them out separately," said one associate director on Glassdoor Community. See hard skills vs. soft skills.

The Society for Human Resource Management (SHRM) 2025 Talent Trends survey found 43% of HR professionals used AI for HR tasks in 2025. That's up from 26% in 2024, and reviewing or screening applicant resumes is one of the common uses, SHRM reported.

Pull the posting's exact skill terms into your bullets where they're accurate. A Glassdoor Community member wrote that "tailoring your resume to each job—using the exact language from the posting—can help you beat the bots." Swap screening tips in the Job Seeker Support bowl.

"Hiring managers don't sit down the day before an interview," says Doody. Instead, they "open up the resume as the interview is starting and skim it."

On a resume, "every single word counts," says O'Sullivan. "Edit with the mindset of 'Does this piece of information directly help sell my experience for this role?'"

"The easiest way to improve your choice of words is to read your resume out loud to yourself," advises Copeland.

Join the Glassdoor Community to trade resume feedback with people hiring in your field.

Do the same resume words work for director or executive roles?

Yes, and vague wording costs more at that level. Joyce warns that a C-suite resume with vague descriptives gets discredited, so tie each verb to revenue or team size. For workers with 20+ years of experience, the share of interviews from online applications fell from 70.5% in 2023 to 61.1% in 2025.¹

Should I let AI pick the words on my resume?

Use it to spot a posting's repeated keywords, then edit every line so it's true. As one manager on Glassdoor Community put it, make sure "it's still accurate and true to your skills and experience."

#3390. Cycling 3,000Km From Samarkand Through the Pamirs

📅 Oct 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We do a lot of stories about adventurous Westerners, so it’s a treat when we’re reminded how many local people from every corner of the globe feel the same itch to wander.

On August 1, 39-year-old Ravshan Amirov from Samarkand, Uzbekistan, left his city’s famous Registan Square. He cycled east through Tajikistan into China, then turned northwest through the edge of the Tien Shan mountains into Kyrgyzstan. Finally, Amirov finished in his own country, in the town of Andijan, on September 2.

He covered over 3,000km in 33 days, camping much of the time, though he did stay in some small hotels and accepted hospitality when strangers offered it.

Amirov works as the library director at the Samarkand International University of Technology — an atypical adventure background. But three years earlier, at Registan Square, Amirov met a French couple who had cycled through 15 countries in four months. Their journey inspired his own. He then spent roughly a year preparing —training, acquiring equipment, and studying books and films about long-distance cycling. He even ran a marathon.

Yet already on the third day, in Dushanbe, Tajikistan’s capital, he discovered that his preparations had not been ideal. His bike and gear felt like they weighed a ton. He spent a day pruning his gear and making a few repairs, which were already necessary.

Troubles continued. His sleeping pad punctured, forcing him to put in a brutally long day to reach a town large enough to have a hotel where he could repair the puncture. “This is a bitter lesson to learn,” he commented on his Instagram expedition page, VeloLife.

Large parts of his journey followed the Pamir Highway, which had some smooth paved sections but also bone-jarring rough patches. About halfway through, he reached the toughest section of the ride: a three- to five-day stretch where he had to carry all his food and water. “Very scary,” he mused in the lead-up.

A rough and dusty section of the Pamir Highway. Photo: Ravshan Amirov/VeloLife

Indeed, Day 18 proved the hardest. He rode all day and didn’t see a single person or vehicle. “I’ve never seen such roads in my life,” he wrote. “I was preparing for something terrible, but it turned out to be horror squared.”

A lonely road. Photo: Ravshan Amirov/VeloLife

By the 22nd day, things began to pick up. He crossed into China, where the road was “smooth as glass.” He spent an extra day soaking up the sights of the ancient city of Kashgar.

A day off in Kashgar. Photo: Ravshan Amirov/VeloLife

The Ak-Baital Pass was always going to be the crux. At 4,655m, it was the highest part of his route. He reached it on Day 26. Although temperatures in his hometown of Samarkand drop to near zero in winter, he had never slept outside in such cold, and his sleeping bag was inadequate.

“It was the first time in my life I was this cold,” he said. “It was just unreal.” He drank a whole pot of coffee in the morning to warm himself up and improve his morale.

After almost a month, he finally reached the Kyrgyz border. The Tien Shan Mountains affected him deeply. “It was so beautiful that I stopped every 100 meters,” he said. “I was overwhelmed with happiness. In all my 29 years, I don’t think I’ve ever been happier.”

On Day 32, he overnighted in the Kyrgyz city of Osh, then cycled back into his native Uzbekistan the next morning, arriving in Andijan after a short two-hour jaunt. From here, as planned, he caught the train back to Samarkand.

To understand why you undertake something as complex and demanding as writing a book, you need to start at the beginning.

I started running in the fall of seventh grade as a pimpeled, slightly-overweight, late-blooming pre-teen. Middle school wasn’t a kind place for kids like me, but I found solace in the cross country team. None of us were cool. Or particularly athletic. None of us knew how to dance. Or flirt. Or talk to girls. But on the team we found community, and that is just about all you need in life.

I’m not sure why I showed up to the first practice, but, if memory serves, it was a con job by my friend Reed, who we’ll bookmark for later. The two of us grew up in a rural Minnesota county where 90% of the land is public and whitetail deer easily outnumber humans. There were plenty of downsides to living in the literal middle of nowhere, but one upside was limitless outdoor access, including running.

Many of our weekend long runs were on the Superior Hiking Trail, which is riddled with roots and rocks, and generally horrible for running. Our coach made us do it anyway. At the time we didn’t call it trail running. No one did. The “trail” denomination was in its nascence, if existent at all. This was well before large-lugged shoes, pro trail contracts, or the now-ubiquitous cultural catalysts like Strava, UTMB, carb-maxxing, and Scott Jurek memes on social media. You might say we were early adopters, but we certainly didn’t know it at the time.

This might all sound like a poetic genesis for a book about trail running, but that’s not how the real story goes. I didn’t fall in love with trail running then. In fact, I hated it. I quit cross country after a few years and didn’t return to trails until after college, almost a decade later. So, why start with that origin story?

Because my why for returning is also my why for writing The 50 Classic Trail Runs of America.

This is the most straightforward question, so I’ll tackle it first. The Fifty is a beautiful coffee table book that aims to do for trail running what The 50 Classic Climbs did for climbing and The 50 Classic Ski Descents later did for skiing: establish the definitive list of classic trails to inspire generations of runners to come.

Wanting to be sure we picked the absolute best routes, we decided to go a step further than our literary forebears and run every trail on the list, some of them many times. There were quite a few surprises, like the remoteness of the Rogue River, stumbling into bears on North Circle, last minute wildfires closures in the Wind Rivers, and snow on multiple desert runs. There were big bonks and even bigger rallies, an entire grocery aisle of gummy bears consumed, and a grueling solo mission on the longest and hardest trail, Wonderland.

Ultimately, we selected each classic for something unique—stunning alpine views, ethereal geological formations, or important pieces of indigenous history. Together, we wanted the fifty to tell the full story of America’s public lands, both good and bad. Each trail also comes with a story of our experience running it, a mix of moving meditations, physical challenges, and forging deeper connections with friends.

You may notice that I’m using “we” in the section above, which is not by mistake. Writing the book has been a journey almost as long as the classic trails within. I don’t know the exact number of trails we ran, which might be a good thing.

We had five criteria that trails had to meet to be considered classics. The first three are simple: Can it be run in 24 hours or less? Does it have a clear beginning and end (i.e. no sections of longer trails)? Is it mostly runnable, with little off-trail, scree, or scrambling? Those first few narrowed the list to around seventy. I hope the book inspires more people to get out the door and explore both their local trails and further into their mind. As far as I can tell, there’s no better teacher than a long day running in the woods, and I want to share that gift with everyone.

#3673. Bike Brands Shouldn’t Charge You Twice for Hardware You Already Bought

📅 Oct 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As bikes get more electronic, brands have more ways to turn ownership into access.

Some corporations are trying to upend traditional notions of ownership. They want to turn buying into licensing. Instead of paying once and owning a product indefinitely, you receive access on terms they control, for only as long as it serves them.

Now, some companies want customers to pay ongoing subscription fees to use features in products they already bought. I fear cycling equipment could be next.

One recent example of the changing definition of ownership: Sony warned PlayStation Video customers in the U.K. that 551 StudioCanal titles they had purchased would be removed from their video libraries.

“From September 1, 2026, due to our content licensing agreements, you will no longer be able to access your previously purchased content from Studio Canal, and it will be removed from your video library.”

One more time: “You will no longer be able to access your previously purchased content.”

You paid for it. Sony made it disappear when a licensing deal changed. The fine print may say that is how digital purchases work, but it is still a hell of an asterisk next to the word “purchased.”

That is the digital-media problem. What worries me is the same logic moving into physical products. As bikes, cars, and other things we own add electronics, software, and connectivity, companies get more ways to decide what works, what stops working, and what we have to pay extra to use.

When does a subscription pay for an ongoing service, and when does it charge you a second time to use something already built into the hardware you bought?

Before anyone cries hypocrisy, yes, Bicycling relies partly on subscriptions. But no surprise, I think that’s different. It is more like a library card for a library that’s open 24/7: You’re paying for access to an ever-expanding collection of content, updated almost daily with stories like the one you’re reading now.

Let’s carry those questions about the shifting definition of ownership over to the physical products in your home and garage.

Imagine buying a house, signing up with the electric company, and finding out your new home’s light switches won’t work until you pay a separate subscription to activate them. Paying an ongoing rate for continuous electricity delivery is one thing. Paying again to use the hardware already installed in the walls of the home you just bought? That’s an inventive interpretation of ownership.

A few years ago, in some markets, BMW tried something very similar. If you bought a new BMW with factory-installed heated seats, those seats wouldn't work until you paid $18 per month to unlock their functionality.

As you might imagine, the idea drew intense backlash, and BMW dropped seat-heating subscriptions.

Cycling has had its own version. Many 2019 Cannondale road bikes came with a Power2Max power-meter spider already installed. But if you wanted to use the power meter? Pay Power2Max roughly $500 to activate it.

Cannondale stopped equipping its bikes with the pay-to-activate Power2Max the next model year.

#2422. Why Wind Is More Dangerous When the Leaves Are Still on Trees

📅 Oct 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Trees with full canopies catch more wind, while wet soil can weaken their hold. Here’s why fall storms can cause serious tree damage.

Trees are vulnerable to powerful storms, even a single gust of wind. While tree damage can occur any time of the year, wind damage is more dangerous in certain seasons.

Fall can be a key season for wind-related tree damage because deciduous trees may still hold their leaves when storms bring strong winds and heavy rain. That’s why anyone who has dealt with mature trees knows how much more movement a leafy canopy creates during a storm.

The reasons leafy trees face more stress during windy weather, and the role wet soil plays, help explain why some fall storms cause serious tree damage.

Tree professionals sometimes describe a dense canopy as having a windsail effect. Leaves and branches give the wind more surface to push against, placing extra pressure on the tree.

A video from arborist education account @fruitstitute on Instagram compared a thick canopy to a sail catching wind. Research published in the International Society of Arboriculture’s journal on red maples found that the makeup of a tree’s crown affects how much wind load it experiences.

A post shared by Fruitstitute (@fruitstitute)

Trees can reduce some of that pressure naturally. Leaves turn and cluster in the wind, while branches bend and move with gusts.

That doesn’t mean a tree should be heavily thinned before a storm. Pruning needs vary by species and tree condition, so major pruning decisions are best left to a qualified arborist.

Foliage can also increase the amount of wind a canopy catches. For example, an eight-year study of street trees in Rochester, New York, showed that most branch failures happened while the trees were in leaf.

Wet ground can add another risk during a windstorm. After heavy or prolonged rain, saturated soil may hold tree roots less securely. The U.S. Forest Service notes that saturated soil and shallow roots can make trees more vulnerable to being uprooted by wind.

Penn State Extension also notes that trees may begin leaning or uproot when wet soil can’t hold the root system securely. Raised soil around the roots can be one sign of movement. A full canopy catching strong wind while the roots sit in soaked soil can create a difficult combination for a tree.

Fall storms can arrive before deciduous trees have dropped their leaves, leaving a full canopy exposed to strong winds. If rain has already soaked the ground, the tree may also have less support around its roots. The combination can increase the chance of broken branches, leaning, or uprooting.

A tree’s condition still matters. Decay, root damage, weak branches, and earlier storm damage can all affect how well it handles high winds. After a storm, homeowners should stay clear of trees with new leaning, lifted soil, cracked limbs, or hanging branches. Penn State Extension recommends professional assessment when a damaged tree could threaten people or property.

There are a few things you should always check when looking through a lumber pile at a hardware store or lumber yard. Are you getting a straight board? Is it free of major knots or defects? Is it the right size and grade for your project?

But what about the paint color at the end of the wood?

Anyone who has spent time sorting through 2x4s has probably noticed that some pieces have paint on the ends of them. But why?

The confusing part is that there isn’t one meaning that applies to every painted board. Learn why lumber ends may be painted, what the colors can identify, and what shoppers should check before relying on the paint alone.

Paint or another coating on freshly cut lumber can help slow moisture loss through the ends of the wood. The exposed end grain can dry faster than the sides of a board, which can cause cracks or splits as the lumber dries.

“End coatings are typically used to help prevent end checks on sawn lumber during the drying process,” according to research done by the U.S. Department of Agriculture, Forest Service, and Forest Products Laboratory. “These drying defects can cause a loss of valuable lumber.”

Timber Custom Woods describes the same process for logs and slabs.

“The ends of the slabs are painted in an effort to keep the ends of the wood from drying out to quickly causing cracking and checking,” according to Timber Custom Woods. “By painting the ends of logs before milling, we can help prevent splitting and cracking by slowing down water loss and allowing the logs to dry more evenly.”

The coating doesn’t stop the wood from drying. It helps slow moisture loss at the ends so the wood can dry more evenly.

There isn’t a standard U.S. color code for painted lumber ends. Blue doesn’t always represent one length, and red or green don’t automatically indicate a certain grade or species.

That helps explain the different answers in a Reddit discussion about 2x4s with blue-painted ends. Commenters described the colors used for certain lengths or products across different mills and lumber yards, showing how the meaning can vary by supplier.

A painted end may help slow drying, identify a particular product, or serve as an internal code for a mill or lumber yard. The color alone usually doesn’t provide enough information to identify the board.

Painted ends can also serve as a simple identification system. Lumber producers and mills may use different colors to separate types of lumber or identify species, grades, products, or brands.

U-C Coatings, which makes paint for lumber, says its end paints can serve these identification purposes. Because mills can choose from many colors, the shades don’t follow one nationwide system.

Individual companies may also use a recognizable color for a specific product. Dunn Lumber, for example, sells its signature blue-end studs. In that case, the blue identifies that company’s line of framing lumber rather than all lumber of a certain size or grade.

Largely in response, more than 6,000 nonprofit foundations have made major investments in local news initiatives, totaling some half a billion dollars or more in direct funding for journalism.

As a result, the number of U.S. nonprofit news organizations has been growing by double-digit percentages year-over-year.

It’s fair to question whether this is sustainable. But in the meantime, at least, what’s not to like about this arrangement — this major push to subsidize local journalism at a time when the for-profit model for it is rapidly vanishing?

Behind the scenes, it seems, there are cracks in the foundation, ones that appear to call for greater scrutiny to understand how sustainable and effective this model truly is moving forward.

As described in an ethnographic study published recently in Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly, Jocelyn McKinnon-Crowley of the University of Memphis spent four months in late 2023 and early 2024 embedded in a four-person nonprofit newsroom in a midsized Rust Belt city, and also interviewed news workers at seven other nonprofit outlets. She focused on the question of labor: What is the impact of this type of journalism on the work and the workers involved?

Her main finding is blunt: “The emphasis on funding, on money, and on organizational concerns has become the top priority for individuals working for nonprofit news entities.” And, as journalists “busy themselves” with these administrative chores and organizational imperatives — to keep grant money coming in, to keep funders happy, etc. — it not only comes at the expense of the journalism product but also extracts a heavy toll of unpaid labor from the news workers involved.

The situation, McKinnon-Crowley writes, “leads to a double worker exploitation where journalists feel morally obligated to do good work as journalists and to do good work for a morally just (nonprofit) organization.”

This exploitation, she says, is perpetuated by the nature of nonprofit reliance, as foundations often “take decision-making power away from journalists and the institution of journalism.” A clear example of this, she says, is the grant-making process, which can require an undue level of administrative burden and can force journalists to put the priorities and interests of funders ahead of those of the community.

The result, she contends, is a pernicious cycle:

Rather than allowing space for journalists to center the needs of their community, the voices and opinions of the funders are prioritized into perpetuity as nonprofits continually need external funding. This reproduces social inequality, as journalists are incentivized to favor those who can distribute wealth, mirroring similar global trends where the news contributes to making the “information rich get information richer.” Part of the challenge is that, unlike the wall that typically exists between editorial and advertising at for-profit news organizations, there are few clear lines of separation. Funders tend to sit on boards overseeing the news outlets. They shape application forms and set renewal cycles. Journalists interviewed described the “mental formulas they are constantly calculating to keep producing the news.” While such concerns have always been present to some degree — and for-profit news managers, no doubt, are often plenty stressed about their own funding arrangements — McKinnon-Crowley suggests these problems are particularly tough for nonprofit newsrooms given how small and precarious they tend to be, and given how quickly funding provided through one grant cycle can disappear entirely in the next. Even more, the researcher found a distressing level of unpaid work that seemed to be the rule, not the exception, in small-outlet nonprofit journalism. In one example, there was a journalist who officially held titles as executive director and managing editor, though he was not necessarily compensated for such higher-level work. Moreover, that was only the start of his duties required to keep the organization humming. McKinnon-Crowley writes: From what I observed, here is a list of some of his unofficial job duties: fundraiser, party planner, grant writer, mentor, teacher, board member, publisher, figurehead, reporter, editor, tech support, building maintainer, sounding board, community-liaison, coffee-buyer, freelance coordinator, radio personality, podcast scripter, scheduler, photographer, and social media manager. The upshot: Much of the actual work that this person wanted to do as a journalist was, by his own admission, “slipping through the cracks.” McKinnon-Crowley summed it up this way: “As I observed in the newsroom, if you have to return cheese trays from banquets and create fundraising emails, the split priorities lead to gaps in what the organization is able to do.” So, what should be done? McKinnon-Crowley recommends that funders give recurring money without restrictions, fund positions on a longer-term basis (5 to 10 years minimum), help pay for administrative overhead, require less paperwork burden in reporting back, and stop dictating coverage decisions. For nonprofit newsrooms, she says it’s time to start having more conversations about these issues — about what counts as unacceptable interference on the part of funders as well as what the risks are of asking journalists to do too much for too little compensation. Alexis Haskell surveyed 695 U.S. journalists and found that disillusionment is a measurable experience among American journalists. The concept is different from burnout, the researcher argues. Burnout describes exhaustion and being overwhelmed by work. Disillusionment describes a deeper break between what journalists expected their careers to be and what they actually experienced.

On average, the journalists surveyed were more disillusioned than not. Haskell measured the concept through their feelings about their work, their expectations for the profession and their sense of purpose.

That disillusionment was also reflected in how journalists viewed journalism as a whole. Those who were more disillusioned were more likely to give the press lower marks for five of six democratic roles examined by the study. They were less positive about journalism’s ability to inform, analyze, provide a forum for public discussion, foster social empathy, and mobilize audiences.

The strongest relationships were found in analysis and the public forum. More disillusioned journalists were particularly likely to believe the press was failing to help people understand complicated issues or provide space for public conversation.

There was one exception: Disillusionment was not related to journalists’ evaluations of investigative journalism.

Haskell suggests that investigative work may occupy a different place in journalism’s professional culture. It is highly prestigious, frequently celebrated through major awards and often associated with individual achievement. The other roles are more directly tied to journalism’s service to audiences and communities.

That difference gets at what makes disillusionment important. Journalists who are disillusioned aren’t necessarily saying they can no longer do good journalism. They may instead be questioning whether journalism, as a profession, is accomplishing what they believed it was supposed to accomplish.

The finding connects the personal experience of working in journalism with a broader assessment of the field. When journalists encounter low wages, layoffs, shrinking staffs, and other pressures, the consequences may extend beyond their own careers.

They may begin to lose faith not just in the job, but in journalism’s ability to fulfill the public purpose that drew them to it in the first place.
