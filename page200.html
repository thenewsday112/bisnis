#3425. Bored With Your Workout? Try a New Level, Not a New Program

📅 Sep 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

This was the first thing a new client said to me.

When we dug into it, here’s the pattern that emerged:

Strength training, then running, then a yoga challenge, then back to lifting with a different app.

Nerd Fitness Coaching was the latest in a long line of fresh starts.

And here’s the thing: all of it was GOOD.

She was moving. She was trying new things. She was having fun (at least at first).

She felt like she kept starting over and never getting anywhere.

And she wanted help figuring out what to do.

Let’s be real: trying something new is exciting.

A new program, a new class, a new challenge. There’s a buzz to it.

And that buzz isn’t bad. Enjoying it is a big part of what keeps people moving.

The catch is that most goals need some repetition.

Getting stronger, running farther, learning a skill. You get better at the things you practice again and again.

So the question isn’t “how do I stop getting bored?”

It’s “how do I keep some of that new-thing fun AND repeat things enough to see progress?” 🧐

When I built her program, I kept its overall shape the same week to week.

But inside it, a few key exercises got a target. Something like:

“Once you can do 12 reps with 25 pounds, we level up.”

The targets were far enough away to stretch her, and close enough that she could hit them in 2 to 6 weeks of focused practice.

When she hit one, we leveled up that exercise: new reps or weight, or a new variation. Everything else stayed put.

There was always a target she was closing in on and a level she’d recently beaten.

But the frame stayed the same. We were still working on the same overall qualities.

And that’s what let her progress stack up instead of resetting every few weeks.

She was able to stay really consistent with her program.

But when she came back, she wasn’t looking for something new.

She was excited to pick up her program and keep progressing.

And sure, sometimes her goals would change.

We spent 6 months training for her first half marathon, then switched gears to focus on building muscle.

If you love bouncing between things and you’re happy doing it, keep bouncing.

This is for when the bouncing starts to feel frustrating.

Before you reach for a new program, try a new level.

✅ Set a target you could reach in 2 to 6 weeks (a rep count or a weight)

✅ When you hit it, level up that one exercise with new reps, a new weight, or a new variation. Leave everything else alone.

I’m excited to see what you come up with. 😃

#4203. Children at risk for depression get “stuck” on sad faces

📅 Sep 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A smile or a frown may reveal more than emotion alone. The faces that capture a child's attention could provide important clues about their mental health and how depression develops.

New research from Binghamton University, State University of New York, suggests that depression may influence how strongly children focus on emotional expressions such as happy or sad faces. The pattern appears to differ depending on whether a child has a family history of depression.

Researchers at the Mood Disorders Institute at Binghamton University study how depression develops in children and adolescents, with particular attention to how family history and emotional experiences may influence vulnerability over time. By identifying these patterns while children are still developing, scientists hope to better understand who may be at greater risk and eventually improve prevention strategies.

"Most of the vulnerabilities that we focus on are still developing during this time period," said Brandon Gibb, director of the Mood Disorders Institute and SUNY distinguished professor of psychology. "You can catch things as they're developing, rather than only studying them once they're already there and pretty stable."

Earlier research has linked depression with greater attention to sad faces. However, those associations have generally been modest, and scientists have not been sure whether biased attention contributes to depression or develops because of it.

The Binghamton study was designed to investigate that question by tracking both depressive symptoms and attention patterns over time. According to the researchers, it is the first study to examine how changes in each may predict later changes in the other among children.

"The real novel piece is that we looked at these transactional relations," said Kelly Gair, a PhD student at Binghamton and lead author of the paper. "Between attentional biases and depressive symptoms, we looked at the way that they were mutually predicting one another across the time points, which is especially novel and hasn't been done before."

The research team, which also included Leslie A. Brick from the University of New Mexico, followed 242 children and their mothers for two years. Participants were assessed every six months.

During each visit, children viewed pairs of faces on a screen. One face showed a neutral expression, while the other displayed an emotion (happy, sad, or angry). Eye tracking technology measured which face held the child's attention and for how long.

Children in the study looked at faces conveying different emotions onscreen, and eye tracking technology recorded where their attention veered. Photo credit: NimStim Set of Facial Expressions (Tottenham et al., 2009).

The results suggest that increasing depressive symptoms do not affect every child's attention in the same way. Family history appeared to play an important role.

Among children whose mothers had a history of major depressive disorder, rising depressive symptoms were associated with greater attention to sad faces. In other words, as these children experienced more symptoms themselves, their attention became increasingly drawn toward expressions of sadness.

"For those who are already at risk, the more these children experience depression themselves, the more they lose their ability to pull their attention away from the sad things around them," Gibb said.

"We know that when you're depressed, it changes what you pay attention to," Gair said. "Our results suggest that these changes may be more long-lasting and may differ depending on family history. One thought is that for children of mothers with depression, who are exposed to more facial displays of sadness from interactions with their mom, these types of facial expressions become even more salient when they experience depression themselves, so their attention becomes increasingly stuck on sad expressions."

A different pattern emerged among children whose mothers had no history of depression.

When depressive symptoms increased in these children, they did not become more focused on sad expressions. Instead, they paid less attention to happy faces.

"In our lower-risk children, what seems to be happening is that experiences of depression are eroding a protective factor, which is how much they pay attention to happy faces," Gibb said.

The distinction suggests that depression may alter emotional attention through different pathways depending on a child's underlying level of familial risk. For some children, sadness may become increasingly difficult to ignore. For others, positive emotional signals may simply become less likely to capture their attention.

The researchers are continuing to follow the children as they enter adolescence, a period when the risk of developing depression increases for many young people. They hope to determine whether these early attention patterns are associated with a greater likelihood of receiving a depression diagnosis later on.

The paper, "Transactional Relations Between Attentional Biases for Affective Stimuli and Depressive Symptoms in Offspring of Mothers With and Without Major Depressive Disorder," was published in the Journal of Psychopathology and Clinical Science.

Most people categorize this as an emotional gauntlet. It’s a time for family and mayhem, making new memories and reliving old ones.

For physicians, or any high earner really,  it’s also a financial trial. Having high income doesn’t magically make holiday spending a breeze. If anything, your wealth-building habits are put to their hardest test in November and December. It’s hard to be a spectator when everyone around you is spending like they’ve never heard of a budget.

Planning now, while the season still feels far off, is one of the ways you can get ahead of the season and its temptations.

Last year, a Gallup survey found that consumers expected to spend $1,007 on gifts alone but that figure dropped to $778 by the time November actually arrived. That’s usually when the early enthusiasm comes face to face with the actual bank balance.

Physicians aren’t immune to this. Heck, they might even be more vulnerable. High earners overspend for the same reason everyone else does. Decisions made in December, under time pressure, during the busiest clinical stretch of the year, have a way of getting out of hand.

There’s also a well-documented cognitive cost to decision-making under pressure. Research on decision fatigue has repeatedly found that the quality of a person’s choices declines the more decisions they’ve already made that day.

Spreading holiday decisions across three months instead of cramming them into three weeks in December means less mental fatigue for the person who has to manage it all.

This is the most understandable objection, and also the one early planning actually solves.

I’m not suggesting you build a full itinerary this weekend. Take one task at a time like booking a flight or automating that recurring transfer. It can also be something as simple as asking family members what gifts they want this year.

These tasks are small enough that they should only take a couple of minutes but each one removes a decision you’d otherwise be making in December, when your schedule will be much worse.

You wouldn’t be the outlier you think you are. And getting the conversation going in the family group chat will help you mentally sort out a lot of the logistics before Halloween, even.

Many of us still believe that Black Friday is the best day to buy. That reputation doesn’t hold up well under scrutiny.

Accroding to Bankrate’s Ted Rossman, the deep discounts people associate with Black Friday now typically show up in early October instead.

WalletHub’s research has repeatedly found that 36% of items marketed as Black Friday deals offer no real savings compared to pricing in the weeks before.

Many of the products marketed heavily during the holiday season (toys and electronics in particular), actually get steeper discounts in December and January once retailers work through leftover inventory.

Waiting for a specific sale date isn’t the savings strategy many of us think it is.

To be fair to the skeptics, planning early isn’t free of trade-offs.

Locking in a flight or a purchase in September means giving up the chance that the price drops later. Booking early is a bet on certainty over optimization, which is reasonable for a flight with limited seats. Not so much for buying a gadget you could just as easily get on sale in January.

Ironically, the same early planning that is supposed to mitigate stress can sometimes morph into its own source of stress. You might start feeling more anxious the more you think about all the holiday stuff you still have to get done.

The planning process should be unintrusive enough that it doesn’t disrupt your current routine. Think of it like soup on the stove set to a slow simmer. Tasted and seasoned occasionally with some stirring here and there.

We tend to make the holidays all about the last two weeks of December. Which is honestly a small pay-off for months of planning but what if you could make the planning part of the fun?

Physicians already spend all year making decisions under pressure. The holidays don’t have to be one more thing squeezed in at the last minute. Start now, while it still feels early, and you’ll spend less money, lose less sleep and get to enjoy the season instead of just surviving it.

The holiday season will not be exempt from the domestic repercussions of overseas conflicts and economic uncertainty.

So, plan ahead and save yourself the last-minute drama.

#4192. Brain scans reveal which disorders are linked to faster brain aging

📅 Sep 29, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

People with dementia, mild cognitive impairment, alcohol addiction, and psychiatric conditions such as schizophrenia may show signs of accelerated brain aging, according to a study published in the open access journal PLOS Medicine. The research, led by Shile Qi of the Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics, China, found that different conditions appear to be associated with distinct patterns of aging across the brain.

Researchers can estimate whether a person's brain appears older or younger than expected for their chronological age. One way to do this is with predictive age difference (PAD), which compares a person's actual age with an age estimated from brain imaging. A positive PAD means the brain appears older than would typically be expected.

To investigate how different brain conditions might influence this measure, the researchers analyzed structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data from 45,900 controls drawn from several brain imaging databases. They compared those scans with data from 2,698 people with a range of conditions and differences, including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), alcohol or tobacco addiction, Alzheimer's disease (AD), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), schizophrenia, bipolar disorder or major depressive disorder.

Alzheimer's and MCI Show the Strongest Associations

Among the conditions examined, the neurodegenerative disorders AD and MCI showed the strongest associations with higher PAD values, suggesting the greatest degree of accelerated brain aging.

Addiction and psychiatric disorders were also associated with increased PAD. By contrast, the researchers found no overall differences in PAD between controls and people with ADHD or ASD.

Different Disorders Affect Different Brain Regions

The team also examined PAD across individual brain regions and looked at patterns of gene expression associated with the different conditions.

One area that stood out was the prefrontal cortex, which showed higher PAD across multiple brain disorders. Psychiatric disorders were associated with elevated PAD in the frontal and temporal lobes, while dementia was linked to higher PAD in the frontal and occipital cortex.

Addiction showed a different pattern. Higher PAD was observed in the default mode network, as well as in the salience network, putamen and thalamus.

The researchers also identified differences in gene transcription associated with particular conditions and divergences, suggesting that the patterns of brain aging may be linked to distinct underlying biological processes.

The findings do not show that these conditions directly cause accelerated brain aging. The study is correlational, and some conditions, particularly psychiatric disorders and addiction, frequently occur together, which can make their individual effects difficult to separate.

Even so, the researchers suggest that studying PAD more closely could eventually help identify biomarkers associated with common brain disorders and provide new clues about the biological pathways involved.

The authors add, "Different neurological disorders appear to leave different signatures on the brain aging clock, which may help researchers better understand the neural and biological pathways involved in these conditions."

The funders had no role in study design, data collection and analysis, decision to publish, or preparation of the manuscript.

#1943. How to Build Your Own AI Apps (No Coding Required)

📅 Sep 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Are you spending too much every month on software subscriptions that don’t actually fit the way your business works? Wondering how to build custom AI-powered apps and tools, even without any coding or technical experience?

In this article, you’ll discover how to brainstorm, build, and launch custom AI apps that replace expensive software, even with zero technical background.

Business owners no longer need to accept the tradeoff of paying for software that partially solves their problems. AI-powered tools now make it possible to build custom apps from scratch, without writing code or hiring a developer.

Erika Stanley describes this as building for a “market of one,” where the software serves one specific business instead of thousands of generic users.

Traditional software is designed for mass markets, which means individual business owners adapt their workflows to someone else’s product. The features they need most are often missing, while features they’ll never touch inflate the cost.

Erika points to her own client management system as an example: it costs more than $300 a year and handles leads, contracts, client communications, and invoicing, but it has never quite worked the way she needs it to. There are features she’ll never use and capabilities she wishes it had.

That dynamic is shifting. With AI-powered vibe coding platforms and tools like Claude, business owners can now build custom AI apps that solve their specific problems, no coding required. Erika is not a software engineer, yet she has replaced more than $1,200 worth of annual software subscriptions in just a couple of months using this approach, all while paying just $100 per month for a Claude Max plan.

To justify upgrading from Claude’s $20-per-month plan to the $ 100-per-month Max plan, she set a simple goal: cut at least $100 per month in software costs to make the upgrade pay for itself.

The benefits extend beyond cost. Custom-built tools do exactly what the builder needs, nothing more and nothing less. When requirements change, the builder can go in and add features rather than emailing customer service and waiting for a response. And once the skill set is in place, the same approach can be applied to new problems.

The app doesn’t have to be business-related at all; once the skill set is in place, any problem in daily life becomes a potential build. Apps built this way can become products for memberships, communities, or direct sales.

Erika’s first MVP project was replacing a $29/month subscription tool she’d used for more than two years. Fixer did two specific things for her business. First, it used AI to sort, tag, and color-code her email inbox while generating draft responses that matched the tone of each thread. Second, it provided an AI meeting note-taker that joined every client Zoom call and auto-generated follow-up emails, placing them in drafts ready to send.

Rather than building a full replacement, Erika identified that she already had access to the same capabilities through existing tools. Claude’s connectors could handle email sorting and tagging. Fathom’s free tier could provide meeting transcripts. And Claude itself could generate the follow-up emails from those transcripts.

The prompt she used to start the project was simple: “Here’s the problem I’m having. Here’s the software I’m using now. Is there a better way?” She described the problem and the desired outcome, not how to solve it. She let the AI determine the best approach.

The solution wasn’t a fancy dashboard or a separate app. It was a scheduled task within Claude that runs every morning at 5:30 AM, a time chosen specifically to conserve tokens. The task processes the inbox, generates an email digest, and sends it via Slack, with each follow-up email delivered as a separate message that can be copied, reviewed, and sent.

The biggest mistake new app builders make is trying to build everything at once. Erika recommends starting with the concept of a minimum viable product, or MVP. The starting point is a friction point: something where, if that one thing were just a little bit easier, the whole day would run smoother.

The analogy is a skateboard. Instead of trying to build a car on day one, Erika suggests starting with the simplest version that solves the core problem. A skateboard has four wheels and two axles. It does the job. It gets from point A to point B faster than walking.

From there, the product can evolve. Add steering, and the skateboard becomes a scooter. Add pedals, and it becomes a bicycle. Each iteration adds functionality while keeping the product usable at every stage. “A working skateboard beats a non-working car sitting in your garage,” Erika says.

Once a friction point is identified, the next step is brainstorming a solution with AI.

Erika recommends opening Claude then to describe the problem and the desired outcome, not to prescribe the solution. The AI’s internal knowledge of available tools, integrations, and workflows often produces a more elegant solution than what a non-technical user would design independently.

Pro Tip: Erika notes that Fable Five, Claude’s highest-level model, is particularly strong at problem-solving. It excels at connecting the dots between a person’s problem and the solution they need.

The next step is creating a product requirements document, or PRD.

In traditional software development, a PRD is the first thing an engineer requests. It’s a comprehensive list of everything the product needs: what it should look like, how it should operate, and what should happen when a user takes a specific action. Branding can be included if the app needs a particular visual identity.

Any LLM can generate a PRD from a conversation. Erika recommends describing the product concept and then asking the AI to “create a PRD for me.” The AI will produce a detailed requirements list that should be reviewed line by line. Because the model has to make some inference jumps when creating the document, it’s important to check that every requirement is accurate, that nothing critical is missing, and that the AI hasn’t misunderstood the intent.

For business owners who aren’t sure what to include in a PRD, the AI can conduct an interview that walks users through PRD creation.. It asks questions back and forth, covering what the product should do, what problem it solves, and how users will interact with it. Each answer fills in the gaps, and the result is a polished requirements document ready to feed into a building platform.

Pro Tip: Erika recommends completing all brainstorming and PRD work in Claude or ChatGPT before entering the vibe coding step. Vibe coding platforms charge tokens for every exchange, and credits run out quickly during active building, so arriving with a finished PRD saves credits. She also recommends paying monthly rather than annually, bumping up to a higher tier during active building, and dropping back to the lowest tier once the app is finished and only needs hosting.

With a PRD in hand, the next decision is where to build the app with vibe coding.

Vibe coding is the practice of describing what an app should do in plain language and letting AI generate the code, no programming knowledge needed. Several vibe coding platforms exist, including Lovable, Base44, Replit, and Bolt.

Erika has used multiple platforms and currently prefers Lovable at $25 per month. Lovable functions as an end-to-end solution, taking a project from brainstorming through development to deployment, ending with a live URL that can be shared with anyone. The platform includes hosting and a database, so there’s no need to set up external infrastructure.

She finds Lovable more intuitive than alternatives for two reasons: the brainstorming and chat experience feels more natural, and the deployment settings are easier to navigate.

Once the PRD is loaded into Lovable, the platform builds the app. Making changes is handled through a chat box in the left sidebar. Requesting a change, such as adding light mode and dark mode, generates a prototype that can be tested within the platform before publishing. Once the update is ready, clicking publish automatically replaces the live version that all users see.

Claude Code offers another path. It can accept the same PRD and build a functional app, but it builds locally on the builder’s computer. This makes it ideal for personal-use apps where only one person needs access. Regular Claude can also publish apps as artifacts, but Erika notes that those are meant to be prototypes, not production-ready applications.

Publishing for multi-user access takes extra steps, covered in the hosting section below.

Pro Tip: To design the app interface with a polished look, Erika recommends using regular Claude to mock up what the login page, homepage, and interior pages should look like based on the PRD. Claude Design can take those mockups further, adding branding, colors, logos, and generating multiple variations to choose from. Pinterest mood boards can serve as visual references for the desired style.

Two hosting platforms work well for vibe-coded apps: Vercel, which is free and suits simple apps with low traffic, and Railway, which costs approximately $5 per month and handles apps that need a database, like the book club app. Both connect to GitHub, a code storage platform that tracks every version of the app, similar to how Google Drive works for documents.

Lovable and Base44 handle hosting automatically, so there’s nothing extra to set up. They also support custom URLs within the platform. To avoid the cost of purchasing an additional domain name, Erika sets up a redirect from her website so that users visit a clean, branded link like AIQueens.com/appname rather than a Lovable domain.

Apps built in Claude Code need a separate hosting service to make them accessible online. Claude can walk through the setup process step by step.

Security is essential for any app that handles user logins, email addresses, or passwords. Erika notes that hackers are specifically targeting vibe-coded apps, making security checks a necessary step before publishing.

Lovable and Base44 include built-in security checks. Before publishing, the platform flags major security issues with a red indicator and can fix those vulnerabilities automatically.

Claude Code can also address security issues, but it won’t flag them on its own. Builders using Claude Code should specifically ask what security vulnerabilities might exist and request fixes before deploying.

For version control, connecting each app to the same GitHub repository used for hosting makes it possible to recover previous versions if an update causes problems. Lovable also allows rolling back several steps without a GitHub connection.

For Claude Code builds, Erika recommends telling the AI to back up to GitHub. With smarter models, instructing the AI at the beginning of a build session to make periodic backups can work, though she notes that reminders are sometimes needed throughout the process.
