This 20-minute Peloton class teaches you about exercise in space, and it's unlike anything else.

Peloton instructor Robin Arzón just took fitness out of this world and into space. Her new 20-minute class features astronaut Jessica Meir, who was broadcasting straight from the International Space Station when the ride was recorded. The class is available for free on Peloton’s YouTube channel or through the Peloton app.

The ride starts with Arzón introducing Meir, who shows riders her view of Earth from the space station’s cupola, the dome-like observatory that many astronauts use to photograph Earth. Meir remarks during the ride that the view of our planet is magical and “never gets old.”

Meir joins the class on an exercise bike that resembles a minimalist, high-tech unicycle mechanism with no seat or handlebars, anchored to the wall, called the FERGO, or Flight Ergometer. In microgravity, pushing down on a pedal would launch an astronaut backward to the ceiling. To stay in place, Meir wears a wide waist belt secured to rigid side struts, with her cycling shoes clipped into specialized pedals with straps.

Meir talks about the importance of cardiovascular activity and “living happily in isolation” in a short video during her prior stint on the ISS in 2020, during the COVID-19 lockdown. There, she and fellow astronaut Drew Morgan showed off an earlier model of the bike, called CERVIS (Cycle Ergometer with Vibration Isolation and Stabilization System), as well as a T2 treadmill for running that uses a harness and bungees to counter near-weightlessness.

The historic, space-themed ride was filmed in September but wasn’t released until last week. Meir, the commander of Crew-12 on the ISS, returned to Earth with her crew on Oct. 7.

Even if you don’t ride, it’s worth watching the class to learn what it’s like aboard the ISS. Meir addresses questions that anyone interested in fitness or space (or both) would appreciate: the importance of strength training for astronauts and the changes the human body undergoes.

“We need the loading on our muscles and our bones to keep our muscles and our bones healthy,” says Meir. “If we didn’t have that, we’d lose a significant amount of muscle mass and also bone density. Up here, when you come into microgravity, your bones start leeching calcium just because they’re not being loaded.”

Meir says that astronauts commit 2.5 hours a day to exercise, spending a lot of time on the ARED, or the advanced resistance exercise device, doing everything from squats and deadlifts to bench presses and sit-ups. The machine, which she describes as an “engineering marvel,” can go up to 600 pounds.

There were other fun moments in the video, including when Meir takes a drink of water by making a blob in midair from her water pouch and gently flinging it into the air before catching it in her mouth, a moment that seemed to genuinely delight Arzón. Meir also talks about using a “space toilet” (consisting of a “big pump” that sucks everything down), and how the ISS recycles up to 98% of its water, including processing urine. She quips that “today’s coffee is also tomorrow’s coffee,” or as astronauts like to say, “from toilet to tap.”

CNET Senior Editor Laura Michelle Davis, an avid Peloton member who frequently chooses Arzón as an instructor, found the ride both instructional and inspirational.

“The ride was full of information about the effects of microgravity and the importance of lifting weights when you’re just floating around,” Davis said. “It was also full of tech and engineering marvels, livestreaming a conversation from hundreds of miles above Earth’s surface to a Peloton studio in NYC and eventually appearing on my bike monitor at home.”

Physical activity is a common talking point for astronauts. Sunita Williams was the first astronaut to run an entire marathon in space, syncing it with the 2007 Boston Marathon and completing the race in 4 hours and 24 minutes. Astronaut Tim Peake was the second astronaut to do so, running in the 2016 London Marathon, which he completed in 3 hours and 35 minutes.

For people on Earth, the Peloton video was a chance to get some reps in while learning a little bit more about the ins and outs of living in space.

“It’s always easy to get lost in the experience when doing a Peloton ride,” Davis said. “This ride kept my brain active, pushing me to think about what it’s like to live (temporarily) in space and how astronauts’ bodies have to adapt. It felt like my own intergalactic journey.”

#969. Vivienne Westwood Spring 2027 Ready-to-Wear

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

For spring 2027, the Vivienne Westwood design team continued its ongoing dialogue with the house archives, finding playful and expansive new ways to interpret its standards. The starting point was Vive La Bagatelle, Westwood’s flirty spring 1997 collection and the prettily tied ribbon being flirtatiously undone that was central to it. Indulging in that push and pull between propriety and provocation, the team drew on central Westwoodisms: the fetishization of Englishness, the intimacy of the boudoir, and the titillating appeal of clothes that refuse to behave themselves.

There was plenty to recognize here in the familiar sweep of a Sunday dress and the off-kilter construction of ‘drunken’ tailoring, interwoven with some thrilling and ambitious developments. The new Mona corset, inspired by the house’s original 17th-century-style corset, introduced reworked side panels that pulled in the waist even further, with softer boning making for a surprisingly comfortable fit. The label has always understood the art of body-contouring, but this season found more subversive ways to cinch, twist, and contain the figure. Ruched and knotted skirts in denim and knit clung and released in unexpected places, leaving the wearer to decide how to indulge.

The newly developed Chi Chi dress was a standout, a dramatic black taffeta gown with a sculptural bodice and arpeggiating skirt that brought together echoes of the archival Bird of Paradise silhouette and the irreverence of the Wake Up, Cave Girl! collection. Black provided a strong undercurrent amid the plaids in off-kilter colors, appearing across cady, crepe jersey, and eveningwear inspired by John Singer Sargent’s Madame X.

Even more fun was to be had with the prints. Ripe grapes, revisited from spring 1997, sprawled across a drunken shirt, with its deliberately distorted construction giving the impression of movement even when standing still. Magnolia blossoms appeared on voluminous skirts and outerwear, and François Boucher’s Mars and Venus Surprised by Vulcan brought the familiar strain of rococo to a casual jersey tee. There were micro-ginghams and broken pinstripes, too—a particularly Westwoodian exercise in articulating respectable conventions of English dressing with a bit of rebel spirit.

Tailoring was deviously compelling. Waistcoat jackets, a popular silhouette in the 2000s, came paired with the louche and architecturally draped dovetail trousers. Elsewhere, double-breasted jackets were paired with Pirate buckle back trousers, and relaxed Wreck jackets featured low-slung pockets. One killer jacket was cinched with belts low on the waist and high on the shoulders, a sartorial bondage-y badinage. Romance abounded with the traditional Nottinghamshire-made Cluny lace—a now rarified artistic practice—as well as with fine, fluid, sparkle-dashed knitwear and shorter iterations of the Sunday dress.

Accessories continued the archival conversation: the stacked Clomper sole made a return, as did the anatomical-toe as slingbacks. This year marks 15 years of the house’s Made in Kenya partnership with the Ethical Fashion Initiative and Nairobi-based social enterprise Artisan Fashion, which supports local craftsmanship and artisan communities. Among the accessories were exquisite hand-beaded and woven bags from Kenyan artisans.

The breadth of references is always a delight, but this they were especially dexterously rearranged and reimagined. The late, great Dame’s design vocabulary has all the more to give when stretched, sliced, and plied with some spirits.

Rajan Patel wrote on X that disabled source clicks on Google Discover AI summaries were a bug in an experiment that has been turned off.

Google says disabled source taps on Discover’s AI summary cards were “a bug in a Discover experiment,” and that it has turned the experiment off.

Rajan Patel, VP of Engineering for Search at Google, said so in a reply on X Monday evening. Damien Andell, who researches Google Discover, had reported the issue on Oct. 23.

Before the test, Andell wrote, tapping a summary’s sources opened a feed of articles from those sources, one path from an AI summary to the sites it cites. The morning after Patel’s reply, Andell posted that the experiment had been removed and replaced with another.

Patel was replying to Lily Ray, who had quote-posted Andell’s report the morning of Oct. 23. Patel wrote:

Hey Lily – this was a bug in a Discover experiment. We’ve turned off the experiment, and the experience works as intended again.

The reply doesn’t say what the experiment was testing, how long it ran, or how many people saw the disabled taps.

Andell wrote on Oct. 23 that Discover was testing an AI summary change that disabled clicks on sources.

He found this test through a flag called “disable_aio_sources_button_tap,” and stated that this flag stopped taps on the sources at the bottom from opening the article feed.

In his two screenshots, Andell is showing a Fox 9 video from YouTube that has appeared in his Discover feed. Each screenshot shows a brief summary underneath the video and a series of icon links below it. The second image circles the icon links and labels them “Sources.”

On Oct. 27, he looked at this again. In a reply on X, he stated:

I just checked and the experiment did indeed remove and replace it with another one called “aio tap falls through to hero”.

His post didn’t explain how the new experiment works or where it is running.

The Discover generative AI report from Search Console reports how many times users see links to your pages in Discover through the generative AI capabilities. Each source page is listed by URL in the Discover Generative AI Report.

The only metric described in Search Console Help for this report is Impression, which represents when someone sees one of your website’s link(s) via the generative AI in Discover, not a source tap.

Until Google or Andell explains the replacement experiment, you can take a quick look for yourself. Tap the sources on an AI summary card in your Discover feed to see whether the article feed opens.

This under-the-radar tool gave me the smoothest blowout of my life.

My vanity is overflowing with hair tools: I own about five classic curling irons, three flat irons, a few thermal volumizing brushes, a trusty blow dryer, and a couple different air-styling devices, including the Shark FlexStyle. While I adore trying the latest and greatest hair innovations—it’s literally my job—trust me when I tell you it’s not necessary to buy all of them. And hot take: The viral multi-stylers aren’t for everyone.

Considering its virality, you might think the FlexStyle would be my tried-and-true styler. However, the tool I end up using most often is the Shark Glossi, an under-the-radar blow-dryer brush. Shark quietly launched it in 2025, and while it doesn’t get nearly as much attention as the FlexStyle, I think it’s a better—and more affordable—option for those who primarily want to achieve silky salon-quality blowouts at home.

Could the Shark Glossi be the right hair tool for you? Below, I break down everything you need to know about the tool, including its price and features. Plus, you’ll find my unfiltered review of the blow-dry brush after using it for nearly a month.

The Shark Glossi is a two-in-one blow-dryer brush designed for all hair types that simultaneously dries and styles hair with airflow and a heated ceramic comb. The traditional blow-dry brush side (which covers about 3/4 of the barrel) combines nylon and boar bristles for optimal tension and control, while the comb side glosses and smooths with minimal heat for extra shine.

One unique design feature is the shape of the barrel—it’s tapered rather than cylindrical, which allows you to completely dry your roots and boost volume. This shape also makes a major difference when styling shorter layers, including bangs and bobs.

While most blow-dry brushes should only be used on wet hair, the Glossi has a wet and a dry mode. The wet mode is meant to dry and style damp hair, whereas the dry mode only utilizes the ceramic comb to tame flyaways, as well as reshape and refresh your style between washes.

Let me preface this by saying I’m a bit of an at-home blowout connoisseur. I’ve tried countless blow-dry brushes over the years, and have perfected my twist-and-flick method for professional-looking waves. The Shark FlexStyle had been my go-to tool over the past few months. However, like I alluded to above, I primarily used the round brush attachment on the multi-styler while the curling rods collected dust under my sink.

I immediately felt like the Shark Glossi was more suited towards my needs. First, it’s extremely lightweight—like, the lightest blow-dry brush I’ve ever used—weighing in at 1.63 pounds. Finally styling my hair didn’t feel like an arm workout! From the first use, I was surprised by how quickly the Glossi dried my ~very~ wet hair. My hair is fine, but there’s a lot of it, so it can take a while to fully dry it, sometimes up to 20 minutes. This dried my hair at warp speed in under 10 minutes.

The shape of the barrel definitely played a role in this. The tapered edge was able to reach my roots to fully lift and dry them. Many other blow-dry brushes fail to do this because their cylindrical design can’t quite get to the roots.

I was initially nervous about running my wet hair through the ceramic comb portion while drying my hair, but I didn’t experience any damage. The two sections of the tool work in tandem to dry and smooth. I almost always have some wispy flyaways when I use a standard blow-dry brush (yes, even the Shark FlexStyle), but this tool ensures my results are completely silky.

Hair styled from wet to dry using the Shark Glossi

The wet to dry mode surpassed all my expectations...as for the dry mode, well, there’s a small learning curve with it.

I’m a daily gym girlie, and I frequently need to restyle my hair because of it. Once I got the hang of the dry mode, the Glossi made this part of my day much less time-consuming. When I dry-style, I focus on swiping the ceramic plates through sections to tamp down on frizz, smooth, and revive shine. Because the comb portion of the barrel is rather small, it takes about 10 minutes to refresh my full head. That’s much less time than having to shampoo, so I’ll take it! I’ll go as far as to say that it’s better than the viral Shark FlexStyle, too, IMHO. #3267. This Self-Coached Data Scientist Has Never Run a Marathon Before. Here’s Why He Could Win NYC.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Andreas Almgren will make his marathon debut on the streets of New York this weekend—and he wants to be in contention.

When the elites line up for the New York City Marathon on November 1, there will be a mix of former winners, Olympic medalists, and two of the top American marathoners in the country, Conner Mantz and Zouhair Talbi.

Looking to spoil the party will be Andreas Almgren, a 31-year-old Swedish runner who won the World Half Marathon Championships in September. This will be Almgren’s debut at the 26.2-mile distance. Despite his inexperience, he’s a legitimate threat for the podium.

Almgren has an unconventional background as a pro runner. He was working as a data scientist just two years ago. He mostly trains alone and coaches himself. But he’s far from a competitive amateur. He’s sponsored by Nike and has set European records in the road 10K (26:45) and half marathon (58:06).

Although his strengths have been on the track and shorter road events, Almgren has leaned fully into marathon training this fall, logging more than 135 miles a week and tackling long runs over 24 miles. He says he’s well-prepared heading into New York.

“I’m actually feeling really confident,” Almgren told Runner’s World ahead of the race. “I might be a bit naive, but the training has gone really well. I’ve done a few really good workouts. But the marathon is a different beast. You don’t really know until you're reaching that 35K mark if you're going to make it or not. But I’m feeling really good.”

Here are six things to know about Almgren ahead of his marathon debut in New York City.

Almgren first made a splash on the world scene as an 800-meter specialist, earning bronze in the event at the World Junior Championships in 2014. For about a decade, he was coached by Urban Aruhn, but around 2019, Almgren began experimenting with Norwegian threshold training and gradually started to create his own training plans.

Aruhn is still involved today, but in a more general role. Before a training cycle, Almgren will run his plan by Aruhn, who plays “devil’s advocate,” trying to poke holes in it or prod Almgren to explain his reasoning.

“If we disagree on something, then we discuss it,” Almgren said. “But in the end, it’s in my hands.”

Before he was a full-time professional runner, Almgren worked as a risk analyst at a financial company and then as a data scientist for the sports tech software company Svexa. It wasn’t until he was sidelined from the 2024 Olympics with a stress fracture that he decided to quit his job and focus on running full-time.

Now, Almgren—who has a bachelor’s degree in industrial engineering and management and has taken graduate coursework in mathematics, optimization, and systems theory—scratches that itch by rigorously tracking his training metrics.

“I like data,” he said. “And I collect basically the things that I think are important for every workout.”

Almgren has an Excel spreadsheet where he logs every workout and numerous data points: interval times, heart rate readings, blood lactate measurements, etc. The spreadsheet data goes back to 2012, and Almgren will look back on past sessions to inform his current training.

Almgren has been preparing for New York with an altitude stint in Flagstaff, Arizona, which sits 7,000 feet above sea level. He’s also introduced heat training during some of his training blocks, and he looks to sports like cycling and speed skating for cutting-edge training methods.

About two years ago, Almgren started experimenting with Nomio, a broccoli sprout extract shot that is quickly catching on among elite runners. Nomio claims the product can reduce lactate during hard efforts and accelerate recovery.

Almgren, who is sponsored by the brand, said it helps keep his legs feeling fresh during intervals.

“I just feel like I can train harder than I did before and more and still recover from it,” he said. “Run a little bit faster on my threshold workouts and everything. So I think if you get those tiny margins over and over, day by day, I think that could help quite a lot.”

Almgren knew he wanted to race a marathon in the fall of 2026, but he didn’t want to sacrifice a full track season before diving into marathon training. He chose New York because it gave him time to recover from the track season, and he wanted to find an honest race, compared to a more time-oriented flat course like Spain’s Valencia Marathon in December.

“In a time trial, I wouldn’t really know what time to aim for,” Almgren said. “There’s a big risk that I blow up. If I’m going in Valencia, you have Yomif [Kejelcha] and John Korir taking off in probably sub-two-hour pace, and I’m not going to follow that. So I think that New York is a better race for me with inexperience, just trying to stay in the front pack and see what can happen.”

Oftentimes, pro runners will “move to the marathon,” implying their days of barreling around the track are over. But Almgren wants to do both. He fully intends to run a track season in 2027, and, looking ahead to the 2028 Los Angeles Olympics, he believes the 10,000 meters could be his best event.

If Almgren finishes in the top five at New York, he will earn the Olympic standard and pave the path for a spot on the Swedish marathon team for the upcoming Games.

Only Emil Zatopek has won gold in the 10,000 meters and marathon at the same Olympics.

Like most elite runners who race the hilly New York course, Almgren doesn’t care what his finishing time is on November 1. Instead, he wants to run with the top group as long as he can.

“The goal is to be in the mindset that I should try to be able to compete for the whole race,” he said. “I don't want to, let’s say, chicken out at 30K. I want to try to be in it for as long as possible. And if that means that I blow up at 37K, then so be it.

“But I hope that I should be able to be in the mix up until Central Park and hopefully even maybe come out on top. We’ll see. You should never underestimate a marathon. And I don’t have the experience, so I don’t know what’s going to happen, but I’m going to try to be in the mix.”

#3943. When 'Love at First Sight' Gets in the Way of Love

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Updated October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

My husband and I have been married almost 25 years. He maintains that it was love at first sight; I maintain that he is mistaken. Regardless of who is correct, as a family scientist, I recognize that beliefs like love at first sight can contribute to myths about romantic love that can negatively impact relationships. Hallmark movies, like other romance movies, tend to be based on these myths, including the belief in soulmates or that love can conquer all. In real life, the more one believes in these myths, the less likely they are to be in satisfying relationships. Some may even end their relationships before they experience conflict out of the belief that, if they were truly meant to be, there should be no conflict.

Hallmark’s Forgotten Holiday introduces us to Rob Ashford and Kathryn Liu. For Rob, it’s love at first sight. Rob proposes at the start of the holiday season since that is his favorite time of year. Kathryn’s fear of failed relationships, fueled by her parents’ divorce as well as her own, has her bolting from the proposal without giving Rob an answer. Confused and heartbroken, he drives to his sister’s house but gets into an accident that leaves him with amnesia. His memories quickly return except for his memories of Kathryn. The doctor prescribes the unconventional therapy that Rob and Kathryn re-date. As his memories return, he eventually remembers the way their relationship ended.

Hallmark has used amnesia to spark relationship development, improvement, or evaluation in previous movies. In this case, it allows Kathryn to understand how her fear of commitment impacted their relationship and allows Rob to identify what he truly wants out of a romantic partner. Amnesia provides an opportunity for both to learn more about themselves and each other, but they each need to make the choice to do so.

Rob’s and Kathryn’s efforts to recreate their previous relationship fall flat. He repeatedly reminds her that he is not “old Rob”; perhaps he is also reminding himself. I found myself cringing at the awkwardness of their re-dating attempts. Things go so poorly that they question how they dated for an entire year. Viewers may be left wondering the same, feeling like Rob and Kathryn can’t seem to be themselves around one another.

We can face a similar challenge in off-screen relationships. Imagine agreeing to every holiday plan while quietly wanting something different, pretending to share an interest because disagreement feels risky, or withholding worries about the future to preserve a happy evening. The intention is understandable, especially if we also feel that we need to choose our battles and protect our connections. But when we're hiding preferences, conforming to partners’ opinions, and withholding needs, we give our partner fewer opportunities to understand us, respond to us, or discover where we differ.

In one study, researchers found that concealing personal information from a partner was associated with lower relationship satisfaction and commitment. This daily diary study also linked concealment with poorer relationship well-being. Authenticity doesn’t mean sharing every thought or giving up privacy, but when we consistently hide important parts of ourselves, we limit the opportunity to discover whether our partner can know and accept us.

One of my favorite scenes comes towards the end of the movie. Kathryn’s father suggests that negative relationship outcomes emerge from choices rather than a doomed-to-be-alone fate. His observation leaves room for agency: We cannot control everything that happens in a relationship, but we can consider how our choices shape the connections we build.

Those choices may seem ordinary but can have a strong impact on the relationship. We can speak honestly about uncertainty instead of pretending to feel sure. We can let a partner express a different preference without treating it as evidence that they aren’t a soulmate. We can ask questions rather than assume what a disagreement means, address hurt instead of hiding it, and examine which expectations we carry from our family experiences. These choices won’t guarantee that a relationship will last, but they can help us understand ourselves and each other more clearly. This can help us build an authentic relationship.

In Forgotten Holiday, amnesia creates the interruption that allows Kathryn and Rob to reconsider their relationship. It is a dramatic storytelling device, but we don’t need to wait until a dramatic interruption happens to reflect on our relationships.

Hallmark: If it’s true love, the spark will bring you back together—even if it takes amnesia and a second first date.

Psychology: That initial spark can draw us to someone, but it cannot build or sustain a relationship. Romantic partners need to commit to getting to know each other, make room for differences, and be honest even when the conversation feels uncomfortable.

The verdict: Forgotten Holiday earns points for letting its couple question the romance they once accepted. Their second chance matters because it offers an opportunity to know each other more honestly. Fortunately, the rest of us can begin without a case of amnesia.

#3705. How Mexican Photographers Made a Nation

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

​​A show at the Getty demonstrates the way modernist approaches to photography shifted the visual culture of Mexico in the 20th century.

LOS ANGELES — In “Tríptico de los martirios” (1950), the late photographer Lola Álvarez Bravo patiently renders her subject anonymous, capturing a tree’s shadow crawling toward her subject’s bare chest with exquisite timing. In Graciela Iturbide’s “Mujer ángel” (1979), meanwhile, a woman with a veil, long white skirt, and portable stereo traverses a field of flowers to encounter a spotty desert. Almost cinematic in their presentation, both works demonstrate a keen attention to light and form as well as an emotional sensitivity to their subjects, demonstrating the way modernist approaches to photography shifted the visual culture of Mexico in the 20th century.

The work of both artists is prominently featured in Instante/revelación: Moments in Mexican Photography at the Getty Center in Los Angeles, which brings together the work of 13 Mexican photographers from the late 1920s to the mid-2010s to trace the arc from post-Revolution nation-building to contemporary life through photography.

The exhibition takes its name from a book published by the late Nobel Prize-winning Mexican writer Octavio Paz entitled Instante y revelación (Instant and Revelation, 1982). In it, Paz pairs his poetry with photographs by Manuel Álvarez Bravo, whose work he encountered in the Mexican literary magazine Contemporáneos (Contemporaries). This relationship between language and visual art informs the exhibition’s framework, allowing for poetic groupings. In this way, artists and intellectuals, including Frida Kahlo, Diego Rivera, and David Alfaro Siqueiros, are shown alongside landscapes, botanical species, and scenes from everyday life.

#2906. Te Araroa blog: in which snoop dog does not die

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Great news everyone, Snoop Dogg did not, in fact, die! This would make sense to you if you were in the kitchen on day 3 of walking Te Araroa and someone announced he died in the three days your phone had been on airplane mode. You’d also have had the relief (mostly for Martha Stewart) of finding out mere moments later that that was fake news.

If that’s not an indication of what it’s like to walk on an expansive-lacking-in-landmarks beach for three days , I don’t know what is.

Section one of Te Araroa is a slog, a hard-packed sand beach (if you’re lucky) that exposes every single tiny part of your shoe that rubs.

Day one was the shortest, only 12.5 km on the map. In the lead up to my start day Fraser and I had spent a really lovely couple of nights in Kerikeri sleeping in an orange grove and walking to little beaches and viewpoints. We fed kunekune (a type of pig) and I played knuckle bones for the first time. I also, of course ran my last minute errands including buying gas, a lighter and some tortillas. My pack was feeling very large and heavy, and even more so when we picked up another walker and he was carrying a bag that looked to be about half the size of mine.

We drove up the last little bit of Northland to Cape Reinga, my nerves and excitement growing. We had been here about five years ago on a family holiday, back when I had no idea about Te Araroa. It was sort of surreal to be doing the drive again, but this time with a very different goal. We took our obligatory start photos at the northern terminus of Te Araroa, and then made our way back up the path to the actual trailhead. This was the hardest part of the day for me, saying bye to Fraser. He stood on the ridge and watched me walk away for the first kilometre or so, all the while I was crying and trying not to fall down the steps. It was all so beautiful and big and overwhelming.

After a few hours I arrived at the twilight beach camp, where the people who would become my constant companions for the next 85 mind-numbing beach kilometres gathered for the first time. We admired one person’s homemade tent, talked about the food we had brought, and ate our dinners. By the end of the night I was feeling a whole mix of things, sad to be away from Fraser and home, daunted by the next 85km of beach walking (and then the other almost 3000km), excited and giddy at the fact that after a year of planning this was it, it was all real. For the next few days I had two goals:

Day two was the first of the big days, with 5km up and over a hill followed by 23km on 90 mile (actually not 90 miles long) beach. The first three quarters went well, I walked quite a bit of it alone after excitingly taking off at my “normal” walking pace. This was a slight mistake as by the time I got to camp my legs were getting very sore and I was approaching wrecked. I knew that the camp this night, at Maunganui Bluff, had cold showers and I was desperate to get there and have a shower before it got chilly for the night.

I managed to snag what I reckon was the best campsite, tucked behind a harakeke (flax) bush to block the wind. I showered and dinner was a pasta feast rehydrated in the bag from night one’s rice and beans. I could not wait for bed!

Despite being tired I didn’t manage to fall asleep until a little later than everyone else, and then was woken in the night by a strange noise. I lay there listening, wondering if maybe a possum had turned up to ransack our food bags. But then I suddenly realized that hey! I knew that sound! That’s the sound of a horse grazing! As quietly as I could I found my headtorch and poked my head out the fly of my tent and there they were, three or four horses just a couple of metres from my tent. It was magical.

Day three was our second of three big days of beach walking and our first day entirely comprised of beach. I wasn’t hungry this morning but choked down a meal replacement shake and then a couple hours later a banana chocolate bumper bar. We left shortly after high tide to make sure we’d be walking in the hard sand all day instead of the dunes. The first 15 or so kilometres sort of disappeared under our feet and then we dragged ourselves through the next 2km to a lunch spot.

Lunch on the beach is so important, even though we were managing to keep our feet dry on the stream crossings, airing out your feet for a while makes a big difference to your blister count. We stopped beside a big truck towing a digger with a basket that looked like a sieve. One of my hiking-mates had a chat with the driver who explained that that’s how they harvest mussel seed – I have to admit that I have no clue what that really means but he seemed to think it was very cool.

Lunch on the beach is also when you start to get in your head. You think “okay I’ve done half the day’s walk so I’ve got this” but then you also think “oh god I’ve only done half the day’s walk! I can’t do this!” I think this is when having walking companions really helps. I can’t imagine what it would have felt like to walk this section alone, the expanse of the beach stretching out in front of me, the constant crashing of waves and the occasional sudden appearance of a car or truck zooming past. As it was it was mind numbing, but also meditative and the sort of experience that makes you feel like you’re really doing something once in a lifetime.

Getting to camp on day 3 was a massive relief. The camp was a much more established, privately owned site. The horses were grazing all around the camp as we hobbled up a gravel road off the beach to the site. The first thing we all did was buy a bag of chips each ($5 each but we would have paid more) and cans of fizzy drinks. There was a kitchen, gas stoves, dishes, cutlery and all the luxuries that you normally only get in town. Most importantly there were hot showers! In private rooms! A significant upgrade from the previous night. I’m already developing a real fondness for the minty smell of my castille soap because it means a warmer, more comfortable sleep.

I slept terribly this night, waking up every couple of hours. Even though I had gone to bed feeling really happy I woke up anxious at around 1am and didn’t fall back asleep until about 3:30am. This whole thing is definitely a journey in accepting the unknown and in being daunted but continuing on anyway. I also drafted the first half of this blog when I gave up on falling back asleep quickly so if you find typos in it, kindly ignore them.

“Somebody get me the HELL off this beach”

That was the sentiment on day four. We had all had enough of the beach, enough of the blisters and the same terrain for 30km a day. We also knew that ahead of us was a campground with showers, laundry, a shop and nice, flat tent sites.

Day four was a mental game. Walk 17km to Ngapae, a campground where we could stay if we wanted. We made it there by 1:30pm and stopped for a long lunch. There’s normally an icecream truck here, but it’s currently broken so the owner sold us fizzy drinks and gave us cushions to lie around on in the shade. She brought out a sliced up orange and banana and showed us where we could fill our water. It felt amazing to lie down. I briefly considered staying here but could not fathom having to get up the next morning and do more beach walking. So after lunch we packed up again and set off for our final 14km.

The second landmark of the day was almost at the end of the beach: the 100km mark. This milestone dangled in front of me like a carrot for the whole afternoon as we trudged down the beach. This end of the beach was much busier so there were more things to look at and more people to chat with. We met so many nice dogs!

By about 6pm we were approaching Ahipara, and I looked at my phone and shouted “oh! A hundred!!”

We took our milestone photos and continued on to the end of the beach where we were able to mercifully get a lift to the holiday park’s reception building. I was in a foul mood by the time I got my tent up but actually ended up sleeping really well and felt a lot better in the morning.

My first zero! I got a lift to resupply in Kaitaia (I bought way too much food) and then to my hostel where I had booked a private room. It felt so weird to be alone but also a little like a relief.

I took full advantage of my space. I pulled everything out of my pack, broke down all my food purchases, ate a real meal that I cooked and was given a glass of wine from some fellow hikers staying at the backpackers. They told me that they had been there for days as they all got injured while walking the beach, which freaked me out and made me worried that maybe I was injured and didn’t realize it yet.

A two hour phone call with Fraser helped me feel less paranoid about that, and I then crawled into bed for another mediocre sleep.

Next stop, Northland Forests. I’m hoping to complete these in four days but have heard that it is a slog up through the Raetea forest so may have to extend it to five. Stay tuned!

Before I left, Fraser added a whole bunch of albums to my phone and i purposely didn’t look at what he added. Each section I plan to pick a song of the section: this time around it’s Night Swimming by REM. This is because Fraser knows REM-induced nostalgia is important to me. I listened to it while unable to sleep at 3 am and had a little cry, it was good and healthy. (Honourable mention is the whole entire Grouper album called dragging a dead deer up a hill which got me to sleep that night).

#2210. If You Spot OTR on a Truck, Here’s What It Means

Those three letters on a semi-truck usually refer to long-distance driving. Learn what OTR means and how different trucking routes work.

Trucks are full of weird and useful features that most people overlook without knowing their real purpose. Some have long spikes on their wheels. Others might feature amber lights on top. You might even see diamond shaped signs. But what about OTR written on a truck?

What can look like a government code can have a specific meaning in the trucking industry. Learn what OTR means, how long-haul work compares with shorter routes, and why the context matters.

In trucking, OTR stands for “over-the-road.” It refers to long-haul trucking, where drivers transport freight across long distances, often through several states or into Canada. According to trucking company Schneider, OTR drivers can spend weeks away from home. Many drive trucks with sleeper cabs that provide a place to rest between shifts.

OTR describes the type of work, not a specific truck. It’s also not a required marking. The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) requires certain identifying information, including a company’s name and USDOT number, on interstate commercial trucks, but OTR isn’t part of that requirement.

The letters can also mean something different depending on where they appear. In the tire industry, OTR stands for “off-the-road,” referring to heavy-duty tires used on construction and mining equipment. OTR Diagnostics is also the name of a maintenance tool that helps truck owners check engine problems and other vehicle information.

OTR truck drivers transport freight over long distances, often making several stops along the way. Their jobs can also involve inspecting their trucks, handling delivery paperwork, and sometimes helping load or unload cargo.

Because these trips can last for weeks, drivers often sleep in their trucks or stop at other places along their routes. The FMCSA limits how long commercial drivers can drive without taking required breaks and rest periods.

Drivers who operate most full-size tractor-trailers need a Class A commercial driver’s license (CDL). Depending on the company and route, drivers may spend several weeks working before returning home.

OTR drivers cover the longest distances and may spend weeks away from home. Regional drivers work within a particular area of the country, often returning home weekly or more frequently. Local drivers generally stay closer to home and return after each shift, although they can still work long hours.

There’s also dedicated trucking, where drivers regularly transport freight for the same customer or between the same locations. These routes can be local, regional, or long-distance, depending on the company.

The exact distances and schedules vary between employers, so the terms don’t always describe the same mileage or amount of time away from home.

#4013. How Ego and Self-Image Change Brain Sync

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A neuroimaging study led by Aalto University reveals that individuals with high levels of grandiose personality traits exhibit disrupted neural synchrony when observing others face social vulnerability. Utilizing functional MRI across 58 participants exposed to realistic, unscripted video scenarios of individuals experiencing social threat and “losing face,” researchers found that while control subjects demonstrated coherent, shared neural activation in cognitive empathy and mentalizing regions, highly narcissistic brains responded with marked neural dissimilarity.

A sense of personal superiority, entitlement, and a persistent drive to command social attention are hallmarks of narcissistic personality traits. While subclinical narcissism exists along a spectrum throughout the general population, individuals who score high in “grandiose narcissism” frequently encounter interpersonal friction, emotional detachment, and volatile relationships.

Historically, cognitive neuroscience investigations into narcissism relied on controlled, static laboratory cues—such as isolated photographs of emotional facial expressions or simple pain stimuli. However, these paradigms fail to capture how narcissistic individuals spontaneously navigate the intricate, shifting dynamics of everyday social life.

Now, a multi-university neuroimaging initiative titled the Faces of Narcissism project, led by researchers at Aalto University in Finland, has examined how grandiose narcissism shapes neural responses in dynamic, real-life social encounters.

Publishing their findings using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), the team discovered that people with elevated narcissistic traits fail to synchronize their brain activity with others during moments of vicarious social vulnerability and embarrassment.

The research team designed a naturalistic experimental framework drawing on the sociological frameworks of Erving Goffman. In the 1950s, Goffman posited that human social interactions rely on “facework”—a collaborative, unspoken effort where participants actively protect both their own and each other’s public self-worth, or “face.” When an individual’s self-image is threatened or undermined, it typically elicits a shared, vicarious wave of embarrassment and discomfort in observers.

To simulate these social stakes, the investigators recruited 58 participants selected from an initial psychometric screening pool of nearly 1,000 adults. The cohort was split between individuals scoring exceptionally high in grandiose narcissistic traits and a matched control group displaying low narcissistic features.

Inside the fMRI scanner, participants watched naturalistic video vignettes of professional actors narrating deeply personal autobiographical stories involving social exclusion or public admiration.

Following each narrative, the storyteller received critical, face-threatening feedback from another conversational partner. For instance, the feedback questioned whether previous admiration was undeserved, or asserted that past social rejection was entirely the storyteller’s own fault. Crucially, participants were given no instructions to empathize, allowing researchers to track unprompted, spontaneous neural processing.

When low-narcissism control subjects observed the storyteller face social threat, their brain activity became highly synchronized. Functional scans revealed consistent, shared neural activation patterns across cortical networks implicated in cognitive empathy, theory of mind, and the interpretation of social contexts. This inter-subject synchronization indicates that typical observers intuitively share and process the emotional discomfort of another person losing face.

In contrast, participants with high narcissistic traits displayed marked neural divergence. Their brain patterns were far less in sync with one another, showing distinct and idiosyncratic neural responses to the victim’s social vulnerability.

“People with a high number of narcissistic traits respond to another person’s vulnerable experience differently than others,” explains doctoral researcher Matias Piispanen of Aalto University. “They may attune to interaction in a different way, pay attention to different aspects of it, or make different interpretations of what is happening.”

The divergence localized to regions responsible for evaluating social meaning rather than basic emotional reactivity, suggesting that individuals with grandiose narcissism perceive social power dynamics differently, perhaps viewing another’s vulnerability through a lens of judgment, contempt, or detachment rather than shared human discomfort.

The findings provide a neurological explanation for why individuals high in narcissism struggle to forge durable, authentic interpersonal bonds.

Because cooperative social cohesion relies on an intuitive, shared neural appraisal of vulnerability and embarrassment, an inability to naturally attune to another’s distress leaves narcissistic individuals out of step with everyday social reciprocity.

“People with a high level of narcissistic traits may not necessarily build supportive or genuine connections with others, which can lead to difficulties and misunderstandings in interaction,” Piispanen notes.

By shifting beyond static emotional stimuli into rich, real-world narrative interactions, the Faces of Narcissism project demonstrates that grandiose narcissism alters the fundamental cognitive lens through which human social vulnerability is perceived, processed, and evaluated.

Reduced Social Brain Coupling to Others’ Face Threats in Narcissism

Although several studies have linked narcissism to reduced empathy, the neural mechanisms underlying narcissistic behavior in socially complex, naturalistic contexts remain poorly understood.

This study investigated how grandiose narcissistic traits are associated with neural dynamics in empathy-related areas of the “social brain” when participants observed another person receiving face-threatening feedback.

We hypothesized that individuals with high narcissistic traits would exhibit lower intersubject correlation (ISC), indicating less synchronized neural responses across participants, in the anterior insula (aINS), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) in response to face threats directed at others. Participants with high (n = 28) and low (n = 30) scores in grandiose narcissism watched videos during functional magnetic resonance imaging in which another person received face-threatening feedback to a personal story.

Consistent with our hypothesis, highly narcissistic individuals exhibited reduced ISC in the dlPFC and PCC. In aINS, the effect did not reach statistical significance. The present findings suggest that trait narcissism reduces convergence in empathic responses particularly in areas related to cognitive empathy when witnessing social threats targeted at others.

These findings further indicate that ecologically relevant experimental designs may help clarify how personality traits are expressed in neural responses to socially meaningful events.
