#627. NPT withdrawal returns to Iran debate as diplomacy gains pace

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The IRGC-linked Tasnim news agency has set out the case for Iran to withdraw from the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), adding to a renewed debate over Tehran's nuclear strategy as the region remains caught between diplomacy and war.

In a commentary published Wednesday, Tasnim argued that the treaty had failed to protect Iran's interests and questioned the benefits of remaining a member after successive wars with Israel and the United States.

The intervention comes as Qatar promotes a diplomatic framework reportedly linking the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, sanctions relief and efforts to reduce tensions involving Yemen's Houthis.

It also follows signals from Tehran that its leadership wants to end the current diplomatic and military stalemate.

Supreme National Security Council (SNSC) Secretary Mohsen Rezaei said Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei had told him during a meeting on Tuesday that it was time to end the state of “no war, no peace.”

President Masoud Pezeshkian said Wednesday that his government would do everything it could to fulfil the leader's wishes.

Tasnim's commentary, titled “Why Should Iran Leave the NPT?”, highlights divisions over how Tehran should respond to international pressure on its nuclear program.

Hardline politicians have repeatedly raised the prospect of leaving the NPT during periods of nuclear negotiations, arguing that the treaty has failed to protect Iran's interests.

Tasnim identified three central objectives of the treaty: preventing the spread of nuclear weapons, encouraging nuclear-armed states to disarm and protecting members' right to use nuclear technology for peaceful purposes.

The agency argued that the treaty had failed on all three counts.

It accused the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) of abandoning its role as an impartial technical body and becoming politically aligned with the United States and Israel.

The agency had failed to defend Iran's rights when its nuclear sites were attacked, so withdrawal would be a legitimate option for Tehran, Tasnim argued.

Tasnim maintained that withdrawal would not necessarily mean developing nuclear weapons. Some lawmakers, however, have explicitly linked the two.

On Tuesday, MP Abolfazl Aboutorabi called for Iran to withdraw from the NPT and begin building a nuclear bomb.

Other hardline figures, including members of the ultraconservative Paydari Front, have advocated withdrawal, arguing that continued membership has failed to prevent attacks on Iranian nuclear facilities despite their being subject to international safeguards.

The calls have met resistance from other establishment-aligned media, reflecting disagreements over the potential costs of withdrawal.

Khabar Online, a pragmatic-conservative outlet, sharply criticized the campaign in an October 25 report, singling out the Paydari Front and questioning the political judgment of those advocating departure from the treaty.

Its headline described the proposal as a radical demand dating back two decades, with “neither head nor tail.”

The reformist Arman Melli newspaper has also taken a position against withdrawal, broadly reflecting the Pezeshkian administration's preference for maintaining Iran's existing treaty commitments.

Nour News, which is close to the SNSC, adopted a more cautious approach in its own October 25 analysis.

It argued that neither withdrawal nor continued membership could guarantee Iran's security and that dissatisfaction with the IAEA did not, by itself, justify abandoning the treaty.

The outlet also warned that withdrawal from the NPT would create further political and diplomatic complications, while remaining in the treaty would still allow Tehran to restrict aspects of its cooperation with the IAEA.

Withdrawal, it concluded, should not be considered without a clear plan for what would follow.

Leaving the NPT would have implications beyond Iran's relations with the IAEA.

Under Article X of the treaty, a state party may exercise its right to withdraw if it determines that extraordinary events have jeopardized its supreme interests. It must give three months' notice to other treaty parties and the UN Security Council, explaining the events behind its decision.

Withdrawal could further complicate Tehran's relations with Western governments and raise questions about the future of international monitoring of its nuclear activities.

Despite the calls from hardline lawmakers and media outlets, Iran has announced no decision to leave the treaty.

The government continues to maintain that its nuclear program is peaceful, and neither the SNSC nor Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei has publicly endorsed withdrawal.

The competing arguments underscore a debate within Iran's political establishment: whether abandoning the treaty would strengthen Tehran's position after repeated military attacks or leave it more isolated and vulnerable as regional powers seek a diplomatic settlement.

The Revolutionary Guards-affiliated Fars news agency has promoted calls for Iran to build an atomic bomb through a video of street interviews, presenting nuclear armament as a popular demand.

“The most frequently repeated demand among Iranians these days: Why should America and Israel have atomic bombs while we don't?” Fars said in its description of the video, circulated on X and Telegram.

The agency presented the selected interviews as evidence of public support for nuclear weapons, although they provide no reliable measure of how widely that view is shared.

The message echoes arguments advanced by several Iranian lawmakers and other establishment figures following the wars with Israel and the United States.

Discussion of changing Iran's nuclear doctrine predates the wars. In May 2024, former foreign minister Kamal Kharrazi, who later advised Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, said Iran could reconsider its nuclear doctrine if Israel attacked its nuclear facilities or threatened the country's existence.

Kharrazi died in April 2026 from injuries sustained in a US-Israeli strike on his home in Tehran.

In August, Supreme National Security Council Secretary Mohsen Rezaei argued that international non-proliferation commitments had failed to protect Iran from military attack.

“When America is waging such a war, people naturally ask: Why shouldn’t we pursue nuclear weapons ourselves?” Rezaei told state television, suggesting that the attacks had strengthened arguments for acquiring an atomic deterrent.

Some lawmakers have gone further. On October 18, Ahmad Naderi, a member of parliament's presiding committee, said he had advocated nuclear testing and developing an atomic bomb for more than a decade.

Naderi also suggested that Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei might take a different position from his late father, Ali Khamenei, who maintained that his religious ruling prohibited nuclear weapons.

Lawmaker Ahmad Bakhshayesh Ardestani called in July for Iran to manufacture nuclear weapons, arguing that the country had already paid the price for its nuclear program.

The conservative newspaper Vatan-e Emrooz also endorsed nuclear armament this month, featuring the headline “Iran Should Build a Bomb” and arguing that a nuclear deterrent could prevent further US and Israeli attacks.

Christine Lagarde is leaving the European Central Bank (ECB) early because she no longer wants to stay silent.

The ECB president, who will step down on 7 February 2027, around nine months before her term ends, said on Thursday that the neutrality expected of central bankers, which she described as "the price of their independence," was no longer something she could accept.

“I don’t think that I can remain neutral,” she told reporters in Frankfurt.

“I’m interested in the truth, and I see the truth being very often damaged, contradicted by misrepresentation,” she added.

It was not the only surprise of the afternoon.

Moments after the ECB left its key interest rates unchanged, with the deposit rate at 2.5%, Lagarde revealed that policymakers had seriously debated raising them instead.

The decision to hold was unanimous, she said. But her message to markets was that the pause may only last until December.

Two things have changed since the ECB’s last forecasts in September, Lagarde explained.

Petrol, diesel and gas prices have kept climbing, and refining margins remain high. Eurozone inflation rose to 3.8% in September, with energy prices up 18.9% from a year earlier.

The second is the cost of long-term borrowing.

Lagarde said the average 10-year government bond yield in the eurozone had risen by around 35 basis points since the cut-off date for the September projections.

That rise matters because it does part of the central bank’s job for it. When governments and companies pay more to borrow over ten years, mortgages and business loans get more expensive too, and spending slows. Mortgage rates already climbed to 3.8% in August from 3.5% in July, while banks tightened lending standards for companies in the third quarter.

“If you combine these two factors, actually, the Governing Council debated whether a hike was appropriate,” Lagarde said.

In the end, the council judged it was “well positioned enough at this stage to hold”, and that a “measured approach” remained appropriate.

Lagarde repeated the ECB’s usual line that it decides “meeting by meeting” and is not pre-committing to any path.

She listed the data the bank will receive before its December meeting, from two more inflation readings to third-quarter growth and wage figures, and said those numbers would be checked against today’s assessment.

“Unless there were major developments, the direction of travel is quite clear for all of us,” she said.

She added that underlying inflation, which strips out volatile items such as energy and food, would carry particular weight.

The country's 10-year borrowing costs rose above 5% last week for the first time since 2002, and the gap over German Bund yields has widened sharply.

On Thursday afternoon, the French 10-year yield eased to about 4.88%, down around 3.5 basis points on the day. That still left it roughly 154 basis points above Germany’s, and about 40 basis points above Italy’s, a country long seen as the eurozone’s weakest link on public debt.

Yet, Lagarde avoided explicitly naming France.

She said the rise in long-term yields was global, driven by inflation expectations, the expected path of interest rates and the wave of bonds issued by large technology companies borrowing to build data centres.

But she was clear that countries with high debt face a particular challenge.

They must plan for interest rates that “will probably remain higher for a period of time”, she said, and keep their budgets within Europe’s fiscal rules.

Asked whether the ECB was watching for the risk of contagion to other countries, Lagarde struck a firmer tone.

“Should market developments threaten the smooth transmission of our monetary policy, including through unwarranted and disorderly market movements, we have instruments available to respond,” she said.

In other words, the ECB is prepared to step in if turmoil in government bond markets becomes severe enough to stop its interest rate decisions from reaching households and businesses as intended.

When another journalist raised the prospect of a new euro crisis, she did not engage with the comparison. “There will be developments, obviously, but this is not going to deter us,” she said.

The ECB has stepped in before, buying the bonds of struggling eurozone countries during the debt crisis that began in 2010 and again during the pandemic, while its 2022 Transmission Protection Instrument has never been used.

Oxford Economics does not expect it to be activated soon.

Its baseline sees the French-German spread approaching 200 basis points by the time of next spring’s presidential election. Leo Barincou, a senior economist at the research firm, said in a note that there is “likely some room” between that scenario “and an actual debt crisis”.

Lagarde said her mandate would have ended in October 2027 in any case.

Leaving in February, she explained, gives her back the freedom to speak and act on issues beyond monetary policy.

After more than 20 years at the IMF and the ECB, she added, there were things she felt she should say. “Whether people will listen or not is another story.”

She declined to say whether she would back a candidate in France’s presidential election next spring, or what job she will take next.

On the succession, she insisted there would be “perfect continuity of business”.

“The institution is solid, strong like a rock,” she said.

Not everyone is so relaxed about the timing.

“The timing for the ECB is far from ideal,” Matthew Ryan, head of market strategy at financial services firm Ebury, told in an emailed comment, pointing to inflation well above target and a French-German spread at its widest since 2011.

Ryan sees former Dutch central bank chief Klaas Knot and Bank for International Settlements head Pablo Hernández de Cos as the frontrunners.

“The Bank has decided to kick the can down the road for now, but a quarter-point rise looks increasingly likely at December’s meeting,” Joe Nellis, head of economic research at accountancy and advisory firm MHA.

Lagarde will chair two more ECB meetings, in December and February. The first may well bring the hike the bank debated this week.

What happens after that is far less clear, because nobody yet knows who will take over once she leaves.

Until a name emerges, that uncertainty will weigh on markets too. Investors “will now trade partly on rumours about the ECB’s new leadership next year, alongside the economic data”, Claus Vistesen of Pantheon Macroeconomics said in a note.
