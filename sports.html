#1002. Klopp wins first game as Germany coach, without 'top dogs'

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

It may have taken a little longer than he would have liked, but Jürgen Klopp has finally won a game as Germany coach. Despite a number of debutants, it was a familiar face who stood out in the victory over Serbia.

After 47 days, three matches and 44 squad players, Jürgen Klopp has a first win as coach of Germany's men's fotball team. A straightforward 2-0 victory over Serbia in the Nations League on Thursday meant the former Liverpool and Borussia Dortmund coach avoided becoming the first Germany boss to win any of his first three games.

"I'm really happy today," Klopp told German public broadcaster ZDF. "We were outstanding when defending and when we had the ball. We simply played wonderful football."

Klopp had raised a few eyebrows by naming two squads for the four fixtures in this extended international window. But after a somewhat fortunate draw against the Netherlands and a shock loss to Greece with the more senior of the squads, it was left to those lower down the pecking order to get things going Thursday in Munich.

That meant a completely new starting XI and debuts to ‌goalkeeper Jonas Urbig ⁠and ⁠winger Derry Scherhant. Such players should "develop without the top dogs" Klopp said before the game. And while Urbig was untroubled by Serbia, his Bayern Munich teammate Tom Bischof made his mark by turning in the opener after Florian Wirtz, one of the sprinkling of senior players, hit the post. It was Bischof's first goal for Germany in only his second national team game.

"It's indescribable," said Bischof after the game at Bayern's Allianz Arena. "The stadium was on fire. I let it all out and was overjoyed." Unfortunately, he said, "my knees hurt a little from sliding on my knees. But that doesn't matter."

After a string of tournament failures, the latest new dawn for Germany comes with a little more patience. As well as Klopp's track record, there is a general acceptance from inside the country and abroad that Germany are no longer a top tier side.

"We're not the Bayern Munich of European football," Klopp said prematch. "We're a team that needs to find its footing."

While one win against a limited Serbia side will not change that, the emphatic nature of the performance impressed the new boss.

"I liked everything — the opening phase, the middle section, and the end. Our counter-pressing was off the charts," said the man whose Gegenpressing (Counter-pressing) tactic became his trademark at Dortmund. "We can afford to take all the liberties and risks we want when we win the ball back together. Everyone was there during our pressing moments."

The second half brought more debuts for players who may not have made a traditional 26-man squad. Cologne winger Said El Mala and Brentford's Vitaly Janelt made their Germany bows while Celtic's Mika Baur became the country's 1,000th men's international.

It was "surreal," Baur said. "Today was a highlight for everyone, not just for me with my debut."

While Klopp may have seen enough from a few of the second string to afford them opportunities in the future, the performance of Liverpool midfielder Wirtz, who scored Germany's second goal, may be the most critical of the night.

Wirtz, 23, has struggled to find his best form in the Premier League and has blown hot and cold in his 47 caps for Germany. A cool and composed finish suggested that the player who was so highly regarded at Bayer Leverkusen can be coaxed to life by Klopp.

"It was loads of fun. Every single one of us gave it our all on the field. We stuck to the game plan and did almost everything right," said Wirtz, who took the captain's armband later in the game. "The players around me made it easy for me. We had a lot of movement in the middle. We were all fired up."

The challenge now for Klopp is to work out which of his wide pool of players are the ones he can rely on to take Germany to Euro 2028 and beyond. The next step is a chance for revenge against Greece in Thessaloniki on Sunday.

#996. Manchester City Premier League charges: What do they mean?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Since being bought by the UAE in 2008, Manchester City has become one of the world's most successful football clubs. A raft of serious financial charges threatens to undermine this and bring chaos to the Premier League.

Manchester City has won the English Premier League eight times over the last 14 years. Whether those titles will remain is one of a plethora of issues at stake after England's top division charged one of its most high-profile clubs with more than 100 breaches of its financial regulations.

On Tuesday, the Premier League announced that an independent commission had found City had drawn up "sham contracts" as well as "relying on sham agreements to artificially inflate the club's revenues and reduce its costs." These contracts, dubious sponsorships and a number of other methods were, according to the commission's report, used to inflate the club's revenue to the tune of about £900 million (€1.05 billion) over the period between the 2009-10 and 2017-18 seasons.

The case has been ongoing for eight years after initial revelations from Der Spiegel, a German magazine, and Rui Pinto of the 'Football Leaks' website. City continues to deny any wrongdoing.

"The Club is innocent of the accusations made by the Premier League and a comprehensive body of irrefutable evidence exists in support of all of its positions, relating to this case," read a club statement. "Manchester City FC will now pursue the appeal avenues open to it, on the basis that the opinion contains clear material errors, of law, principle and fact, and is unsafe."

On Friday, the club issued a further statement saying it had lodged a "comprehensive Appeal."

The 750-page report was damning, accusing City of inflating contracts and sponsorship deals, running secondary contracts off the books, making "concerted efforts to stop and frustrate the Premier League investigation" and signing off inflated image rights deals to artificially boost their earnings. The report says all this enabled them to pass both Premier League and UEFA financial spending limits.

The Premier League's chief ­executive, Richard Masters, described the case "as the most significant in Premier League history," adding that the club "systematically broke Premier League Rules for nearly a decade."

Although the Premier League, the world's most commercially successful football league, has drawn some praise for its actions, others point out that it took Pinto leaking emails and media reports to take action. The report shows that Manchester City, which had won the league once in 71 years before being taken over by the United Arab Emirates (UAE) in 2008, were consistently posting revenues way higher than any of their more established and successful rivals.

In this context, its the owners. The Abu Dhabi United Group, essentially an investment vehicle for the UAE royal family, bought the club in 2008. Mansour bin Zayed Al Nahyan, know simply as Sheikh Mansour, fronted the takeover. He is a member of the ruling Al Nahyan family and the UAE's vice president and deputy prime minister. Khaldoon Al Mubarak, who also holds several government positions in the UAE, was brought in as chairman.

Nation-state ownership of this type is controversial in European football. Manchester City's owners have long been criticized for the country's human rights record and using the club for sportswashing. Similar criticisms have been made of European champions Paris Saint-Germain, which Qatar owned and City's Premier League rivals Newcastle United, which is owned 85% by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund.

City's CEO, Ferran Soriano, joined in 2012 as City expanded their ownership into foreign leagues, buying up clubs in the US, Japan and Australia. He replaced former head coach Roberto Mancini with fellow Spaniard Manuel Pellegrini before former Bayern Munich and Barcelona coach Pep Guardiola took over in 2016, ushering in the club's most successful period.

The club immediately started splashing out on big-money transfers after the UAE investment arrived. At the time, neither UEFA nor the Premier League had the tight financial rules they now have. UEFA introduced its Financial Fair Play (FFP) rules for the 2011-12 season, while the Premier League followed with its Profit and Sustainability Rules (PSR) in 2013-14.

City's free spending and relatively low commercial income meant they were likely to fall foul of such rules. The club was handed a conditional €60 million UEFA fine for losses in the 2011-12 and 2012-13 seasons. They would later be banned from the Champions League on similar grounds in 2020 before being reinstated on appeal.

In an attempt to try and comply with these financial rules, City set up a system they called "Project Longbow" in 2012. The commission said much of this was genuine, but one arm of it was an agreement with a third party called Fordham that was, according to the ruling, "little more than a front." Essentially, it reads, the club's UAE owners "would be used (and were used) to enable Fordham to acquire from the club (at a sizeable, artificially inflated price) the club's entitlement to benefit financially from its players' image rights."

In other words, City's owners were pumping money into their own club through vastly inflated sponsorship and image rights deals so their income looked higher and they could spend more, particularly on player transfers and wages. The report also claims that City paid three individuals, whose names are redacted, through a third party to keep them off the books.

That's the question on everyone's lips. So far, no sanctions have been imposed on City, and the appeal will likely drag proceedings out even longer. Meanwhile, the club, which is top of the Premier League, faces Liverpool on October 11, after the international break.

While there is no precedent for such a tranche of charges, clubs have previously fallen foul of the Premier League's PSR regulations. Everton were handed a 10-point penalty for a £19.5 million breach over a period ending with the 2021-22 season. This was based on a Premier League tariff of six points for the initial breach, plus an additional point for every £5 million the club overspent. Given that City's overspending is close to a billion pounds, applying that would certainly relegate them, though the Premier League does not operate England's second-tier league, the Championship.

For now, it looks like City and the league will play on under a cloud. But if the verdict remains after appeal, clubs and individuals whose careers and honors stand to be impacted will likely come forward and litigate. City themselves could be relegated, docked points over successive seasons, face transfer bans or any combination of those things. The titles won in the relevant period could also be stripped.

#991. Germany's Franz Reindl elected new IIHF president

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Franz Reindl has been elected president of ice hockey's world governing body. The former forward was a member of West Germany's Olympic bronze medal-winning team.

Franz Reindl came from behind to narrowly beat Petr Briza of the Czech Republic in a run-off vote to become the International Ice Hockey Federation's new president on Friday.

Reindl had trailed Briza 48-36 in the first round of voting but picked up crucial votes after Aivaz Omorkanov of Kyrgyzstan was eliminated. He then beat Briza 60-57 in the runoff held on the Spanish island of Tenerife. Six of a total of nine candidates had withdrawn prior to the election.

The 71-year-old German, who had been a member of the IIHF Council since 2016, succeeds Canadian-born Frenchman Luc Tardif, who had announced in March that he would not seek a further term in office. Reindl's election comes 5 years after he had finished as runner-up to Tardif. His term runs until 2030.

Reindl has done it all in German ice hockey, having been a player and coach, as well as serving as the first CEO of the top-flight professional league, the Deutsche Eishockey Liga (DEL), from 1994 to 19<96. He also headed the organizing committees for three World Championships — in 2001, 2010 and 2017. From 2014 to 2022 he served as president of the German Ice Hockey Association (DEB), overseeing an upturn in the fortunes of the men's national team, which won the silver medal at the Pyeongchang Winter Olympics in 2018.

"Franz Reindl has shaped German ice hockey for decades—as a player, but above all through the various key roles he has held within our federation," the current DEB chairman, Frank Lutz, said.

"The fact that the international ice hockey family is now entrusting him with the leadership of the IIHF is a great tribute to his work."

Like Briza, who was a goalie for the Czechoslovakia team that won bronze in Albertville in 1992, Reindl was also an Olympic bronze medalist, having been a member of the 1976 West Germany team who beat the United States and Finland to finish third in Lake Placid.

Domestically, Reindl played 669 games and scored 423 goals for SC Riessersee and SB Rosenheim over 16 seasons in the Eishockey-Bundesliga, the predecessor to the DEL.

Tardif's years as IIHF president were marked by two major events: the return of National Hockey League players to the Olympic tournament in Milan Cortina this year, and the exclusion from international play of Russia in 2022 over the country's full-scale war on neighboring Ukraine.

Reindl is the second German to be elected IIHF president after Günter Sabetzki, who held the post from 1975 to 1994.

#968. Munich up against at least six competitors for 2036 Olympics

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The International Olympic Committee has revealed the six other countries intending to bid for the 2036 Olympic Games. This comes after Germany selected Munich as its Olympic candidate.

Munich's bid for the 2036 Summer Olympics will be up against at least six candidates from Europe, Asia and Africa. On Tuesday, the International Olympic Committee (IOC) announced that, in addition to Germany, Hungary, Turkey, India, Qatar, South Korea and South Africa all intend to bid for the Games. All are currently engaged in the Continuous Dialogue stage of the bidding process with the IOC.

However, there could be further applicants, as the deadline for interested parties to submit a bid is October 31. The candidates are to present their plans virtually to the Future Host Commission in November. In March 2027, the IOC is to deliberate, analyze, and narrow down the field of applicants to a group selected to enter into the Strategic Dialogue stage.

A subsequent Targeted Dialogue stage with one or more bidders is planned for 2028, with the final decision on the 2036 Olympic host expected at the IOC Session in the summer of 2029.

However, the IOC did not disclose exactly which cities, alongside Munich, are in the running – although Ahmedabad, Doha, Budapest and Istanbul are seen as possible hopefuls.

Munich secured its bid late last month after beating the Cologne/Rhine-Ruhr region in a vote by delegates of the German Olympic Sports Confederation. The city last hosted the Games in 1972. Berlin had withdrawn its bid in the days leading up to the vote.

"Wherever the Games take place, one thing must never change: Athletes have to be at the heart of everything we do," IOC President Kirsty Coventry said.

"As an athlete, you spend years working to make your Olympic dream come true. The experience you have when you finally reach the Olympic Games stays with you for the rest of your life," added the former swimmer who won gold in the 200-meter backstroke for Zimbabwe at the 2004 Athens and 2008 Beijing Summer Games.

"That is why, for every future host, delivering the best possible experience for the athletes must always be the starting point."

The IOC is to evaluate the bidders' proposals based on "technical factors." These include a master plan for competition venues and the athlete experience, the financing strategy, political and public support, the capacity to deliver the event, and a sustainability strategy. The German Olympic Sports Confederation believes Munich is well-positioned in all these areas.

The Munich bid is not focused solely on 2036 but was also designed with 2040 and 2044 in mind. If it misses out on 2036, Munich can expect to face a similarly large pool of competitors for the following two Summer Games. According to an IOC statement, there are National Olympic Committees "taking part in noncommittal talks with the IOC about hosting future Olympic Games beyond 2036."

Just hours before Lionel Messi played his last match for Argentina, Cristiano Ronaldo posted his latest salvo in a row with Portugal. Ronaldo wants to play on despite the troubles, while Messi bathed in adulation.

They are the two players who have defined the last two decades. As football became ever more popular, commercial and dominant in the cultural and sporting landscape, Cristiano Ronaldo and Lionel Messi remained its twin peaks.

Along with that has come endless social media posts, stories and discussions about who is better. Does Messi's World Cup and two Copa America titles with Argentina trump Ronaldo's Euro 2016 win and two Nations League titles? Or does the Portuguese's greater number of goals mean more? How about their club honors?

It is, essentially, a subjective argument and not one that can ever be resolved. It remains to be seen whether that's also true of the public spat between Ronaldo and his Portugal coach Jorge Jesus.

On Tuesday, Messi scored a 126th goal in his 208th game for Argentina. Shortly before he did, Ronaldo posted a 2,000-word statement in which he stopped short of quitting the national team but said there was an "absolute breakdown of trust" between him and the coach.

Ronaldo's comments refer to Portugal's recent match against Norway in which he said he was initially to be rested before being asked to warm up as a substitute without ever being brought on. The striker, 41, walked away from the team's camp for the remainder of the international break, but said the story is not over.

"I can promise all my fans, and naturally especially all Portuguese people, that we will have one or more farewell matches. There will be time for that," said Ronaldo, who plays for Al-Nassr in Saudi Arabia.

"Despite the absolute breach of trust in the coach's word, as anyone who witnessed these conversations can attest, I should not have done it," Ronaldo said. "The responsibility is entirely mine. I apologize to the Portuguese people once again, but the breaking of one's word has, for me, a defining impact.

"I felt that, from that point on, my staying would only harm the group, and I did what I have always said I would do if I didn't feel useful: I picked up my bags and left," he continued before suggesting he accepted there may be a need for the coach or federation to punish him.

"Not coming anymore is going to be deeply painful. I swear I'd like to play for the rest of my life, because playing for the Argentina national team is the most beautiful thing that can happen to you," Messi, who now plays his club football for Inter Miami in the US, told the 80,000 fans who had packed in to see him score a penalty in a 3-0 win over Benin.

For some, the contrasting evenings offered a neat synopsis of the pair. Ronaldo, less naturally gifted than his counterpart, has made the most of his talents with a singular focus which many believe often makes him prioritize himself over the team. He has had similar fallouts with managers and head coaches before, including Jose Mourinho at Real Madrid, Erik ten Hag at Manchester United and Portugal's 2022 World Cup coach, Fernando Santos.

Ronaldo's much-discussed aim to reach 1,000 career goals is held up by some as further evidence of where his motivations lie.

"You know what my goal is. I want to win trophies, and I want to reach that number (1,000 goals) that you all know. I will reach the number for sure, if no injuries," he said last December. He is currently on 979.

For others, that is simply what is required to be the best. While Messi's quiet character and general refusal to play out his life in public suggest a more humble character focused on helping the team, his genius has undoubtedly changed the teams around him. In the 2026 World Cup, he barely broke into a run but still led an otherwise fairly limited Argentina to the final with eight goals.

There will be no more Messi goals for Argentina and, despite his promises, no absolute assurances that the world will see Ronaldo in a Portugal shirt again. For now, both will head back to their club sides to play out their twilight years away from the intensity of Europe's top leagues. But wherever they go from here, the spotlight will keep following.

#910. Germany's women: Ena Mahmutovic makes her case to become Germany's next No.1 goalkeeper

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Germany's win against Australia saw Ena Mahmutovic excel in goal. The 22-year-old Bayern Munich goalkeeper is considered the successor to Ann-Katrin Berger and is making a name for herself.

With determination, pace, and four beautifully crafted goals, Germany's women won its friendly against Australia, thrilling not only the fans but also head coach Christian Wück.

"You can see how fired up the girls are, how well they're already playing together, and that we have an incredible amount of talent on the field and on the bench," said a delighted Wück after his team's well-deserved 4-2 victory.

Since there were several notable absences, including captain Giulia Gwinn and top scorer Lea Schüller, others had to step up and fill the gaps. And they did so with flying colors. Melissa Kössler made a strong case for herself as a striker by scoring her first two international goals. Center back Camilla Küver and Sjoeke Nüsken were also on the scoresheet.

Much like it was with the men's team during Jürgen Klopp's first international break as Germany head coach, the question as to who will emerge as the next number one goalkeeper for the women was also a hot topic.

The difference is that unlike Manuel Neuer, who has retired from international football, Ann-Katrin Berger is sticking around for at least one more tournament cycle.

However, with just three matches to go before the start of the playoffs in the National Women's Soccer League (NWSL), where she plays for Gotham FC, Berger is giving these two Germany friendlies a miss.

This means 22-year-old Bayern Munich goalkeeper Ena Mahmutovic, who is seen as a potential successor to Berger, took her her chance.

"Ena deserves to play the next match," Wück said ahead of the game. "We must not forget that she already kept a clean sheet in Slovenia (Germany's last World Cup qualifier). And I hope she can repeat that tomorrow."

Some will draw parallels to Jonas Urbig, who many see as a future number one for Germany's men. Both he and Mahmutovic were born in 2003 in the western state of North Rhine-Westphalia, and both are seen as up-and-coming stars for their club and country.

The difference is that while Urbig remains an understudy to Neuer at Bayern, Mahmutovic is already the first-choice keeper at her club, having won the starting job over Maria Luisa Grohs prior to the start of the season.

For Germany, though, Mahmutovic still has to bide her time, while there are already strong indications that Klopp intends to make Urbig his number one.

"I'm obviously delighted," Mahmutovic said ahead of the games.

"It's always something special, of course – especially playing against a team from another continent. I think testing ourselves against them is very helpful as we look ahead to the World Cup."

Bayern director Bianca Rech recently described Mahmutovic as "the future of the national team," calling on Wück not only to give her a callup, but also regular playing time.

Wück still sees Berger as slightly better than Mahmutovic, but he is also full of praise for the young keeper.

"Ena's performances have been very good," he said. "She will get her playing time."

The match against Slovenia was just Mahmutovic's second appearances for Germany. Her first, which came almost two years ago, was memorable for the wrong reason. In the dying minutes of a friendly in December 2024, she lost control of the ball after a back pass, allowing an Italian forward to score the winner in a 2-1 defeat for Germany. Still, Mahmutovic was included in Germany's squad for the 2025 Euros.

Had it been up to her father – who is from Montenegro – Mahmutovic would never have become a goalkeeper.

"He still says I would have been better as an outfield player because I'm good with both feet," she said.

That's a quality that evidently wasn't lost on Thomas Gerstner, when her coach at her then-club Duisburg, gave her her Women's Bundesliga debut in May 2020. With the team coping with several absences, the then-16-year-old, who had been on the bench as backup goalkeeper, came on as an outfield player.

"The coach brought me on during stoppage time," Mahmutovic recalled. "I got to make two sprints – that was my debut."

That, however, was a one-off, and nobody questions Mahmutovic's strengths as a goalkeeper.

"She is one of the most talented goalkeepers in the world," said Bayern coach Jose Barcala, noting that she is the kind of player who can decide a match.

Mahmutovic will be hoping she gets many more opportunities to do just that for Germany.

#895. Formula 1: Rwanda to bring sport back to Africa

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The last time Formula 1 hosted a race in Africa was way back in 1993. But a new track in Rwanda is set to host a Grand Prix in "the early 2030s," continuing the country’s investment in international sport.

After nearly 40 years of false starts, motor racing's premier sport is returning to Africa, after Formula 1 announced on Tuesday that Rwanda would soon host a Grand Prix.

The race will be know as the African Grand Prix after a 10-year contract was signed between the parties. With no suitable track ready, Rwanda will build one in Bugesera, southeast of the capital Kigali. F1 said it will be ready "early in the next decade."

The last time F1 raced in Africa was the South African Grand Prix in 1993. While there has since been plenty of talk of the sport returning to the continent, most often mentioning South Africa itself, nothing concrete had happened, until now.

"Today is a landmark moment for Formula 1," F1 President and chief executive Stefano Domenicali said. "With Rwanda joining the calendar from the next decade our sport will race in every inhabited continent, bringing the world championship closer than ever to fans around the globe.

"Africa has always been part of Formula 1's future, and from the beginning we were determined to return in the right way, with the right partner, a long-term commitment and a clear vision for creating meaningful impact."

One of the most vocal proponents of a race in Africa has been seven-time world champion Lewis Hamilton. The 41-year-old Black British driver, currently with Ferrari, has said he has been "fighting in the background" for years to have a race in Africa. With the date not yet set in stone for the African Grand Prix, it remains to be seen whether he can prolong his career enough to be on the grid when the lights go out in Rwanda.

For Rwandan President Paul Kagame, the event is "a success and a milestone for the entire continent, especially for the African fans." He added that he hoped it could lead to more F1 events in Africa.

Kagame has been president of Rwanda for 25 years, and essentially ran the country for the six years before that, following the genocide in 1994. He routinely wins over 99% of the vote in elections, and the country is often criticized for its human rights record.

The 68-year-old has bet big on sports. Rwanda has had sponsorship deals with a number of top European football clubs, including Arsenal, Atletico Madrid, Paris Saint-Germain and Bayern Munich. However, fan and media backlash over the deal caused Bayern to drop the commercial element with the country's tourist arm, Visit Rwanda, last year. Arsenal did not renew its deal. Many critics have labeled the policy as sportswashing.

The country also has deals with top US basketball and American football teams, and hosted cycling's Road World Championships last year.

But Formula 1, one of the world's richest sports, will likely be seen as Kagame and Rwanda's biggest sporting coup to date.

#885. FIFPRO report highlights growing pains in women's football

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Expected global revenue of over $1 billion and new faces at the U17 World Cup show women's football is booming, but a recent report also highlights need for better structures globally.

At first glance, women's football is healthier than ever. A recent report by FIFPRO, football's global players' union, said worldwide revenue is expected to reach over $1bn (€894m) in 2027. The World Cup in Brazil, a first in South America, will be the last with 32 teams and is expected to be the most watched and biggest revenue-generating women's tournament ever.

The good news continues with the U17 Women's World Cup in Morocco, where the tournament is being held every year for five straight years. Germany, France, Spain, Brazil, the United States and defending champions North Korea are all at the 2026 edition of a competition whose reputation continues to grow.

"You can just see where the game is going, which is so exciting," US head coach Ciara Crinion told FIFA. "The level has just gone up. We have to continue to stay with that and compete as well. [The U-17 Women's World Cup] almost feels like that first stepping stone in a major tournament for young players, and now you see how serious it is taken."

Puerto Rico, Australia and Argentina are making their debuts. For Puerto Rico, qualification is historic, as no football team in the men's or women's game has ever reached a World Cup tournament before.

"We've embraced this idea of being Puerto Rico's first team to qualify for a World Cup," head coach Andres Mirabelli told FIFA. Alongside national development plans, player recruitment was aided by a diaspora of nearly four million in the United States, perhaps the strongest place for women's football in the world. According to FIFPRO, the regional football average salary in North America is $5,940 (€5,547) per month, which is far higher than any other continent.

But even for the handful of players who make it out of these youth tournaments to a professional career, creating a living out of the beautiful game remains a real challenge. The aforementioned FIFPRO report details that many professional female players across the world barely earn minimum wage, with some even falling below it.

The report, which sourced its data from player surveys across the world, found that only 66% of professional players are paid for 12 months a year. The global average salary is $2,600 (€2,300) a month, but the range varies drastically across the world. In Eastern Europe, for example, that average is $880 per month. In Africa, it is $432, but in North America it is just under $6,000.

"I think what you also find in the women's game is the gaps are not just between the top five leagues and the rest. There is a gap there, but even within leagues themselves [there is one]," FIFPRO head of research and strategy for women's football Frankie Brown told DW.

"In some of the other leagues you're dealing with teams that are operating professionally, so will be paying their players on a reasonable wage, and playing in the same league as them you'll have players that are on amateur contracts and not being paid at all."

With economic and job security an issue for many, the report reveals that psychological stress factors are now the second main reason many consider leaving football.

"This is one of the most concerning parts of the report," Alex Culvin, the director of women's football at FIFPRO, said in a statement.

"Of the 49% of women’s football players considering leaving the sport early, 46% cited their mental health as the reason. In interviews, players say the sport's growth brings new pressures — including extra workload and social media abuse — but that clubs do not have the necessary safeguards in place to support these changes. We want clubs and national associations to provide mandatory mental health support focused on wellbeing, which is independent of sporting performance objectives."

FIFPRO's report is asking FIFA to set a so-called floor for the women's game so that minimum standards in key areas such as contracts, pensions and health can be enforced. Perhaps inspiration can be taken from the labor deal the WNBA agreed in 2026. It was long-negotiated deal that ultimately sent a message to female athletes everywhere to push for better pay, conditions and a greater share of the revenue they help generate.

This is not an official job posting, but Germany's federal government is indeed looking for someone to fill the nation's highest office. Having already served two terms, current President Frank-Walter Steinmeier is no longer eligible for another, leaving the position open for a successor. "The person should have a certain level of public recognition, be politically unbiased, capable of bringing people together, approachable and a reasonably good [public] speaker," political scientist Ursula Münch told DW.

In Germany, a federal parliamentary republic, the president serves as head of state and a representative of the country as well as someone who embodies moral authority for the nation. Unlike in the US, France, or Russia, the president does not wield extensive political power or direct government affairs.

Steinmeier was nominated by his own party, the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD), in 2009. However, once elected via a convention of parliamentarians and state electors, a president-elect has to suspend their party membership in order to serve in the office apolitically. This time, it is Chancellor Friedrich Merz's center-right Christian Democrats (CDU), the senior partner in the coalition government with the SPD and the conservative Christian Social Union (CSU), which has the right to nominate a candidate.

Political scientist Münch said that "the three parties must agree on a person who is acceptable for all three." Since the convention that will select Steinmeier's successor is currently controlled by these three parties and their delegates, it is very likely that whomever Merz nominates will be elected.

"Well, I would really like it to be a woman this time. We've had 12 male federal presidents since 1949 — it's about time for a woman. But if the right man were to be nominated, I'd be fine with that too," said Münch. According to an August survey conducted by the Forsa polling institute for the RTL television network, about 74% of German respondents say they don't care whether it's a man or a woman. Ilse Aigner, president of the Bavarian State Parliament in Munich, appears to have a good chance. Aigner is a member of the smallest party in the coalition, the Bavarian CSU, and is considered a unifying figure. She also has experience in government in Berlin, having served as agricultural minister at the federal level. Even members of the opposition Greens and the SPD are full of praise for Aigner's candidacy. "What are we actually waiting for? Surely Ilse Aigner has long since secured a majority across party lines?" asked SPD parliamentary group leader Matthias Miersch recently. #171. Dozens are dead in a high-speed train crash in Spain

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Emergency services attend to the victims at the scene of a collision between two trains in Urda, Spain, on Wednesday. Juanma Jiménez/Europa Press via AP hide caption

URDA, Spain (AP) — A high-speed train slammed into another that had stopped on the tracks in Spain Wednesday, killing at least 68 people and injuring dozens in the second deadly rail collision this year.

The regional government of Castilla La Mancha confirmed the death toll and said another 58 people were receiving medical treatment at hospitals. Rescue workers searched the wreckage through the day for survivors and bodies.

The collision came just nine months after another deadly crash involving high-speed trains operated by the same two companies in southern Spain in January.

"Today all of Spain is grieving," Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez said from the crash site, where he announced that his government would declare three days of national mourning.

This framegrab taken from a video provided by Spain's Guardia Civil shows an aerial view of carriages and first responders on the site of a collision between two trains in Urda, Spain, Wednesday. Guardia Civil via AP hide caption

Sánchez called for increased safety measures to restore confidence in a high-speed rail system that was once a source of national pride.

"It is unacceptable for a country like Spain to suffer two high-speed rail accidents in the same year," said Sánchez, who faces a national election on Nov. 29.

Spain's rail system operator ADIF said the crash occurred Wednesday morning in the central province of Toledo, involving two high-speed trains carrying hundreds of passengers and heading south in the same direction from Madrid to Seville.

A train operated by the state-owned RENFE company struck the rear of another train operated by private company IRYO that was stopped. The collision derailed two carriages on the tail of the IRYO train and the front of the RENFE train.

Samy Paul, a British tourist, described the shock of the impact to The Associated Press after being rescued from the RENFE train.

"Suddenly, everything started falling apart. Bang! We fell down," he said. "We realized carriage two, carriage one, all gone, all smashed. There was blood all over."

David Gómez, a construction worker, was working nearby when he heard the crash and ran to help, he told the newspaper El País.

"People were trying to get out through the windows ... We cut through some fences to access the area and treated people with the first-aid kits we had brought until emergency services arrived," he said. "As I was trying to pull a young woman out, there was a piece of a leg."

Rail service was suspended between Madrid, Ciudad Real and Andalusia.

Spain's Transport Minister Óscar Puente said that the crash was caused by what he called the "inexplicable" order from a central command center for Spain's trains after a security system had failed. He said the train engineers were not to blame.

Puente said that at 8:30 a.m. the security system on a stretch of 63 kilometers (39 miles) stopped working due to a computer malfunction, causing all trains on that stretch to stop. But the central command told the RENFE train to start running again without any speed restrictions, and it ended up colliding with the stopped IRYO train at 8:53 a.m.

Puente said that the train should never have been told to start moving again since there was still a "stop signal" active on that line. And if it had, at least it should have been told to go at reduced speed.

Authorities were still studying information to see how fast the train was going, but it could have been at 200 kph (124 mph) for that stretch of the track, Puente said.

"I am not attributing responsibility to anyone, I am just stating the facts," Puente said.

The crash, which occurred in a flat stretch surrounded by olive fields in between the towns of Urda and Los Yébenes, left carriages shredded into bits of metal and other material, scattering debris down a gravel slope.

Passenger Ignacio Relaño was traveling on the RENFE train. He told state broadcaster TVE that the train had stopped momentarily on the line before it started again and then crashed into the IRYO.

"We all felt the train abruptly slowing down and then the impact," he said. "The car leaned to one side like it was about to tip over. I hit my nose, everything went flying, luggage, cell phones. Everything shook."

Authorities said 397 passengers were in the RENFE train and 147 passengers in the IRYO train.

Ambulances and medical helicopters made trips to and from the site, while unscathed survivors were put on buses. A military emergency brigade was deployed to help.

Spain's King Felipe VI also visited the crash site.

"We share in the grief of the families and loved ones of those who lost their lives, and we send our heartfelt support to the injured and all those affected," Spain's royals said in a statement.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said "My heart goes out to the victims' families and loved ones. Today, Europe mourns with you."

Spain's soccer federation postponed all 24 Copa del Rey games scheduled on Wednesday.

The wreckage of Wednesday's crash reminded many of the aftermath of another train collision in January, when 46 people died in southern Spain when an IRYO train jumped the track and hit an oncoming RENFE train. That sent the first two cars of the impacted train plummeting down a four-meter (13-foot) slope.

That crash was the deadliest in Spain since a 2013 crash that killed 80 people.

Before this year, there had been no fatal crash on Spain's dedicated high-speed lines since the network opened in 1992.

Over recent decades, Spain has invested heavily in high-speed rail — which the country's railway union defines as rails for trains going 250 kph (155 mph) and over — turning it into a world leader.

Spain has laid some 3,900 kilometers (2,400 miles) of such tracks over more than three decades for its 49 million residents. Only China, with 45,000 kilometers (28,000 miles) for its 1.4 billion people, has more high-speed track, according to the International Union of Railways.

Only recently did Spain open its rail network to private companies to compete against RENFE. The Italian-owned IRYO started operating in Spain in 2022.

A spokesperson for Spain's train workers' union, Joan Rodríguez, said increased traffic brought by privatization has put extra strain on rail infrastructure.

"We are not convinced that the liberalization of the high-speed passenger rail service was matched by the necessary upkeep of the lines," Rodríguez told TVE on Wednesday. "I am not saying it was a determining factor (in the crashes), but yes, the increase in use of the network is a factor to keep in mind."
