QUITO, Oct 29 - For years, the neighborhood watch group in the working-class area of Solanda in Ecuadorean capital Quito had patrolled their nighttime streets armed with little more than whistles and sticks, on the lookout for cellphone snatching and other petty crimes.

But then they realized the criminals were changing. Members of notorious national gangs began to move into the area, and shootings, contract killings and extortion took hold. The watch suspended the patrols, fearful they might be targeted themselves. Their place was taken by heavily armed troops, sent by the national government.

Ecuador's importance as a transit point for cocaine exports from Colombia and Peru has spiked over the last half-decade, stoking an explosion in violence and crime in the once tranquil South American country that has caught up everyone from Pacific fishermen to politicians, journalists and grandmothers forced from their homes.

President Daniel Noboa, an ally of the administration of US President Donald Trump, has responded with a widespread military, police and legislative crackdown on crime, sending soldiers into the streets and hardening sentences, among other measures. The government has notched several major captures or killings of gang leaders, but that has led to fragmentation, creating vicious internecine violence and splinter groups seeking new territory.

That now includes Quito. This historic Andean city, with its whitewashed colonial buildings, cobbled streets, and backdrop of cloudcapped volcanoes, has long been a draw for tourists and digital nomads. But it is becoming an increasing focus of criminal activity, residents, police and analysts say. Gangs are using local drug sales to gain a foothold in neighborhoods, taking advantage of the capital’s strategic location to distribute drugs to the country’s ports, from where cocaine destined mostly for Europe and the US is shipped.

“Criminal organizations have tried to establish themselves in certain neighborhoods through small-scale drug trafficking," police Lieutenant Colonel Juan Chacon told Reuters as his team conducted a raid on an alleged drug distribution site in central Quito. "That connects them to a range of other crimes."

Los Lobos, Los Choneros and Los Tiguerones, all gangs which the US has designated as foreign terrorist organizations, are present in Quito, according to police.

Solanda, built as a public housing project in the 1980s, with a busy commercial district and narrow streets of modest houses, appears on a national government list of high-risk Quito areas. Residents have installed a surveillance camera system on every street corner and metal gates at entrances to side streets to prevent unknown people from entering.

“With the cameras, you can identify the criminal and avoid exposing yourself,” said Maria Eugenia Alban, a community leader in Solanda. “Now criminals are no longer acting alone, and they are armed.”

The pushback from authorities has had some success. An increased security response in Solanda lowered violent death figures to just two between January and September of this year, down from 13 in the same period of 2025, according to government records.

Violent death figures in Quito overall fell in 2024 and 2025 from a high of 294 in 2023, according to Interior Ministry data, as Noboa sent soldiers onto the streets. And violence still remains below that of Ecuador's port cities like Guayaquil and Duran.

But, the armed forces say, it is a constant battle.

“Organized crime groups are trying to establish a foothold in Quito,” Lieutenant Colonel Miguel Saavedra, head of a tactical army brigade which is responsible for controlling guns, ammunition and explosives in the capital, told Reuters at a checkpoint where his unit was checking cars for weapons. His troops were seeking to "safeguard and restore security" in the city, he added.

About 4,000 police and military personnel were deployed at entry points and along strategic corridors in Quito as part of a security plan announced by Noboa in August. The city has been under a 60-day state of emergency since September.

During the first six weeks of the plan, police arrested more than 300 people, according to national police figures. The military joined police operations to check for weapons at entrances to high-risk neighborhoods and conducted targeted patrols.

Drug seizures in Quito were up nearly 500% between January and September, compared with the same period a year before, according to police figures.

Military and police operations across Ecuador cost gangs involved in drug trafficking, illegal mining and fuel and arms trafficking nearly $20 billion in losses between January and July, according to the Defense Ministry.

However, crackdowns in one place can prompt criminal groups to seek out more favorable territory where residents are less wary of crime, said Colonel Mario Pazmino, a former head of army intelligence and current security analyst.

He said Quito is part of the “cocaine triangle," serving as a transit and temporary storage point for drugs coming from the Colombian coastal city of Tumaco and Putumayo province, before being transported across Ecuador along Pacific and Amazon road corridors.

The capital’s strategic position has been exploited by a growing number of criminal groups over the past year, said Tiziano Breda, an analyst at conflict-monitoring organization ACLED, although its status as a "transit point" lowers the risk of it being the focus of the sort of violent disputes between groups seen elsewhere.

“In Quito’s case, international cartels and national gangs are beginning to converge and establish themselves,” Pazmino said. “Quito is a hub city. The gangs come to protect the merchandise and look for other activities.” REUTERS

ZIBQIN, Lebanon Oct 29 - In a field overlooking a lush valley in southern Lebanon, residents of the village of Zibqin balanced on branches picking their olives while Lebanese Army soldiers stood nearby.

Zibqin is adjacent to Israel’s self-declared “security zone” in southern Lebanon, where Israeli troops have demolished dozens of villages, saying armed group Hezbollah had embedded itself in civilian infrastructure — a claim the group denies. Israeli shelling frequently strikes the valley below the olive groves despite a June ceasefire between the two sides.

As Palestinians, Israeli rights groups and the UN report increased violence and olive thefts by Israeli settlers in the Israeli-occupied West Bank, Lebanese farmers also fear for their safety, illustrating how conflict is again upending the annual harvest.

Despite the danger, Lebanese farmers are returning to fields they have tended for generations, determined to harvest a crop that is one of their main sources of income and nutrition.

Ali Bzieh, a 65-year-old farmer from Zibqin, said he had no choice but to come to the fields. “This is my land, this is my livelihood, this is a harvest we wait for from one year to the next,” he said, sitting under the branches of an olive tree. “We have an attachment to this land, to the plants, and to the crops — we don't leave them.”

Many families came back to Zibqin after the ceasefire between Israel and Iran-backed Hezbollah. But the situation remains unstable, Raef Bzieh, a local official, said. “The ongoing bombing and the aggressions taking place on the outskirts of the village have of course caused worry and panic among the people.”

The danger prompted residents to ask the Lebanese Army to accompany them to their fields. But Mustafa Barakat, a 50-year-old carpenter, said that even with the army, the villagers did not feel safe. “But if the Israelis decide to cause harm, they can harm us, and the army,” he said. “They have struck the Lebanese Army more than once.”

Israeli attacks have killed 26 Lebanese soldiers in 2026, according to statements posted by the Lebanese Army on X. The Lebanese Army is not party to the conflict between Hezbollah and Israel.

In response to questions from Reuters, the Israeli military said its strikes in Lebanon are against Hezbollah and its infrastructure and are not directed at the Lebanese Army or civilians. It also said it is working to "enable the olive harvest and assist residents in carrying it out".

Thirty-eight Israeli soldiers have been killed in southern Lebanon in 2026, according to posts on the Israeli military's website.

Barakat's days in the olive grove used to start in the early morning and stretch until sunset. Now, he limits his time in the field to when the Lebanese Army is present.

The danger has reshaped what was once an occasion for families to gather and share the hard work of harvesting olives.

“In the past, we used to treat this (harvest) season like an outing," he said. He used to come with his family to the fields and prepare food together. "Now, you can’t bring children… bombing could occur, and they could get hurt," he said.

The conflict has affected the harvest in other ways too: “We weren't able to tend to our olive trees, so our olive trees did not yield as they usually do,” said Ali, who was displaced for several months. He said the harvest was half what it normally was.

Ali said that he hoped the situation wouldn't escalate further and force his family to flee again. But many in Zibqin were bracing for that possibility: some residents continue to pay rent for apartments in areas further north. Others have already reserved a place to stay in the event of further escalation.

Yet as war casts its shadow over the harvest for the fourth consecutive year, the farmers refuse to abandon their fields.

“This land is our identity, it is the passport we carry," said Barakat. "If we abandon it, we are truly left without an identity." REUTERS

#556. Growing old alone: The parents Iran’s emigrants left behind

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As more Iranians build new lives abroad, many parents back home face a less visible consequence of migration: years without seeing their children, family relationships maintained through phone screens, and growing uncertainty about who will be there for them in old age.

For Maryam, a 68-year-old retiree in Tehran, that distance has become part of everyday life. Her son lives in Canada and her daughter in Germany, and she has not seen either of them in years.

“I know they're building their futures, and I'm happy for them,” she told Iran International. “But sometimes I just wish they were here, even for an ordinary dinner.”

Maryam says regular calls and photographs help her stay connected with her family, particularly her grandchildren. But watching them grow up from thousands of miles away is difficult.

“They know me through the phone,” she says. “I wish I could be more than just a face on a screen in their lives.”

Last winter, Maryam was hospitalized with kidney problems. Her children arranged help through relatives, and a family member accompanied her to medical appointments.

“My daughter was very worried, and I kept telling her everything was fine,” she recalls. “Sometimes, especially when I'm ill, I just wish we weren't so far apart.”

Caring for a spouse without the children nearby

In Karaj, 72-year-old Hamid has spent the past two years caring for his wife after a stroke left her with limited mobility.

Their two sons live in Sweden and Australia. Although they call regularly and provide financial support, most daily responsibilities fall on Hamid.

He describes the exhaustion of managing medication, medical appointments and household chores.

“Some people think you're fortunate when your children live abroad,” he says. “But when your wife needs help getting out of bed, what matters is having someone nearby.”

The family has hired a part-time caregiver, but Hamid still handles much of his wife's care.

“Sometimes I want to tell my sons how exhausted I am,” he says. “Then I think about how far away they are and wonder what they could possibly do.”

Hamid does not blame his children for emigrating, but says family life has changed.

“We're still very close, and we talk all the time. But it's different when you can't sit together in the same room.”

For Nahid, a 64-year-old widow in Tehran, the question is whether she should leave Iran herself.

Her only daughter has lived in London for eight years and has encouraged her mother to move closer.

“My daughter tells me to come and live with her, but I've spent my whole life here,” she says. “My sisters, my friends, all my memories are here. At the same time, every year that passes, I wish I could be closer to her.”

Nahid worries about losing her independence in a country where she does not speak the language fluently or have an established social circle.

But staying in Tehran also means facing old age far from her daughter.

“I don't want to become a burden to my daughter,” she says. “But I don't want to leave behind everything I've built here either.”

Phone and video calls have helped families separated by migration remain part of one another's lives despite years of living thousands of kilometres apart. They share news, celebrate birthdays and check on each other's health, sometimes communicating more frequently than families living in the same city.

But as parents grow older, that distance takes on a different meaning. A sudden illness, a medical emergency or the growing need for help with everyday tasks can expose the limits of even the closest long-distance relationships.

For children who have emigrated, Iran remains the place where their parents are growing old. For the parents who stayed behind, each passing year brings a difficult reality into sharper focus: the people they may need most are the ones living furthest away.

Iran could face an economic contraction comparable in scale to the Great Depression in the United States, economists say, as the loss of oil revenue and wartime disruption threaten to drive an ailing economy into a deeper crisis.

Their assessments follow a prediction by US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who told Iran International that Iran's economy would shrink by more than 25% between March 2026 and March 2027.

Iranian-American economist Ali Dadpay told Iran International that he found Bessent's forecast plausible, particularly as Tehran attempts to finance wartime spending by expanding the money supply.

That risks accelerating inflation and weakening the rial further, he said, creating a cycle of rising prices, falling purchasing power and declining economic activity.

Mohamad Machine-Chian, an Iran International economist, offered a stark comparison.

"The United States lost about 26% of its output in the Great Depression, over four years, from 1929 to 1933. Secretary Bessent is describing a loss of Great Depression size in twelve months," Machine-Chian said.

Iran's economy contracted by 21.6% in 1980, the first year of the Iran-Iraq War, World Bank data shows.

Even the severe sanctions of 2018 and 2019 produced a smaller cumulative contraction of approximately 12%, despite oil exports plunging from about 2.7 million barrels per day to roughly 650,000.

Economic output was already approximately 10% lower in spring 2026 than a year earlier, according to figures from Iran's Statistical Center.

For the economy to contract by more than 25% over the full year, the decline would have to accelerate considerably.

Machine-Chian estimated that even with an 8% contraction over the summer, output would have to fall far more sharply during autumn and winter for the annual decline to approach Bessent's forecast.

The disruption of oil exports is central to that outlook. Energy intelligence firm Kpler reported no new Iranian crude loadings since late August, while revenue from earlier shipments is running out.

But the damage extends beyond the oil industry. Factories depend on imported materials, transportation companies require replacement parts, and businesses need financing to sustain operations.

As supplies and revenue dwindle, manufacturing, construction and other industries face further disruption.

The economic deterioration is already visible in the price of everyday necessities.

Food prices in September were more than double their level a year earlier, according to Iran's Statistical Center. Eggs cost three and a half times as much, rice nearly twice as much, and official bread prices in Tehran have doubled.

“The minimum wage increased 60% this year while inflation approached 90%,” Machine-Chian said. “In dollar terms, a minimum-wage worker now earns approximately $61 a month, compared with $96 a year earlier.”

He also warned about savings being eroded. With the dollar costing two and a half times as much as a year ago, money held in rials has lost approximately 60% of its value against the US currency.

Further disruption to imports could bring shortages of medicines, industrial materials and vehicle parts, potentially forcing factories to reduce production or close and putting more jobs at risk.

Dadpay described a possible future resembling aspects of the economic crises in Venezuela and Zimbabwe, where severe currency depreciation and inflation pushed people toward dollars, gold, secondhand markets and barter.

As production declines and essential goods become harder to obtain, even those who remain employed could struggle to afford basic necessities.

Sanctions policy analyst Max Meizlish expects pension payments and civilian services to come under pressure as revenues decline, while military and security personnel could continue receiving salaries.

That could allow the Islamic Republic to preserve institutions central to its political survival even as living standards deteriorate.

Economic devastation does not necessarily translate into political collapse, particularly if Tehran can preserve its security institutions and access to financial channels abroad.

Washington has not fully targeted the Chinese financial institutions facilitating Iranian transactions, partly because of concerns about wider economic consequences, Meizlish said.

"It's just not clear that this level of economic constraint on its own will be enough to produce some sort of measurable political change," he said.

Dadpay argued that years of economic mismanagement and decisions prioritizing the survival of the political system had left Iran particularly vulnerable to the current crisis.

"As long as Islamic Republic is in place with this mindset, with this dynamic of decision-making, this is what's going to happen," he said.

For ordinary Iranians, the consequences are already evident in collapsing purchasing power, dwindling savings and rising prices.

Whether Bessent's forecast proves accurate or not, the economic damage could outlast the confrontation that has accelerated it.

The disruption of oil exports is central to that outlook. Energy intelligence firm Kpler reported no new Iranian crude loadings since late August, while revenue from earlier shipments is running out.

But the damage extends beyond the oil industry. Factories depend on imported materials, transportation companies require replacement parts, and businesses need financing to sustain operations.

As supplies and revenue dwindle, manufacturing, construction and other industries face further disruption.

The economic deterioration is already visible in the price of everyday necessities.

Food prices in September were more than double their level a year earlier, according to Iran's Statistical Center. Eggs cost three and a half times as much, rice nearly twice as much, and official bread prices in Tehran have doubled.

“The minimum wage increased 60% this year while inflation approached 90%,” Machine-Chian said. “In dollar terms, a minimum-wage worker now earns approximately $61 a month, compared with $96 a year earlier.”

He also warned about savings being eroded. With the dollar costing two and a half times as much as a year ago, money held in rials has lost approximately 60% of its value against the US currency.

Further disruption to imports could bring shortages of medicines, industrial materials and vehicle parts, potentially forcing factories to reduce production or close and putting more jobs at risk.

Dadpay described a possible future resembling aspects of the economic crises in Venezuela and Zimbabwe, where severe currency depreciation and inflation pushed people toward dollars, gold, secondhand markets and barter.

As production declines and essential goods become harder to obtain, even those who remain employed could struggle to afford basic necessities.

Sanctions policy analyst Max Meizlish expects pension payments and civilian services to come under pressure as revenues decline, while military and security personnel could continue receiving salaries.

That could allow the Islamic Republic to preserve institutions central to its political survival even as living standards deteriorate.

Economic devastation does not necessarily translate into political collapse, particularly if Tehran can preserve its security institutions and access to financial channels abroad.

Washington has not fully targeted the Chinese financial institutions facilitating Iranian transactions, partly because of concerns about wider economic consequences, Meizlish said.

"It's just not clear that this level of economic constraint on its own will be enough to produce some sort of measurable political change," he said.

Dadpay argued that years of economic mismanagement and decisions prioritizing the survival of the political system had left Iran particularly vulnerable to the current crisis.

"As long as Islamic Republic is in place with this mindset, with this dynamic of decision-making, this is what's going to happen," he said.

For ordinary Iranians, the consequences are already evident in collapsing purchasing power, dwindling savings and rising prices.

Whether Bessent's forecast proves accurate or not, the economic damage could outlast the confrontation that has accelerated it.

Neither Gadi Eisenkot (left) or Benjamin Netanyahu (right) are projected to win enough seats for a majority

Israel looks to be in political deadlock.

With more than 97% of votes counted after Tuesday's election, neither the incumbent Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's coalition, nor the bloc of his key challenger Gadi Eisenkot look set to win a majority.

In order to a form a government, it looks likely one of the leading parties would have to break their campaign promises - meaning the most probable outcome may well be new elections.

Could Netanyahu persuade Eisenkot to join him in a national unity government?

Eisenkot would have to abandon his key platform to do so. He's framed himself as the only alternative to Netanyahu, and built his campaign on ousting him from power.

On election night, he took to the stage at his campaign HQ and called on Netanyahu to "announce his retirement from political life today," telling the crowd that Israelis are fed up with Netanyahu's "culture of lies", "division and incitement".

But last night at Likud HQ he thanked his "loyal partners" and called for a "strong right-wing government" - remarks some have interpreted as meaning he intends to preserve his alliance with them.

This would no-doubt be framed by Netanyahu's critics as an attempt to save his own political skin by entering another regional conflict.

This would be a seismic shift for Israeli politics. Only one independent Arab party has ever formally been part of a coalition, in 2021, and it didn't last long.

Eisenkot and every other Jewish party in his bloc bar one have already ruled out entering government with them. But right now, it's the opposition's only path to a majority.

The Arab parties were the story of election night. They look to have defied expectations and could gain a record 19 seats - helped by an unprecedented turnout from Israel's Arab population, who account for about one-fifth of the country and often describe themselves as Palestinian citizens of Israel.

For years, many boycotted Israeli elections, saying voting would legitimise a system that they say treats them as second-class citizens. But their turnout last night was inspired by a desire to oust the most right-wing governing coalition in Israel's history.

The United Arab list, who are currently projected to win eight seats, have said they'd join an opposition government, while The Joint List, who are projected to win 11 seats, have said they'd support one from outside.

Netanyahu may also bet that the estimated 70,000 Israelis who flew home from abroad to vote, and are thought to skew against him, wouldn't do so again.

Additionally, several right-wing parties who failed to pass the electoral threshold of 3.25% may attract fewer votes or may not even run, thereby bringing more votes to Likud and its allies.
