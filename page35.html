And if it’s not what’s coming out of their mouths on the mic, it’s what they’re doing when they’re not “on air.” The latest news cycle only confirms this.

Now, I am proud to say that before this week, I knew virtually nothing about Ethan Klein aside from the fact that he was sort of a public figure and I didn’t like him. From the very few clips of the Frenemies podcast I saw (and very quickly scrolled away from) with Klein and Trisha Paytas, all I gathered was that Klein is sort of a dick. Now that I know he’s allegedly been cheating on his wife for 6 months...I know that my initial assessment was too tame.

Klein is one of many male podcasters who are quite simply put: bad dudes. Aside from some of the obvious offenders like Andrew Tate, Myron Gaines, and Joe Rogan—who all spew toxic, brainless misogynistic trash on their podcasts—even the “positive” faces of male podcasting (Jay Shetty and Andrew Huberman) have faced revelations that complicate their squeaky-clean personas.

I think we need to stop treating the male podcaster archetype as inherently harmless—or, worse, as evidence that a man is particularly emotionally intelligent. With very few exceptions, these men suck—and it’s time to stop dating them.

“A podcast microphone attracts a very distinct psychological profile,” sex and relationship expert Gemma Nice tells Cosmopolitan. “Men who confuse monologue with communication, and external audience validation with genuine self-worth.”

This isn't to say every man with a microphone is secretly a pathological liar, misogynist, or cheater (or all three like Klein, sorry). Obviously, there are plenty of perfectly normal men who happen to record themselves talking for a living. Well, I haven’t seen them, but I’m sure they’re out there. Because what does it actually mean when a man decides he needs an audience for his opinions?

“Hosting a podcast gives an individual an uninterrupted platform where they talk for hours, control the narrative, and are rewarded with applause, downloads, and social media validation without anyone answering back in real time,” Nice says.

But, she explains that healthy romantic relationships require the exact opposite skill set. A healthy relationship consists of active listening, emotional humility, and sitting comfortably in silence. When these men step away from the studio and into their private relationships, they often don't know how to relate to a partner without trying to win the conversation or lecture them.

“When a partner pushes back or expresses hurt, a man addicted to the microphone immediately becomes defensive because his ego cannot handle being challenged without an audience on his side,” Nice says.

When a man becomes a podcaster, it’s typically because he has discovered that there is an enormous market for men telling other men how to live—and that market is not exactly known for producing well-adjusted feminists.

Research into the manosphere has found that podcasts can serve as a particularly effective vehicle for spreading misogynistic ideas because they package them inside more palatable formats like self-improvement, financial advice, mental-health discussions and conversations about masculinity. One analysis of popular “manfluencer” podcasts found that hypermasculine and male-supremacist ideas were frequently disguised as advice about self-reliance, success, and personal empowerment.

And the guy doesn't necessarily have to come out and say women are inferior. He can just spend three hours explaining why modern women are “too demanding,” why men “aren't allowed to be men anymore,” why women should be more feminine, why dating apps are ruining society, why men need to “reclaim” their masculinity, and why the problem with your relationship is probably that a woman hasn’t learned how to communicate with a man properly. He can also spend multiple episodes making gross comments about their cohost’s fertility—like Klein did to Paytas.

The men whose entire public persona is built around being obnoxious, inflammatory, condescending, cruel, misogynistic, chronically online, or just generally kind of a dick is not going to make a good partner. Let’s stop dating and marrying men whose favorite hobby is publicly humiliating other people—doing so only asks you to convince yourself that, with you, he'll suddenly become tender.

Now, I’m not blaming Klein’s wife, Hila at all. But as potential dating prospects, we can learn from this situation. Some people out there see a man behaving terribly in public and immediately start imagining the private version of him they could unlock. They tell themselves, “He's just playing it up for the podcast,” “he's actually really sweet,” “he’d treat me differently,” or “he has a soft side.”

We need to realize that the public persona is not a performance but an exaggerated version of who he actually is.

“In a genuine partnership, moving forward and staying healthy requires being open, honest, raw and real with each other and having a genuine accountability without being the victim,” Nice explains. “But public podcasters often build brands around being infallible authorities.”

So when they’re held accountable in real life, they don’t know how to process it. Nice explains that they instead become passive aggressive, dismiss hard conversations, or even use their platform to publicly air out their grievances.

There are, of course, a few exceptions. Hearon (mentioned earlier) and podcast duo Joe Santagato and Frank Alvarez come to mind. What makes these men with mics not only tolerable but actually enjoyable is that they don't seem particularly interested in convincing you that they have unlocked the secret to being a man. Hearon gives pop-culture commentary similar to Klein, but he isn't constantly positioning himself as the final authority on human behavior. Santagato and Alvarez are, for the most part, just goofy. They aren't selling you a masculinity framework.

This is the only reason a man should have a podcast, in my mind. I don't need you to teach me how to become a “better woman” or explain “female nature.” I don't need a six-part series on why modern dating is broken. I don't need you to tell 400,000 strangers about your relationship philosophy without any sort of credentials—or when you’re cheating on your wife. I just need you to be normal! Put the microphone down. Go outside. Have a conversation with one woman who isn't your producer.

And for people who are in the dating pool right now: whether you’re on the apps or meeting someone in real life, if you see that a potential male partner has a podcast, RUN.

#3921. 'Cobwebbing' Can Help You Move On in Dating

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

Are your head and heart websites, so to speak—meaning, are they still filled with the remnants, or "cobwebs," of a previous relationship? Well, you might want to go "cobwebbing" then and remove all of them. "Cobwebbing," a term popularized by the dating app Bumble, means clearing away any "threads" that may be keeping you mentally or emotionally tied to an ex.

This is not the same as spider-webbing, which I've described before for "A Funny Bone to Pick" as trapping someone in a bad, toxic relationship—a much creepier thing to do. No, cobwebbing is the opposite. It's about you getting away.

Cobwebbing entails getting rid of any physical reminders of your ex—like that Slanket that you used to cuddle together in, that alpaca figurine that was your birthday gift from your ex, or that lair that you used to share. Oh, and if your ex is somehow still hanging out with you in some way, your ex would qualify as a physical reminder of your ex.

Cobwebbing can be about simply discarding such objects or perhaps donating them to charity or someone else. Of course, some things—say, your ex or an apartment—may not be so easy to just throw down the trash chute or carry to Goodwill. In such cases, you may have to be the one who leaves and maintains as much physical distance as possible. This can include establishing very clear boundaries.

Oh, and don't forget about the digital reminders, too. That includes deleting your ex's digits—meaning that person's contact info—photos, messages, playlists, and other such things from your phone, watch, computer, and appliances. It can be a good idea to unfollow your ex on social media as well, because do you really need to see your ex post stuff like "Living my best life," "Humbled to share that...," or "Ugh, 20 people told me I looked hot today, so annoying?"

If you find it difficult to discard any of these things, that may be a clear sign that you are still holding on to your ex in some way. After all, nothing should be completely irreplaceable short of an original copy of Leonardo da Vinci's Mona Lisa—which really should be in the Louvre, by the way—or the inventory logs for Area 51. The only other exception may be if you and your ex had kids together, which aren't so easy to put in the trash bin, of course.

Without such cobwebbing, the risk is that your ex will continue to haunt you—to live rent-free in your head and heart. And since both of those locations are like attics in that they don't have unlimited headroom and heartroom, such cobwebs may leave little space for anyone new. Still thinking about and having feelings for your ex—even if those feelings are resentment and hatred—could end up jeopardizing any future relationship.

Plus, chances are any potential quality dates—those who can sense who you are and what you are feeling—will also have some kind of Spidey sense for cobwebs in you. Such cobwebs can end up spooking them because no one of good quality will want to play second fiddle to an ex of yours for long. You could end up being like some kind of creepy attic where the only ones willing to enter are the ghouls and goblins.

Moreover, any remaining entanglements with your ex can keep you from moving on with your life in general. This can keep you caught cycling through denial, grief, regret, anger, resentment, and all those other emotions that accompany a breakup in the near term. As a result, you may never learn and heal properly from this previous relationship.

Now, getting rid of anything that's been a significant part of your life can initially seem scary. You may be tempted to hold on to keepsakes and the memories so that all the time you spent with your ex doesn't seem to be a waste. But tell yourself that it wasn't a waste, because the true keepsakes from previous relationships are the experience, lessons, and wisdom gained.

It may feel easier to gradually rid yourself of the cobwebs. That may work if you establish a clear end point and stay on schedule. The risk, otherwise, is procrastinating and stretching out the cobwebbing process to infinity and beyond.

Therefore, a better alternative may be choosing a single purge day to sweep away everything. While doing so, avoid examining or reading through anything too much—unless it contains something like key crypto account information or the map to help Odysseus return home to Ithaca. Otherwise, you may end up reminiscing and becoming too attached to the items.

If all of this is still too hard, then consider enlisting the aid of someone else, like a friend or a professional. Someone else may be more objective and, thus, more ruthless about tossing things. A friend can tell you stuff like, "Do you really need that banana slicer?"

Once you clear all the cobwebs, you may be pleasantly surprised at how clear your mind and heart feel as a result. Yes, it can be dangerous to stay on the web, so to speak, and remain haunted by an ex. Because it's only after your headroom and heartroom are truly clear of cobwebs that you can clear the way for someone new to move in—someone who may be a better fit for you.

#3572. 2,500-Year-Old Mesoamerican Observatory Discovered in Sierra Norte Mountains of Mexico

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A team of researchers at the National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH) in Mexico recently announced the discovery of a 2,500-year-old structure that they believe functioned as a solar observatory.

The structure is part of the pre-Hispanic Teteles de Ávila Castillo archaeological site in Sierra Norte de Puebla and has been under excavation by INAH since 2015. Alberto Diez Barroso Repizo, Teteles’s archaeological project director, told the Art Newspaper that Teteles “flourished during the Middle Preclassic period (900BC-600BC) but declined around AD100, possibly because nearby Cantona expanded.” Diez points to the discovery of green obsidian beads as an indication that Teteles, which is along a trade route, was connected to the much larger Mesoamerican city Teotihuacan, since that city controlled a quarry where the obsidian originated. Related Articles Turkish Authorities Seize 2000-Year-Old Marble Head of Zeus Mystery Skeleton in Roman Sarcophagus Beneath Westminster Abbey May Be a Medieval Abbot, Study Says

The discovery indicates that Teteles’s architects and inhabitants had a sophisticated knowledge of astronomy: the observatory was designed to track the beginning of the Mesoamerican calendar in February and the harvest in October, according to a statement from INAH. One of the project’s researchers noted that the eastern base of the complex “constitutes a solar calendar,” based on observations of the trajectory of the sun aligning with the front of the structure during the beginning of equinoxes and solstices.

Diez told TAN that INAH plans to open the site, and eventually a museum, to the public.

Claudia Curiel de Icaza, Mexico’s Secretary of Culture, noted that the discovery expands the country’s understanding of its own history. “The architecture of Teteles de Ávila Castillo tells us about societies that developed astronomical knowledge and incorporated it into the organization of their ceremonial spaces,” she said.

#3581. A Painting Owned by Dutch Jewish Collector Once Hung in Hitler’s Home, New Research Finds

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A Nazi-looted painting that once belonged to a Dutch Jewish family, and at one point hung in Adolf Hitler’s home, has been traced in new research, the Dutch newspaper Het Parool reports.

The work in question is German painter Carl Gottlieb Peschel’s Joseph Sold by His Brothers (1831), which shows the biblical scene of Joseph being sold into slavery in Egypt.

The painting belonged to Bob Nijkerk, whose family had made its fortune in the iron and scrap-metal business in the 19th century. Nijkerk, who died in 1987, used that wealth to build a collection of bibliophile editions, modern Dutch typography, and paintings. He received the Peschel as a gift from his brother-in-law in 1941. When the Nazis occupied Amsterdam in 1940, he was a managing director of the family company, which the Nazis seized in 1941. Related Articles California Attorney General Argues for Return of Nazi-Looted Painting Held by Madrid Museum Nazi-Looted Monet Painting Held by FBI Expected to Be Returned to Owner's Descendants

Starting in 1942, the Nazis deported more than 100,000 Dutch Jews to killing centers. Nijkerk, his wife, and their three children were spared at first. Willem Sandberg, a curator at Amsterdam’s Stedelijk Museum, where Nijkerk had loaned and donated artworks, intervened on their behalf and got them placed on an exception list of about 700 Jews. In 1940 the two men visited the museum’s art bunker together, and the collector likely stored important works there.

The family was first taken to De Schaffelaar Castle, about 45 minutes outside Amsterdam. They were later deported to the Westerbork camp in the Netherlands and then to Theresienstadt in what is now the Czech Republic. “Ultimately, all five survived the war because they were brought to Switzerland by train in 1945 along with a select group of camp prisoners from Theresienstadt,” Het Parool reports.Nijkerk’s grandson Willem Nijkerk knew that his family had included bibliophiles but didn’t know how large his grandfather’s collection was. He learned of the family’s link to the painting from Diederik Oostdijk, a professor of English and American literature at the Vrije Universiteit in Amsterdam.

“I mainly knew about the period during and after the war. Through Oostdijk’s research, I was given a glimpse into their prewar life. They lived in relatively grand luxury,” Willem told Het Parool.

Oostdijk tells the Nijkerk family’s story in his new book, Overlevingskunst (The Art of Survival). According to its description, the book follows “three Jewish families [who] miraculously escaped the Holocaust thanks to their art collections.”

Oostdijk’s research shows that the Peschel spent some time in Hitler’s Berlin home. It was then moved to Hitler’s storage facility in Munich, where prized works were held for a planned Führermuseum.

It is still unclear whether Bob Nijkerk sold the painting in late 1941 or early 1942, or whether it was confiscated later, when German forces or their Dutch collaborators raided Jewish homes. After the war, Nijkerk recovered many of his books but “never uttered a word about Peschel’s painting,” Willem said. “I don’t think he had much of a connection to the painting himself. The painting does not evoke much emotion in me either.”

Nijkerk’s seven descendants have not filed a formal restitution claim for the Peschel. They also have not claimed three other paintings that Oostdijk has linked to the Nijkerk collection: two 1633 portraits by Caspar Netscher and a 1673 painting by Gerbrand van den Eeckhout. Those three works are at the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam. Joseph Sold by His Brothers is now in the Martin von Wagner Museum in Würzburg, Germany. Willem said that if the paintings are ever restituted, the family would likely sell them.

“It is a bizarre thought that a painting belonging to my grandparents was in Hitler’s residence, one of the most horrific human beings who ever lived, and to think that he found this work so beautiful,” Willem told Het Parool. “I don’t necessarily want Peschel’s painting on my wall at home. I also find it an unpleasant thought that it lay in Hitler’s home.”

#4370. Discord will let public server messages be discoverable in Google Search

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Finding good Discord servers relevant to your interests can be tough unless you sift through Discord's own database or get an invite to one.

The popular messaging and chat service is looking to solve that problem with an obvious yet big change to the platform. Discord will now make public servers discoverable through Google Search.

On Tuesday, Discord announced that starting Nov. 16, 2026, all Discoverable Servers will receive a Server Web Page. These public Server Web Pages will include summaries of discussions happening in that public server. In addition, starting in mid-December, some messages within these servers will be made publicly accessible on the Server Web Page. There will be no need for visitors to join the server or even have a Discord account in order to see these messages.

On Discord, users can join public servers or private servers, which require an invite. Public servers can also be Discoverable Servers, meaning they are listed in Discord's searchable database of 63,000 Discoverable Servers, which anyone can join.

However, Discover servers, even Discoverable Servers, are not listed in Google Search, as servers don't have a public web page for Google to index. While public servers are open to anyone, users must join with their Discord account to see messages and discussions.

There are many reasons for Discord to make this change. In its announcement, the company explained how it will help some of its main user types — indie game developers, fandoms, and content creators — grow their communities and more easily spread their work.

Other community platforms like Reddit have long benefited from search engine traffic as discussions on the platform tend to rank well for various user search queries on Google. Discord could also benefit from this.

However, Discord seems to be aware that some users on the platform have long valued its privacy stance. The company announced that both server owners and users on a server can opt out of making their content searchable by search engines.

Discord server admins can opt out of specific channels starting today, before anything goes live on Nov. 16, 2026. Admins can go to Server Settings > Web Presence on their public server and choose which channels to make searchable. Admins can opt out of every one of their server's channels. If an admin does not opt out, however, all public channels will be added to the Server Web Page when it goes live next month. Admins can also choose to turn off channel summaries on their Server Web Page.

In addition, users can opt out of having their messages displayed on the Server Web Page. Discord users can simply go to User Settings, then Data and Privacy, and then change their Channel Messages settings. Users cannot opt out of having their messages used in Channel Summaries on Server Web Pages. However, usernames and avatars won't be shown next to messages on these public Server Web Pages.

Private servers and DMs will remain completely private on Discord. Users do not need to change any settings for these features.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Kaja Perina

When you get a positive test after years of trying to get pregnant, the joy is usually accompanied by some fear. After so long, your efforts finally worked—and now there’s something that could be taken away.

If that pregnancy, in turn, ends in a loss, there’s a distinct type of grief along with the disappointment and sadness, as well as a new kind of fear that compounds on top of the initial fear. If you've been struggling with infertility, getting pregnant is a relief. It finally worked, and you know your body can actually get pregnant. Losing that pregnancy can feel like having no say over what happens, with no guarantee that next time will be different.

Beyond proof your body can get pregnant, it can feel like the hard part is over. It may be the first time in a long time that you've let yourself plan.

If you've been in treatment, there’s often a moment of graduating from the fertility clinic to regular pregnancy care. After a loss, you go back to the clinic, the waiting room and the protocols, and it can feel like starting over from zero.

You may have followed every instruction this time, and it still ended, which can make the loss of control feel even greater.

When we think of grief, it is basically a response to loss. A loss can be tangible, emotional, or even imagined.

A pregnancy loss after infertility is complex because it involves losses at all these levels.

There's the loss of that reproductive story we imagined. Clients usually describe this timeline loss as a disruption in the order of things, at least the order many of us expect.

Then we have losses along the way: the loss of control, identity, belonging, hope, innocence, and the loss of planning.

Infertility and pregnancy loss also involve grieving a future. What makes this grief different is that it’s for something that never happened, something you wanted but never had.

When pregnancy finally happens, especially after fertility treatments, we get to see the light at the end of the tunnel. Unfortunately, 1 in 4 pregnancies end in a loss, which can make that moment very brief (Koert et al., 2023).

A loss can also take away the joy of celebrating when a pregnancy happens again. In session, this is often described as losing the innocence of getting pregnant before knowing that it can be lost.

After one pregnancy loss, the brain learns that loss is very much possible. From then on, it holds both possibilities: a pregnancy that could continue and one that ends in loss. Since this loss can be significant and traumatic, it is now focused on the negative possibilities mostly to keep you safe from future suffering.

When the next pregnancy happens, there's no more of that carefree joy of planning the nursery, choosing baby names, and picking out cribs and newborn onesies. You are now experiencing the mature side of pregnancy after loss, and with the maturity comes the bracing for negative results. Couples who became pregnant again after a loss have described this as never being “pregnant in the same way again” (Koert et al., 2023). Clinicians call this anticipatory grief, when part of you starts grieving a loss before it happens.

Pregnancy after loss brings its own fears, especially after infertility and fertility treatments. Part of what makes it so hard is trying to hold on to hope that this time could end well. I wrote more about this in an earlier post, “The Trauma of Repeated Hope in Infertility.”

Having gone through fertility treatments brings on even more losses, such as loss of a cycle, the money spent, and sometimes the last embryo.

There’s also the time. Each cycle takes weeks of appointments and waiting, and after a loss there’s often another wait before you can try again, while you’re aware of every month that passes.

In a recent study, women going through fertility treatment described the process as an “extended present,” where time moves back and forth between hope and uncertainty (Karaca Biçakçı et al., 2026). They also talked about their bodies becoming the focus of medical attention, and about the pressure they felt from the people around them. The researchers described infertility as a “biographical disruption,” something that changes how we see ourselves and where we fit in with others.

This study looked at women in treatment, and I see the same thing happen after a loss. When a pregnancy ends after fertility treatment, it can pull you right back into that extended present, just when the end finally seemed in sight. And this time, hope can feel harder to reach.

Once you’ve lost a pregnancy after infertility, loss is no longer something that only happens to other people.

It’s understandable to want control over what happens next, especially after everything you’ve done to get here. A lot of the pain comes from the struggle itself, from trying to make sure it never happens again when so much of it was never in your hands.

In therapy, a big part of the work after a loss is letting go of that struggle to be in control. Of course, this is hard to do when so much of trying to conceive has already been about losing control.

When the struggle starts to ease, it often becomes a little easier to hope again, even with the fear still there.
