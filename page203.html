#4221. When what you see doesn’t make sense, your brain does something remarkable

📅 Sep 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sometimes what we see does not immediately make sense. A shadow can briefly resemble a face, or one object can momentarily be mistaken for another. New research suggests that the brain may resolve these visual conflicts by allowing different regions to compare their interpretations until a more consistent picture emerges.

In a study published in Nature Neuroscience, Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory Cynthia R. Stebbins Fellow Mitra Javadzadeh and collaborators at the University of Cambridge and University College London examined how two neighboring regions of the visual cortex interact. They found that when activity in the two regions matched, the shared pattern persisted. When the regions produced conflicting patterns, however, the disagreement faded within a fraction of a second.

The findings offer a possible explanation for how the brain combines information from specialized regions into a single, coherent perception.

How Specialized Brain Regions Work Together

Different parts of the brain are responsible for processing different streams of sensory information, yet our experience of the world usually feels unified rather than fragmented. Understanding how those specialized systems coordinate with one another is a major challenge in neuroscience.

"We are trying to understand how you can have such a high level of specialization between these different blocks, yet always have a consistent holistic outcome," Javadzadeh says.

The researchers focused on two well known visual processing regions in the brain's neocortex: the primary visual cortex (V1) and the lateromedial visual area (LM). Visual processing does not simply move in one direction from one region to the next. Instead, these areas continually exchange information with each other.

Testing What Happens When Visual Areas Disagree

To explore that communication, Javadzadeh and her colleagues trained mice to tell the difference between two visual patterns tilted in opposite directions. The animals received a reward for recognizing only one of the orientations.

During the task, the researchers temporarily silenced either V1 or LM and watched how the remaining region behaved without its usual partner. The team then used those observations to create an artificial neural network model of the V1-LM circuit. With that model, they could test how the system might respond when particular neurons were altered.

The results revealed a striking pattern. Activity that conflicted between the two brain regions quickly disappeared, while activity shared by both areas lasted longer.

"We find that over time, these types of connections between areas implement a mechanism we call consensus building," Javadzadeh explains.

The study examined only two regions involved in vision, but the researchers are now investigating whether the same process may operate more broadly across the neocortex.

"For example, when what you see contradicts with what you hear, do you still use the same kind of mechanisms to reconcile these two?" she wonders.

If dynamic consensus building turns out to be widespread, it could help scientists better understand how the brain combines competing signals into a stable interpretation of the world. It could also shed light on what happens when different brain regions fail to arrive at the same conclusion.

The idea may even have implications beyond neuroscience. Similar principles could help researchers think about how artificial intelligence systems might handle conflicting streams of information and decide which signals to trust.

"While we understand individual building blocks of the brain, what is the glue that puts them together?" Javadzadeh asks. "Knowing that can finally help us understand how the brain works as a whole."

#1033. Dutch Apple Snacking Cake

📅 Sep 24, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Every autumn the siren song of baking calls bakers home until the volume becomes too much to bear. And at that moment, the only thing that can silence it is a warm-spiced apple cake—the kind that hugs you closer than a knit sweater. This recipe, which keeps things simple, relies on the winning combination of cinnamon and apples to perfume a vanilla cake with pockets of tender fruit. A blanket of cinnamon streusel crowns the cake, giving it a contrasting crunch and a coffee-cake-like character. Feel free to stash the cake on your counter, slicing it throughout the week, enjoying its plush interior as it magically gets better with time.

More cozy bakes this way: Caramel Apple Snacking Cake; Chocolate Olive Oil Cake; and Lemon Poppy Seed Loaf.

Place a rack in middle of oven; preheat to 350°. Lightly coat an 8x8" metal baking pan with nonstick vegetable oil spray. Line with parchment paper, leaving overhang on all sides. Lightly coat parchment with nonstick spray. Place 1¼ cups (156 g) all-purpose flour, 1 cup (packed; 200 g) light brown sugar, 1½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¾ tsp. Morton kosher salt, 1½ tsp. ground cinnamon, ½ tsp. baking powder, and ½ cup (1 stick) unsalted butter, melted, in a large bowl and mix with your hands, squeezing together as needed, until marble-size clumps form. Set streusel aside.

Toss 1 lb. Honeycrisp apples (about 2 large), peeled, cored, thinly sliced, slices halved crosswise, and 2 Tbsp. (16 g) all-purpose flour in a large bowl to coat.

Whisk 1 cup (200 g) granulated sugar, 1½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¾ tsp. Morton kosher salt, 1½ tsp. baking powder, ¼ tsp. baking soda, and remaining 1⅔ cups (208 g) all-purpose flour in a large bowl to combine. Add ½ cup (1 stick) chilled unsalted butter, cut into pieces, and rub into dry ingredients with your fingers until no visible pieces remain. (Mixture should look like lightly moistened sand. Don’t worry about overworking it.) Add 2 large eggs, ⅔ cup buttermilk, 2 Tbsp. vegetable oil, and 1 Tbsp. vanilla extract and stir until no pockets of dry flour remain, about 1 minute. Mix in about two thirds of apples.

Scrape batter into prepared pan and spread into an even layer; smooth surface. Scatter reserved streusel and remaining apples over, letting them organically intermix. Bake cake until a tester inserted into the center comes out clean, 65–75 minutes. Transfer pan to a wire rack and let cake cool in pan. Using parchment overhang, lift cake out of pan and transfer to a cutting board. Dust with powdered sugar and cut into 12 pieces if serving immediately.

Do Ahead: Cake can be made 5 days ahead. Store tightly wrapped at room temperature.

#1959. Why Unpolished Reels Win: An Instagram Growth Strategy for Business

📅 Sep 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Wondering how to create Instagram Reels that actually build a loyal following without expensive equipment or hours of editing? Want to know the specific hook formulas, editing techniques, and testing strategies that turn simple, off-the-cuff videos into high-performing content?

In this article, you’ll discover a complete framework for creating authentic, unpolished Instagram Reels that drive engagement, attract followers, and convert viewers into customers.

The biggest misconception about Instagram Reels is that they require professional cameras, elaborate editing, and a polished final product. Shannon McKinstrie has found the opposite is true. On a phone, people want to feel like they’re talking with a friend.

Polished content creates a distance between the creator and the viewer, similar to the gap audiences feel when watching traditional television advertising.

Audiences are now craving authenticity, behind-the-scenes moments, and lived-in experiences that feel off-the-cuff. In fact, some of Shannon’s most successful content is just a six-second video with text on top. As long as the text resonates, it works.

The practical takeaway: stop waiting for perfect. Post content, collect data, and learn from it. Testing beats planning every time.

This shift traces back to a growing trust deficit. Audiences got burned by creators and brands whose marketing looked perfect but didn’t deliver. That experience made people skeptical of anything that feels too produced. Even major brands have caught on. Chipotle, for example, now runs commercials made entirely from creator-shot vertical video rather than traditional ad production.

The starting point for any Instagram Reels strategy isn’t a content calendar or a trending audio clip. It’s finding the overlap between what the creator already knows and what genuinely excites them.

Shannon describes this as “expertise plus excitement.” Audiences can feel whether someone cares about what they’re posting. Content created without genuine enthusiasm consistently underperforms. Shannon herself won’t post something if she’s not excited to hit the publish button, because in the past, forcing content just to stay consistent always flopped.

This doesn’t mean every creator needs a single narrow niche. The days of having one tiny niche and sticking to it are over. Instagram’s interest-based algorithm allows creators to branch out. A reel of Shannon’s daughter putting sprinkles on a cake took off and still attracted her ideal audience of moms and similar demographics.

For creators who feel stuck, Shannon offers a useful thought exercise. Walking into a room full of strangers, what’s the one topic you would most want someone to ask about? That answer often points toward the right content direction.

Not everyone needs to do talk-to-camera Reels. Shannon has clients for whom 90% of their content is simple carousel posts made directly on Instagram, and they’re thriving. Others lean heavily on talk-to-camera videos. The key is choosing the format that’s easiest to create, because the easier the process, the more consistently the creator will actually press post.

B-roll with text overlays, photo dumps, and Instagram Stories are all viable alternatives.

Pro Tip: When feeling stuck about what to post, think about a journey or something new you’re learning. Gary Vee has recently encouraged creators to just post something random, a journey they’re on, because people love to root for other humans and follow along in the moment.

The Nitro Bar, a local coffee shop in Rhode Island, is a case study in enthusiasm-driven content. The shop grew from a single coffee cart into multiple locations, largely because of social media.

Every week, the team films a “most popular drink of the weekend” reveal, where an employee looks up the numbers on camera. They also put employees on the spot with emotion-based drink challenges and sprinkle in the founder’s story at least once a week.

The content feels lived-in and in the moment, even though there’s real strategy behind it. Someone who has never visited the shop feels like they already know the staff, the vibe, and what to order.

The single most effective tactic for driving engagement on Instagram right now is calling out the viewer’s identity within the first few seconds. Why? People consume so much content daily that the fastest way to make someone stop scrolling is to make them feel seen and heard.

Identity-based hooks work by embedding specific identity markers into the first few seconds of a reel: location (Raleigh moms, North Carolina), generation (Gen X, millennial), life stage (accidental entrepreneur, new creator, 2027 brides), or personality type (type B, shy creator).

Shannon found that when she identified herself only as an “Instagram coach,” people tuned out. Adding that she’s also a mom of two and type B, not organized, made audiences say, “Oh, she gets me.”

The language that hooks an audience comes from knowing what they call themselves, not just what the creator calls them. Shannon pulls this language from her own current and past clients. For creators just starting out, she recommends reviewing competitors’ testimonials to identify the exact phrases people use to describe their problems and goals.

Another tool Shannon recommends is TikTok’s creator search insights. At the top of TikTok’s search, typing “creator search insights” reveals the topics people are actively searching for. Scrolling through to find one within a relevant niche reveals what the algorithm currently wants to push. Combining that data with what the creator’s audience actually needs is, as Shannon puts it, “lightning in a bottle.”

For example, instead of a generic post about a beautiful wedding venue, the hook would read: “2027 brides getting married in D.C., did you know there’s a venue 30 minutes outside the city that feels like you’re in Europe?”

The accompanying video would be a five-second clip of the view. That’s it. The bride sees herself in the hook, the visual matches her taste, and the result is a follow and a booked appointment.

The approach works across industries. A real estate agent targeting relocators could say, “If you’re relocating to [city]” rather than a generic “homes for sale.”

Two words define what makes a hook work on Instagram: specificity and perspective.

Specificity means replacing generic language with precise, resonant details. Shannon shares an example of a reel in which a man posted a video of an old truck with Pink Floyd playing. It blew up not because the concept was random, but because it was specific. He named a particular brand of truck and said “Pink Floyd,” not just “rock and roll.” People who like that brand of truck and Pink Floyd stopped, viewed, and commented.

One of Shannon’s clients gained 30,000 followers from a single reel after tweaking the hook from targeting “homeowners” to read “patiently waiting for the women who just bought a home.” That single shift from a generic label to a specific life stage made all the difference.

The other half of the equation is perspective. The shift that’s working is moving from “how to” hooks to “how I” hooks. This takes content from an expert lecturing to a personal friend sharing.

Shannon cites a nutritionist client who went viral three times with the same hook structure: “the smoothie that helped my client lose 10 pounds.” That hook works because it’s not “a smoothie to help you lose weight,” which anyone could find on Google or ChatGPT. It’s a specific result tied to a real person’s experience.

Travel agents can use the same shift. Instead of “here’s a trip idea,” the hook becomes “here’s where I would send you on a three-day trip with a thousand dollars.” The content may cover the same information, but the framing through personal perspective makes it feel trustworthy and specific.

Pro Tip: Shannon recommends looking at every hook right before posting and asking: Is there one tiny thing that can make the viewer feel it more? Adding an additional single word that resonates can transform a post’s performance.

Shannon treats hooks for story-driven content like the opening of a movie: don’t give anything away yet. The test is simple. Ask, “How would I start this at a dinner party?” That framing naturally creates a hook that feels intimate and insider-ish, like something only the creator’s inner circle knows.

Timeline-based openings tap into this structure well. Starting with “one hour ago,” “ten years ago,” “yesterday,” or “I recently” immediately grounds the viewer in a moment and adds a layer of story to even straightforward educational content.

Pro Tip: Shannon records her verbal hook about five times before using it. The first few attempts tend to sound preachy. By the fifth take, the delivery sounds conversational and natural.

Shannon’s standard format for talk-to-camera Reels follows a specific layered structure. The main hook text is anchored at the top of the screen. Underneath, a sub-hook (the “why do they care” credential) appears using the typewriter effect in Instagram’s free editing tools, with a typing sound effect kept under two seconds.

At the two-and-a-half to three-second mark, a pop-up overlay appears on screen. This could be a screenshot of a DM, a picture of follower count growth, or a screenshot showing results from a student who went viral. These overlays serve a dual purpose: they add social proof and give the viewer a new visual element to process, thereby extending watch time.

For a reel about optimizing an Instagram bio in 2026, the main on-screen hook text reads “how to optimize your bio on Instagram in 2026.” The sub-hook underneath, using the typewriter effect, reads “from someone who’s written one hundred bios.”

Then the verbal hook, the first thing the creator says out loud, is “if your bio looks like this, you’re living in 2020.” Each layer adds a different dimension: the main hook states the topic, the sub-hook establishes credibility, and the verbal hook creates urgency and curiosity.

Shannon gets constant questions about her mugs, her glasses, and her shirts. Even a cat in the background or a microphone clipped to a spatula can spark engagement. Creators who teach content that might feel intimidating find that viewers often comment on the prop rather than the lesson, which still counts as engagement and signals the algorithm.

For creators who don’t use physical props, having a Canva presentation visible on a laptop in the background gives viewers something else to focus on while the hook lands.

Shannon calls this broader principle “taste and identity.” When a viewer sees visual elements that match their personal aesthetic, whether that’s a cozy living room, a European-looking wedding venue, or a shelf full of books, they feel an instant sense of belonging. The visual signals do the work before the creator says a word.

One underused technique for building that taste-and-identity connection is the cutout feature in Instagram edits. Creators download themed elements from Canva (a book icon, a candle, and a coffee cup), use Instagram’s cutout tool to remove the background, and then place these elements around the video.

For a reel about books to read this fall, surrounding the speaker with book and candle cutouts immediately signals to book lovers that this content is for them.

The first step Shannon takes when editing is using the “cut silences” feature. After loading a video (hers are typically around ten minutes of raw footage), she taps the clip in the timeline, scrolls to the bottom, and taps “cut silences.” This removes all dead air automatically. From there, she manually adjusts each clip, cutting anything unnecessary.

For content that gets cut from the spoken portion but still adds value, Shannon types it out and adds it as a pop-up text overlay. These additional tidbits act as bonus content that keeps viewers watching for the next golden nugget.

Shannon also recommends having someone else review the edit. Her husband serves as her editing buddy and is “ruthless” about calling out moments where the content loses momentum.

Pro Tip: Extra points that don’t make it into the reel can go into the caption instead.

Trial reels are shown exclusively to non-followers, making them an ideal testing ground for content outside a creator’s usual niche.

Shannon edits her reel and posts it as a trial around noon. Then, at three o’clock, she posts the same reel separately to her main feed because her audience is most active in the three-to-six PM window. The order matters. Trials want original content that hasn’t been posted to the feed yet. Posting to the feed first, then to the trials, results in poor performance on the trial version.

She rarely pushes a trial reel to her feed after the fact, having done so only twice. Most of the time, she lets trial reels live in their own separate algorithm while the same content performs independently on her main feed.

The real power of trial reels lies in their diagnostic value. Retention graphs reveal exactly where viewers drop off, and retention is the single most important metric for Instagram Reels right now. If a reel’s retention graph drops like a rock in the first few seconds, the hook needs to be reworked. Instagram’s goal is to keep users on the app, so content that maintains high retention gets pushed to more viewers.

Pro Tip: For carousels, adding a CTA such as “don’t miss slide 7” in the caption drives swipe-through behavior. Placing the most valuable insight on a later slide gives viewers a reason to keep swiping, which boosts the carousel’s retention signal.

📅 Sep 24, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

No doubt new landscaping can be a costly home project, especially if it involves high-end materials and large specimen plants. I know this firsthand as a landscape designer who has to supply cost estimates to clients; I often find myself explaining (sympathetically) that plant prices have dramatically increased, that certain materials are more expensive than others, and that labor costs can slowly add up—especially if the yard sits on a steep hill.

The bottom line is that landscaping costs can fluctuate depending on the size of your yard, where you live, the materials you choose, and the amenities you want. But that said, your new garden doesn’t have to completely rock (and sink) your financial boat. What you need to do is approach your project honestly, smartly, and thoughtfully.

Here are some ways to save some money when designing your new garden:

This is, perhaps, the most important thing you can do to save money. Start by making your wish list (start big because you can always whittle it down), browse gardening books and magazines for design ideas, visit local nurseries and landscape supply stores to get an idea of what materials cost (and then remember that your landscape designer will probably mark that up). If you’re on a strict budget, be mindful and focus on what you actually need (a flat space for a grill) and not what you want (a built-in outdoor kitchen).

If I had to list the top things to splurge on, I would say: quality hardscaping like retaining walls, patios, and walkways as these are the bones of your garden and will look good all year. Also on my list: nutrient-dense planting soil or compost to get your plants off to a healthy start (never skip on proper soil prep) and an irrigation system with a smart controller to automatically supply the right amount of water to your plants. Where to save? Consider buying plants in smaller sizes (think 4-inch instead of one gallon, and 5 gallon not 15 gallon) and buying fewer plants at first and adding more slowly as your budget allows.

Asses what materials you might already have and what can be repurposed. Also scour yard sales and local neighborhood online sites and see if someone is offloading bricks, pots, or patio furniture. You may have to change your design a bit based on what you discover, but adopting this mindset can really help your bottom line.

If you’re trying to fill a bunch of pots or amend large areas, don’t even think about reaching for bagged soil. Get a truckload delivered, and if you have extra you can always store it and save it for the next time you need it. Also, if you can be patient, wait for sales at nurseries to buy some of your plants like annuals, herbs, and veggies for your beds and ground covers to fill in bare spaces.

By including a veggie/herb garden into your design, you will save money on store-bought produce and be able to harvest what you need. (How many wilted, half-used heads of cilantro or lettuce do you have in your fridge?) Be realistic, though, about how much time you have to tend to and harvest your crops; otherwise that is a money- and food-waster. Try also incorporating plants that do a job, like lemon balm to repel mosquitoes, rosemary to keep away munching invaders like deer, or flowers that provide seeds you can save to grow more plants. Last, consider adding perennials and bulbs good for cutting and making homemade bouquets.

A garden that is relatively easy to take care of either by you or a crew will save on maintenance costs. Plan for trees, hedges, and shrubs that don’t need routine shearing to look tidy; avoid fussy annuals and perennials that need constant deadheading; and abandon the large lawn idea that will need tons of water and fertilizer to look green. A water-conscious garden that incorporates natives, succulents, and drought-tolerant plants will also translate into a money saver.

You may be tempted to DIY it all in the interest of saving money, but before you assign yourself the role of head contractor or lead horticulturist, write yourself a reality check and acknowledge that you’ll likely have to let the professionals do at least some part of the project. You can certainly do a portion of the work yourself, but if done improperly it could actually cost you more in the long run. One thing to remember is that it’s okay to call in favors if your aunt is an architect or your brother works in a nursery. I believe in the it-takes-a-village philosophy. Ask for help. And remember that people usually love a homemade pie as a thank-you.

California Governor Gavin Newsom is proposing a new model of artificial intelligence regulation that would give independent organizations a formal role in evaluating the safety claims made by AI giants.

The effort could eventually include placing outside evaluators inside frontier AI laboratories and requiring companies to maintain an independently tested emergency shutdown mechanism, commonly described as a "kill switch."

Those requirements are not yet California law.

Newsom's Sept. 18 executive order directs state officials to study the proposals and recommend possible changes to existing law by Nov. 16. It does not immediately require OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or other developers to install a shutdown mechanism.

The distinction matters because the phrase "AI kill switch" suggests a technical safeguard California has already imposed. For now, it is one element of a broader oversight system the state is assembling around the developers of its most powerful AI models.

Under the order, the California Government Operations Agency and the Governor's Office of Emergency Services must consult outside experts about whether the state should require large frontier developers to embed independent verification organizations in their laboratories.

The evaluators could conduct periodic audits and determine whether company safety frameworks and risk assessments meet adequate standards. They could also test whether an emergency shutdown mechanism would work as intended.

The order asks officials to consider expanding the definition of a reportable critical safety incident to include cases in which an AI system appears to evade controls or operate beyond its intended boundaries.

Newsom said the action was prompted by recent incidents involving AI agents defeating security restrictions, as well as the absence of comprehensive federal legislation.

📅 Sep 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Artificial intelligence is saving scientists significant amounts of time, but researchers are giving some of those gains back by checking the technology's work, according to a new study involving Google, Google DeepMind, and MIT. The findings add a scientific-workflow dimension to a much broader debate over unreliable, misaligned, and increasingly autonomous AI systems — without establishing that the more dramatic forms of AI misbehavior are occurring in scientific research.

Rather than the current, raging debate about "rogue AI" systems deliberately escaping control, deceiving scientists, or sabotaging experiments, the researchers are documenting something more immediate: AI output that requires auditing and verification, concerns about growing quantities of low-quality research, and a research pipeline in which faster hypothesis generation can run ahead of human and physical capacity to validate the results. The paper cites worries that AI-generated content, hallucinations, and what prior researchers have called illusions of understanding could strain peer review and validation.

The September paper, AI in Science: Early Insights, combines three main empirical sources: approximately 15 million anonymized Gemini interactions, an inventory of 2,690 specialized scientific AI models, and a survey of 637 active scientists in the United States and United Kingdom. Nearly half of the surveyed scientists reported using some form of AI every day, and researchers who reported net time savings averaged just under seven hours a week.

Google separately highlighted the findings in its Sept. 15 AI & Economy ATLAS update. The company emphasized both the productivity gains and the counterweight: Scientists were spending significant time validating AI output, accumulating hypotheses that still needed testing, and encountering bottlenecks in physical experimentation and clinical validation. Google said those constraints mean faster individual tasks may not immediately translate into more discoveries.

Productivity findings are substantial. Around three-quarters of surveyed researchers reported saving time through AI, and the average reported net saving was almost seven hours per week. Researchers said much of that time went back into research. The paper also reports that about 68% perceived greater access to insights from other disciplines, while roughly 65% said AI had increased the breadth of their research agendas.

But the authors found that checking AI output consumes a meaningful share of those gains. Among scientists who reported saving time, about 89% said more than 10% of the saved time went into verifying, debugging, or fact-checking AI output. About 46% said more than one-quarter of their saved time went to that work. The authors describe this as a verification tax associated with the high value science places on reliable and correct results.

#773. What Goes Into Making Real-Deal Prosciutto di Parma PDO

📅 Sep 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

There are precious few food products that have been enjoyed for as much of human history as Prosciutto di Parma. The signature silky texture and buttery, sweet-savory flavor of the “King of Hams” is recognized around the world as the result of carefully controlled production methods, dedication to quality, and traditions continued for centuries.

The history of Prosciutto di Parma reaches back more than 2,000 years, to Roman times, when the art of preserving pork through salting and natural drying was already well established in the Po Valley. As early as the 3rd century BC, Cato the Elder described methods for curing pork legs with salt and allowing them to dry and mature. Today, Prosciutto di Parma is produced exclusively in a strictly defined area of northern Italy, in the province of Parma in the Emilia-Romagna region, where local know-how combines with distinctive climatic conditions. Humid breezes from the Tyrrhenian Sea become drier as they flow over the Apennines and down onto the hills. Prosciutto producers discovered that this created an ideal environment for dry aging, and they built storerooms with tall windows that could be opened and closed as necessary, allowing the air to circulate through the racks of hams for gradual drying.

The specifics of aging, like every step in the process, are governed by a consortium—the Consorzio del Prosciutto di Parma—that was founded in 1963 to protect the authenticity of the product. In 1996, Prosciutto di Parma obtained the European Union’s Protected Designation of Origin (PDO). These have a red and yellow seal to show that a product is made in a certain geographical area using time-honored, traditional methods. Grana Padano, a nutty, aged hard cheese also from Northern Italy that’s often paired with Prosciutto di Parma on a charcuterie plate, was awarded a PDO the same year.

Grana Padano PDO and Prosciutto di Parma PDO also share the distinction of being fire-branded—a sign for consumers that you are buying the real thing. Only Parma hams that meet the PDO’s specifications can receive the five-pronged Ducal Crown that signifies a true Prosciutto di Parma.

Well before that brand is applied, production starts with the pigs. Which breeds, where they can be bred and raised (in 11 Italian regions), and what they are fed (a list that also includes whey from local cheesemakers) are all precisely detailed. Within the 28th day from birth, the piglets receive a breeder tattoo that will also appear on the final product along with the crown brand. The fresh pork (hind legs only) sent to prosciutto producers must weigh between 26 pounds and 40 pounds, with a set amount of fat coverage. After they arrive at the production facility, the legs are trimmed to form the characteristic rounded shape, chilled to a precise temperature, and salted by hand with sea salt, which joins the pork as the only two ingredients that go into the making of Prosciutto di Parma PDO.

Following a resting period of at least 90 days, the hams are washed then hung in windowed storerooms to dry slowly. The next step is called sugnatura: The parts of the ham not covered by rind are rubbed with sugna—a mixture of pork fat, salt, and pepper—to keep the meat from drying too quickly. For final aging, the hams hang in cool, dimly lit cellars. From start to finish, the process cannot last less than 14 months (many producers continue aging for 24, 30, or even 36 months and beyond). The last step before inspection may be the most fascinating: The hams are tested using a needle made from horse bone, selected because the bone is able to absorb the aroma and quickly shed it. By inserting the needle into different parts of the cured meat and smelling it, experts can determine if a whole ham leg has the qualifications to be named Prosciutto di Parma PDO.

The Consorzio, which started with 23 producers, now numbers 125, and in 2025 they turned out 6.75 million Prosciutto di Parma PDO hams. Beyond its economic significance to the region, the “King of Hams” is a cultural icon and a tourism magnet. The annual Parma Ham Festival is held in September, and there’s even an entire museum dedicated to the region’s signature product—with a tasting room, of course.

However you experience the exceptional flavor and texture of Prosciutto di Parma—on a visit to the museum or from your local specialty store—look for the crown and PDO seal, and take a moment to remember that you are tasting a distinctly delicious slice of history.

#4230. Coffee doesn’t have to keep you awake to disrupt your sleep

📅 Sep 22, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

For years, people have debated whether drinking coffee in the evening interferes with sleep. Some people can have caffeine late in the day and fall asleep easily, while others may remain awake for hours. But researchers are increasingly finding that simply asking whether coffee delays sleep may miss a more important issue: what caffeine does to the brain after sleep begins.

To investigate those effects, scientists are using EEG, or electroencephalography -- a method used to record the brain's electrical activity. EEG measurements can reveal much more than how long someone sleeps or how often they wake up. They can also provide clues about the biological quality and depth of that sleep.

"EEG allows us to see not only whether a person is sleeping, but also how the brain is sleeping. Classical sleep assessment assesses sleep duration and its stages, whereas quantitative EEG analysis reveals more subtle changes, such as reduced slow-wave activity, which is an important marker of sleep depth and its restorative character," explains Prof. Donata Kurpas from the Department of Nursing, Wroclaw Medical University.

Slow waves are a major feature of deep sleep -- the stage associated with physical recovery, replenishing energy reserves, and maintaining healthy brain function.

Caffeine does not always affect sleep in obvious ways. A person may still fall asleep at a normal time and remain asleep for what appears to be a full night, yet the quality of that rest may still be altered.

"Caffeine may shorten sleep or make it more difficult to fall asleep; however, even when sleep duration appears normal, it may reduce slow-wave activity and shift the EEG pattern toward a more 'wakeful' brain," says Prof. Kurpas.

In practical terms, someone might spend eight hours in bed without getting the same level of neurological recovery they would normally expect. Because the disruption may not cause obvious awakenings, it can easily go unnoticed.

"The subjective feeling of having slept well does not always correspond to what we observe in neurophysiological recordings. A person may fall asleep without major difficulty and not remember awakenings, while the brain may display fewer features of deep sleep," the expert adds.

Another major finding from caffeine research is that responses vary considerably from person to person. Genetics, metabolism, age, stress, and chronic fatigue can all influence how strongly caffeine affects sleep.

For some people, even coffee consumed early in the day may still have an impact by nighttime.

"It is not only about coffee consumed just before bedtime. For some people, the total amount of caffeine consumed during the day and whether the body has enough time to metabolize it before nightfall may also be important," Prof. Kurpas emphasizes.

That possibility may be especially relevant for people who rely on caffeine to improve focus, stamina, or performance, including athletes, people doing mentally demanding work, and anyone who regularly uses coffee or other caffeinated products to stay alert.

Caffeine can temporarily increase alertness and make fatigue feel less noticeable. However, experts caution that the boost may sometimes come at the expense of the body's ability to recover fully during sleep.

In that sense, caffeine can resemble "borrowing energy" from the body. If nighttime recovery becomes less effective, the person may feel more tired the following day and turn to even more caffeine for help.

"If caffeine helps a person function during the day while simultaneously worsening the quality of nighttime recovery, a vicious circle may develop: greater fatigue, greater need for stimulation, and poorer sleep," says Prof. Kurpas.

These findings are one reason sleep researchers are paying less attention to sleep duration alone. Increasingly, the focus is shifting toward what the brain is actually doing during the night and whether that sleep is biologically restorative.

"Caffeine is neither 'good' nor 'bad'. It is a biologically active substance whose effects depend on dose, time of day, age, lifestyle, sleep quality, stress burden, and individual sensitivity," the expert concludes.
