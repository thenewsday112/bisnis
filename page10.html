#163. More than half of Victorian lawyers use AI – but only a quarter have cut client fees as a result

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Research finds 55% of lawyers are using AI in legal practices, up from 37% last year, though efficiency gains aren’t as high as expected

Get our breaking news email, free app or daily news podcast

More than half of Victoria’s lawyers have reported using AI in their work, according to a new study, but just over a quarter say they have reduced fees as a result of efficiency gains.

They also say clients seeking advice from chatbots have added to their workload, forcing them to correct errors made by AI.

The 2026 census of 2,221 of the state’s lawyers, conducted by the Victorian Legal Services Board and Commissioner’s legal services research centre, found a big increase in lawyers using AI in their practices, rising from 37% to 55% in the last year.

While the 2025 report found AI use was highest among those under 30 years of age, in 2026 this disparity had largely disappeared, although usage remained low for those aged 60 and over.

AI use was most common in trade practices (85%), banking and finance (78%) and information technology and telecommunications (78%), while it was used much less in criminal law (29%), family law (45%) and administrative law (46%).

While there are AI tools designed specifically for legal services, 79% of lawyers reported using consumer chatbots such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Copilot. Legal-specific tools alone were used by 22% of respondents.

The increased use in AI has not translated to a reduction in fees for most, the survey found, with just 26% of lawyers responding that their use of AI had resulted in lower client fees.

Reduced fees were more likely to be reported by those working for business clients (34%) compared with individual fee-paying clients (23%).

Some reported AI had enabled them to take on work that would have previously been uneconomical (18%).

The principal researcher and report co-author, Dr Georgie Rychner, said fee reductions in corporate and big firms could be attributed to the fact clients in those settings were repeat users of legal services.

“They have a high level of legal capability,” she said. “They also are perhaps using AI in their own work, so they have a better understanding and are able to advocate for lower prices … whereas we’re not seeing that as much in the community legal system sector.”

Rychner said the efficiency gains of using AI were not as high as expected, but that in 2025 the survey found 89% of practitioners said they had taken steps to verify the accuracy of AI output, which might have negated the gains.

In 2026 there had been a rise in clients using AI for their own legal research, she said, with 46% of lawyers reporting their clients were arriving with inaccurate or misleading information, and 41% presenting AI-generated documents that required correction.

Nearly half of lawyers said there was an increase in time and effort required if their clients used AI, compared with 19% who said it led to a reduction in time and effort.

“What we are hearing is that clients are more frequently arriving with misleading or inaccurate information about their legal problems, more so than they’re arriving with a better understanding of legal problems,” she said, adding that more research was needed to understand client perspectives.

Courts and legal practitioner bodies across Australia have been grappling with the rise in AI use among lawyers and the public since 2023.

A Victorian lawyer last year was the first in the country to face sanctions for false citations discovered, being stripped of his ability to practise as a principal lawyer. Investigations were also launched by regulatory bodies in Western Australia and New South Wales in similar cases.

The federal court in April issued new rules around using generative AI, while the chief justice of the high court, Stephen Gageler, said last year the use of AI-generated content in legal cases had reached an “unsustainable phase”.

#668. Youth theatre raises more than $68k to carry forward 50-year legacy

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Shopfront Arts Co-Op hosted a youth conference, featuring a silent disco, to celebrate its 50th year. (Supplied: Shopfront/Lucy Parakhina)

Joining Shopfront Arts Co-Op changed Paul Capsis's life.

Fresh out of high school, the writer and performer — known for the 1998 movie Head On — was dragged by a friend to watch a show at the youth theatre in southern Sydney in 1983.

Seeing performers his own age on stage was "remarkable", he recalls.

"It was a magical experience," he says. "I was like, 'What have I been missing out on all these years?'"

After the show, Shopfront's co-founder, director Errol Bray, invited him to get involved in its upcoming production of A Midsummer Night's Dream.

The theatre became a refuge after Capsis's difficult school years.

"I was severely bullied," he says. "You start to think that is how the world is going to be outside."

At Shopfront, Capsis began writing and performing in his own shows, created in collaboration with other young people in the company.

In 1986, Paul Capsis played the mock turtle in Shopfront's adaptation of Alice in Wonderland. (Supplied: Shopfront)

"It was a major thing to be with people that liked me," he says. "We laughed and we performed and we hung out.

The youth theatre that shaped Capsis's life turned 50 this month.

Over the past five decades, it has kick-started the careers of artists including Olivier Award-nominated performer Trevor Ashley, former Sydney Theatre Company artistic director Andrew Upton, and playwright Hilary Bell.

It also continues to empower young people as performers, writers and decision-makers, and has also been acknowledged by the wider industry at the Sydney Theatre Awards.

One of its award-winning shows, The Lies We Were Told, created with Australian Theatre for Young People, was remounted at the Sydney Opera House this month in celebration of the company's 50th anniversary.

The Lies We Were Told won best production for young people at the Sydney Theatre Awards in 2024. (Supplied: Shopfront/Lucy Parakhina)

It followed a youth conference, featuring Heartbreak High star Thomas Weatherall and musician Dobby, and a 50-hour act-a-thon fundraiser, which aimed to raise $50,000 for the theatre. It raised more than $68,000.

The act-a-thon was a fitting tribute to the company's punk past, not only bringing together alumni with the current ensemble, but replicating its successful fundraiser of 1978.

That year, to avoid eviction, the young people of Shopfront resolved to buy the building and raised a $43,000 deposit through an act-a-thon, raffles and government support.

It is a history Shopfront's Young Leader, Liv Tsigaropoulos, who works as a conduit between the theatre's staff and young people, knows well.

When she was in Year 9, she made a mini documentary about the theatre for the National History Challenge, which won her the national Democracy Matters award.

By then, she had already been part of Shopfront for a couple of years, having started when she was 13.

Now 19, she's thrilled to be a custodian of the company during its anniversary year.

"It's the best time to be Young Leader," she says.

"It's such a full-circle thing. I remember reading about the act-a-thon when I was doing my research."

The performance of The Lies We Were Told is part of that full-circle feeling; Tsigaropoulos auditioned for that show when she first joined Shopfront in 2021.

"Since that, I never left, and they could never get rid of me," she says.

Over her six years with the theatre, she has participated in its drama workshops, made short films, learned about technical production, and joined one of its five advisory panels.

“I just do whatever I can; I just love being part of Shopfront,” Liv Tsigaropoulos says.  (Supplied: Shopfront/Joshua Morris)

For Tsigaropoulos, the special thing about being part of the theatre is the lengths it will go to bring a young person's ideas to life.

She recalls how that gave her the "confidence to stand by [her] ideas".

"In a space like Shopfront, you can say a crazy, big, unrealistic or impossible goal, and the Shopfront staff make it happen," she says.

"You're gonna see your words expressed on stage, what you think expressed on stage, and that's so special … People watch it and laugh and cry."

For example, she suggested she start a show stuck outside.

"It was so silly," she says. "I was like, 'I've missed the show.'

"It's small things I think are funny or cool and would want to see. Getting that put in a show is really fulfilling."

Now, it is up to her as Young Leader to help make those things happen for the next generation of young people.

"The only thing more beautiful than [your idea being brought to life] is working at Shopfront, and a young person tells you an idea, and you make it happen," Tsigaropoulos says.

The money from the fundraiser will help Shopfront keep bringing young people's ideas to life at a time when funding for arts companies can be precarious.

It is something Shopfront knows all too well, having lost multi-year funding from the federal government alongside many youth arts companies in 2016, and again in 2021.

Shopfront is currently receiving funding from Creative Australia and Create NSW.

More than 19,000 young people have taken part in Shopfront's programs since 2018. (Supplied: Shopfront/Lucy Parakhina)

The money raised will also help the theatre stay open to everyone, through scholarships and free tickets to performances.

"We never turn young people away if they can't afford it," Tsigaropoulos says.

"We'd rather lose money and let a young person come for free and get to experience Shopfront."

Capsis recalls his own fundraising efforts for Shopfront, standing on a box outside Hurstville Station, singing Janis Joplin.

"We're raising money, we're making shows, and that became my blueprint," he says.

"The big world of professional theatre and musical theatre didn't see me as somebody they wanted, so I made my own work."

It was his training ground, which saw him tour shows to the UK and Canada, and sing on stage for the first time.

"Some people go to NIDA [National Institute of Dramatic Art] and they do three years intensive," Capsis says. "I did Shopfront theatre one night a week and every weekend."

It also helped him make connections, including with Hillary Bell and Julian Meyrick, who collaborated on his award-winning one-man show about his Maltese grandmother, Angela's Kitchen.

For Capsis, what sticks out is how the theatre empowered him and the young people around him.

"I think it's extremely important young people have a place to voice how they feel, and to learn," he says.

Paul Capsis spent five years at Shopfront and went on to become a mainstay of Australian stage and screen. (Getty Images: Don Arnold)

That centring of young people may be what's behind the longevity of Shopfront Arts Co-op, Australia's longest-running and only youth-owned and led theatre company.

"[That longevity is] a testament to all the people who have ever come through Shopfront and the determination, the belief they have in amplifying young people's voices, and that has stayed at its centre since the very beginning," Tsigaropoulos says.

She adds empowering young people remains important, even if they don't want to pursue careers in the arts.

"We're not an acting school pumping out mini DiCaprios and Margot Robbies," Tsigaropoulos says.

#449. US agency votes to ban all Chinese labs from testing US electronics

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sign up now: Get ST's newsletters delivered to your inbox

US Federal Communications Commission Chairman Brendan Carr said that in 2025, 82% of electronics were tested in China, which does not have an agreement with the US.

WASHINGTON - The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) on Oct 29 voted to bar all Chinese labs from testing electronic devices including smartphones, cameras and computers for use in the US, expanding a previous agency action targeting Beijing.

The FCC said it will prohibit test labs and certification bodies in countries including China that deny reciprocal treatment to US-based test labs. The FCC prohibitions are slated to take effect in December 2028. FCC Chair Brendan Carr said that in 2025, 82% of electronics were tested in China, which does not have an agreement with the US.

“These labs are a critical gateway into American communications infrastructure – and their privileged role gives them access to sensitive and confidential information about electronic products often months before the devices enter the market,” Carr said, adding they could “allow insecure gear into the US market, disrupt communications networks, or even steal intellectual property”.

The Chinese Embassy in Washington did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

In 2025, the FCC banned testing of US electronics by labs owned or controlled by the Chinese government, and several dozen labs were barred.

About 4% of all electronic devices are tested in labs inside the US, Carr said.

Separately, the FCC streamlined its approval process for devices tested in US labs or labs from countries not posing national security risks.

This is the latest move by Washington to crack down on China over technology issues and equipment.

In December, the FCC banned the import of all new models of Chinese drones and also has banned imports of new models of Chinese-made consumer routers, the boxes connecting computers, phones and smart devices to the internet.

In July, the FCC voted to bar sales of devices ​in the US that contain key hardware from Chinese companies deemed to ‌be national security risks.

The FCC maintains a list of firms, including Chinese telecom company Huawei and ZTE, whose equipment is barred for sale in the ​US due to national security concerns.

In June, the FCC banned the import of more equipment from a group of Chinese manufacturers including Huawei and ZTE and has proposed to ​ban the import ⁠of most military-grade drones. REUTERS

#459. Bukele's gang offensive goes abroad as El Salvador rushes troops to Haiti

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Sign up now: Get ST's newsletters delivered to your inbox

FILE PHOTO: An armored personnel carrier of Haiti's UN-backed Gang Suppression Force (GSF) patrols an area after El Salvador announced a rescue mission for four air force members in the GSF that went missing after a gang ambush, in Port-au-Prince, Haiti, October 23, 2026. REUTERS/Patrice Noel/File Photo

Oct 29 - El Salvador's deployment of thousands of troops to Haiti, days after four Salvadoran members of a UN-backed force went missing in a gang ambush, is the first major overseas test of President Nayib Bukele's anti-gang tactics.

Around 2,000 Salvadoran soldiers were in Haiti as of Wednesday night, a security official told Reuters. Two sources with knowledge of the matter said these would for now be capped at 2,500. Yet authorities have not clarified the troops' command, or rules of engagement, fueling concerns of more risks to civilians in a country with a long history of harmful foreign interventions.

Bukele billed the deployments as a rescue mission for four aircraft technicians who were part of the UN-backed Gang Suppression Force in Haiti. They have been missing since they jumped from a burning vehicle during the ambush in a gang-controlled area on the outskirts of the capital Port-au-Prince.

Haitian and Salvadoran authorities have said the troops' main mission is to rescue the missing technicians. But the deployment's size - matching in 10 days what the UN-backed force struggled to deploy over more than a year - has prompted questions over whether the soldiers could be part of a broader and longer-term operation.

On Thursday, Bukele said the deployments were nearly finished and his government was covering 90% of the costs.

Bukele, in office since 2019, won huge popularity at home with tough-on-crime policies, including a gang crackdown that relied heavily on mass incarceration. Other governments have sought to replicate his approach, despite rights activists' warnings over due process and innocent deaths.

Bukele would, however, likely encounter a profoundly different security environment in Haiti, analysts warned, as institutions have been whittled down by corruption and faltering funds, prisons are vastly overcrowded, the judiciary paralyzed and many gang recruits are underage.

"This could help increase support for Bukele if this helps his narrative of being an anti-gang force in the region and delivering results," said Maria Fernanda Arocha, an analyst at conflict monitoring group ACLED.

"We don't know if this will bring the results he is expecting."

A CID Gallup poll on Wednesday showed 86% of Salvadorans approved of the Haiti mission, though 82% also expressed concern for their countrymen.

In El Salvador, security analysts said they were cautious of a risky mission without a formal agreement, UN backing, or support from the Shield of the Americas - a US-led anti-crime coalition the Trump administration launched earlier this year.

Haiti's unelected leaders have since 2022 called for international forces to help fight heavily-armed gangs that have expanded their influence beyond the capital and are responsible for massacres, gang rapes and widespread extortion.

But successive UN-backed missions were slow to deploy, leaving a fractured security response. Haiti hired a private military company run by Blackwater founder Erik Prince to fight the gangs, which nonetheless continued to make gains.

Sam Vigersky, a policy expert at the Council on Foreign Relations, said he believed the UN-backed mission - renewed for just six months in September - was under pressure to produce results but the Salvadorans' arrival signaled more chaos.

"The overall security picture is not pointing towards one of stabilization, it's pointing towards one of escalation," he said.

Since the October 18 ambush, the area has been the site of continuous armed clashes. A security official said operations there were largely run by Haitian police.

Romain Le Cour Grandmaison, a security expert at the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime, said residents were skeptical of yet another foreign intervention and had reported heavy gunfire, drone strikes and possible civilian deaths.

He said he was not aware of humanitarian corridors to help people escape: "The gang leaders do not want people to leave the area because they probably want to use them as shields and make sure the population actually slows operations against them."

Washington has not publicly supported the mission but a US military official said Pentagon C-130s helped it deploy.

Irwin Stotzky, a rights expert at the University of Miami, said the US administration, which ended immigration protections for about 350,000 Haitians, has reasons to be invested.

"We've got to make it appear as if it's safe to send 350,000 people back to Haiti, when they actually are in great fear of their lives," he said. REUTERS

#677. The thunderstorm that arrives like clockwork at 3pm every day

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The northern wet season has officially commenced, coinciding with the annual arrival of the Top End's largest resident.

Looking north from Darwin, a marvel of meteorology, one of the largest storm clouds on the planet towers over the Tiwi Islands from about 3pm every day.

It is the world's only individually named thunderstorm, with locals calling it Hector.

World War II pilots labelled Hector, using its dependable location as a navigation beacon when travelling between Darwin and Papua New Guinea.

But you do not need a plane to spot Hector. The thundercloud is clearly visible from Darwin, and that is because he is no ordinary storm.

Hector in all his glory during a past Top End wet season. Note the flat top which is caused by Hector breaching the top of the troposphere. (Supplied: Paul Thomsen, Weather Obsessed Facebook page)

While the storm got the name Hector 80-odd years ago, the Indigenous communities of the Tiwi Islands were well aware of its occurrence with the traditional calendar tracking the changes in the land and sky across three major and 13 minor seasons.

The two major seasons which overlap with Hector are Tiyari and Jamutakari, which run from September to February.

During Jamutakari, pakitiringa (rain) falls consistently every day and the swamps, creeks and rivers are full. There is much pumurali (lightning) and thunder with the rain.

One of the minor seasons also references the arrival of a daily storm. Pumwanyingari is the season of thunder when humidity increases and clouds develop every afternoon.

Hector is a colossus, rising vertically up to 20 kilometres in altitude.

Hector is so tall he soars through the troposphere, the layer of the atmosphere where weather occurs, and punches into the lower stratosphere, a hostile environment that flattens his head.

Containing millions of tonnes of water, Hector is also known as Hector the Convector because he transfers massive amounts of heat and moisture from the warm tropical surface high into the atmosphere.

He can easily drop 50 millimetres of rain in a couple of hours and contributes to the Tiwi Islands being the wettest part of the Northern Territory with up to 2,000mm annually.

Forecasting the exact location and timing of an individual thunderstorm before it forms is one of meteorology's greatest challenges; just ask Doc Brown from Back to the Future.

In fact, it is nearly impossible because the three key ingredients for a storm rarely occur simultaneously in the same place each day.

So how does Hector overcome weather's chaotic, inherently unpredictable nature?

Hector on October 23 this year viewed from Darwin, as he awakens for the season. (ABC)

Hector's existence results from the unique geography of the Tiwi Islands.

The first ingredient for a storm is moisture, and since the Tiwi Islands are surrounded by warm tropical seas, water vapour is abundant.

Ingredient two is instability, which utilises the property of physics that warm air rises.

The largest Tiwi is Melville Island, the second-largest in Australia after Tasmania, measuring roughly 50 by 100km.

What is critical is that Melville Island is small enough that the centre is still inundated by moist maritime air, but it is large enough that the middle sees peak daytime temperatures up to 5 degrees Celsius warmer than the coastline.

The result is sufficient heat and humidity at the surface to ensure a parcel of air moving vertically upwards will remain warmer than the surrounding air and therefore keep on rising to form a towering cumulonimbus storm cloud.

The hotter interior of the Tiwi Islands draws in a daily sea breeze, completing the Goldilocks geography for Hector's existence. (ABC News)

The third and final ingredient, and often the missing link that prevents reliable storms elsewhere, is a trigger.

The trigger is the mechanism required to launch air from the surface and overcome gravity, and heat alone will not do the job, otherwise the entire layer of surface air over the sunlit side of the planet would simultaneously rise.

What you need is another force to initiate uplift, which could be a mountain range, a cold front, or perhaps converging winds.

Again, the Tiwi Islands has Goldilocks geography. Since the centre is warmer, and warm air is light, a sea breeze develops each day that carries in cooler, denser air from the ocean.

But unlike a mainland sea breeze which just blows in one direction from the ocean to the land, an island's sea breeze draws in air from all directions.

Eventually, all that inward-blowing wind meets in the middle where it is then forced to rise vertically (since it cannot blow into the ground), a process also aided by a slight increase in elevation towards Melville's centre.

Hector forms when converging sea breezes meet in the middle of the Tiwi Islands and force the development of a thundercloud. (ABC News)

And thanks to a lack of variation in the day-to-day temperature near the equator, this convergence occurs at the same time every afternoon during wet season.

The now rapidly upward-flowing column of hot, humid tropical air then cools and condenses, giving birth to a cumulus congestus cloud, which eventually rises high enough for ice particles to create the friction required for lightning.

And thus, Hector the Convector, now a mammoth cumulonimbus cloud, is born.

Meteorological advances mean living in the Top End is no longer a requirement for appreciating Hector's daily eruption in the wet season.

Hector can be viewed remotely using observational instruments like radars and satellites, and modern modelling can predict the localised temperature, wind and moisture conditions that will ensure his formation.

For example, the satellite loop above shows small cumulus clouds converging towards the centre of the islands as the sea breeze moves inland, and the resulting inland clash of airstreams which triggers a larger storm cloud.

High resolution modelling also picks up on the daily temperature contrast and wind fields which contribute to Hector's development.

During the next six months, as long as monsoon winds or cyclones are not blowing, Hector the Convector will spawn on most days.

That is before May arrives and the dry season south-easterly winds off the mainland send Hector back into a deep hibernation for the austral winter.

#130. Colorado students killed on hiking trip studied to be a wildland firefighter and a mountain guide

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Two college students found dead after they went missing on a hiking and climbing trip in Colorado last weekend were mountain enthusiasts who had been studying to become a wildland firefighter and an outdoor recreation guide, the school said Thursday.

Kaden Matsuda, 19, and Jackson Donlon, 20, set out last Friday from Colorado Mountain College's Leadville campus for what was supposed to be a weekend trip to Ice Mountain, a 13,958-foot (4,254-meter) peak.

It was raining at the time, according to the Chaffee County Sheriff’s Office, and despite a break in the weather on Saturday morning, a severe storm arrived that afternoon, dumping up to 2 feet (0.6 meters) of snow. They were expected back by Sunday, but college staff reported them missing Monday morning, and their bodies were found Wednesday afternoon in a basin of West Apostle Mountain, about 100 miles (160 kilometers) southeast of Aspen.

“They came here to learn, to explore and to build their futures,” Ben Cairns, the campus dean, said in a written statement. “Their lives were full of promise, and their loss is felt deeply by the students, faculty and staff who knew them.”

Colorado Mountain College, which has campuses throughout the Colorado Rockies, is known for its programs focusing on the state's robust outdoor recreation economy, including mountaineering, ski patrol and guiding careers.

Matsuda, of Ridgway, Colorado, and Donlon, of Queensbury, New York, became fast friends at the small campus in Leadville, which bills itself as America’s highest city at 10,119 feet (3,084 meters) above sea level. Located about 100 miles (160 kilometers) west of Denver, the city is home to about 2,600 people and is flanked by some of Colorado’s tallest mountains.

Matsuda was studying to be a wildland firefighter. Classmates and faculty members described him as kind, intelligent and welcoming, the statement from the college said. Donlon, an avid climber and skier, was studying outdoor recreation leadership and had come to Colorado to pursue his love of the mountains.

Ski or Die NY, a group of upstate New York skiers, called Donlon "one of the most ambitious skiers on the East Coast.” It said on its website that he became the youngest person to complete a strenuous 28-mile (45-kilometer) ski tour in the area’s High Peaks last December.

“He lived life with a passion that was contagious, embodying everything we love about skiing in New York,” the group posted on social media. “He was a friend, an adventurer, and someone who was always hungry for what was next.”

Chaffee County Sheriff Andy Rohrich credited Matsuda and Donlon with being capable, strong and prepared, saying in a social media post, “Almost all the safety measures we tell backcountry explorers to use, these men did.”

“We are proud of these young men concerning how prepared they were, but sometimes mother nature throws you a curveball,” he said.

Weather can be changeable in Colorado's mountains

Jason Marsteiner, the founder of The Survival University, a Colorado company that teaches wilderness skills, noted how quickly the weather can turn in the state's high mountains.

“The weather rolls in fast and the mountains are steep,” he said. “It's an unforgiving environment.”

The sheriff said in a phone interview Thursday that the men's bodies remained in the basin and officials were waiting for gusty winds to calm so a helicopter crew could retrieve them.

About 60 search and rescue volunteers, aided by helicopters, drones and dogs, searched for the two men.

The helicopter crew discovered a tent collapsed under the snow Tuesday with their sleeping bags, a stove and clothing near a lake northwest of Ice Mountain — but the students were nowhere to be found.

Based on the tent's location, Rohrich said it appeared Matsuda and Donlon changed plans and were aiming for the 13,597-foot (4,144-meter) West Apostle Mountain, instead of Ice Mountain. He said the location of the bodies indicated they were well into a hike when they died.

The sheriff's office said their climbing equipment was not in their dorms, tent or vehicle, suggesting they left their tent prepared for a technical climb. Trash inside the tent indicated they had eaten before starting their hike, officials said.

Authorities had been concerned that aerial searches could be limited in upcoming days because of poor weather that was expected to begin Friday.

Johnson reported from Seattle, Yu from Phoenix. Associated Press writer Dave Collins in Hartford, Connecticut, contributed.

A federal regulator is assessing an application to allow farmed salmon in Tasmania to be treated with a higher dose rate of antibiotic florfenicol.

The salmon industry's permit was revoked after drug residue was detected in wild fish several kilometres away.

The Greens are calling for the permit application to be rejected.

An animal health company has applied to "increase the dose rate" of the controversial antibiotic florfenicol in salmon farms in Tasmania, the federal regulator has confirmed.

Florfenicol was first used in the state's south-eastern waters in November last year after an emergency permit was approved by the Australian Pesticides and Veterinary Medicines Authority (APVMA).

Florfenicol was previously used in south-east Tasmania's salmon farms. (Supplied: EPA)

However in March this year, the emergency permit for florfenicol was revoked by the APVMA on the basis of "unacceptable risk of residue exposure to non-target species".

The revocation occurred after traces of the antibiotic were detected in wild fish species up to 10 kilometres from the nearest salmon pen in which the antibiotic had been used.

Florfenicol was later found in abalone up to 14km away.

At a Senate estimates hearing earlier this week, APVMA chief executive Scott Hansen outlined details of a renewed push by pharmaceutical company Abbey Animal Health, known as Abbey Labs, to use florfenicol in Tasmanian waters this summer.

"There's a second application, which is a minor use permit," Mr Hansen said in response to questioning from Greens senator Vanessa Bleyer.

"It's looking to move the location to not just the south-eastern biosecurity zone, but the eastern biosecurity zone as well.

"And to increase the dose rates and the treatment regime that's proposed.

"That obviously has a significant amount of work that is being done on that to look at environmental impacts, to look at any microbial resistance impact, as well as residues and depletion."

Scott Hansen says there is a second application for a minor use permit. (ABC News)

Mr Hansen said Salmon Tasmania had separately sought approval for another emergency use permit with the same dose rates and treatment frequency as its previously revoked permit.

The previous permit allowed for 10 to 20 milligrams per kilogram of fish per day for 10 consecutive days, with no re-treatment permitted.

No details were disclosed during the hearing about the degree to which Abbey Labs wants to increase its dose rate or treatment regime.

The APVMA said it could not comment on the new proposed dose rates because they were still under assessment.

The use of florfenicol last summer forced the closure of some rock lobster and abalone fisheries amid concerns any detections could trigger international export bans.

The Tasmanian Rock Lobster Council [TRLC], Abalone Council and Commercial Divers Association have previously warned of further fishery closures if florfenicol use resumes.

"Increasing dosage rates and treatment duration will increase both the likelihood and extent of environmental exposure," the groups said in the TRLC's newsletter in July.

Florfenicol was used in response to a bacterial disease affecting salmon. (Supplied: Bob Brown Foundation)

In June, it emerged that a total of 5.4 tonnes of the antibiotic had been used last summer, during which about 9,000 tonnes of salmon died.

Tasmania's Health Department has said there were no reports of adverse human health effects from eating seafood with florfenicol residues.

However, as a precaution it has said people can avoid seafood caught within 3km of a treated salmon pen.

In a statement, Senator Bleyer called on the APVMA to reject the florfenicol applications from Abbey Labs and Salmon Tasmania.

"We saw last time around that the antibiotic ended up in other animals such as rock lobster and abalone in our wild fisheries, jeopardising exports and threatening human health by increasing antibiotic resistance.

"Despite being labelled by the Australian Pesticides and Veterinary Medicines Authority (APVMA) as a 'minor use' permit, the risks are anything but that."

Earlier this month, the Department of Natural Resources and Environment (NRE) released a plan designed to reduce the risk of antibiotics entering wild fisheries.

The state's environment watchdog has released its insights from a mass salmon mortality event in which dead fish washed up on Tasmanian beaches.

The plan involves setting up multiple zones, in which rock lobster and abalone fisheries would be closed for at least six weeks if an antibiotic is administered.

Mr Hansen told the Senate hearing that the NRE's plan would form part of the APVMA's assessment of Salmon Tasmania's emergency use permit application.

Salmon Tasmania chief executive John Whittington said florfenicol was one part of a "much broader fish health strategy".

"It provides veterinarians with an important treatment option when additional support is required to protect fish welfare," he said.

"Its use is tightly regulated, clinically justified, and managed within a framework designed to protect animal welfare, food safety, environmental management, commercial fisheries and market access."

John Whittington says the antibiotic is one element of a broader strategy for fish health. (ABC News: Monty Jacka)

If a new florfenicol permit is issued, Mr Whittington said the number of fish needing treatment, according to veterinary advice, would dictate how much florfenicol is used.

"Our aim is to reduce the need for medication as much as possible through a range of strategies, including selective breeding, vaccination and other management practices."

The ABC contacted Abbey Labs for comment, but did not receive a response.

Stewart died in Los Angeles after battling Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia.

Patrick Stewart, the acclaimed British actor who played Captain Jean-Luc Picard in “Star Trek” and Professor Charles Xavier in the “X-Men” films, has died. He was 86.

Stewart died on Wednesday in Los Angeles following “a brief and poignant journey” with Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia, spokesperson Kelly Bush Novak said in a statement on Thursday.

Although best known for his film and television roles, Stewart spent much of his career onstage, performing Shakespeare and other classics.

His career included performances as Claudius and Macbeth, a one-man adaptation of “A Christmas Carol”, and West End and Broadway productions of Harold Pinter’s “No Man’s Land” and Samuel Beckett’s “Waiting for Godot” alongside longtime friend Ian McKellen.

Stewart earned some of the best reviews of his career for his 2007 portrayal of Macbeth, including his only Tony Award nomination. The Times of London described his Macbeth performance as “an enthralling creation of frailty, appetite, egotism and grim humour”.

“Nearly 50 years I’ve waited,” Stewart told the Associated Press news agency in 2008. “I learned it when I was 14. I memorized all the soliloquies just because I thought they were great. And they stuck, so I didn’t have to learn those bits of the play, which is a blessing, because learning lines has now become the one curse of this job.”

Raised in Mirfield, West Yorkshire, a working-class corner of northern England, the future 24th-century commander of a starship grew up without a bathroom in his house.

He joined an amateur drama group at 12 and, as a teenager, memorised Shakespeare for fun. A scholarship to the Bristol Old Vic Theatre School helped launch his acting career.

Stewart, who developed alopecia at a young age, had been bald since he was 19.

His childhood was also marked by domestic violence. His father, a former regimental sergeant major in the British Army, was prone to outbursts of “repeated violence against my mother,” as Stewart wrote in a newspaper piece. “Violence is always a choice a man makes and he alone is responsible for it.”

Stewart later became a patron of Refuge, a charity supporting survivors of domestic abuse.

Stewart spent 14 years with the Royal Shakespeare Company, playing everyone from Mark Antony and Henry IV to Shylock and Oberon.

“People who were not in the company would say to me, ‘Give it a break. Why don’t you go somewhere else?’ And I would say, ‘To do what?'” he told the AP. “Telly?”

He eventually left and appeared in the 1981 film “Excalibur”. In 1987, shortly after performing in “Who’s Afraid of Virginia Woolf?”, he was offered a role in a science fiction series.

“I found myself in Hollywood shooting a syndicated science fiction series, which I was assured would fail,” he said.

But “Star Trek: The Next Generation” ran for seven seasons and 178 episodes. Stewart became known worldwide as Captain Jean-Luc Picard, whose command, “Make it so”, became one of the show’s best-known lines.

Stewart also played Professor Charles Xavier, the leader of the X-Men, beginning with the 2000 film “X-Men”. He returned to the role in several sequels, including 2017’s “Logan”, alongside Hugh Jackman as Wolverine.

Stewart died just days after Walter Koenig, who played Ensign Pavel Chekov in the original “Star Trek” series, which aired from 1966 to 1969.

Queen Elizabeth II knighted Stewart in June 2010. He also served as an ambassador for the International Rescue Committee.
