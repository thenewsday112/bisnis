#1065. Climate leaders called for solar energy for 'everyone, everywhere' by 2030, but with three years to go, is that goal attainable?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Prominent climate leaders have called on governments, businesses, institutions and the public to make a push for solar power before the decade is out, repowering the world with cheap, clean energy. Is that possible?

In a world "set ablaze by war and  warming," with ever-worsening climate disasters such as wildfires in Europe and collapsing glaciers in Nepal, solar energy provides a solution in the form of clean, affordable power, prominent climate leaders wrote in an open letter.

Signed by author Bill McKibben, former UN Climate Secretary Christiana Figueres and Canada's former Minister of Environment and Climate Change Catherine McKenna, the letter makes a clear call. It urges governments, businesses, institutions and the public to come together to provide clean, affordable energy to "everyone, everywhere by 2030" — from the close to one billion people who still lack access to reliable electricity, to the billions more who pay too much for dirty fossil fuels.

With just a little over three years to go until the decade is out, how realistic is this push?

"It's an ambitious target, but we've blown away in terms of where we thought we would be on solar and renewables," McKenna told DW. "So let's just keep on going."

According to global energy think tank Ember,solar power has grown at its highest rate in eight years. In 2025, it increased by a record 30% over the previous year and has risen more than tenfold since 2015.

Solar is the cheapest form of electricity in the history of the world, said McKenna, who founded the climate advisory firm Climate and Nature Solutions, and the global coalition Women Leading on Climate.

"It is scaling way faster than anyone ever imagined."

As is battery technology used to store excess renewable energy.

"The cost of batteries have as well come down as the cost of solar modules," said Eicke Weber, a physicist and former director of Germany's Fraunhofer Institute for Solar Energy Systems who went on to found the European Solar Manufacturing Council, among other things.

"We have the technologies available and we have enough money to finance it," he said, of a transition to widespread solar access.

In 2025, for the first time in 100 years, renewable energy sources overtook coal power in the global electricity mix, Ember's most recent analysis showed.

At the moment, installed solar capacity around the world stands at 3 terawatts. 1TW equals 1000 gigawatts, and a single nuclear power plant has a capacity of 1GW.

Currently, the world produces about 1TW of new photovoltaic capacity each year, most of it in China. Weber says in order to fully change global energy supply to renewables, we would need about 50TW, adding that "we have more production capacities than we are selling."

Roughly 655 million people still lack accessto electricity.

"The pace of electrification in Sub-Saharan Africa has to triple to achieve universal access by 2030," McKenna said, pointing out how quickly this gap could be closed with solar.

Areas without electricity have the opportunity to leapfrog straight into clean technologies, she says, bypassing the fossil fuels that are heating the planet. Rising global temperatures are fueling extreme weather events such as drought, storms and flooding around the world.

"And there's a misunderstanding, which is often propagated intentionally by fossil fuel companies, that it's expensive, it's hard, it's unreliable. And that just isn't the case anymore," she said. "To be honest, the most unreliable thing is if you're trying to pass fossil fuels through the Strait of Hormuz."

Hans-Josef Fell is used to the argument that certain things cannot be done. Widely known as a pioneer of renewable energy, he drafted Germany's Renewable Energy Sources Act, which established feed-in tariffs that catalyzed clean energy growth at home and beyond.

"The Renewable Energy Sources Act unleashed exponential growth that shattered every assumption about what was achievable. It overturned the scenarios and projections that policymakers and experts had considered realistic. In the end, reality outperformed all expectations," said Fell, who now heads the think tank Energy Watch Group.

Fell, who has lived off grid for five years, said achieving a global energy transition requires political and societal will that is "currently lacking around the world because public debate is dominated by fossil fuel interests."

According to calculations by his think tank, if global energy demand were met entirely by renewables, solar energy would account for roughly 60-70% of the supply. The rest would come from wind power, and other sources, such as bioenergy and hydropower.

Countries like China and Pakistan have already pushed ahead.

"It is in our hands right now in the year 2026 to say we drop everything, we concentrate on this problem. We don't need to wait for new research," Weber said.

"It is possible to achieve this, but it would require a very kind of moonshot program," he added, saying it would need serious political backing.

He says while it might be possible for Germany to pull off such as transition in two or three years, if the political will were there, it would likely take longer to achieve on a global scale. "We would need probably till 2040 to say that we have a goal of a worldwide 80%, 90% electricity from renewables."

"What is irrational is continuing down the road of fossil fuels," she added. "That's irrational on the costs, the risks, the impacts. It's an old technology."

#1057. Germany's Franz Reindl elected new IIHF president

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Franz Reindl has been elected president of ice hockey's world governing body. The former forward was a member of West Germany's Olympic bronze medal-winning team.

Franz Reindl came from behind to narrowly beat Petr Briza of the Czech Republic in a run-off vote to become the International Ice Hockey Federation's new president on Friday.

Reindl had trailed Briza 48-36 in the first round of voting but picked up crucial votes after Aivaz Omorkanov of Kyrgyzstan was eliminated. He then beat Briza 60-57 in the runoff held on the Spanish island of Tenerife. Six of a total of nine candidates had withdrawn prior to the election.

The 71-year-old German, who had been a member of the IIHF Council since 2016, succeeds Canadian-born Frenchman Luc Tardif, who had announced in March that he would not seek a further term in office. Reindl's election comes 5 years after he had finished as runner-up to Tardif. His term runs until 2030.

Reindl has done it all in German ice hockey, having been a player and coach, as well as serving as the first CEO of the top-flight professional league, the Deutsche Eishockey Liga (DEL), from 1994 to 19<96. He also headed the organizing committees for three World Championships — in 2001, 2010 and 2017. From 2014 to 2022 he served as president of the German Ice Hockey Association (DEB), overseeing an upturn in the fortunes of the men's national team, which won the silver medal at the Pyeongchang Winter Olympics in 2018.

"Franz Reindl has shaped German ice hockey for decades—as a player, but above all through the various key roles he has held within our federation," the current DEB chairman, Frank Lutz, said.

"The fact that the international ice hockey family is now entrusting him with the leadership of the IIHF is a great tribute to his work."

Like Briza, who was a goalie for the Czechoslovakia team that won bronze in Albertville in 1992, Reindl was also an Olympic bronze medalist, having been a member of the 1976 West Germany team who beat the United States and Finland to finish third in Lake Placid.

Domestically, Reindl played 669 games and scored 423 goals for SC Riessersee and SB Rosenheim over 16 seasons in the Eishockey-Bundesliga, the predecessor to the DEL.

Tardif's years as IIHF president were marked by two major events: the return of National Hockey League players to the Olympic tournament in Milan Cortina this year, and the exclusion from international play of Russia in 2022 over the country's full-scale war on neighboring Ukraine.

Reindl is the second German to be elected IIHF president after Günter Sabetzki, who held the post from 1975 to 1994.

Not just for humans: gene therapy to extend longevity is under investigation for dogs, too.Credit: Juliana Yamada/Los Angeles Times/Getty

Vera Gorbunova has attended many a longevity conference over her decades of studying ageing. But never one quite like this.

Earlier this month, Gorbunova, a researcher at Rochester University in New York, joined scientists from industry and academia — and a fair few of their dogs — for the Animal Longevity Summit in Toronto, Canada. Given it was the inaugural summit, it was small. But it highlighted a growing interest in the science of extending the lifespan of pets.

On the agenda were presentations by academic researchers such as Gorbunova, who studies ageing in various animal species. Others presented projects about understanding cancer in dogs, regeneration in axolotls and the genetics of mammals that thrive in extreme environments. Alongside the university scientists were researchers at companies pursuing gene therapies for dogs, probiotic kibble for cats and cryopreservation of pets.

“It was maybe a somewhat different crowd of people than you see at the typical ageing conferences,” Gorbunova says.

The conference reflected a market reality: as the pursuit of increasing human longevity and healthspan has grown, fuelled by hefty investment, it only stands to reason that some of that interest would spill over to pets, says Cameron Hurlburt, a senior analyst at Ani VC, a venture-capital firm in Miami, Florida. The US pet- and animal-health market is estimated to be worth US$150 billion and could grow as more people in the United States purchase pet health insurance, he says.

Most of the current efforts to promote longer pet healthspans focus on improving animal diets, for example with probiotic food formulations, Hurlburt says. And when it comes to therapies, many companies are looking to repurpose drugs that are already approved for humans, rather than starting from scratch.

But not everyone is taking that approach. The Cat Health Company in Bucharest presented its strategy for identifying new drugs for ageing-associated diseases in cats, including a muscle-wasting condition called sarcopenia, and kidney disease. The company has two compounds in clinical trials now, with others on the way, says Alex Băciţa, co-founder and chief operating officer of the company.

Others are venturing into gene therapies. Rejuvenate Bio, a biotechnology company in San Diego, California, is working on one to treat mitral valve disease, the most common heart condition in dogs. But co-founder and chief scientific officer Noah Davidsohn hopes that this will be just the first use for the therapy, and that the same treatment will eventually be used to treat metabolic syndromes and improve longevity. Rejuvenate has worked to decrease the cost of manufacturing gene therapies, which are complex treatments, to a level that would be compatible with the pet market.

Another company, called Genflow Biosciences, is pursuing a gene therapy to enhance longevity and reduce sarcopenia in dogs. If the drug shows success, the company hopes to sell it to a larger animal-health company, and then use the proceeds to help to fund studies of the same approach in humans, says Eric Leire, founder and chief executive of the firm in Brussels.

Academic researchers also stand to gain by interacting with the latest companies to spring up, said Noah Snyder-Mackler, a comparative geroscientist at Arizona State University in Tempe, before the meeting. Government grants for studying dogs often focus on applying the results to human health, rather than canine health. That means that academic researchers might need to collaborate with industry to conduct studies that lead to treatments for dogs.

“We don’t have the resources to follow up with dog-centric interventions,” says Snyder-Mackler, a researcher on the Dog Aging Project, a large collaboration to study ageing in pooches. Gorbunova says she enjoyed the meeting. “It was a revelation to me to see how interested people are in extending the life of their pets,” she says.

#1047. "NAZA": Israeli military issues statement on film

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The award-winning documentary "NAZA" continues to spark controversy in Israel. The Israeli military has now published a multi-page statement calling the film's portrayal "distorted."

When it premiered at the Venice International Film Festival in mid-September, "NAZA" received a 25-minute-long standing ovation and the Special Jury Prize. But in Israel, the documentary has fueled heated debate.

Israeli Culture Minister Miki Zohar described the work of filmmakers Rachel Szor and Yuval Abraham "treason against the state" and urged that their citizenship be revoked. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has also sharply criticized the documentary.

Now the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) has also rejected the film's allegations about its conduct during the war in Gaza. In a multi-page document, the military accuses the filmmakers of presenting a "distorted, misleading and disingenuous picture." The film, it says, is based on "substantial factual errors" resulting from "manipulative misrepresentations of reality."

The 80-minute documentary examines the Israel Defense Forces' operations during the war in Gaza. It is based on interviews conducted with 24 anonymous military and intelligence personnel, who describe internal procedures and decision-making processes involved in selecting targets in the Gaza Strip. According to the interviewees, high civilian casualty estimates were, in some cases, deliberately factored into these decisions.

The IDF rejects those claims. It says it has never planned or carried out attacks while anticipating hundreds of civilian deaths. It also disputes the film's claim that artificial intelligence selected targets without human oversight, saying people have always made decisions about targets and attacks.

The military also accuses the filmmakers of omitting important facts. According to the IDF, the film fails to show the role of military legal advisors and commanders in reviewing and authorizing attacks or the measures it says it takes to protect civilians. It also argues that the film does not adequately address the Hamas attacks on Israel on October 7, 2023, in which over 1,200 people were killed and 250 people were taken hostage.

Several human rights organizations and a UN commission have accused Israel of committing acts of genocide in Gaza. According to health authorities in Gaza and other sources, at least 74,000 Palestinians have been killed since the war began.

Speaking at the awards ceremony in Venice in mid-September, co-director Yuval Abraham said: "We believe that the denial of a crime is what helps it persist. That is why it's really, really especially important to us that Israelis watch this film."

Rachel Szor and Yuval Abraham also worked on the film "No Other Land," which won an Oscar for Best Documentary Feature in 2025.

Linda Nordling is a freelance journalist in Cape Town, South Africa.

This article is the second in a series discussing the results of Nature’s 2026 science-job satisfaction survey. It’s the latest edition of such surveys, which started in 2010; the last two took place in 2018 and 2021. The 2026 survey, created in partnership with Thinks Insight & Strategy, a research consultancy in London, launched in May and was advertised on nature.com, in Springer Nature digital products and through e-mail campaigns. It had 3,882 self-selecting respondents across 133 countries with 31% describing themselves as female and 19% as belonging to a minority ethnic group in their country of work. The full survey data sets are available at go.nature.com/4b2drrc.

Umar, a mathematics lecturer in northern Nigeria, has worked at his university for more than ten years. In that time, he’s had only one pay rise: in 2024, when the government raised the national minimum wage. With no inflation- or promotion-based adjustments, his income no longer covers his basic living costs. Instead, he has to borrow money, take on extra jobs and cut back on essential spending. “Unfortunately, my experience is not an isolated one” for Nigerian scientists, he says.

On the other side of the Atlantic Ocean, Ashley — an early-career physicist employed on a fixed-term contract in a university in the eastern United States — says she is much worse off than her parents, neither of whom have degrees. “It’s really messed up that I have a job that requires a PhD in science, yet I’m paid US$20,000 less per year than my father earned at his job 20 years ago, before accounting for inflation.” Ashley lists the things she can’t afford, including children (her pay won’t cover childcare, nor putting them through university) and home ownership. She thinks she did everything ‘right’ — for example, having jobs alongside her undergraduate studies to avoid debt. “Yet I am still locked out of the middle-class existence I have worked myself half to death for,” she says, adding: “It sucks. It really, really sucks.”

Umar and Ashley (not their real names) live in countries with vastly different resources for research and costs of living. But their stories reflect a global theme. When Nature’s careers team surveyed them and nearly 4,000 other scientists earlier this year about their salaries, less than half said they had received a pay rise in the past 12 months. Around one-third said that their salaries covered only basic expenses, and one in ten said their income failed to do even that.

The survey asked respondents to convert their gross (before-taxes) salary into US dollars, then choose the range into which it falls. More than half earn less than US$50,000 a year, and 22% make at least $80,000. In 2021, when Nature last ran this survey, 38% earned below $50,000 and 32% earned $80,000 or more. But this apparent drop in global salaries might be because of geographical shifts in those surveyed — particularly a rise in respondents from Asia, who made up 10% in 2021 and 39% this year. From continents with enough respondents for comparison, North American scientists earn the most, followed by those in Europe, then Asia (see ‘The scientist’s wallet’).

Industry salaries exceed those in academia by 66%, and more scientists in industry are satisfied with their salary (58%) than are those in academia (43%). But even in the private sector, some think that they should earn more. “It feels like I’m making less and less every year,” says a researcher working at a biomedical technology company in a large city in the midwestern United States, who, like many scientists Nature spoke to, asked to remain anonymous when discussing pay concerns.

She moved from working at a university to her current job four years ago after discovering that she was pregnant, because she judged childcare expenses to be unaffordable on her previous salary. But despite having a dozen years of work experience in both industry and academia, she says that she is still paid like a recent graduate.

“I love science and what I do, but I also feel undervalued,” she says.

Scientists in the United States earn the most out of the ten countries in the survey that had more than 100 respondents, which means the results from these nations can be compared sensibly. Some 65% of United States-based scientists report earning more than $80,000 a year. The lowest wages are found in India, where 53% earn less than $15,000 a year. Comparing continents, the lowest salaries are found in Africa, where 61% earn below $15,000 per year.

However, the survey’s self-reported salary figures in converted US dollars do not reflect what those incomes can buy on the ground. A dollar buys more in India than in the United States: nearly five times more. According to purchasing-power calculations by the International Monetary Fund, a $15,000 salary in India is comparable to a $70,000 salary in the United States. When adjusted for purchasing power, average salaries in the survey for Canada overtake those in the United States, salaries in Germany come close to US ones and the wide salary gaps between emerging economies and wealthier nations shrink. And it’s important to note that a nation’s average salary in the survey can be skewed by sample biases with respect to factors such as location within a nation, employment sector and career stage.

In Brazil, where 21% of respondents report earning less than $15,000, a low or modest salary by US or western-European standards can “provide a reasonably comfortable life locally”, especially outside the most expensive cities such as Rio de Janeiro or Sao Paolo, says Haroldo Ribeiro, a physicist at the State University of Maringá in Brazil. “Still, I would not conclude that Brazilian scientists are generally well paid,” he says. Although salaries in public universities there have increased in the past few years, this comes after a long period of stagnation, he says, making it a recovery, not a raise.

Only 44% of people in the total sample are satisfied with their pay, but this varies between countries. Significantly more scientists in Canada (61%), Germany (68%) and the United States (55%) are satisfied with their income than the global average. The countries with at least 100 respondents that had the lowest numbers of scientists satisfied with their salary are Turkey (27%), China (39%) and Italy (40%).

China’s lower-than-average satisfaction with salary comes despite survey data suggesting that Chinese salaries have risen in US-dollar terms since Nature asked scientists the same question in 2021. Some 11% of Chinese respondents reported earning more than $80,000 this year, nearly doubling from 6% in 2021. Over the same time, in the United Kingdom, the proportion earning more than $80,000 grew from 19% to only 22%.

Salaries can vary substantially between China’s economically developed coastal areas, and its more-sparsely populated west, as well as between universities, says survey respondent Jingrui Sun, a freshwater ecologist at Yunnan University in Kunming, China. He describes his salary as “sufficient for me to live comfortably”, and says that it is common for research-intensive universities and institutions in wealthy parts of the country to offer employees performance-related pay, as well as housing subsidies and laboratory start-up packages.

But good salaries in China come with extra demands, Sun says. “Part of the package is linked to periodic assessments, and if the required targets are not met, remuneration in the following period can be reduced substantially.” He says a few of his colleagues have had their pay docked this year, after their university introduced more-stringent assessment criteria. “Such changes can have real consequences for staff retention, and have prompted academics to leave and take positions at other institutions,” he says.

Annual pay rises are not the global norm, the survey shows. Only 45% of respondents say they had received a raise in the previous 12 months, and 6% report a cut in pay. Industry researchers are more likely than the average result to have had a pay rise (54%), whereas the opposite is true for those who earn less than $50,000 a year (42% of whom received one).

Many survey-takers are frustrated that they aren’t getting paid more. One non-tenure-track US researcher who works in a biology department says that she exceeds her department’s tenure criteria and outperforms several of her tenure-track colleagues. Those colleagues are paid at least $20,000 more than her annually and get more research funding. “Yet my department chairs keep looking me dead in the eye and saying there’s no appetite for raises” for people like her, she says.

When salaries grow, it’s often not enough. One researcher with a fixed-term contract in Pune, India, says his public-sector salary has not kept up with “skyrocketing urban inflation” in the city of seven million people. He says that rent, public transport and groceries consume most of his pay. “At this salary, no one can even think of home or car loans,” he says. Add a child, he says, and “it’s just a matter of survival”.

In Europe, where 27% of respondents report earning more than $80,000 a year, salaries vary massively between countries, says Norbert Bencze, president of Eurodoc, an organization for early-career researchers in Europe. “A good salary in the Netherlands is something very different from a good salary in Poland or Hungary.” However, living costs outpacing pay increases are a common problem across the continent, he says. “Some researchers have seen their actual purchasing power fall quite radically, in some cases cut in half over the past ten years, even though they got regular pay rises.” Although Nature’s salary survey was of post-PhD researchers and lab technicians, Eurodoc’s survey of doctoral pay in 2025 showed that PhD students earned below minimum wage in five European countries: Hungary, Ireland, Poland, Romania and Ukraine (see go.nature.com/4s7m1tu).

Fairer, more transparent salary-setting would be welcome, even if scientists’ wages continue to underwhelm, survey participants say. “It is not only about paying more, but about making compensation feel clear, consistent and linked to contribution,” says a materials scientist in India. People should understand how salaries are decided, what the path to a raise or promotion looks like and whether their pay is competitive with the market, he says.

Researchers who had not taken the survey felt similarly when Nature spoke to them about the results. In Portugal, academic salaries have risen in recent years, mainly to keep up with inflation, says Ronaldo Sousa, an aquatic ecologist at the Interdisciplinary Centre of Marine and Environmental Research near Porto. However, to get a pay rise that is substantially above inflation, scientists must move up the academic ladder, for example from assistant professor to associate professor. “We might publish a dozen high-impact papers in a year, but this will not necessarily result in any increase in salary,” he says.

#1035. Jürgen Klopp faces myriad of issues after rough start with Germany

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Jürgen Klopp has done things a little differently since becoming Germany coach. Casting a wider net for talent and trying to connect with players and country haven't made an immediate impact, but he is not bowed.

Four games, three goals, two draws, one loss and one win. Jürgen Klopp would have hoped for better results from his first international break as Germany coach. But though the comfortable win over Serbia (2-0), draws against the Netherlands (1-1) and Greece (0-0) and a shock loss to Greece (0-1) in Germany leave them third out of four in their Nations League group, Klopp saw mostly positives.

"Everyone showed what they're made of; everyone earned their place. We've shown signs that we can do some things better than people gave us credit for. I wanted to get something going, and I definitely saw that happen," he said after Sunday's 0-0 draw in Thessaloniki.

For all the positivity of the former Liverpool and Borussia Dortmund coach, the four matches have, in reality, been a mixed bag for Germany.

Having fallen in the last 32 of the 2026 World Cup and exited in the group stage in 2022, Germany's footballing stock has never been lower.

Klopp tried to address this by casting his net wider, announcing a 44-man squad that split over the matches. He then said this would be a one off, but that his strategy of trying to find a diamond in the relative rough who could adapt to the international game had given him valuable insights.

"Hard work, lots of information — these guys are brimming with talent," he said of those given a surprise opportunity.

The origins of writing and script remain unclear, but those of speech and language are simply unknown. This is because no physical evidence remains of the earliest language, which was probably spoken in Africa, the birthplace of Homo sapiens. By contrast, inscribed marks on clay tablets that constitute the earliest known form of writing — however mysterious — have survived from ancient Mesopotamia, dating to the fourth millennium bc.

That’s left a lot of room for scholars to speculate on how speech and language began, starting with thinkers from the ancient world, including Plato, who could not decide whether language had a natural or a purely human origin. By the nineteenth century, anthropologists had grown tired of so much talk with scant evidence to go on.

In 1866, the founding statutes of the Paris Linguistic Society forbade all discussion of the subject, note anthropologists Chris Knight and Jerome Lewis in The Revolutionary Origins of Language. “Anyone theorizing about the origins of language risked being dismissed as a crank.” Even so, “from time to time, this or that brave maverick would risk whatever reputation they had by attempting to reopen the debate”.

The best known, if controversial, attempts since then come from linguist Noam Chomsky, starting in the 1960s. The authors discuss his contributions at length, drawing on Knight’s book Decoding Chomsky (2016).

In 1987, Chomsky remarked in Language and Problems of Knowledge that: “There is a long history of study of the origin of language, asking how it came from the calls of apes and so forth.” But, in his view, this investigation is a “complete waste of time”.

That’s because language is based on “an entirely different principle than any animal communication system”, he wrote. Although human gestures might have evolved from animal communication systems, the same could not apply to human language, Chomsky thought.

This view was supported by an academic study, launched in 1973, of a young chimpanzee (Pan troglodytes) with the punning name Nim Chimpsky. Nim learnt from his human supervisors how to make dozens of hand signs to procure food and other rewards, but not how to communicate in American Sign Language or to speak a language. “Give orange me give eat orange me eat orange give me eat orange give me you” was Nim’s longest recorded sign-language sequence, as cited by Knight and Lewis.

In Chomsky’s view, human language arose suddenly because of a single genetic mutation, not from Darwinian natural selection or through a gradual, adaptive cultural process. In other words, argue the authors, by declaring language to be “an especially intriguing and mysterious aspect of the natural world, to be investigated using the normal methods of natural science”, Chomsky elevated the study of linguistics to a scientific challenge in the field of anthropology.

However, they add, “there is no measuring device, magnifying glass, telescope or other instrument which can grasp the meaning of a word.” So how can conventional science investigate language?

Rejecting Chomsky’s view, Knight and Lewis instead offer a complex mixture of evidence, both scientific and cultural, to support their own theory. This evidence is mostly drawn from prehistoric and present-day Africa, where Lewis spent three decades studying hunter-gatherers, notably the BaYaka people in Central Africa.

“To qualify as science, a hypothesis must suggest testable predictions,” the authors remark. Given that there can be no direct evidence for events in the distant past, scientists must therefore “construct tests from a wide range of telltale clues”. These include: “recurrent structures of magical myth, initiation rituals, hunters’ taboos, patterns of menstrual prohibition, lunar and solar influences, and critically, archaeological evidence for symbolism in the form of red ochre pigments and personal ornaments”. All of these clues are examined in their intriguing, if rambling, book.

Although anthropologists have often suggested that speech and language arose from hunting or warfare, Knight and Lewis refute these two theories. While hunting in the forest, male hunter-gatherers “do their utmost to avoid having to speak aloud”, so as to lure animals towards them. Instead, they use sign language to alert each other to the nearby presence of animals such as gorillas, antelopes, leopards and elephants.

She studies how the Nobel Prize, as a global institution, influences the international circulation of literature, power dynamics in the literary world and translation markets.

"The committee can also add names itself, such as authors who were nominated in previous years," she says.

In the spring, the Nobel Committee then selects five names through a multistage process and presents them to the Academy. Over the summer, Academy members read the nominees' works and discuss them. The Academy always announces the decision in early October.

As for the criteria the Academy uses to make its decision, it prefers to keep them to itself.

The public doesn't learn which nominees missed out until 50 years later — that's how long the Academy waits before releasing the list.

Sometimes decades pass between an author's first nomination and actually receiving the prize. Danish writer Johannes V. Jensen, who won the Nobel Prize in literature in 1944, waited 40 years. Doris Lessing received the award in 2007 after 35 years, while Thomas Mann, who won in 1929, had to wait 27 years.

In his will, Alfred Nobel, the prize's founder, stipulated that it should be awarded to the person who had produced "the most outstanding work in an idealistic direction." That leaves plenty of room for interpretation.

According to Gisele Sapiro, the Academy aims to honor ambitious, influential literature that has, for example, given rise to a new literary movement. "At the same time, the work has to be universal, being able to transcend its own culture," she says.

Translations can be one indication of this. "I noted in my research, however, that all the laureates since 1990 had been translated into English, French and German — except for Abdulrazak Gurnah [the Tanzanian-British 2021 Nobel laureate], who had not been translated into German before winning — meaning that I think these three languages are the most important," Sapiro explains.

Sapiro says it is also a major advantage if nominees have already won other major literary awards, such as the International Man Booker Prize, the Cervantes Prize or the Büchner Prize.

Politics can also influence the Swedish Academy's decisions.

On several occasions, the prize has gone to writers who openly opposed the regimes in their home countries, such as Belarusian writer Svetlana Alexievich in 2015. She is an outspoken critic of authoritarian President Alexander Lukashenko.

Genre, on the other hand, does not matter. In the past, laureates have been honored not only for novels but also for poetry and plays. In 2016, singer-songwriter Bob Dylan received the prize for his song lyrics.

So far, only 33 of the 122 laureates have come from outside Europe. There is also a clear gender imbalance: only in the past eight years have men and women alternated as winners, significantly increasing women's share of the total number of laureates to 14.9%.

#966. Emerging economies are embracing solar power and skipping fossil fuels much faster than Europe or the US

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Renewable electricity is cheaper than ever. While the West got rich on coal and oil, some of the world's poorest countries are banking on solar, wind and water instead.

As emerging economies and global living standards rise, is the world in for another fossil fuel boom? The data says not. Countries from Southeast Asia to Sub-Saharan Africa aren't investing in fossil fuels as the West did; they're investing in electricity powered by renewables.

When the US and Europe industrialized in the 19th and 20th centuries, they did so using fossil energy. From coal ovens, gas heating and combustion-engine cars to electricity produced in coal and gas power plants, entire societies relied on polluting fuels.

Yet while burning fossil fuels allowed industrialized countries to boom, it also caused the climate crisis that is linked to extreme weather events around the world today.

Many of the same countries that became economically powerful off the back of fossil fuels are now trying to transition to renewables, but centuries of legacy infrastructure hold them back.

Because a gas boiler won't run on wind power and a diesel truck cannot be solar-powered, societies looking to shift to green energy need to use more electricity and increase their share of renewables.

That is where many middle- and lower-income countries have an advantage. With less existing energy infrastructure built around fossil fuels, they are better placed for a faster rollout of renewable electricity sources.

In many instances, solar power in particular is now cheaper to produce than fossil electricity. And unlike large coal or gas plants, which take years to construct and millions in investments, solar and wind farms can be built quickly and with much less upfront cost.

At the same time, according to an analysis by renewables think tank Ember, electronic devices like refrigerators, electric stoves or air conditioners have all become much cheaper in the last decades, making electricity a more affordable option as an energy source for cooking, heating and more.

While electric cars are still a luxury item in most places, their prices are coming down too. And a UN study found that in India, Indonesia and Vietnam electric scooters and motorbikes already cost the same as petrol models. Yet a lot less to fuel.

All this means that renewable electricity is quickly becoming the best choice for the wallet — not just for the climate. And countries are taking note.

"We're seeing a leapfrog take place right across the emerging markets,” said Kingsmill Bond, energy strategist at Ember. "The whole of Asia is electrifying quickly, and the whole of Europe and the United States is stagnant."

In 2023, only around a quarter of global electricity was generated by renewables. But that figure is rising fast, and almost all new capacity added these days is based on solar or wind power.

Lower-income countries already use less fossil fuels and more renewables than higher-income ones.

And large middle-income economies are outpacing richer ones as well. In 2023, solar power alone made up at least six percent and ten percent of electricity in India and Vietnam, respectively. The global average was five percent.

This boom has meant middle- and lower-income countries are electrifying fast, consuming less energy directly from fossil fuels than richer countries. Vietnam, for instance, is already using more electricity than the US, Europe and even China, and India is following the same path.

But the potential for energy leapfrogging might be particularly high in low-income countries, especially across Sub-Saharan Africa.

Countries such as Kenya, Ethiopia and Namibia are rapidly expanding their energy infrastructure to meet the demands of a growing, young population. But rural areas in many countries are not connected to electricity grids.

Until a few years ago, he says, people defaulted to diesel generators to meet their energy needs. That came at a cost because importing fuel is particularly expensive in many African countries, meaning energy crises and oil price fluctuations hit much harder.

These factors make solar power a solution for both energy access and independence. And it has become so cheap that Nana says it's a "no-brainer."

"Some businesses can make back their investment in just two years, so why would you want to deploy anything else?” he said. "Solar has rapidly become the de facto default technology that everyone falls back on.”

And once it is set up, it is very effective. Sub-Saharan Africa is extremely well-suited to solar power. It gets more sun than almost anywhere in the world, and has enough space for solar farms to cover electricity demand hundreds of times over.

"If you add solar plus batteries, you can get cheap electricity about 90 percent of the time from the sun alone”, said Kingsmill Bond.

Countries like Namibia, South Africa, Kenya and Senegal have expanded their solar capacity massively in the past few years.

Similar trends are emerging all across the world. In Pakistan, for example, people have been buying solar panels from China en masse in the past few years to avoid steep electricity grid prices and frequent power outages.

Aleksi Lumijärvi, director for knowledge, policy and finance at the International Renewable Energy Association says these decisions can have a major impact on the global economy in the coming decades.

"Industrial companies will find that it will be cheaper to move production to, for example, somewhere in Sub-Saharan Africa, because they have this cheap, clean solar power plus batteries available.”

Kingsmill Bond says where electricity is cheap and abundant, wealth will follow.

"You're going to have faster growth in the emerging markets, you will have fewer financial crises. It will make a material difference to people's quality of lives.”

Nana says the next step for African countries is to establish policies that ensure everyone benefits from the solar boom.

"We are heading towards the most decentralized grid in the whole world within the next five years,” he told DW. His mantra for governments, he says, is: "This is happening, whether you like it or not.” He expects a wave of new energy regulations, many of which are now in the final stages of negotiation, to come into effect across Africa in the coming months and years.

"Countries are realizing it's in their interest to integrate this technology into national strategies, to share the benefits and to make use of this competitive advantage."

#955. Nobelitis — How winning a Nobel Prize can give you delusions of grandeur

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

After being chosen for one of the world's most prestigious awards, some Nobel laureates lose touch with reality and suddenly start spouting unscientific nonsense. They're afflicted by what some have called "Nobelitis."

Linus Pauling won not one, but two Nobel Prizes — the first, for chemistry, in 1954 for his research into the nature of the chemical bond, and the Peace Prize in 1962 for his work in the anti-nuclear weapons movement.

The American chemist not only won two Nobel Prizes, but won them on his own, a rare feat for any scientist. Such an honor often turns laureates into global superstars overnight, with millions hanging on their every word.

That instant fame can be dangerous, said Nils Hansson, a professor in the Department of History, Theory and Ethics of Medicine at Heinrich Heine University in Düsseldorf. Especially, he said, when these world-renowned experts "start spreading crazy, unproven scientific theories" — ideas that, when disseminated by Nobel laureates, risk being taken seriously.

That's what happened with Pauling. Beginning in the mid 1960s he began claiming that vitamin C was a universal remedy, a cure for everything from cancer to cardiovascular conditions and degenerative diseases like Alzheimer's disease, multiple sclerosis or osteoarthritis.

Pauling developed an alternative treatment he called orthomolecular medicine, based on specific doses of vitamins and minerals for each patient. To this day, some 60 years later, evidence for his methods remains inconclusive.

Pauling's case is a classic example of "Nobelitis," or "Nobel disease" — not an official diagnosis, but an informal term used to describe how some laureates begin to suffer delusions of grandeur later in life. It was first coined by Paul Nurse, president of The Royal Society, the UK's national academy of sciences, and himself a winner of the Nobel Prize.

"Be skeptical of everything, including yourself and your own ideas," Nurse said in a 2019 interview with KU Leuven University in Belgium, when asked what made someone a good scientist. In cases of Nobelitis, that self-skepticism is the first to go.

That was also the case with virologist Luc Montagnier, who was jointly honored in 2008 for his discovery of the human immunodeficiency virus, HIV, with colleague Francoise Barre-Sinoussi in the early 1980s.

Montagnier would later go on to support homeopathic medicine, another alternative treatment of disease and claim that HIV transmission in Africa could be diminished through better hygiene and nutrition.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Montagnier said the SARS-CoV-2 virus had been created in a lab with parts of the HIV genetic code. "It's difficult for a practicing virologist to say that a Nobel laureate in virology is spreading nonsense," said German virologist Christian Drosten on NDR's Coronavirus Update podcast in May 2020. "But that is utter nonsense."

James Watson, who was never known for his modesty, also let fame go to his head. In 1962, he was awarded the Nobel Prize along with Maurice Wilkins and Francis Crick for their discovery of the structure of the DNA molecule. But in the early 2000s, Watson repeatedly spread racist theories that Black people were less intelligent than white people.

For medical historian Hansson, Watson is a prime example that the Nobel Prize is not automatically awarded to a person who should be considered as a role model.

"You may have made a fantastic discovery, but that doesn't make you a universal genius," he said.

But the great expectations of an admiring public may also contribute to the development of Nobelitis. Paul Nurse, who won the Nobel Prize in physiology or medicine in 2001, soon found himself getting daily requests for interviews on all sorts of topics.

"As if suddenly I was acknowledgeable about all workings of the world and of human beings," he told DW.

Nurse had this advice for researchers: "Be careful quite what you say. Talk about things that you do know something about that would be fine. And if you don't know so much or you're not sure about something, be really clear that you're not sure."

Nobelitis sufferers are leaders in their fields and rightly honored for their work with the Nobel. But they're also regular individuals, not immune to megalomania and other behavioral problems — meaning that homeopathy, orthomolecular medicine and other pseudosciences don't automatically become credible just because they're backed by a Nobel Prize winner.

Just hours before Lionel Messi played his last match for Argentina, Cristiano Ronaldo posted his latest salvo in a row with Portugal. Ronaldo wants to play on despite the troubles, while Messi bathed in adulation.

They are the two players who have defined the last two decades. As football became ever more popular, commercial and dominant in the cultural and sporting landscape, Cristiano Ronaldo and Lionel Messi remained its twin peaks.

Along with that has come endless social media posts, stories and discussions about who is better. Does Messi's World Cup and two Copa America titles with Argentina trump Ronaldo's Euro 2016 win and two Nations League titles? Or does the Portuguese's greater number of goals mean more? How about their club honors?

It is, essentially, a subjective argument and not one that can ever be resolved. It remains to be seen whether that's also true of the public spat between Ronaldo and his Portugal coach Jorge Jesus.

On Tuesday, Messi scored a 126th goal in his 208th game for Argentina. Shortly before he did, Ronaldo posted a 2,000-word statement in which he stopped short of quitting the national team but said there was an "absolute breakdown of trust" between him and the coach.

Ronaldo's comments refer to Portugal's recent match against Norway in which he said he was initially to be rested before being asked to warm up as a substitute without ever being brought on. The striker, 41, walked away from the team's camp for the remainder of the international break, but said the story is not over.

"I can promise all my fans, and naturally especially all Portuguese people, that we will have one or more farewell matches. There will be time for that," said Ronaldo, who plays for Al-Nassr in Saudi Arabia.

"Despite the absolute breach of trust in the coach's word, as anyone who witnessed these conversations can attest, I should not have done it," Ronaldo said. "The responsibility is entirely mine. I apologize to the Portuguese people once again, but the breaking of one's word has, for me, a defining impact.

"I felt that, from that point on, my staying would only harm the group, and I did what I have always said I would do if I didn't feel useful: I picked up my bags and left," he continued before suggesting he accepted there may be a need for the coach or federation to punish him.

It was a long way from the emotional scenes in Rosario, Argentina. Instead of anger and recrimination, the overriding emotions there were gratitude, nostalgia and sadness.

"Not coming anymore is going to be deeply painful. I swear I'd like to play for the rest of my life, because playing for the Argentina national team is the most beautiful thing that can happen to you," Messi, who now plays his club football for Inter Miami in the US, told the 80,000 fans who had packed in to see him score a penalty in a 3-0 win over Benin.

For some, the contrasting evenings offered a neat synopsis of the pair. Ronaldo, less naturally gifted than his counterpart, has made the most of his talents with a singular focus which many believe often makes him prioritize himself over the team. He has had similar fallouts with managers and head coaches before, including Jose Mourinho at Real Madrid, Erik ten Hag at Manchester United and Portugal's 2022 World Cup coach, Fernando Santos.

Ronaldo's much-discussed aim to reach 1,000 career goals is held up by some as further evidence of where his motivations lie.

"You know what my goal is. I want to win trophies, and I want to reach that number (1,000 goals) that you all know. I will reach the number for sure, if no injuries," he said last December. He is currently on 979.

For others, that is simply what is required to be the best. While Messi's quiet character and general refusal to play out his life in public suggest a more humble character focused on helping the team, his genius has undoubtedly changed the teams around him. In the 2026 World Cup, he barely broke into a run but still led an otherwise fairly limited Argentina to the final with eight goals.

There will be no more Messi goals for Argentina and, despite his promises, no absolute assurances that the world will see Ronaldo in a Portugal shirt again. For now, both will head back to their club sides to play out their twilight years away from the intensity of Europe's top leagues. But wherever they go from here, the spotlight will keep following.

#938. Suspected pneumonic plague in Siberia: What we know about the disease

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

After a worker at a Russian plague research institute in Siberia died October 2, 2026, nearly 200 of her contacts were under medical observation. But authorities there denied pneumonic plague had caused the death.

UPDATE: Following initial reports raising concerns that a lab worker had died accidentally of pneumonic plague, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) said it was now also "closely monitoring the situation."

However, in a statement published October 6, 2026, the ECDC said that "based on the limited information currently available, there are no reports of secondary cases and no evidence of sustained human-to-human transmission that would cause further spread of the disease."

Following the death of a Russian laboratory worker, there is speculation about an outbreak of pneumonic plague, a highly contagious bacterial infection of the lungs. Russia denies there is a broader risk to the community.

Russian officials have said the laboratory worker's death was not caused by a plague pathogen. The 28-year-old died of "pneumonia of unknown origin," according to Russia's Interfax news agency, citing Rospotrebnadzor, Russia's disease-control agency. According to that agency, samples taken from the woman showed no microorganisms "related to her professional activities." This contradicts earlier information.

Local media had previously reported that the woman, who worked at an institute dedicated to fighting plague, had dropped a test tube.

She reportedly died on Friday, October 2, 2026, at the district hospital in Shelekhov, a town near Irkutsk in Siberia.

According to official Russian figures, authorities placed two hospitals and a children's clinic under quarantine. Local reports said staff at the institute were being quarantined at their workplace. The US news site Axios has reported that around 200 people who had been in contact with the woman had been placed under observation. There were no further reported infections.

Even so, distrust of how Russia handles information persists. On one of DW's Russian-language social media channels, a user wrote: "It wouldn't surprise me if this turns into a disaster now, both in terms of the spread and the quarantine measures."

All that is known for certain, at the time of writing and first publication (October 5, 2026), is that pneumonic plague is highly contagious.

Pneumonic plague is a form of plague that infects the lungs. Like every form of the disease, it is caused by Yersinia pestis, a bacterium usually found in small mammals, such as rodents and in the fleas that feed on them.

The World Health Organization describes the pneumonic form as the most virulent type of plague.

Its incubation period can be as short as 24 hours, and it is the only form that passes easily from person to person through respiratory particles released when a sick patient coughs. This is why, according to the WHO, it can trigger severe epidemics.

Symptoms appear quickly: high fever, headache and weakness, followed by a rapidly worsening pneumonia with shortness of breath, chest pain and a cough that often brings up blood.

A person can catch pneumonic plague by breathing in the bacteria directly, or by developing it when bubonic and/or septicemic plague go untreated and spread to the lungs.

Pneumonic plague is not a separate disease. It is one of three forms of plague: bubonic, septicemic and pneumonic.

What sets them apart is where the infection settles in the body, and that shapes how the disease spreads and how deadly it is.

Bubonic plague is the most common form and the one most people think of when they hear the word "plague." It usually follows a flea bite. The bacteria travel to the nearest lymph node, which swells into a painful lump known as a bubo. According to the WHO, it rarely spreads between people.

If bubonic plague goes untreated, the bacteria can move further through the body. If they spread into the bloodstream, the disease can become septicemic plague, which causes bleeding under the skin, tissue death in the fingers and toes, and organ failure. If the bacteria reach the lungs, the illness becomes pneumonic plague, the form that can pass from person to person through the air. Both septicemic and pneumonic plague can also develop directly, without a bubo appearing first.

Left untreated, bubonic plague kills between 30-60% of patients. The septicemic and pneumonic forms are always fatal without treatment.

Pneumonic plague is curable, but only if treatment starts quickly. The WHO warns that without treatment it can kill within 18 to 24 hours of the first symptoms.

Common antibiotics can cure the infection, and recovery rates are high when patients are treated within about a day of falling ill.

Speed is what decides the outcome. Doctors are advised not to wait for laboratory confirmation and to start antibiotics as soon as plague is suspected, according to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

The drugs themselves are not rare or specialized. They are standard, widely used antibiotics, and patients usually need a course of up to two weeks. To stop the infection spreading, patients are kept in isolation, and wearing masks helps protect those around them, the WHO says.

People close to a patient are treated as well, even if they have no symptoms. Both the WHO and the CDC recommend a seven-day course of preventive antibiotics for close contacts of anyone with pneumonic plague.

The WHO does not recommend a plague vaccine for the public. It reserves vaccination for high-risk groups, such as laboratory staff who work with the bacterium and health workers.
