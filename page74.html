2026 might not be the Year of Linux on the Desktop, but it is certainly the Year of Linux on the Beachfront, judging by some digital signage at the Spanish resort of Lloret de Mar.

As this signage is likely connected to the Internet, that Ubuntu update is probably a good idea. 26.04.1 is the latest LTS version and contains a raft of tweaks and improvements. Sadly, we expect beachgoers are probably more interested in sun, sand, and sangria than in keeping a Linux installation in tip-top shape.

The sign is a recent addition to the Passeig d'Agustí Font. Behind the photographer stand palm trees, with a beach and the azure waters of the Mediterranean Sea beyond.

Either way, this is a tourist destination, and the last thing the average administrator wants to be reminded of on their vacation is the need to keep code up to date. As such, we suspect many will simply ignore the distressed digital display and instead seek out somewhere shady to sit, where the seafront can be admired, beverages consumed, and the bork…? #3569. OHLAB experiments with colour-blocking and cork in old and new buildings of Terreno Barrio Hotel in Mallorca

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Mallorca-based architecture and interiors studio OHLAB has overseen the design of a hotel in Palma, including a renovated heritage building from 1935 and a new structure built from cross-laminated timber and cork.

The 41-room Terreno Barrio Hotel was envisioned as “a catalyst for the neighbourhood” of El Terreno, in the west of the Mallorcan city, serving as a community hub as well as a destination for tourists.

“Terreno is not conceived as a tourist object imposed on its context, but as a device for exchange – a hotel that, before being a hotel, aims to be an extension of the barrio,” said OHLAB founders Paloma Hernaiz and Jaime Oliver.

“This is not a slogan; it is the principle that orders programme, thresholds, atmospheres and materiality.”

The hotel’s reception is in the renovated 1935 building, a five-storey apartment block designed by local architect Francesc Casas, along with the restaurant and bar, a club, and a roof bar.

OHLAB refers to this building as “the stage”, the eye-catching focal point of the complex, while the new structure is described as “the bazaar”, its civic heart.

Wrapping a new public plaza, this second block houses the community-led spaces, including a cafe, coworking areas, a bookshop and gallery, pop-up retail, a gym, a spa and a cinema.

OHLAB adopted different strategies for the architecture and interior design of the two structures, creatively exploring the themes of building and material reuse in various ways.

“Here, circularity was understood not only as an environmental or material strategy, but also as an economic, social and territorial one, a way of keeping value within the local ecosystem by activating residents, culture, crafts, suppliers and everyday life,” they said.

In the renovated building, interior design revives the existing structure rather than covering it.

Communal spaces have a theatrical character, with patterned floor tiles and heavy curtains. Highlights include a golden mirror bar and a wool curtain installation by artist Sylvia Sanchez Montoya.

In the bedrooms, colourblocking creates a horizontal divide between top and bottom.

Floors and furniture feature matching terrazzo, produced by local tile manufacturer Huguet using the project’s demolition waste.

Materials are even more prominent in the seven-storey new building, thanks to the choice of materials. Cork clads the courtyard-facing north facade, while the other exterior walls feature timber louvres that shade the windows from direct sunlight.

Inside, the exposed CLT structure is brought to life through more playful use of colour and materials. The cafe and shop is a key example, with shades of brown and lime green extending across to the terrazzo floors and wooden furniture.

The bedrooms in this block have a different character, with murals by Mallorcan artist Albert Pinya, bathrooms housed inside mirrored boxes, and timber flooring that merges with joinery.

OHLAB was appointed by José Taltavull, who developed the concept for the hotel after being brought on board by hotelier Lydia Piñero.

Taltavull appointed graphic design office StudioRoses to oversee the hotel’s visual identity, which fed into OHLAB’s scheme.

Various elements were picked out in the same red as the hotel logo, including the ramped floor between the bar and reception, the stage in the club, and the sculptural stools located in the bar.

Most of the furniture was either designed by OHLAB or sourced from Mallorca-based manufacturers. A DJ table was made from cork leftover from the construction, while Mallorcan artist Pedro Óliver contributed the neon lighting pieces that feature throughout.

Terreno Barrio Hotel is the latest in a series of major openings in Palma in recent years, with the city in the midst of a cultural renaissance.

El Terreno is a major part of that story, particularly since Mallorca-based shoe brand Camper completed its 15,000-square-metre Project Gomila in 2023, which includes the new Jasper Morrison-designed Can Pa bakery.

OHLAB’s other projects in the city include the Can Santacilia residential development and the Paral·lel apartment complex.

#572. A tiny laser beam could make computer memory 1,000 times faster

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Scientists have developed a new way to control magnetism using light, a breakthrough that could eventually make digital data storage faster, more compact, and more energy-efficient.

The research, conducted by engineers at the University of California San Diego, was published in the journal Nature Communications.

Modern hard drives and other storage systems rely on tiny magnetic regions to encode digital information as 1s and 0s. Writing new data usually involves applying an external magnetic field to change the orientation of these regions. However, this process consumes considerable energy and places limits on how quickly information can be recorded.

The UC San Diego team has demonstrated an alternative that uses light to change these magnetic states, a technique known as optical switching. Light can deliver energy in extremely short bursts and can be focused onto remarkably small areas, potentially allowing magnetic information to be written much faster and with less energy. According to the researchers, optical switching could achieve speeds more than 1,000 times greater than conventional methods that depend on external magnetic fields.

Shrinking Laser Light To Improve Data Storage

To make this possible, the engineers used a specialized ultrafast laser and carefully modified the shape and size of its beam. The resulting beam was dramatically smaller than those employed in earlier experiments, with the researchers describing a reduction of tens of orders of magnitude.

Such precise control over light could also make it possible to store significantly more information within a limited amount of space. By directing laser energy onto smaller magnetic regions, future memory devices could potentially hold more data without increasing their physical size.

"The smaller the size of the beam, the smaller and more dense the optical memory," said study senior author Abdoulaye Ndao, professor in the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the UC San Diego Jacobs School of Engineering.

Until now, optical switching faced two important restrictions. Earlier experiments had succeeded only with extremely thin magnetic structures containing no more than three magnetic layers. The process also required light with a particular polarization, meaning its electromagnetic waves had to be oriented in a specific way.

"Past experiments had found that increasing the material's thickness beyond three layers suppressed optical switching," Ndao said. "That put a constraint on the overall thickness of the magnetic material and thus, its ability to retain memory in the long term."

These restrictions limited the potential usefulness of optical memory, particularly because thicker magnetic structures can help preserve stored information over extended periods.

Scientists Overcome a Major Magnetic Memory Barrier

By carefully reshaping and reducing the size of the laser beam, Ndao and his colleagues found a way around both obstacles.

They successfully demonstrated optical switching in a substantially thicker magnetic structure containing nine alternating layers of platinum and cobalt. The researchers also showed that the process worked without requiring a particular polarization of light.

"We've optically engineered the light to change the physics that's happening in the material at the micro- and nanoscale," said study first author Muhammad Waleed Khalid, an electrical and computer engineering Ph.D. student in Ndao's research group.

The technique works by concentrating laser energy into an extremely small region and delivering a sequence of ultrafast pulses.

The first pulses briefly heat a localized area of the magnetic material, causing a small region to reverse its magnetic orientation. Additional pulses gradually enlarge that reversed region until the new magnetic state becomes stable.

This carefully controlled sequence allows the researchers to manipulate magnetism in ways that had not been achieved using conventional laser beams.

"Working with a specialized laser allowed us to optically engineer the beam to have a certain shape and size," Khalid said. "That gave us the room to explore more fundamental physics, which cannot be done with conventional beam lasers."

An Unexpected Discovery That Required Repeated Testing

The unusual results initially proved difficult to establish within the scientific community. Because the effects differed so greatly from what earlier experiments had suggested was possible, the researchers needed extensive additional testing to confirm their observations.

"The effects we were seeing were so new and unusual that we had a difficult time convincing others in the field that our discovery was not a one-time fluke. We spent a lot of time and effort repeating and verifying our experiments to substantiate our work to the optics community," Khalid added.

The breakthrough emerged from a collaboration between researchers specializing in two areas that do not often intersect: optics, which focuses on light, and magnetic materials.

Ndao's optics research team worked with Eric Fullerton, an expert in thin-film magnetic materials at UC San Diego. Fullerton is a professor of electrical and computer engineering and of chemical and nano engineering, as well as the Endowed Chair Professor of the university's Center for Memory and Recording Research.

Combining these areas of expertise allowed the team to investigate an established problem in magnetic memory from a different perspective. Rather than developing a magnetic material with new properties, the researchers focused on changing how light interacts with an existing material.

"Instead of designing a new material to enable optical switching, we redesigned the light itself and showed new properties that were not previously thought to be possible," Ndao said.

The Next Challenge for Ultrafast Computer Memory

Although the results point toward promising possibilities for future storage technologies, significant technical obstacles remain before optical switching could be used in everyday electronic devices.

One major challenge is the specialized ultrafast laser required for the experiments. Current versions are not easily incorporated into computer chips, making practical integration difficult.

The researchers are considering other ways to achieve similar results, including identifying magnetic materials that could respond to laser systems more suitable for integration into electronic hardware.

They are also attempting to reduce the laser beam's size even further, with a target of just a few hundred nanometers. To accomplish this, the team is exploring optical structures capable of concentrating light into progressively smaller regions.

These efforts are aimed at understanding how precisely engineered light can manipulate magnetism at extremely small scales. If the remaining engineering challenges can be overcome, the approach could help enable a new generation of compact, high-density memory devices capable of writing digital information at much greater speeds.

#2109. 5 horror novels that take you to a cabin in the woods

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

You’ve driven all day. You’re tired. Your muscles are stiff and tight from sitting too long. You can’t wait to relax. Unwind. You smile when you see it. Finally! The quaint and cozy little cabin beckons from the end of a long dirt driveway bordered by tall trees on either side, their branches and leaves blocking what’s left of the sunlight.

You get out of the car. You stretch and take a spin, your eyes absorbing your surroundings, so quiet, so remote. Just what you needed. A weekend away from everything. To put your feet up. Recharge. Never expecting that you might not make it home alive.

How many horror books or movies have you read or watched that start with a similar setup? The cabin in the woods is an enduring horror trope because of its immediate ability to insert a cast of characters into an isolated situation where cellular service is nonexistent and neighbors (law enforcement included) are too far away to be of assistance. The cabin itself is a womb of sorts, safe and warm, flames crackling in the fireplace, providing protection from whatever dangers might be hiding out in the darkness, dangers that inevitably find their way in.

You feel looser than you have in weeks. Less stressed. The crick in your neck is finally gone. The peace and quiet of your isolated surroundings have done you well. But then you’re ripped from your restful sleep in the dead of night. You hear a thud. Then a screech. It’s the same screech you heard when you entered the cabin, the door’s hinges in need of oil. You bolt upright in bed. Hit the light. It doesn’t shine. The power is out. Your mind cycles. There’s a knife in the kitchen. A poker by the fireplace. Someone or something just opened your bedroom door.

Trapped with limited resources, the characters in these stories are often left to their own devices, making us wonder what would I do? in turn.

A longtime fan of the trope, I put my spin on it in my new book Savage Lake, about a mother and son who get stranded in the Northwoods of Wisconsin while trying to offload their decrepit family cabin, where more than just horrific memories linger.

For more family secrets, monsters, and madness, check out these five novels that will take you to a cabin in the woods.

A key component essential for many cabin in the woods stories is the inciting event that traps the characters in their isolated surroundings, making it impossible for them to escape the imminent horror. For some, it’s a demonic entity, an infectious disease, or a looping road that prevents them from fleeing. For novelist Paul Sheldon, it’s his number one fan who rescues him from a snowy car crash and then holds him hostage in her secluded Colorado farmhouse.

With no mention of anything otherworldly in this book, King masterfully reinforces that humans are the most frightening monsters of all.

Paul Tremblay, The Cabin at the End of the World

As if the idea of a home invasion isn’t scary enough, Tremblay found a way to make it heartbreaking too, with the fate of the world on the line, no less. Vacationing at a cabin on a New Hampshire lake, Eric, Andrew, and their daughter Wen have their peace cut short by four weapon-wielding strangers who claim that the world is going to end unless the family makes an impossible decision.

The ambiguity with which Tremblay writes makes the outcome even more horrific.

Forget the idea of a serene cabin retreat. And expect more than one monster, in more than one form, as Henry layers horror on top of horror in a secluded snowy mountain cabin, where a young woman named Mattie lives with her domineering husband, a man who won’t let her leave. But when Mattie discovers evidence of something ruthless in the snow—something with claws and razor teeth—she’s forced to make decisions that just might get her killed.

But which monster is she more afraid of? The one inside or the one outside the cabin?

Katherine Daniel, When the Night Comes Knocking

There’s a myth about a monster that seeks out guilty hearts for the sole purpose of consuming them up on Blood Mountain, deep in the North Georgia Appalachian range. For siblings Jane and Thomas, however, the myth becomes distressingly real.

Headed to the cabin their parents rented on Blood Mountain for the Thanksgiving holiday, they stop at a convenience store for a bag of marshmallows, and it’s there (the convenience store taking the place of the cabin—different location, same rules) that they get stuck after a bloodied man comes storming in, claiming that a monster with talons just attacked a man, and that the monster is still outside, waiting.

#2119. 5 crime novels set in grand hotels, from Peter James

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

People who read are intelligent, and it is my firm view that when we read a novel, the vast majority of us want more than just want a page-turning story, we want to learn stuff too. And all these novels I’ve chosen fulfill role that wonderfully, taking us behind the scenes into almost every conceivable aspect of life in a grand hotel.

We love hotels for so many different reasons. One is because they give us freedom from responsibility.  Someone else does everything for us, from making our bed, cooking our food and washing the dishes afterwards. Another is the kind of anonymity, the thrill of adventure, pampered luxury and the suspension of reality hotels give us. Another is perhaps the opportunity to be sexually naughty or criminally naughty….

My latest novel, Dead in Three Two One, begins with this paragraph: “The tanned man of forty standing at the front desk of Brighton seafront’s fabled Royal Courteney Hotel looked pleased with himself. The confident air of someone who had it all and knew it. Good looks, great hair, expensive suit, nice watch. His arm was draped around a beautiful woman, he had a smile on his face and a bomb in his suitcase.”

I had such fun researching this novel, including spending at day at Brighton’s Grand Hotel as a uniformed concierge—and getting a £5 tip for carrying the bags of an elderly couple! It is fascinating in a hotel what the staff see that guests don’t. One of my favorite observations I garnished from my research was from a hotel restaurant manager.

He said, “Look around a hotel restaurant at breakfast. You will see all the couples reading their papers or looking at their phones. If you see a couple actually talking to each other you can be pretty sure that either they are newlyweds, or they’re having an affair!”

In Hotel, Arthur Hailey encapsulated all of the above and this novel is as fresh and compelling today as when I first read it back, in 1966, soon after it was published. The elderly owner of the luxurious but financially troubled St Gregory Hotel in New Orleans aware of predators—in the form of major hotel chains—circling. Charismatic General manager, Peter McDermott, struggles to keep the establishment functioning and to make desperately needed improvements.

Hailey interweaves numerous stories involving guests and staff: a wealthy couple implicated in a hit-and-run, a professional thief preying on guests, romantic entanglements, racial prejudice and the wonderful ticking clock of an elevator that is going to fail and plunge many storeys.

When I put this book down all those years back, I was in awe of Hailey. I could not think of any ficition author who had done more detailed research, and it made me realize that’s what I wanted to do too. To write novels where the subject matter was as much a character as the people themselves. And above all to one day write a novel set in a magnificent hotel – and now I have!

In The Shining, Stephen King takes us into an altogether different experience—that of a hotel, closed for the winter and cut off from the world by snow. In Agatha Chrstie’s hands it would have been full of characters, one soon to be dead. But in King’s hands the occupants are not living guests and staff, but malevolent spirits.

Jack Torrance, a recovering alcoholic and frustrated aspiring author believes life has not delivered the success he feels he’s due.  When a job comes up as caretaker of the remote Overlook Hotel in Colorado, he hopes this will give him the time to really knuckle down to write, and moves in with his wife and young son Danny who turns out to possess a strong psychic ability—the shining—of the title.

But once snow cuts them off from the outside world, everything changes.  The hotel’s malevolent supernatural forces begin exploiting Jack’s weaknesses and gradually drive him towards madness and violence. Danny discovers that the Overlook contains terrifying echoes of past murders.

The novel turns an empty luxury hotel into a claustrophobic trap, and Jack Torrance, loving husband and father, into a monster. What I particularly love about this novel is how an empty hotel can turn so instantly from a joyful pleasure dome into a place of gloom and terror.

Here is yet another very different vibe—this time the hotel as a prison. Moscow’s Metropol Hotel is not a prison in the sense it having officers and cells, but for Russian aristocrat  Count Alexander Rostov, an aristocrat spared execution in 1922 by a Bolshevik tribunal, it is where he is sentenced to spend the rest of his life under at first a loose, but then increasingly, tight house arrest.

Slowly his privileges are taken away when he is evicted from his luxurious suite and confined to a tiny attic room. The impact of the communist regime gradually erodes his quality of life, including to his dismay, the quality of his beloved wines. But it forces him to accept the situation, moving from a life of privilege to a more humble citizen, stripped of his title and trappings of wealth, and to adapt.

Over the following three decades he befriends staff and guests, against the almost unseen backdrop of enormous political upheaval in Soviet Russia, Rostov’s restricted physical world becomes emotionally expansive. The Metropol functions as a miniature society—sanctuary, prison, workplace and observation post on history.

But for me the richest aspect of this beautifully written novel is how it it shows, as Rostov comes to realize,  that in some ways the less someone has, the more they have, and that nothing can ever totally destroy the human spirit.

This novel gives us another different perspective of a hotel, seen through the eyes of a maid. Molly Gray, on the spectrum and socially awkward, works at the luxurious Regency Grand Hotel at an unspecified location on the North American continent. She struggles to read people, taking everything literally and is dangerously trusting. She likes her ordered life where she finds comfort in rules, in routine and carrying out her work immaculately.

The curveball comes when she enters the suite of a rich but very obnoxious guest Charles Black, to find him dead. The shock discovery soon spirals into a nightmare for Molly when her trusting nature and connections with some distinctly dubious hotel guests turn her into the prime suspect for the murder. But as she struggles to clear her name, she finds behind the respectable façade of the hotel lurks a hotbed of criminal activity.

The joy of this novel, in the context of this article, is seeing the hotel through Molly’s highly ordered, literal view of the world. The hotel setting works so well because it allows a maid like Molly to enter private spaces while at the same time being virtually invisible to guests.

Here the hotel setting is a perfect “locked room” mystery and in absolutely classic—and delicious—Agatha Christie style.  What could be more idyllic than the setting of the very posh and exclusive Jolly Roger Hotel on an island off the coast of Devon? What better place to get away from it all and relax, safe and secure in a little piece of paradise, and surrounded by fellow guests. All of them are seemingly respectable and there for the same reason—to chill out in pampered luxury—not to chill out in a mortuary fridge….

But then this is Agatha Christie, where no good intention goes ununished….And the fact that Hercule Poirot has decided to holiday here himself makes us absolutely certain that a murder is about to happen. We are not to be disappointed! Beautiful actress Arlena Marshall is soon found strangled on a secluded beach. She had been openly flirting with another guest, Patrick Redfern, despite the presence of both their spouses.

But then, in true Christie style, almost everyone at the hotel appears to have a motive. The beautifully described elegance of the hotel is a wonderful counterpoint to the grim brutality of murder.

#2125. America’s lady Bluebeard: the strange murder trial of Lyda Trueblood

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The following is an excerpt from Ordinary Wife: The True Story of America’s Forgotten Serial Killer.

It was nearly Halloween, the spooky season of mischief, ghost stories, and macabre costumes. While children might have been unaware, adults in Twin Falls knew that the tale unfolding in court was a homegrown horror story of unthinkable proportions. Disturbed graves, reexamined corpses, poison used to murder the unsuspecting—it was worse than the makers of silent horror films could imagine, and darker than any Bram Stoker novel.

Because at the center of the unthinkable was a woman who had lived among them. Grew up there and attended high school. A woman who married and buried one after another.

Lyda Trueblood—and whatever other surname she had— brought a kind of evil they’d never imagined, but at the same time, they couldn’t get enough of it either.

Would she testify? Her attorneys gave no indication. Speculation was rife among onlookers, newspapermen, and officials. Lyda had proved candid—too much so—and no one knew if her counsel would risk putting her under oath.

For nearly five weeks, Lyda sat silently in the courtroom. She watched, saying nothing, as witness after witness took the stand. Ranch hands, physicians, nurses, insurance clerks, neighbors, chemists—all recounting statements she allegedly made over six years. Through it all, she remained composed at the defense table, hands clasped in her lap, eyes forward, her back to the gallery and onlookers.

On the morning of October 25, 1921, before calling its first witness, the defense, led by Big Bill Guthrie, asked the judge for a special recess. The court withdrew. Ten minutes later, proceedings resumed. Judge Babcock looked to the defense: “Call your next witness.”

This was what everyone had come for. They wanted to see her expression, draw their own conclusions about who she was and what she did.

Lyda walked to the stand wearing the same outfit she’d worn every day of the trial, a brown suit and fur collar. She seemed nervous. Her voice was barely audible as she placed her hand on the bible, swearing to tell the truth. She took her seat, her hands folded in her lap.

Chief defense counsel Guthrie began his direct examination.

He started at the beginning, asking his client about her relationship with Ed Meyer and the months before their marriage.

“I had known Edward Meyer about a year before I married him, August 10, 1920, at Pocatello. During our marriage, I lived at the Rogerson Hotel here in Twin Falls. I would go down and visit him off and on, and towards the last I stayed with him the three days before he was taken sick.”

She moved quickly from courtship to illness.

“I saw him every day after he had been taken to the hospital, until he died. Right from the start, he was a sick man, and on September 2, one of the nurses told me my husband came near dying that day. I could see no improvement from day to day.”

Lyda described the final night without embellishment or any real emotion.

“Sunday, I stayed with him all through the night. The next day, he hadn’t improved. September 7, he died at two o’clock, while I was there.”

When asked about her husband’s final hours, she spoke slowly, with a well-modulated voice. She told of her visit to the Blue Lakes Ranch on August 25, 1920, where her husband fell sick soon after.

She described his condition in careful detail, symptoms the prosecution claimed were arsenical poisoning, while the defense insisted were really from tainted food. He asked about what had made up the meal Carrie Howe had served.

“Only warmed-over beans, boiled potatoes, and canned corn. That was all.”

When asked about her husband’s health before marrying him, she disclosed something she hadn’t mentioned before.

“He wasn’t well and kept complaining to me,” she said. “On July 3, we drove to Salt Lake City, and I had to drive the machine because he wasn’t well enough. He kept coughing. I asked him what the matter was. He said he had never recovered from the effects of pneumonia, which he had had in 1913.”

Earlier in the trial, Meyer’s examining physician had testified that he was in sound health when he applied for the life insurance. Hospital records from 1913 reflected a diagnosis of typhoid.

Under oath, Lyda’s account stood in sharp contrast to that earlier testimony. She had traced the story carefully—a husband already weakened, a steady decline, a wife at his bedside through the night.

Illness, she suggested, had been inevitable.

Defense lawyer Guthrie narrowed his focus.

“At any time did you administer arsenic to Meyer?”

Next, the questioning shifted from the trial’s key accusation to the source in question, the flypaper.

“Please explain about the flypaper, which you purchased and took to the Blue Lakes ranch.”

Lyda testified Ed called her from the ranch on August 25 and asked her to bring flypaper when she arrived the following day.

“I went to a Twin Falls drug store and purchased two packages and took them with me the day I went to the ranch. When I got there, I gave the packages to Mrs. Carrie Howe, the cook at the boarding house, and she placed them around in saucers of water.”

“You heard the drug clerk testify that you bought twenty packages. Did you ever purchase any such amount?”

Lyda told the court she hadn’t done any such thing.

“I bought the flypaper from that store,” she said, “but I only bought two packages.”

The state had presented evidence that arsenic could be extracted by soaking the small black squares of flypaper in water.

Under oath, Lyda denied bringing more flypaper than the prosecution claimed. She maintained it was set out openly, in plain sight, as any household might do to combat summer flies.

She also testified that Ed had been taken to the hospital at her direction and that he had not appeared gravely ill during the journey.

Under her lawyer’s questioning, the defendant recounted the illnesses of the other men who’d died while in her orbit. She said Edward Dooley had been in poor health before his fatal sickness, and that she knew of no insurance carried in her favor.

Gordon McHaffe, she testified, had an accident with a colt, followed by influenza.

“During his fatal illness,” she went on, “I gave him all the attention any wife could.”

When the death of her third husband, Harlan Lewis, was raised, Lyda attributed his demise to a salmon salad and alcohol.  She denied packing his clothing before his death and any knowledge of any flypaper found concealed in a barrel in the cellar.

Throughout direct, Lyda calmly rejected all suggestions that she had anticipated insurance proceeds of any kind, nor had she concealed prior marriages.

With Guthrie finished with direct, Prosecutor Frank Stephan stood for cross-examination. His questions were shorter, a rapid-fire grilling.

“Why was it then you gave your first name as Eva Edith on the marriage license issued to you and Paul Southard?”

“My husband put that down,” she said. “He used to call me that, and so did my fourth husband sometimes.”

“Why did you sign yourself Lyda McHaffe after your marriage to Edward Meyer?”

“That was my business name,” she said without any hesitation.

The prosecutor moved on to her other marriages.

“Didn’t you deny your marriage to Harlan Lewis of Billings to Charles Thomas, Twin Falls insurance man, and tell A.W. Davidson, Billings insurance man, not to mention that fact in Twin Falls while he was there?”

“Did you ever say to Mr. Johnson, Twin Falls automobile dealer, relative to the purchase of a car, you expected ‘about ten thousand dollars from the sale or rental of land in Canada’ as he testified here at the trial?”

Again, another shake of her head. “I did not.”

“On August 16, 1920, did you not inform Mr. Magel, Twin Falls auto dealer, that you would have in your possession from eight thousand to ten thousand dollars, September 15? Mr. Magel testified this on the witness stand.”

“I made no statement like that,” she said.

The state produced a purchase memorandum signed by Lyda McHaffe.

“You didn’t forget your name then, did you?”

Lyda glanced at the defense table before answering.

“No,” she said. “My husband advised me to sign my name ‘McHaffe’ in contracting for the purpose of the machine.”

Stephan made a face and looked over at the jury.

Lyda shrugged. “I don’t know my husband’s handwriting.”

Two automobile dealers. Two cars, one contracted while wed to Harlan Lewis. The other while married to Ed Meyer. The money to pay for both? Well, the prosecutor was trying to prove that the life insurance policies would pay out as soon as Lyda became a widow. Lawyer Stephan shifted the testimony.

“Didn’t you tell Mrs. Howe, the cook, you were coming down with typhoid as the reason for not coming down to the ranch and that you were a typhoid carrier?”

Lyda shook her head, this time emphatically.

“When you purchased it at the Twin Falls drug store, did Mr. All not say, ‘My gracious woman, what are you going to do with all this poison flypaper?’ and did you not reply that you were taking it to the ranch because they used a great deal there?”

“I made no such statement,” Lyda answered.

She went on to deny any knowledge of the flypaper found in the bedroom at the Blue Lakes boarding house. She said ranch employee Ben Squier was mistaken in his testimony that her husband had come to the tent house after the disastrous evening meal.

“He was in bed with me until one o’clock at night,” she said. She also denied telling Ben Squier that Dr. Coughlin said her husband could not live, or that she had replied, “I am going to work on him anyway.”

She never asked Mrs. Howe to serve as a witness “if anything happens.” She denied making the oft-repeated remark: “They can cut him all to pieces.”

“Denial after denial,” wrote the Statesman’s staff correspondent. “The accused woman scoffed at the testimony of prominent Twin Falls businessmen, former friends and employees at the Blue Lakes ranch.”

Headlines highlighted in on her demeanor. Some reported she was calm, others noted nervousness.

Fittingly, it was Halloween when the defense rested.

#2135. Jim Ruland on writing a campus heist novel inspired by Bad Bunny and ‘Bullet Train’

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I should have suspected my wife was up to something.

When we go to the movies together, usually one of us has to compromise. I like horror movies and action flicks; Nuvia prefers comedies and animated features. She works long hours and travels a lot. She doesn’t want to spend her down time watching something that’s going to stress her out or make her anxious. Nuvia wants to be entertained.

So in the fall of 2022 when she suggested we go see Bullet Train, I was puzzled.

Bullet Train, directed by David Leitch, is a comedy action movie about five assassins who are all passengers on a high speed train in Japan. One of the assassins is played by Brad Pitt. Another has beef with the yakuza. The trailer promised a gory mess with nonstop action. I bought the tickets faster than you can say “arterial blood spray.”

I didn’t stop and wonder why Nuvia wanted to see a Brad Pitt shoot-em-up, but I got my answer when Benito A. Martínez Ocasio appeared on the screen as a Mexican assassin known as “The Wolf.”

I didn’t know Benito was the birth name of Puerto Rican superstar Bad Bunny, but I knew what he looked like, and as soon as The Wolf was introduced I knew why she’d picked this movie. I looked over at Nuvia as if to say, “Really?” and she hid her smile behind a fistful of popcorn.

I didn’t mind being tricked by my reggaeton-loving spouse because I loved everything about Bullet Train. I loved the conceit, which is preposterous, and the over-the-top action, which is first rate—Leitch has a background as a stuntman and stunt coordinator.

But what Bullet Train lacks in realism it makes up for in lore. Sins of the father visited upon the son. The daughter searching for payback upon her cruel father. The son who has disgraced his family’s honor. Brothers who can’t stand each other but will take a bullet so the other might live. And we haven’t even gotten to the yakuza gang known as the White Death decked out in dark suits and frightening masks.

One of the reasons why Bullet Train is so rich in lore is because it was adapted from a 2010 novel by the award-winning Japanese writer Kōtarō Isaka, who also writes short stories and manga. Bullet Train’s plot isn’t complicated, but it’s complex. It’s packed with information. Sorting through all the assassins’ shifting allegiances and loyalties distracts the viewer from how implausible it all is, which makes it so much more satisfying than the one-note, revenge-obsessed thrillers that Hollywood is so obsessed with.

Bullet Train made me yearn for the nineties when it seemed like there was a crafty new crime thriller in the theaters every week, and John Woo, Quentin Tarantino, Robert Rodriguez, and Guy Ritchie were showing us things we’d never seen on the big screen.

I loved Bullet Train so much I went back to the theater to see it a second time. Now that I had a handle on the plot, I was able to appreciate how each assassin represented an archetype and brought their own perspective to the story. Walking out of the theater, I wondered why crime stories like that rarely get made anymore. So I decided to write my own.

In 1993 I moved to Flagstaff, Arizona, to pursue a graduate degree in English. The university waived the tuition and paid a modest stipend to candidates willing to teach composition to incoming freshman. I signed the contract and was crammed into an office with three other part-time English instructors.

One of those instructors was an Arizona native named James who took delight in my obsession with old paperback crime novels. James was a study in contradictions. He was a poet who set the bench press record at his high school gym. In his spare time he made his own ammunition.

On more than one occasion he approached me while we were alone in the office. “Hey, Ruland. Let’s go rob a bank. Who would suspect us? We’re English teachers!”

James was enamored with our status as part-time professors. I suppose we all were. Even though we were paid a pittance and exploited for our intellectual labor, we weren’t that far removed from working shitty, part-time jobs at bars, bookstores, and record shops. You could still do that in the late eighties and early nineties and earn just enough to pay for your rent—as long as you didn’t expect anyone to take you seriously.

Now the script was flipped: our roles as adjunct professors at a university conferred dignity and respect, and required that we take the job seriously. Some of us were better at that than others. Our students referred to us as “Mister” and “Missus,” which was hilarious and strange because we lived check-to-check in shitty apartments and when we went out at night it was for happy hour specials in awful bars our students shunned.

We didn’t know that cosplaying college professors without the benefits, compensation, and status that tenured faculty enjoy was the dirtiest job of them all, but we would soon find out.

I never did take my colleague up on his offer to rob a bank, but the idea of a group of English teachers who plot a heist and embark on a crime sprees rattled around my imagination for thirty years. Bullet Train showed me a way in to the story. Instead of five assassins, I had four adjunct English teachers. Instead of a claustrophobic high-speed train, I had an insular college town. Instead of the yakuza I had university administrators who have perfected the art of death by bureaucracy.

I turned these English teachers into archetypes and set the ambitions of The Poet, The Punk, The Gambler, and The Storyteller against corrupt university boosters, local leg breakers, and fraternity drug dealers who conspire to defeat their outlaw ambitions.

As for weaving lore into the story, that was the most enjoyable part of the project. Hovering over our heroes is the legacy of a cult writer of paperback westerns, and a mysterious golden pen that passes in and out of the hands of all the major and minor characters like a MacGuffin in a Hitchcock movie.

Naturally, I set the novel at a mid-size university that resembled the school I attended in Flagstaff, Arizona in 1993. Flagstaff is a place that many people pass through but few people really know. Of course, by 1993 Arizona was no longer the Wild West of yore, but it was a lot wilder back then than it is now.

I’m glad I waited as long as I did to write this story because now I understand the desire to rob and steal, at least for my crew of overeducated English teachers, is rooted in class. These aren’t hardened criminals like the yakuza who make up the White Death in Bullet Train, but people driven by desperation because they are being exploited by a system that doesn’t value them. The university could pay them a living a wage, but chooses not to. Who are the real criminals here?

Less than six months after I saw Bullet Train (and unofficially joined the Bad Bunny fan club), I had a draft of a new crime novel sitting on my desk. It starts as a campus novel and ends in territory mapped out by the four apostles of neo-noir: Ritchie, Rodriguez, Tarantino and Woo.

I have remained lifelong friends with my former colleagues from Flagstaff. There’s not a week that goes by where I don’t communicate with someone from those bad old days, and most of them have read Mightier Than the Sword and offered me their input. They encouraged me not to play it safe, and I honored those requests.

While The Punk, The Gambler, and The Storyteller are all composites, The Poet is pretty clearly based on my old friend and colleague James. After teaching composition and poetry for a few years he bought a dingy old pool hall and turned it into a beloved institution in downtown Flagstaff where a story, poem, or song is always welcome.

I can’t think of a better use of a poetry degree. It’s also a great place to plot a crime spree—a fictional crime spree, of course.

And Benito, if you’re reading this, I’ve got just the part for you….

Jim Ruland is an old punk who lives by the sea. He is a bestselling chronicler of punk rock, writes the weekly Substack Message from the Underworld, and is a veteran of the U.S. Navy. His new novel, Mightier than the Sword, will be published by Rare Bird on October 27, 2026.

#2787. 3 Sports Science Hacks That Don’t Work

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

It’s more fun for everyone when a study works. I love writing about research suggesting that, say, drinking beet juice or zapping your brain with electricity or simply smiling will make you faster.

But this weakness—which, to be fair, I share with just about everyone—has a cost. If I only write about positive studies, we get the impression that pretty much everything works. Ten scientists might study a new supplement, and nine of them find it doesn’t work, but if we only read about the tenth study, we assume the new supplement is awesome. This is publication bias in action.

So, in recognition of this danger, I’m sharing here the results of three studies that didn’t work—or at least, where my first reaction to the findings was “Oh, that’s too bad.” In reality, all three of these studies were successful, because they answered a question that researchers were wondering about. There’s some valuable data and interesting insights here—just not necessarily the ones we were hoping for.

First up is a test of the idea that ketones boost recovery from hard training or competition. I first wrote about ketones back in 2018 when they were all the rage in the pro cycling peloton as a potential performance-booster. That idea has gone out of fashion, but subsequent research (which I wrote about here) has suggested that the real magic of ketones might be in enhancing recovery and warding off overtraining.

The new study, which appears in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise, comes from the same Belgian group at KU Leuven that has pioneered the idea of ketones for recovery. They decided to put their theoretical ideas to the test by having 18 cyclists complete a brutal eight-day simulated cycling stage race, modeled after the Absa Cape Epic mountain bike tour. The cyclists were given either KetoneAid ketones or a placebo after each stage and just before bed each evening.

The researchers hoped that the ketones would accelerate post-exercise recovery, enhance sleep time and quality, and preserve physiological and cognitive function in the face of fatigue. Put all this together, and they hoped the cyclists would go faster on ketones. In the end, none of this happened. The cyclists got tired and slower, but there was no difference between the ketone and placebo groups.

There are a few possible explanations. One is that the sample size was too small to pick up differences. Another is that eight days of racing more than three hours per day didn’t fatigue the cyclists enough for the benefits of ketones to show up. A further possibility is that, despite earlier promising studies, ketones simply don’t make a practical difference in the real world. At this point, we don’t know which of these is correct. But the results should put a ceiling on our expectations: even if ketones work to fight cumulative fatigue, the effect can’t be enormous or it would have shown up here.

What used to be called low-level laser therapy (or sometimes red light therapy) has now been rebranded as photobiomodulation, because you can use LEDs instead of lasers. The idea is that certain wavelengths of light in the red and infrared range can interact with your cells to produce beneficial effects. It’s been around for a long time: Hungarian scientists in the 1960s claimed that it accelerated hair growth and wound healing in mice; more recently, Alberto Salazar reportedly shipped a full-body photobiomodulation unit to Brazil for his athletes to use before the Rio Olympics in 2016.

There are a lot of claims about what miracles this technology can perform, but a big one is that the relevant wavelengths of light are absorbed by a specific enzyme in your mitochondria, boosting mitochondrial function and other metabolic pathways. This has been demonstrated in test-tube experiments, but never in humans—so Luc van Loon’s research group at the University of Maastricht decided to test the claim, publishing the results in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise.

The study involved 12 volunteers who got three five-minute doses of photobiomodulation with a K-Laser Cube Plus 30 on one leg, and three placebo doses with the light turned off on the other leg. The researchers took muscle biopsies and skin samples to test mitochondrial function and the expression of genes related to muscle metabolism half an hour after the treatment.

The results are easy to sum up: there was no difference between legs, other than a bit of surface warming at the skin. The claims for this kind of technology are so broad (Achilles tendinopathy! lumbar discopathy! pressure ulcers! range of motion!) that a single study can’t possibly debunk all of them. But these results should give you pause.

This study in the Journal of Sports Sciences, from a French group led by Thomas Rupp and Sébastien Racinais, is a “glass half-empty or half-full?” test. They put 22 volunteers through a simulated soccer game, then gave them 25 minutes in either an ice bath (at 57 degrees Fahrenheit) or hot tub (at 108 degrees Fahrenheit) immediately afterwards, a day later, and two days later. They tested soreness, muscle fatigue, and neuromuscular function.

Once again, the results are easy to sum up: there were no differences between the groups. To me, the logical conclusion is that neither of them does anything. It seems highly unlikely that doing a thing, and also doing the opposite of that thing, will both produce positive effects. Whatever cellular mechanism you think is at play here—changes in blood flow, inflammatory signals, antioxidant levels, or whatever—cold and heat should have opposite effects. If they both produce the same results, those changes probably don’t matter.

That’s not how the researchers frame it, though. The actual purpose of the study was to test hot-tubbing for recovery; the ice bath group was the “standard of care” control group. If you assume that ice baths are effective, then the fact that hot-tubbing produces the same result means that it’s “a practical and effective alternative” for recovery, as the paper concludes.

Personally, much as I love how ice baths feel after a hard workout, I’m not sold on their physiological benefits. There’s still debate among scientists, and I’m open to being convinced, but it looks to me like a placebo—which means “just as good as an ice bath” is a half-empty glass.
