#1252. The Outdoor Plants You Should Bring Inside Before the Weather Turns Cold

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Plants such as tropical flora and citrus trees need a little extra attention when temperatures drop.

Every year, your outdoor plants spend the summer sunbathing on the patio and enjoying the fresh, humid air. But their alfresco era must come to an end once temperatures begin to drop. Since they can’t just put on a sweater, the next best option is to transfer them inside.

Not every plant needs to make the move at once, though. Knowing which ones are most sensitive to cold can help you prioritize. Many tropical varieties, for instance, might become stressed when frigid temperatures arrive. And if you wait too long, one cold night could leave them a lot less healthy.

Here are the plants you should bring in first once the weather starts to turn, and how you can help them adjust to indoor life.

Before moving your outdoor plants inside, inspect them thoroughly. Check under the leaves, along the stems, and in the soil for pests like aphids, mealybugs, scale insects, and spider mites. Rinse foliage with water, wipe down the pots, and get rid of any dead leaves and debris. It’s also worth keeping these plants separate from your other indoor plants for a few weeks to stop any undetected pests from spreading.

You’ll also want to prepare your plants for the change in environment. Indoor spaces usually have less sunlight and humidity than outdoor spaces, and plants tend to grow more slowly in the winter. So pick a good location for each plant based on how much light it needs. Once inside, check the soil moisture to avoid waterlogging before giving the plants a drink.

If you have a monstera, philodendron, bird of paradise, or palm outside for the summer, bring these in first. Many favorite houseplants come from tropical or subtropical areas and can’t handle cold nights. Protect tropical plants before it gets frosty, or below 50 degrees Fahrenheit.

Find a spot with bright, indirect light, like near an east-facing window or a few feet from a sunny south-facing window. Tropical plants can also struggle with dry indoor air when the heat is on, so put them close together to make the air more humid (a humidifier can help as well).

After you relocate your plant, don’t worry if it loses a few leaves while adapting to lower indoor light—just give it time to adjust.

Potted citrus trees such as lemon, lime, and calamondin trees aren’t fans of cold weather and should be moved inside before frost arrives. Put them in a sunny place and water just enough to keep the soil from drying out, but not so much that it gets soggy.

A bright window with several hours of direct sunlight is best. If your home doesn’t get a lot of light, try using a grow light. Like tropical plants, citrus trees might drop some leaves as they acclimate. But if they keep losing leaves, they might need more light or water.

If you decide to prune, don’t cut too much just to make a tree fit; only trim a little so it keeps as many healthy leaves as possible.

With its vibrant flowers, tropical hibiscus (Hibiscus rosa-sinensis) offers a burst of summery color as the seasons change. But it needs to be sheltered inside since it can’t survive freezing weather.

Move yours to a sunny window before nights become consistently cold. If it’s too big for its winter spot, you can moderately trim it. Once inside, it might shed leaves or stop blooming as the days get shorter, but that doesn’t mean it’s dying. Be patient, and keep the soil moist but not sodden.

A grow light can also help if there isn’t enough sun, though your hibiscus might not bloom as much as it did outside.

Don’t forget about your outdoor herb garden, especially if you want fresh herbs for cooking during the chillier seasons. Rosemary is a good choice to bring inside if it can’t survive the cold in your area. Bay laurel and lemon verbena are other tender herbs that might need protection.

Put these herbs by your sunniest window, and use extra lighting if there isn’t enough natural light. Most indoor herbs need at least five hours of sunlight each day. Make sure the pots drain well, especially for rosemary, which likes its soil to dry out a bit between waterings.

Not all herbs need the same care. Basil is very sensitive to cold, but perennial herbs like mint and chives can withstand cooler temperatures, depending on the type. Check how hardy each plant is before deciding if it needs to come inside for the winter.

Annual geraniums (Pelargonium) and coleus can’t really handle frost, but you can preserve them for another growing season.

Take a cutting from a healthy stem, remove the lower leaves, and put it in a rooting medium. Keep it moist while roots grow, then move the new plant into a pot and put it somewhere bright.

You can keep established plants indoors for the winter if you have enough space and light. But for large or sprawling plants, cuttings might be easier since they take up less space and give you a jump start on next year’s garden.

Succulents are known for surviving dry conditions, but that doesn’t mean they can tolerate the cold. A lot of the popular ones, such as echeverias and jade plants, can be damaged if left in cold conditions for too long.

Place sensitive varieties inside before the weather freezes, and check what each type needs instead of using the same rule for all of them. Some cacti and succulents can handle cooler temperatures better than tropical houseplants, but others need to stay much warmer.

Since succulents grow slowly or rest during the winter, they usually need less water than in the summer. Let the soil dry out between waterings, and make sure they get several hours of sun or bright light each day.

#4064. Daily Exercise Weakens the Grip of Genetic Risk for Depression

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A nationwide genomic and wearable tracking study led by Kansas State University reveals that daily physical activity significantly attenuates the impact of high genetic susceptibility to major depressive disorder. Utilizing multimodal data from the NIH’s All of Us Research Program, combining genetic profiles, electronic health records, and longitudinal Fitbit metrics, researchers found that higher daily step counts and minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity directly weaken the relationship between polygenic vulnerability and clinical symptom severity in adults.

Major depressive disorder remains one of the leading causes of disability worldwide, contributing to profound personal, social, and economic burdens. While large-scale genome-wide association studies have firmly established that depression possesses a complex, polygenic architecture driven by thousands of small-effect DNA variants, having a high genetic predisposition does not guarantee a clinical diagnosis.

Understanding which lifestyle factors can effectively buffer or modify this inherent biological vulnerability is a paramount goal of precision psychiatry.

Now, a multi-institutional study led by researchers at Kansas State University, in collaboration with the University of Texas at San Antonio, the University of Rhode Island, and Vanderbilt Health, provides objective evidence that regular physical activity directly weakens the expressive weight of genetic susceptibility to depression.

Publishing in the journal Nature Mental Health, the team harnessed rich longitudinal datasets from the National Institutes of Health’s All of Us Research Program to evaluate how wearable-measured movement interacts with polygenic risk.

A critical limitation in historical psychiatric research has been the reliance on binary classifications—treating depression merely as present or absent—and subjective, self-reported activity surveys that are prone to recall bias.

To overcome these barriers, the research team analyzed comprehensive electronic health records, genomic data, survey responses, and continuous, objective movement metrics gathered from participants’ personal Fitbit devices.

“Depression is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide, so understanding how we might reduce its burden is important,” said lead investigator Denver Brown, PhD, assistant professor in the School of Health Sciences in K-State’s College of Health and Human Sciences. “As we move toward more personalized medicine, understanding a patient’s genetic risk could help inform conversations about lifestyle, including the potential role of physical activity in helping prevent depression or manage its symptoms.”

The investigators evaluated two primary dimensions: formal clinical diagnoses and the spectrum of symptom severity.

“If we’re looking at depression as just a ‘yes or no’ for diagnosis, we’re missing a lot of the variability that comes with a diagnosis,” Brown explained. “Someone may have some symptoms, but not a formal diagnosis. Some might have moderate symptoms, and others experience substantial symptoms.”

When assessing common wearable activity benchmarks, specifically daily step counts and total minutes spent in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), the data demonstrated a consistent, protective interaction.

Among adults carrying high polygenic risk scores for major depression, those who consistently maintained higher levels of daily movement exhibited markedly lower symptom severity than their sedentary genetic peers. In essence, regular physical activity altered the phenotypic expression of their DNA, dampening the expected biological burden of inherited vulnerability.

The physiological mechanisms underlying this buffering effect are multifaceted: aerobic movement stimulates neurogenesis in the hippocampus via brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), downregulates systemic neuroinflammation, and recalibrates hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis stress reactivity—pathways frequently dysregulated in individuals with high genetic depression liability.

While the present investigation centered on adults within the All of Us cohort, Brown’s broader research program explores movement behaviors across critical neurodevelopmental inflection points, particularly in adolescents and emerging adults.

“Our research is important, particularly for adolescents and emerging adults, because these life stages coincide with the peak onset of anxiety and depression, and they also coincide with a substantial drop-off in physical activity engagement,” Brown said.

By integrating wearable telemetry with genomics, the findings pave the way for clinicians to draft individualized lifestyle interventions tailored to a patient’s biological profile, prescribing specific daily step thresholds or activity durations to mitigate familial mental health risks.

“With health, there is not a one-size-fits-all approach,” Brown concluded. “By drawing on the wealth of data available from programs like All of Us, we can better understand who may be most vulnerable to chronic health conditions and mental disorders, and inform lifestyle recommendations tailored to their needs. We’re really making strides toward a more personalized approach to health, and this study is a step in that direction.”

Associations and interactions between genetic vulnerability and physical activity with MDD in the All of Us Research Program

Both genetic liability and physical activity (PA) are well-established contributors to major depressive disorder (MDD), yet large-scale studies integrating device-assessed PA with genomic risk remain limited, and prior gene–environment research has focused almost exclusively on MDD onset rather than symptom severity.

We analyzed 23,517 participants from the All of Us Research Program (mean age, ~56 years; range, 18–92 years; 67.2% female) with whole-genome sequencing and long-term Fitbit-derived PA data (mean wear time of 582 days). PA was operationalized using daily minutes of moderate-to-vigorous PA and steps, and MDD was defined using a dual-source phenotype combining electronic health records and self-reported lifetime diagnosis.

Here we examined both MDD diagnosis and electronic health record-derived symptom severity using regression models testing main and interaction effects of polygenic risk scores (PRSs) and PA, adjusting for demographic covariates.

Across all PA metrics, a higher PRS was robustly associated with increased likelihood of MDD diagnosis, whereas higher PA was associated with lower risk; however, no significant PRS × PA interactions were observed for MDD onset.

By contrast, for symptom severity, both PRS and PA showed significant main effects, and consistent negative interactions emerged across all PA operationalizations and different severity definitions, indicating that higher PA attenuated the association between genetic risk and depressive symptom burden. These interaction patterns were robust across different model specifications.

Together, these findings suggest that genetic and behavioral factors contribute largely independently to MDD onset but interact in shaping symptom expression, highlighting PA as a potential modifier of genetic vulnerability in depression and a potential target for precision mental health interventions.

It is about being black and young in America; feeling so full of potential and ambitions, but caught inside an antiquated and violent system of racist restriction.

The narrative, based on the 1974 novel of the same name by James Baldwin, is about two partners, a nineteen-year-old girl, Tish Rivers (Kiki Layne), and her twenty-two-year-old fiancé Alonzo Hunt, who goes by “Fonny” (Stephan James). They have been best friends their entire lives, and when they are old enough, their friendship grows into a beautiful, sincere love. Fonny, who is a sculptor residing in New York City’s West Village, is excited to find a loft where he and Tish can raise their future family. But then he is arrested, by a white cop with a pre-existing vendetta against him, on charges of raping a woman. Fonny is innocent, but he is sent to jail to await a trial that keeps being postponed, while Tish discovers that she is pregnant, and has to begin raising their family without him.

As a social justice argument, the film clearly soars; but its articulation of this necessary claim is made so creatively and effectively through the expectation of genre conventions. If Beale Street Could Talk is a stirring, moving, intimate love story—a love story that should not have to turn into another genre. But it does—it is forced to sidetrack into a legal drama because of an external, racist justice system that inserts itself into the love story and ransacks it. So clearly does this show how the broken legal system in the United States ruins lives, families, and, bonds—the kinds of things we watch grow in other films in the “love story” genre without worrying that one of the parties will be falsely imprisoned. The film is about being young, black, in love, and in New York City in a dreamy 1970s, and it is about the reality that existing in these ways means being automatically targeted—unfairly, unjustly, and unexpectedly.

Tish’s family is incredibly supportive of their daughter, their future grandchild, and their (surrogate, soon-to-be) son—the scenes of the family together provide the film with gorgeous community and warmth. Tish’s fiercely caring older sister, Earnestine (Teyonah Parris) gets her acquaintance, a young (white) lawyer named Hayward (Finn Wittrock) to work on Fonny’s case, while Tish’s kindly father Joseph (Colman Domingo) and Fonny’s father Frank (Michael Beach) do whatever it takes to rustle up the money to pay for his defense—and to pay to track down the rape victim, an immigrant named Victoria Rodgers (Emily Rios), after she flees home to Puerto Rico. And Tish’s mother Sharon (a scintillating and ever-sincere Regina King), who I swear has pure goodness shining in her eyes, travels to Puerto Rico herself, to track down Mrs. Rodgers, to beg her to confirm Fonny’s innocence. Meanwhile, Fonny’s very religious mother, Mrs. Hunt (Aunjanue Ellis) and two stern sisters (Ebony Obsidian and Dominique Thorne) are less supportive, believing that Fonny’s artistic and secular nature has led him astray, and ultimately, to sin and punishment.

If Beale Street Could Talk is a film about incredible, wide-ranging support or even just moments of “doing the right thing” from many good people (which manifests in delightful cameos from very famous actors). It also reveals, heartbreakingly, how none of this actually matters to a racist justice system. Fonny and Tish have wonderful friends, including Daniel Carty (Brian Tyree Henry), a haunted young man who has just served two years in jail for stealing a car that he did not steal, and Pedrocito (Diego Luna), a waiter at a nearby Village restaurant who knows they’re good to pay him back for their meals that they have to temporarily eat for free.

They make a new friend in Levy (Dave Franco), the Jewish landlord of the loft where they want to live—the first white landlord not to turn them away (in one of two scenes in which the West Village Jewish community is unflinchingly supportive of Fonny and Tish’s rights to live without harassment or discrimination). And in Puerto Rico, Sharon is able to convince the man who is hiding Mrs. Rodgers, Pedro Alvarez (Pedro Pascal), to make contact between them, to help save Fonny’s life. Moreover, Hayward, the compassionate lawyer, is enlightened by his experience, terrified for and working hard to try to help his client. But the support of all these people, including a powerful white male ally, is not enough to undo an arrest by one resentful, prejudiced white policeman.

Jenkins’s reverence for Baldwin’s prose is readily apparent in this adaptation—many of Tish’s narrations in voiceover are gently lifted straight from the novel. The general impression is that Jenkins, knowing a masterpiece when he sees it, does not want to remove Baldwin’s voice from the story, considering how much of the story’s thematic essence and artistry that voice provides. This gives the film the feeling of being a visual and sonic illustration, a companion to the novel, rather than a fully autonomous piece of art — but (since I am a stickler for narrative fidelity myself) I like this about the film. A lot. I think it reveals how films and their literary progenitors may work together, and that Jenkins’s “if it’s not broken, don’t fix it” attitude, if I may put this so reductively, helps make the practice of adapting a novel to film into a collaborative, generous, and humble practice of love, rather than a desire to possess, harness, or remake in one’s own image, a thing of existing beauty. Jenkins is here to paint Baldwin’s book.

And paint it he does. Basically, every one of the 171,360 frames, give or take, that compose the film could be mounted on a gallery wall—so gorgeous is the film’s constant use of color, its chiaroscuro, its many rain-slicked cobblestone reflections, its use of warm, golden, shafted light. The palettes are gorgeous, complimentary, and bold—and they are illuminated brightly like stained glass (an effect which itself symbolically underscores the tragic and unsung martyrdom of so many black men in America). The music is also magnificent—heartbreaking string instruments sighing and weeping.

If Beale Street Could Talk is a beautiful, shattering film—a collection of misty morsels of love and hope that are set to stew in the garish lights of interrogation rooms, jail cells, and visitation rooms. In its just-under-two-hours, it gives us a taste of beautiful possibilities, and a drop of what will be, for this damaged family, everlasting pain. With his film, Barry Jenkins has provided a visual and emotional glimpse of the great tragic tradition of American life. His film is endlessly poetic, but it’s not poetry—it’s prose from a narrative that goes on and on.

#751. A Merger Solves a Puzzling Paradox in Orion

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

When the Hubble Space Telescope was launched in 1990, an eager astronomy community had a massive list of targets they hoped the new telescope would observe. One of them was the well-known Orion Nebula, visible with the naked eye. At about 1400 light years away, Orion is the closest massive star-forming region to Earth. For its first few years, the Hubble suffered through a flawed mirror problem, but once that was fixed, the telescope examined Orion.

One of its discoveries was the unusual, dark objects that appeared to have tails. They'd been seen before, but not in such detail. In a 1993 paper, researchers determined that they were protoplanetary disks, and coined the term proplyd. But these proplyds were different than others because of their tails. These tails are part of a paradox in the Orion Nebula.

Some early Hubble images of proplyds in Orion. Image Credit: O'Dell, Wen, and Hu 1992. ApJ.

Why do the disks and their extended gas shells appear to be so much older than the stars themselves?

New research in Astronomy and Astrophysics has an answer. It's titled "A triple-induced merger in Î¸ 1 Ori C solves the proplyd lifetime problem and the curiously young shell of M42." The lead author is Maite Wilhelm, an astronomer at Leiden University in the Netherlands.

"A longstanding tension exists between the stellar ages in the Orion Nebula Cluster (ONC) and the rapid external photoevaporation of its protoplanetary discs," the authors write.

This research focuses on Orion's most massive star: 1 Ori C1 (Theta-1 Orionis C1). 1 Ori C1 is also the most massive star in the famous Trapezium Cluster, dominated by a group of four stars in the heart of Orion. Its powerful radiation is stripping material away from protoplanetary disks in the region. The disk material streams away, forming a tail-like shape.

The Trapezium Cluster in the heart of the Orion Nebula. 1 Ori C1 is the brightest star in this image. Image Credit: Data: Hubble Legacy Archive , Processing: Robert Gendler

But the amount of material stripped away so far doesn't match the age of 1 Ori C1 and other stars. The stars are one million years old, so the material should've been stripped away long ago.

There's an additional puzzle. "More recently, the shell of the Orion Nebula has also been found to be dynamically younger than its stars," the authors write.

We've learned a lot more about Orion since the Hubble's early days. Many things have been observed more deeply and measured more accurately, including how rapidly Orion's shell is expanding.

"More recently, the expansion velocity of the shell component of the Orion Nebula was measured to be â¼13 km/s," the researchers explain. The shell has a radius of about 2.5 pc, so this indicates an age of only about 0.3 million years. Since the shell's expansion is mostly driven by the stellar winds coming from the stars in the Orion Nebula Cluster, this creates another dilemma.

"It is striking to observe that two processes powered by the ONCâs massive stars both appear to have younger dynamical ages than the inferred stellar age," the authors write.

The large image of the Trapezium cluster in Orion is from the Hubble. The inset image is a closeup of one of the stars in Orion, showing how the stellar wind is eroding its proplyd. Image Credit: NASA/STScI/Rice Univ./C.O'Dell et al / O. BernÃ©, I. Schrotter, PDRs4All)

Other researchers proposed an explanation for these discrepancies. Maybe there are different populations of stars with different ages, driven by the ejection of massive stars that disrupted and triggered different episodes of star formation. But that explanation didn't stick.

In this work, the authors propose that a stellar merger explains Orion's puzzle.

"We propose a new scenario aimed at resolving both age issues," the researchers write. "In our scenario, the most massive star of the ONC, Î¸ 1 Ori C1, formed from the merger between two binary stars..."

1 Ori C1 has a current binary partner, 1 Ori C2, and its presence triggered the merger between 1 Ori C1 and another partner star, according to the authors.

This created the 1 Ori C1 we see today. It's a massive and hot O-type star, the most massive type of star on the main sequence. 1 Ori C1 has more than 33 solar masses, and its UV radiation provides most of Orion's illumination.

Lead author Wilhelm and her co-researchers tested this idea against simulations. They showed that the merger scenario is plausible. Prior to the merger, the radiation from the stars was much weaker, and the proplyds were better able to withstand them. That means that the dusty disks survived for most of their stars' lifetimes. But after the merger, the outpouring of radiation from 1 Ori C1 changed the game.

This image shows part of Orion. The star LL Orionis, though not part of this work, illustrates the power of stellar winds. It's just up and to the left of center, highlighted by its bow shock. The star is emitting a powerful stellar wind that creates the shock, as its wind slams into the gas in Orion. The complex gas morphology in Orion is created by winds from different stars. Image Credit: NASA, ESA and the Hubble Heritage Team

"This merger, taking place between the last episode of star formation and today, resulted in an increased luminosity, leading to stronger external photoevaporation and accelerated expansion of the shell," the authors explain.

But this can't explain how the Orion Nebula's shell appears to be younger than its stars. "However, the required increase in the stellar wind luminosity would be greater than a simple merger would be equipped to explain," the researchers write. The gas shell's rapid expansion required an additional element. The merger alone couldn't have driven all of the expansion.

The researchers say that cooler gas from the evaporation disks played a role. By mixing with the hotter gas inside the expanding shell, it could've generated the additional force needed to explain the shell's expansion.

Bacon (STScI), Lisa Frattare (STScI), Zolt G. Levay (STScI), K. Litaker (STScI) Acknowledgment: Axel Mellinger, Robert Gendler, Rogelio B. Andreo

This work also explains a couple other curiosities: 1 Ori C1 has a tilted magnetic field and excessive nitrogen, both of which can be explained by a merger.

"This is the first time we have witnessed the consequences of a major stellar collision in a our own cosmic neighbourhood," co-author Simon Portegies Zwart said in a press release. "It also shows that stellar collisions may be much more common than we previously thought, and that they can have far-reaching consequences."

"Our idea shows that subtle effects in triple-star systems can have a major impact on the environments in which stars form, and potentially even on planets that are still taking shape," said lead author Wilhelm.

"We propose a novel scenario that can help explain both the proplyd lifetime problem in the ONC and the Orion Nebula shell lifetime problem. Our scenario involves the merger of a stellar binary into the present-day massive star Î¸ 1 Ori C1, mediated by vZLK oscillations induced by the present-day binary companion Î¸ 1 Ori C2," the authors conclude.

#2773. What 272 Climbing Accidents Reveal About the Risks Climbers Face

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

The 2026 Accidents in North American Climbing reports on accidents that occurred in late-2024 and 2025. (Photo: Maya Silver)

A couple weeks ago, the American Alpine Club released its annual roundup of misfortunes in the mountains. The 2026 Accidents in North American Climbing covers accidents reported across the United States and Canada that occurred in 2025 or late in 2024.

On a trip to Grand Staircase-Escalante National Monument last weekend, I read this year’s Accidents in North American Climbing (ANAC) cover to cover. Then I caught up with editor Pete Takeda to find answers to my questions about his reporting and uncover some of the stories and data behind the trends I observed. I also pulled from Climbing’s coverage of some of these incidents shortly after they took place to get the bigger picture.

In 2025, Pete Takeda said he received 30% more accident reports from climbers than in 2024, resulting in 16 more pages than last year’s book. That makes the 2026 ANAC the biggest edition on record in terms of number of accidents reported since the American Alpine Club (AAC) started printing the book in 1948. Takeda even had to omit a number of accidents because they simply ran out of space.

But that doesn’t necessarily mean that more accidents are occurring. Takeda told Climbing last week that he thinks the uptick is due to the fact that more climbers are reporting their accidents. “People are becoming aware of how their individual accident has significance and meaning if they push it out there to inform people,” Takeda observed. He added that climbers are no longer “a bunch of hardened” folks on the edges of society willing to put it all on the line for the send.

As the culture of climbing evolves to become more mainstream, climbers care more about risk and safety than in the past. “It’s a cultural shift,” Takeda said. He also added that the AAC saw increased accident submissions after Shane Johnson, their VP of Media, established the Prescription video series in late 2020 to analyze them.

The theory that more climbers are simply reporting their accidents also could explain why 2025 saw a notable increase in accidents and injuries, but a decline in fatalities. Perhaps climbers are becoming more likely to report less significant accidents as the culture shift that Takeda sees occurs.

But it could also be a fluke. “Year to year, you get significant swings,” Takeda said. “Some years, fewer people get killed and more people break their ankles.”

Aside from year-to-year swings, here are some of the key takeaways and trends from the 2026 Accidents in North American Climbing.

I noticed a number of incidents this year on the approach or in alpine terrain involving sketchy talus fields—and climbers approaching them perhaps a bit too casually. One involved a guy hiking with a coffee mug in one hand. He lost his footing and tumbled 20 feet down a scree field.

Another involved a climber who dislodged a rock that then landed on his foot while scrambling to a summit in the High Sierras. The rock threw off his balance and he fell. Wearing a helmet may have prevented further injuries beyond his concussion and whiplash. “I now plan to wear the helmet for any scramble ascent or descent,” the anonymous climber wrote in the report.

One fatal incident involved a climber scrambling off trail to pick up some litter on the way back to the car. He slipped and fell 25 feet, landing chest down on a rock. The climber’s shoes were untied. And in alpine terrain in British Columbia, a loose boulder even resulted in the amputation of one climber’s thumb.

Maybe it’s time for rock climbers to take a cue from ice climbers, who often gear up for the approach—and take it a lot more seriously. “Maybe put on some approach shoes, maybe wear a helmet, maybe have one to two hands ready,” Takeda recommended.

Yes, we said it: A helmet on the approach. While many climbers may still skip the helmet while climbing or belaying, the safety-oriented among us ought to consider adding a helmet on the approach if it involves sketchy scree fields, overhanging walls above, or rockfall risk.

Several accidents this year involved small gear ripping out. Two of them occurred in Joshua Tree. In one incident, two Black Diamond #.3s ripped, resulting in a 40-foot groundfall. The other involved three “marginal nuts” ripping out, resulting in a 40-foot deck onto a boulder. The nuts in question were DMM Peenuts, sizes 3, 4, and 5. And in the Leavenworth area, a #.2 ripped out of a horizontal crack. Climbing also reported not too long ago on a deck in Scotland that involved a #.2 Z4 cam failing.

“Like with any gear, with smaller pieces, the margins of safety are a lot smaller,” Takeda said. He observes that smaller cam placements aren’t as strong, require better finesse, and involve a steeper learning curve. “We’re just going to see more and more of this,” he predicted.

Derek Gustafson, who helped design Black Diamond’s Ultralight Camalots, recommends backing up small cams with a nut. With small cams, it’s even more important than with mid-sized cams to make sure your small cams are placed well with the ideal level of lobe retraction. Instead of the 25-75% lobe retraction you’d aim for with, say, a #3, Gustafson suggests an ideal spectrum for small cams of 30-60%. That’s because smaller cams have a narrower range of effectiveness, especially in softer rock like sandstone.

Takeda also advised more climbers to take the time to learn how to place nuts. “A few years ago, we reported 15 accidents involving pulled protection, novices to pros. All were cams,” Takeda said. He added that he’d rather trust a nut than a cam “any day, for a bunch of reasons.” He prefers nuts in the RP to finger range. “They’re simple, light, and easy to visually assess …  more so than tiny cams,” Takeda said.

One of the most memorable accidents in this year’s book involves four climbers bailing off an alpine route in Washington. As we reported last May, they decided to all rappel off a single piton on one of the pitches. The piton failed, sending all four on a 500- to 700-foot fall down steep rock and ice. Only one climber survived. This year’s ANAC goes into the tragic story in more detail, but the takeaway is clear: Don’t trust that piton without testing it and backing it up.

Two other incidents in 2025 involved pitons failing. In West Virginia’s Seneca Rocks, a climber fell over 60 feet when a piton ripped that a climber had tested by yanking on it. She carries the broken piton in her climbing pack as a reminder. And on a route in Utah’s Little Cottonwood Canyon, a piton—that a climber had hung on previously—failed under dynamic forces in a fall. “Backing up fixed pitons is a wise choice,” the climber concluded.

In September in Little Cottonwood Canyon, I in part decided not to lead a route for exactly this reason: the first possible piece of protection was an old piton with no opportunity to back it up. Rust or material deterioration aside, the cracks into which pitons are wedged often widen slowly over time due to melt-freeze cycles. For that reason, some recommend doing a bounce test. You can also re-hammer a piton in to get it deeper in the constriction (if you happen to carry an actual or improvised hammer).

Even if you think that piton is bomber, what these accidents show is that you shouldn’t trust your life to this antiquated gear without verifying its strength. When in doubt, back it up with at least one other piece.

In the intro to this year’s ANAC, Takeda called toprope soloing accidents “a growing trend” that he’s observed since at least 2024. As people with busy lives try to get outside, it’s difficult to coordinate their complicated work and family schedules with partners. That makes toprope soloing an alluring option. Especially with the rise of purpose-built products from companies like Avant, climbers increasingly see this as a casual and low-risk way to get out there.

But Takeda observes climbers getting in over their heads who don’t fully understand the mechanics of the systems they’re using. Pair that with a lack of self-rescue skills, and some toprope soloists are bound to get themselves into a pickle.

One accident, which ended up getting cut from this year’s ANAC due to space constraints, involved a climber in Devil’s Lake, Wisconsin. That climber was trying to get back into the sport. “He used to climb difficult routes,” Takeda said of the climber, who had set up a 5.8 or 5.9. “He ended up getting stuck and having to get rescued. Basically, the guy couldn’t do the moves and lacked the knowledge of how to escape his rig.”

Takeda recommends hiring a guide to help you dial your toprope soloing devices and setups before you go it alone.

This year’s report includes a couple of knees hopelessly wedged into offwidths. One even bordered on the possibility of amputation. This is unfortunately a topic that Climbing associate editor Sam MacIlwaine is all too familiar with.

Takeda once viewed this as a relatively ridiculous accident category that typically only befell the Brits—until his own knee got wedged into a crack.

We asked him to share his own knee epic from the early 2000s, guest starring the late great Hayden Kennedy. Takeda was climbing a 5.11 offwidth in Indian Creek when his knee became stuck. His friends showed up, including Kennedy. He recalls his knee was stuck for around 15 minutes—or “long enough for me to panic and then go, ‘Just be cool, dude.’” Eventually, Kennedy came to the rescue, tapping on Takeda’s heel with a hammer.

“This is going to happen more and more,” Takeda said of the stuck knee incidents, as offwidth climbing rises in popularity. Check out MacIlwaine’s tips for how to avoid a stuck knee—and what to do if it happens.

In a few cases this year, climbers decided to bail when the retreat from their bail point was far from ideal. Instead of finishing out the remaining pitches, they chose to rappel across rope-eating terrain without descent infrastructure. If they’d gone up, a safer rappel with fixed anchors or a walk-off awaited. While it may seem like the responsible thing to do is to retreat rather than continue on—especially in the event of encroaching weather—consider whether you may end up actually spending more time building marginal rappel anchors, dealing with stuck ropes, and losing gear in the process.

In one case on the East Buttress of Mt. Whitney, climbers who got off-route by following another party in the wrong direction actually made a good decision to continue upward rather than hazard a series of rappels with bail gear. “We made logistical, thought-out choices—the first was to bail up instead of down,” wrote Micah, one of the climbers involved.

Recently, we reported on an incident in the Wind River Range, wherein two climbers decided to bail off Pingora Peak due to weather, sickness, and dehydration. But the low-angle crack ate their rope. Without the skills to self-rescue, they were condemned to a night on the wall until rescuers could reach them. The safer, faster option would have been to carry on, top out, and descend via the bolted rappel anchors to the climber’s left of the summit.

Every year, fresh rappelling accidents make it onto the pages of ANAC and the web pages of Climbing. But while rappelling comes with well-documented risks, Takeda said that lowering accidents are increasingly becoming a cause of accidents, too. He attributed this in part to “a multiplicity of anchor styles,” particularly in the U.S. “People don’t know or misinterpret the anchor,” Takeda said. “It comes down to education and standards across North America.”

#4072. Amyloid Fibrils Protect the Father’s Genetic Message

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Proteins best known for driving neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s play an unexpected physiological role in human reproduction. Researchers at Columbia University’s Mortimer B. Zuckerman Mind Brain Behavior Institute discovered that amyloid fibrils in semen bind negatively charged RNA, protect it from degradation, and shepherd it directly into sperm cells. Published in Molecular Cell, the findings reveal a key biological vehicle for paternal epigenetic inheritance and introduce a human-derived delivery scaffold capable of enhancing CRISPR gene-editing technologies.

Source: Columbia University Zuckerman Institute

In neurobiology, amyloid proteins are notorious as the pathological hallmarks of debilitating neurodegenerative disorders, aggregating into neurotoxic plaques and tangles that destroy synapses in Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and related dementias. Yet across the human body, functional amyloids perform diverse, non-pathological tasks that are only beginning to come to light.

Now, a study led by structural biologists and neuroscientists at Columbia University’s Mortimer B. Zuckerman Mind Brain Behavior Institute reveals that seminal fluid relies on amyloid fibrils to perform an essential reproductive feat: shepherding genetic material directly into sperm cells to influence the next generation.

Publishing October 26 in the journal Molecular Cell, the team demonstrated that amyloid fibrils serve as molecular escorts for delicate ribonucleic acid (RNA) molecules, solving a long-standing mystery regarding how paternal epigenetic information travels from seminal plasma into mature spermatozoa.

The investigation originated from an unexpected intersection between neurodegeneration and virology. Prior infectious disease research showed that amyloid fibrils present in human semen facilitate the transmission of HIV by binding viral particles and aiding their entry into host cells within the reproductive tract.

Senior author Anthony W.P. Fitzpatrick, PhD, an expert on neurodegenerative amyloid structures and principal investigator at Columbia’s Zuckerman Institute, questioned whether the reproductive tract harnessed these durable fibrils for normal physiological cargo. Specifically, scientists have long known that seminal fluid is enriched with small non-coding RNAs that regulate embryonic development and paternal epigenetic inheritance, transmitting traits like diet-induced metabolic disorders and stress responses across generations. However, the precise physical chaperone transporting fragile RNA across the sperm membrane remained unknown.

“This is wild biology,” said Dr. Fitzpatrick. “Amyloids are most often thought of as a hallmark of Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s and other neurodegenerative diseases in the brain, but they also have functional roles throughout the body, and keep turning up in unexpected places.”

Because seminal amyloid fibrils carry a positive electrostatic charge, they naturally attract the negatively charged phosphate backbone of RNA molecules. William S. Mallen, PhD, the study’s lead author, used fluorescent tags to confirm that amyloids bind RNA tightly into stable complexes, providing a protective structural scaffold that shields fragile genetic sequences from enzymatic degradation.

To verify whether these protein-RNA complexes actually enter the sperm or merely coat the exterior, the researchers turned to cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET), an advanced imaging modality that uses electron beams on vitrified samples to reconstruct three-dimensional macromolecular architectures at near-atomic resolution.

Using gold-nanoparticle-labeled amyloid proteins, the cryo-ET reconstructions provided unambiguous proof: intact amyloid fibrils successfully breached the sperm cell membrane and lodged within the cytoplasm.

“Seeing those amyloid fibers inside sperm cells was just so extraordinary to me,” said Dr. Mallen. “There were hints of this happening earlier in our other experiments, but actually seeing this just totally blew us away.”

Subsequent functional assays showed that once the amyloid-RNA payload enters the intracellular environment, clusterin, a known molecular chaperone abundant in reproductive tissues, acts as an uncoating agent, dissociating the RNA from its amyloid scaffold so the genetic cargo can participate in cellular processes.

The discovery provides a direct mechanistic explanation for paternal epigenetics, illuminating how paternal lifestyle factors, such as diet-induced insulin resistance or chronic stress, can package regulatory RNAs into sperm to alter embryonic gene expression programs.

Beyond basic biology, the findings point toward practical biotechnological applications. Standard gene-editing tools like CRISPR-Cas9 rely heavily on synthetic messenger RNAs and guide RNAs, which are notoriously delicate and often require modified viral vectors or synthetic lipid nanoparticles for delivery. Viral vectors can trigger severe host immune responses, while synthetic lipids often exhibit off-target cytotoxicity.

The Columbia team demonstrated that seminal amyloid fibrils can transport large, functional mRNA transcripts, specifically those encoding green fluorescent protein (GFP), into sperm cells. Embryos fertilized with sperm treated with amyloid-RNA complexes generated at least ten times more GFP than those exposed to naked RNA.

Because these amyloid fibrils are naturally occurring, human-derived protein structures, they represent a biocompatible, low-immunogenicity delivery vehicle capable of transporting gene-editing machinery and therapeutic RNA payloads into embryos and cells without viral vectors.

Amyloid-mediated RNA uptake by sperm for embryonic delivery

Seminal fluid (SF) RNA is essential for embryonic development, but the mechanism by which extracellular RNA is transferred from SF to sperm remains unclear.

Here, we identify a role for functional amyloid fibrils in SF that bind and traffic RNA to mammalian sperm and, upon fertilization, to the zygote. We show that highly cationic peptides in SF phase separate with RNA to form amyloid fibrils.

Cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET), volume electron microscopy (volume EM), and fluorescence live-cell imaging reveal that these amyloid fibrils are internalized by mammalian sperm.

Furthermore, biophysical and in vitro fertilization (IVF) experiments show that amyloid-bound RNA reaches the zygote, where molecular chaperones can control RNA dissociation from amyloid fibrils.

Our work uncovers a mechanism for RNA trafficking from SF to the zygote, establishing amyloid fibrils as potential tools for RNA delivery during fertilization.
