#749. We Tried 6 National Pizza Chains—And This One Is A Slice Above The Rest

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Pizza is practically impossible to screw up. I was raised on the principle that even a bad pie is still good, and I still swear by it. That said, I do have preferences. While I’ll die on the hill that Emmett’s on Grove, Chrissy’s, and Ceres in New York City round out my personal top three, I’m not above ordering from a chain either. They’re notoriously reliable and their own brand of nostalgic.

So I decided to run an official taste test to find the best pizza chain, gathering the entire Delish office together in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit (bread, cheese, and sauce).

We zeroed in on top standbys: Domino’s, Pizza Hut, Papa John’s, Little Caesars, Sbarro, and Jet’s. Everyone rated both pepperoni and cheese slices, and scored accordingly. Then I tallied everything up for the final ranking. As for the results? They weren’t what I expected...

It’s cheap, it’s fast, it’s “Pizza! Pizza!” There were a few fans of the Hot-N-Ready chain—one person said the crust "has a really nice crunch that none of the others have"—but not quite enough to tap into the top five.

In case you missed the memo, the mall food court staple also delivers—at least in New York. The pizza arrived hot and filled with a sense of nostalgia. While the slices weren't quite as gigantic as I remember (probably because the last time I had them I was a child with hands half the size), the sauce was pleasantly sweeter, and the crust was pretty crisp. One taster described it as “really good [with] delicious sauce (not too much) and cheese.” They added that it’s “greasy but somehow works.”

Known for its Detroit-style pizza (which I love), Jet's also does a New York thin-crust option. To keep everything consistent, we ordered that—never mind that it arrived looking completely disheveled from transit. Some participants gushed over this one, while others were somewhat underwhelmed. Overall, the consensus landed in the middle.

The Pizza Hut fans showed up for this one (or maybe Pizza Hut just really showed out). While the doughy crust wasn't for everyone (one person said they had to “be in the mood” for it), it impressed others. “Tastes like focaccia,” one Delish staffer said of the crust, while others described it as “light and fluffy” with a “good flavor” and “sweet sauce.”

Birthday parties, classroom celebrations...Pizza Hut was that girl, and apparently still is.

Domino’s had some vocal fans in the office—and for good reason. The crust is packed with flavor, the cheese tasted fresh, and the balance between doughy and crispy won over the crowd.

“Domino’s has always been my go-to takeout pizza,” one taster said. Another said the crust “always hits the spot,” with another complimenting its garlicky punch. A separate taster went as far as to describe the thin crust as “god’s gift.” Bold, but seemingly deserved given its ranking.

And your winner is...Papa John’s! While Domino’s came close, Papa John’s took the crown with its presentation, flavor, and freshness. I mean, the pepperonis and garlic sauce gave them an edge alone. You don’t even have to ask for them!

“Very cheesy, good flavor,” one fan said of a John’s slice. “I think Papa John’s is the perfect take-out pizza—the peppers, the garlic sauce, the crust.” While Domino’s garlicky, herby crust has its place, Papa John’s simple, bready crust has a loyal following.

One staffer summed it up perfectly: “The best pizza chain ever.” Agreed!

Elon Musk claims Ukraine violated Starlink’s terms of service before the recent slowdown, as reported earlier by Ars Technica. According to Musk, “Ukraine military violated Starlink terms of service and used it for offensive drone strikes to kill people. That loophole was closed last week,” however Ukrainian officials have denied that happened, or that it is actually possible for them to do.

The statement comes nearly two weeks after Ukrainian officials accused SpaceX of putting limits on the military’s satellite internet service that disrupted its drone missions and operations. As reported by Axios, Ukraine uses Starlink for communications, logistics, and to defend against incoming drones. For offensive missions, officials say that Ukraine uses Starshield, the US military-controlled variant of Starlink.

Musk points to a post from Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, which said Ukraine uses “Starlink from mid-range strikes against enemy military targets,” in violation of Starlink’s policies. However, Serhii Beskrestnov, a defense technology advisor for Zelenskyy, pushed back on this in a post on Telegram, saying Starlink’s systems can only connect to devices traveling between 70 and 90 kilometers per hour (around 43 and 55 miles per hour) and don’t work in Russia. “Therefore, we physically cannot use commercial Starlink for deep strikes or medium-range strikes,” Beskrestnov wrote.

Ars Technica also points out a tweet from another former Ukrainian advisor, Serhii Sternenko, that went into more detail, saying that while stationary terminals could be registered (with Starlink) by military personnel or civilians, “for any satellite terminal to work while on the move, it had to be added to a special list and connected to the military Starshield system… In other words, using civilian Starlink for drone missions was technically impossible. All drone missions that required satellite communication could only be carried out using Starshield.”

Zelenskyy said on Saturday that Starlink and Starshield services had been fully restored. With the restriction lifted, Ukraine’s military can now connect to devices traveling at speeds of more than 400km/h (248mph), the Kyiv Independent reports.

Though Musk initially offloaded the blame for the slowdown in Ukraine onto the US government, President Donald Trump responded by saying he “wasn’t involved” and didn’t know who throttled SpaceX’s services. A report from The Wall Street Journal later said Trump asked Musk to lift restrictions on Ukraine.

#1747. YouTube tests reduced payment threshold for creators

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The platform is experimenting with a cash-out option set at $20 rather than the current $100, which could open doors to more video producers.

YouTube is testing an option that would enable creators to cash out their Partner Program earnings at $20, rather than the longstanding $100 minimum.

This payout flexibility gives creators easier access to their YouTube content revenue share.

As explained by YouTube: “To offer creators more flexibility, we’re temporarily running an experiment that lowers the minimum payment threshold to receive payments from $100 USD to $20 USD (or local currency equivalent). This will be applied starting from October 2026 payments for a randomly selected group of creators in the U.S., India and Indonesia.”

So at the end of this month, a creator in the test pool will be able to access their funds even if they haven't reached the $100 minimum. Whatever funds remain will be rolled over to the next month.

The update comes as Instagram challenges YouTube with a renewed push on long-form content. The Meta-owned platform may be hoping to compete with YouTube on both content and for creators.

But YouTube’s revenue share system is more advanced than what’s on offer on IG, and it will be a tough sell for Meta to convince creators to put more focus on Instagram, even with more content sharing options.

Decreased payment thresholds could be another element in YouTube’s favor, providing expanded opportunities for a broader range of creators to make money in the app.

#4526. China’s first fully AI movie flops months after ‘worse than AI’ film makes millions

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

China’s first fully AI-generated feature film has bombed at the box office, just months after a bizarre animated movie mocked for looking “worse than AI” became a surprise hit.

Artificial intelligence might be taking over Hollywood, but getting actual humans into theater seats is proving to be a much bigger challenge.

Sanxingdui: Future Past, billed as China’s first fully AI-generated theatrical feature, opened on October 23 with more than 10,000 screenings scheduled for its debut.

However, according to Cartoon Brew, the sci-fi movie earned just 1.21 million yuan (approximately $181,000) through October 26, despite costing more than $1.5 million to produce.

Even more awkwardly, production company Bona Film Group says nearly 100 people spent over two years making the film, despite its characters, voices, and backgrounds being AI-generated.

And the attendance figures are somehow even worse.

According to China’s National Business Daily, screenings on October 24 and 25 attracted an average of just two viewers each. By October 26, the movie’s reported theater occupancy rate had fallen to a staggering 0.1%.

One person who attended a free preview told AFP she wouldn’t have paid to watch it.

“If AI generation can match the theatrical experience of Avatar, I think I would be willing to pay for it,” she said.

It’s another setback for AI-generated cinema after AMC pulled an AI movie from theaters earlier this year following backlash.

The flop is particularly amusing considering what happened just two months earlier. Back in August, Dexerto covered Niu Lai, a bizarre Chinese animated movie branded “worse than AI” that initially attracted only around 200 viewers.

#4021. Walking Speed Sharpens Visual Sensitivity to Backward Motion

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A visual psychophysics study led by researchers at the National Institute for Physiological Sciences (NIPS) in Japan reveals that human motion sensitivity depends directly on movement velocity. Testing adults across both planar and three-dimensional dot-motion simulations, investigators found that at a typical human walking speed of 1.5 meters per second (5.4 km/h), the human visual system is significantly more sensitive to contracting optic flow (backward motion) than expanding flow (forward motion). This specialized perceptual tuning may serve as an evolutionary early-warning reflex to detect backward falls and preserve postural stability.

Source: National Institutes of Natural Sciences (NIPS)

As we navigate through the physical world, self-motion generates a dynamic, continuous streaming of visual vectors across our retinas known as optic flow. Stepping forward causes the visual landscape to expand outward from a central focus of expansion. Conversely, moving backward produces contracting optic flow, with the peripheral environment streaming inward toward a central point of contraction.

The brain continuously calculates these streaming patterns to gauge velocity, estimate heading direction, and regulate bipedal balance.

For decades, perceptual psychologists and visual neuroscientists have debated how the brain prioritizes these vectors, with conflicting studies arguing whether the visual system is fundamentally more sensitive to forward or backward movement.

Now, an experimental investigation led by Junxiang Luo at the National Institute for Physiological Sciences (NIPS), part of Japan’s National Institutes of Natural Sciences, resolves this controversy. The findings demonstrate that human sensitivity to optic flow is closely calibrated to locomotion speed.

To determine whether contradictory findings in earlier literature stemmed from the velocities tested, the NIPS research team designed psychophysical discrimination paradigms spanning multiple speeds.

The researchers tested 27 adults using two-dimensional planar dot arrays moving either radially outward (simulating forward translation) or inward (simulating backward translation). To verify whether ecological depth cues altered processing, a separate cohort of 30 adults was evaluated using three-dimensional stereoscopic displays simulating physical motion through deep space.

During the trials, participants observed two sequential optic flow presentations and indicated which display moved faster. The investigators systematically measured the speed discrimination threshold—the smallest difference in velocity a subject could accurately detect between expanding versus contracting patterns.

The experiments revealed a clear velocity threshold: at an intermediate speed of 1.5 meters per second (approximately 5.4 kilometers per hour), the exact biomechanical velocity of a typical human walking pace, participants were significantly better at detecting subtle speed variations in contracting patterns than in expanding ones.

This perceptual advantage for contracting flow emerged consistently across both simple two-dimensional dot displays and immersive 3D spatial simulations. At speeds slower or faster than normal walking pace, this perceptual difference disappeared entirely.

The speed-specific advantage for contracting optic flow explains why previous research arrived at divergent conclusions, as earlier trials often evaluated arbitrary velocity thresholds uncoupled from natural human gait.

Furthermore, the selective tuning carries clear evolutionary and biomechanical implications for bipedal navigation.

“This heightened sensitivity to contracting flow might help people immediately respond to backward instability, such as losing balance,” explains Junxiang Luo, lead researcher of the study at NIPS.

When walking forward, humans possess anticipatory visual fields, vestibular cues, and proactive stepping mechanisms to catch themselves if they trip. However, backward balance loss presents an acute hazard: humans lack rear vision and cannot easily extend a protective backward stride to prevent catastrophic impacts to the spine or head.

By maintaining heightened sensitivity to the subtle contracting optical streaming associated with backward sway at walking speeds, the visual cortex can rapidly register loss of equilibrium, triggering rapid motor corrections to preserve posture.

Unraveling the neural circuitry that computes this walking-speed visual advantage opens practical avenues for clinical rehabilitation and preventative medicine.

In older adults and patients suffering from neurodegenerative conditions like Parkinson’s disease or vestibular ataxia, impaired motion perception directly correlates with an elevated risk of falling.

The authors suggest that evaluating an individual’s speed discrimination thresholds for contracting optic flow could serve as a non-invasive diagnostic metric for subclinical balance impairment. Additionally, integrating velocity-calibrated contracting optic flow regimens into virtual-reality gait training and physical therapy programs could help patients retrain automatic postural reflexes, reducing falls and enhancing mobility.

Anisotropic speed-discrimination sensitivity for expanding and contracting optic flow

Retinal optic flow provides critical visual cues for locomotion and self-navigation. Forward self-motion, such as walking or driving, typically induces an expanding radial flow pattern on the retina. Despite the predominance of expanding optic flow in everyday self-motion, several studies have reported higher sensitivity for contracting flow that arises under relatively rare conditions, such as walking backward or falling onto one’s back.

The underlying basis of this anisotropy remains elusive. In this study, we compared discrimination sensitivity to speed for expanding versus contracting optic flow, given that speed is a fundamental attribute of motion and that sensitivity to it provides insight into underlying visual mechanisms.

We found that sensitivity in speed discrimination was comparable between expansion and contraction at low and high speeds but was significantly higher for contraction at an intermediate speed corresponding to ecologically relevant self-motion, such as walking.

This anisotropic pattern was observed for both two-dimensional planar (Experiment 1) and simulated three-dimensional (Experiment 2) optic flow stimuli.

Together, these findings suggest that anisotropic speed discrimination sensitivity to expanding and contracting optic flow at ecologically typical egomotion speeds may reflect an adaptive visual mechanism that supports postural stability and balance during relatively rare backward self-motion.

If I asked you why you admire these individuals, you would likely be able to quickly list several of their positive qualities, such as honesty, personal integrity, talent, creativity, intelligence, or physical abilities.

Similarly, I’ve been thinking recently about several people I admire who are advocates in the areas of social change, mental health, and humanitarian efforts. I’ve noticed there seems to be a common set of qualities among those who are highly skilled in fields that promote efforts to improve the lives of others.

So here’s my list of 10 qualities that I think exceptional advocates often exhibit:

Enthusiastic, driven, compassionate, caring. All of these characteristics convey the passion of exceptional advocates. Their work is more than a job requirement or a professional responsibility. It’s a calling and a lifelong mission.

Great advocates are often the most knowledgeable person in the room (region, state, nation) on their area of focus. They are continually learning, plugged into current streams of information, and always ready to share their knowledge and expertise to educate others.

Whether expressing themselves verbally or in writing, and regardless of the medium (print, video, social media), these amazing advocates communicate clearly, persuasively, and respectfully to share information and viewpoints, and to advance their mission.

Without a clear goal, there is no clear focus and no clear path forward. Exceptional advocates have laser-sharp goals, whether the goal is passing helpful legislation, creating new programs, or garnering support for worthy causes.

Great advocates seem to have connections everywhere, with extensive networks at all levels. They are adept at building partnerships and coalitions and in connecting people with useful resources. They are often the first person people go to for information, support, and advice.

The most effective advocates know the power of collaboration and compromise. Not every battle can be won. Sometimes small victories are the only road to eventual success. These advocates know that flexibility is essential in moving steadily forward to achieve their longer-term goals.

What does it mean to be inspiring? It’s hard to articulate, but great advocates move us, and they stir our thoughts and feelings. They give us hope for a brighter tomorrow, and they outline a path for how we can reach it.

The best advocates not only inspire us, but they also instill in us a desire to pitch in and help out to collectively achieve a worthwhile goal. They show us how to raise our own voices to be heard and how to help others find their voice. They also remind us that we all matter and we all have a part to play.

Advocacy is not a sprint; it’s a marathon. Important goals can take years or decades to achieve. Great advocates show up and keep showing up. They are not deterred by setbacks or disappointment. They are the first to arrive and the last to leave.

In their hearts, great advocates are public servants. They do their work not for glory or fame, but for the quiet satisfaction of helping others and the steadfast belief that the work is the right thing to do and the only thing to do.

While most of us may not reach the level of these exceptional advocates, we can certainly follow their example and try to learn how to use some of these same qualities in our own causes and passions.

And remember, you don’t have to be exceptional. You just have to have a desire to make a difference and a willingness to put in a little work and effort toward your goals. Let’s get started.

#2319. Drought-Tolerant Perennials for Mid-Atlantic Gardens

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When we think of drought-tolerant plants, we often picture prairie plants with deep roots that can reach far into the soil for moisture. But after years of gardening in the Midwest and Mid-Atlantic, I’ve learned that deep roots aren’t the only reason a plant can withstand dry conditions. Some of the most resilient plants have surprisingly shallow root systems.

The following three perennials have impressed me with their ability to handle drought once established. Two are excellent choices for sunny gardens, while the third proves that even difficult, dry shade can support beautiful plants.

Read More: Best Plants for Your Shopping List in the Mid-Atlantic

Size: 12 to 30 inches tall and 12 to 18 inches wide

Conditions: Full sun; dry to moderately moist, well-drained soil

Butterfly weed is one of those plants that I wish more gardeners would grow. With all the encouragement to plant milkweed for monarch butterflies, many people immediately think of common milkweed. But common milkweed can spread vigorously through rhizomes and seed, sometimes crowding out neighboring plants. Butterfly weed offers a much more compact alternative, with a wonderful mounding habit and gorgeous bright orange flowers.

I love orange in the garden and use it extensively, particularly alongside white flowers. But if orange isn’t your favorite color, there are yellow-flowered selections of butterfly weed, too. I recently visited a friend’s gravel garden in Wilmington, Delaware, where orange and yellow plants were growing together and producing seedlings with different flower colors.

As a prairie plant, butterfly weed needs excellent drainage. Its long, tuberous roots help it withstand dry conditions once established, but those roots also mean it’s best to choose a permanent location rather than move mature plants around the garden. Be patient during establishment. Butterfly weed may take a little time to get going, but it can live for many years. And beyond its value to pollinators, I think it’s every bit as pretty as the more traditional ornamental perennials we grow.

Conditions: Full sun; dry to moderately moist, well-drained soil

If you asked me to name my favorite perennials, lesser calamint would probably make my top five. I spent about 25 years gardening in the Madison, Wisconsin, area, where I was fortunate to be surrounded by some remarkable horticulturists. Roy Diblik was among those who introduced many of us to this plant through his work at Northwind Perennial Farm. Once we saw how well it performed, we started growing it ourselves.

Lesser calamint produces masses of tiny white flowers that remind me of baby’s breath. It has a lovely, rounded habit, and that fine texture is especially effective when mixed with larger-leaved or coarser plants. There are selections with pale blue or lavender flowers, but I’m perfectly happy with the bright white blooms. When the plant is in full flower, it can be absolutely covered with pollinating insects.

And this is one tough perennial. I’ve seen lesser calamint used in difficult roadside plantings where it performed exceptionally. The important thing is to keep excess moisture away from its roots. Give it plenty of sunlight and well-drained soil, and it can handle dry conditions remarkably well.

Gardeners sometimes worry about plants that seed around, but I’ve never found lesser calamint particularly troublesome. It may produce a few seedlings, but they’re easy to recognize and remove. It’s a reliable, good-looking plant that plays well with others.

Size: 9 to 18 inches tall and 12 to 24 inches wide

Conditions: Partial to full shade; well-drained soil; tolerates dry shade once established

Epimedium is one of my favorite examples of why we shouldn’t assume that drought-tolerant plants need deep roots. Its roots are shallow and fibrous, forming a relatively flat mat that’s easy to lift when dividing plants. Looking at that root system, you might expect epimedium to be one of the first plants to suffer during a drought. But I’ve seen exactly the opposite.

Several years ago, I attended an American Public Gardens Association conference in Toronto during a severe drought. We visited a garden where the perennial borders were suffering terribly. Nearly everything had wilted from the lack of moisture, yet the epimedium foliage looked as though it had been watered all along. That experience has stayed with me.

Not every epimedium is equally drought tolerant, but many selections perform wonderfully in dry shade once established. They’re especially useful as ground covers beneath trees and in woodland gardens.

One of my favorites to recommend is ‘Lilafee’ (Epimedium grandiflorum ‘Lilafee’), a vigorous selection with lovely purple flowers. Epimedium flowers are delicate, almost like little fairies hovering above the foliage. They’re the sort of flowers that make you stop, kneel down, and look closely. I think we need more plants that encourage us to slow down and enjoy our gardens.

Epimediums can be expensive, particularly when you’re trying to establish a large planting. My advice is to buy a couple each year rather than waiting until you can afford a dozen. Over time, those few plants can develop into a beautiful planting, and you’ll be glad you started.
