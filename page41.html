Christine Lagarde will leave her post as president of the European Central Bank (ECB) on 7 February 2027, the central bank announced on Thursday, around nine months before her eight-year term was due to end.

The news immediately kicks off a closely watched race for one of Europe's most powerful jobs at a time of economic stagnation and global turmoil. Her succession will be decided by the 21 countries that share the euro.

The announcement came hours before the ECB left its deposit rate unchanged at 2.5%, as turmoil in France's bond market weighs on borrowing costs across the eurozone.

Lagarde informed the ECB's Governing Council, which sets interest rates for the eurozone, and European Council President António Costa of her decision, the central bank said in a statement.

"The Governing Council thanked President Lagarde warmly and gave her a long standing ovation," it read.

In a separate public statement, Lagarde said she was leaving with a sense of a job “well done” and an ECB that was “fit for the future.”

During her press conference after the rate decision, the ECB president also said she is stepping down before the end of her term so that she has "greater freedom to express her views and to engage in non-monetary matters", because she cannot "remain neutral when she sees the developments taking place" in her own country and in the world.

"I can be useful as a French national, European citizen and citizen of the world," Lagarde added.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen reacted to the news, thanking Lagarde for her service and stating that "from the pandemic to Russia's war of aggression, your steady hand has helped keep our economy stable and resilient".

European Council President António Costa also thanked Lagarde, praising her "dedication as President of the European Central Bank since November 2019".

"To ensure a smooth transition to the next leadership of the ECB, the process for the appointment of her successor will be initiated today and will proceed without delay," Costa added.

French President Emmanuel Macron expressed "deep gratitude" for Lagarde's commitment and "for the determined action she has led at the helm of the European Central Bank in service of the stability of the eurozone".

Greek Economy and Finance Minister Kyriakos Pierrakakis, who chairs the Eurogroup of eurozone finance ministers, also thanked Lagarde on their behalf, saying her time in charge had strengthened confidence in the euro and credited her leadership through difficult economic times.

Her successor will be appointed by the European Council, which brings together EU leaders.

Under EU rules, leaders choose the ECB president by qualified majority, on a recommendation from EU finance ministers and after consulting the European Parliament and the ECB's Governing Council.

While the ECB gave no reason, speculation about Lagarde's early departure had been building for months.

As Euronews reported, rumours first emerged in February that Lagarde planned to leave early so that French President Emmanuel Macron and German Chancellor Friedrich Merz could oversee the choice of her successor before France's presidential election in April of next year.

Macron is barred from running for a third term, and the far-right Rassemblement National (National Rally) has been polling strongly, raising the prospect of a eurosceptic government in Paris that could complicate the appointment of a new ECB chief.

Leaving next February gives EU leaders time to settle on a name well before French voters go to the polls.

Bank of France Governor François Villeroy de Galhau announced his own early exit in February, reportedly for similar reasons. However, Lagarde had kept her plans close to her chest for months.

The departing ECB president said in February that chairing the World Economic Forum, the organisation behind the annual Davos meeting, was "one of the many options" for her future, and in July she told Euronews that she was "not a candidate for anything" dispelling speculation that she would run in the French elections.

Two names currently lead the race to succeed Lagarde.

Pablo Hernández de Cos, a former governor of the Bank of Spain who has run the Basel-based Bank for International Settlements since 2025, was put forward by Madrid in July in Spain’s first-ever bid for the ECB presidency.

Earlier this month, he also won the backing of Spain's main opposition leader, Alberto Núñez Feijóo, whose party is leading most polls ahead of next month's snap election.

His main rival is Klaas Knot, who ran the Dutch central bank from 2011 to 2025 and is seen as a former inflation hawk turned consensus-builder. Lagarde herself has praised his "rare and very necessary" ability to foster inclusivity.

Bundesbank President Joachim Nagel has also been mentioned, though Germany has never held the job.

The presidency is also only one piece of a bigger puzzle.

Isabel Schnabel, the German board member in charge of market operations, is leaving on 3 January 2027 to join the IMF, while chief economist Philip Lane's term ends shortly after on 31 May 2027.

That means half of the ECB's six-member Executive Board, which runs the central bank day to day, will change hands within five months, and the three posts are expected to be negotiated between capitals as a package.

Eurozone governments had until Wednesday to put forward candidates for Schnabel's seat, but this deadline has been extended until 27 November. The Eurogroup did not make an announcement but a spokesperson has confirmed it to media outlets.

France, Germany and Italy have long held seats on the board, while Spain wants to return after losing its place when Luis de Guindos' vice-presidency passed to Croatia's Boris Vujčić this year.

Germany is expected to push to keep the market operations portfolio.

Meanwhile, Paris is widely expected to aim for the chief economist job rather than a third French presidency. "France knows how to play the game so they are very likely not going to try for a third French ECB President," Carsten Brzeski, global head of macro at Dutch bank ING, told Euronews in February.

Countries traditionally hold only one seat each, meaning a Knot presidency would also likely mean fellow Dutch board member Frank Elderson stepping aside.

The wardrobe of a woman from ancient Pompeii has come to light again. Thanks to the work of archaeologists it has been possible to recover the interior of a cabinet that held everyday items in house IX 11, 7, or the house of Cornelius Maximus, in the central area of the city buried by Vesuvius.

Looking at these objects, it is almost as if we can conjure up the life of a woman of Pompeii before the site was destroyed in AD 79. Among the finds are make-up tools(tweezers, a spatula for mixing pigments), a glass balsamarium, a perfume burner to fill the home with pleasant fragrances, a bronze lamp, alongside lead weights, probably used for a commercial activity and kept in a small wooden box of which only faint traces remain.

Vases and pots in bronze, glass and ceramic were also found: everyday household objects, but perhaps also heavy with memories, as in the case of an elegant little jug decorated with pine-scale motifs. There is also a necklace composed of at least 24 miniature objectsin faïence, bone, glass paste and rock crystal, all pierced for suspension and representing deities (Bes and Mercury can be identified), feet and hands, pine cones and bunches of grapes.

According to experts, such necklaces were sometimes regarded as "magical" objects used in spells and potions; in reality they are what are known as crepundia, literally 'rattles': necklaces with amulets that were given to children to protect them, but also so that they could be heard when they moved.

It was not unusual for adults to keep them too, often in small boxes or, as in this case, in cloth bags inside wardrobes, apparently as keepsakes of a childhood of which little else remained. The Roman world was characterised by a high degree of geographic mobility at all levels, from slaves to wealthy merchants, so that many people found themselves living far from relatives and parents and from the places of their childhood.

Another secret kept in the wardrobe unearthed in the excavation is a small hoard of coins: there are 38 of them, all bronze, preserved in a "cistella", a small container made of plant material using basketry techniques, the remains of which were analysed after being recovered during the dig. The presence of the coins in particular suggests that the owner or owners did not have time to recover the objects of value, whether economic or emotional.

If they were in the city at the time of the catastrophe, they will have tried to flee. If they managed to escape, they would not have returned to dig under the floors for their possessions, unlike others whose efforts have left traces revealed by recent excavations.

But perhaps they did not succeed and may be among those who, as the historian Tacitus recalls, died without heirs and whose property would have been used for the refounding of Pompeii, which ultimately never took place. These are questions destined to remain open.

"Pompeii is not just a place of great works of art; it is also somewhere where the most ordinary objects restore the human dimension of the past. The value of this discovery lies in the possibility of reading together the different traces of a life: personal habits, work, memories," says the director of the Archaeological Park, Gabriel Zuchtriegel.

"We cannot reconstruct with certainty the identity of the person who lived in the house, but these fragments bring us closer to their everyday life," the director adds.

The excavation has helped clarify some aspects of the house in which the wardrobe was found. The facade of the dwelling, tentatively attributed to a figure named Cornelius Maximus on the basis of inscriptions now no longer visible, had already been excavated in 1912. Only now, however, has part of the rooms behind it been investigated, as part of the major restoration and accessibility project for the adjacent Insula dei Casti Amanti.

Although our knowledge of the house remains partial, some highly interesting data have emerged: on the one hand, the refined Fourth Style wall paintings in the room with the wardrobe, attributable to the workshop on Via di Castricio, indicate a certain standard of living, in line with the central position of the house, which overlooks one of the main arteries of the ancient city.

On the other hand, at the time of the eruption various works were nevertheless in progress that must have affected liveability considerably. Added to this is the discovery of at least two mouse skeletons, found inside an amphora on a mezzanine. According to analyses carried out in the park's laboratory, they probably belonged to the species Apodemus sylvaticus, also known as the "wood mouse".

Mice of this kind normally live in natural environments and gardens, from where they often invade attics and storage areas in human dwellings in search of food. The two specimens found at Pompeii were not fully mature, as indicated by the degree of development of their skeletons (117 tiny fragments). The discovery adds to evidence of the presence of rodents in other Vesuvian contexts, such as the service quarters of the suburban villa at Civita Giuliana or the sewers of Herculaneum, and offers the image of an urban society that also had to contend with unwelcome guests.

The United States is at risk of losing its measles-free status as cases surge, raising fresh concerns about the vulnerability of European communities where vaccination coverage remains below the level needed to prevent sustained transmission.

More than 4,500 cases have been reported in the US this year — roughly twice the total reported last year. A forecast published in JAMA suggests that measles could become endemic again in the US if vaccination rates fail to improve, potentially resulting in 851,300 cases, more than 170,000 hospitalisations and 2,550 deaths over 25 years.

The outbreak across the Atlantic could also signal a wider risk, Kitty Mohan, vice-president of the Standing Committee of European Doctors (CPME) and its rapporteur on vaccination, told Euronews.

“Due to international travel routes and the ease and speed at which people move across borders, combined the infectiousness of measles before the rash starts, people can travel quite far whilst infectious and so the disease can spread rapidly,” Mohan said.

Measles is among the most contagious infectious diseases. People can transmit the virus for around four days before the characteristic rash appears, meaning they may travel across borders before realising they are ill.

“15 mins face to face with someone who is infectious will mean you are likely to get measles,” Mohan said.

The US is not the only country where measles continues to circulate. The biggest outbreaks currently are in Bangladesh, with cases surpassing 65,430, Indonesia with 46,578 and Guatemala with 31,222 cases.

“Whether an imported case leads to further transmission in Europe depends primarily on immunity levels in the affected community in Europe and whether the virus reaches groups with low vaccination coverage,” the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) told Euronews.

Vaccination coverage was 92% for the first dose and 88% for the second dose — a “strong population protection,” ECDC said.

But that remains below the recommended 95% two-dose coverage needed at national and local levels to prevent sustained transmission and achieve measles elimination. National averages can also conceal areas or communities with lower vaccination coverage.

“Most measles cases occur in unvaccinated people,” the ECDC said, recommending that countries close immunity gaps, ensure both vaccine doses are administered on time, offer catch-up vaccination to eligible adolescents and adults, and maintain strong surveillance and outbreak-response systems.

The consequences extend beyond those who can choose to get vaccinated. Infants who are too young to receive the vaccine, as well as people who cannot be vaccinated for medical reasons or whose immune systems cannot mount an adequate response, depend on high levels of immunity in the wider population.

“Herd immunity or maintaining vaccination rates above a threshold would protect these groups - so lower rates make them more susceptible to disease,” Mohan said.

The problem is not confined to countries with low national vaccination rates. Clusters of unvaccinated or under-vaccinated people can emerge within otherwise well-protected populations, creating local conditions in which outbreaks can take hold.

Coverage gaps can reflect a range of factors, including difficulties accessing vaccination services, limited information or awareness, and concerns about vaccines.

“There are traditional risk groups - such as traveller communities and some religious groups who historically have been reluctant to vaccinate,” said Mohan.

However, ECDC underlined that “the presence of a group of unvaccinated people does not automatically mean that a large outbreak will spread across the wider population,” using the Urkult festival in northern Sweden as an example. The outbreak at the festival occurred between 30 July and 2 August this year, mainly affecting unvaccinated children and adolescents. It involved 28 cases, including 12 secondary cases, but did not lead to sustained transmission in the wider population.

"The fact that there were relatively few secondary cases among those exposed at the festival is due to the high level of immunity within the population, a result of strong participation in the childhood vaccination program," said Johanna Rubin, Acting Head of the Vaccination Unit at Sweden’s public health agency.

“This once again highlights the importance of high community-level protection,” ECDC said.

For now, the risk for Europeans remains the same, ECDC said, as per its latest assessment, with the overall risk set as moderate for infants under 12 months, children aged one to five and immunocompromised people. For older unvaccinated children and adults, the risk is estimated to range from low to moderate. People with immunity from vaccination or previous infection are considered to have a low risk of measles.

ECDC surveillance data indicate fewer reported measles cases in 2026 than in 2025, although 2026 figures remain preliminary and should be interpreted with caution.

Measles is endemic in twelve European countries: Bosnia and Herzegovina, France, Georgia, Germany, Italy, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Poland, Romania, Russia, Türkiye, and Ukraine.

Romania reported most of the cases, with numbers reaching 30,692 in 2024, up from 3,371 in 2023.

Six European countries: Armenia, Austria, Azerbaijan, Spain, the United Kingdom, and Uzbekistan have re-established endemic measles transmission based on the number of cases in 2024 earlier this year.

The European Trade Union Confederation (ETUC) argues that the Commission’s reassurance that employment law is unaffected only answers part of the problem.

Labour lawyers broadly accept that ordinary employment rights survive. Under EU conflict-of-law rules, choosing another country’s law generally cannot strip a worker of the mandatory protections of the country where they habitually work.

The gap, unions say, is in rights tied to the company itself, especially board-level worker representation. The Council’s mandate adds an optional safeguard for branches. Host countries with participation rules can opt in. A foreign company whose branch workforce passes their threshold must either create a local subsidiary or grant equivalent rights. Unions say this falls short because it is optional, covers branches rather than every group structure, and could keep workers away from the parent board.

#264. Building boom puts 300,000 people in the path of potential Himalayan disasters

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Construction of tourism and housing facilities has surged in parts of Nepal and India's Uttarakhand

At least 300,000 people in parts of the Himalayan region could now be living in the path of potential disasters, a new study suggests.

Researchers found that the amount of built-up land in high-risk zones in northern Nepal, parts of south-eastern Tibet and a corner of India's Uttarakhand had grown by about 45% between 2016 and 2023.

In areas particularly threatened by flooding from glacial lakes, development increased even faster, rising by almost 78% over the same period, according to the peer-reviewed study published in Scientific Reports.

The team of scientists behind it said the true number of people under threat could be much higher, as they only analysed a specific territory and not neighbouring areas which face similar risks.

The study was carried out before August's devastating mass floods which left more than 1,400 dead and thousands missing in Nepal. More recently there have been several smaller incidents, including flooding along the Budhi Gandaki river on 11 October that damaged 200 houses, swept away five bridges and left five people missing.

The major tourist hub of Pokhara has seen major development in recent years

Nine researchers from the UK, Nepal and India looked at areas up to 50km (30 miles) downstream from Himalayan rivers, as well as around 1,360 glacial lakes.

They found significant development in these areas - including an expansion of tourism facilities, infrastructure development and real estate.

It's clear in Pokhara, a major tourism hub at the foot of the Himalayas that's grown significantly in the past 30 years to cater for a massive increase in visitors.

Mayor Dhana Raj Acharya says it's a huge challenge to deal with the risk posed by the Seti Gandaki River, which stems from the Annapurna mountain range and flows right through the city. Many old buildings are just 5m (16ft) from river banks, he says, despite new rules requiring them to be further away.

"Where we move them, and how we do that, is the real challenge."

To the north is the world-renowned Annapurna trekking region that has also seen a major increase in settlements along river banks - some hit by recent floods and landslides.

Phurpa Chhiring Lama, who runs a homestay there, feels helpless. Normally all of his 20 rooms are occupied at this time of year - the peak trekking season - but now only five are. "We have been doing this business for more than 40 years, but we've never seen this kind of disaster."

Krishna Gauchan, whose hotel is close to the glacier-fed Modi river, is worried too. "This river can flood if the glacier collapses or if the glacial lake bursts out and we are living in constant fear."

Tourism investors promote some of these spots as destinations without factoring in the risks, suggests Khagendra Poudel, a geographer at a local campus of Tribhuvan University. It's similar to what's happening in parts of north India's Himalayan valleys which host major pilgrimages.

The president of the Hotel Association Pokhara admits tourism businesses have expanded "without any proper planning" in the region and been promoted "haphazardly".

"By the time disasters hit, it is too late," says Laxman Subedi. "As a professional body we are trying to create awareness among tourism entrepreneurs and investors."

Moment buildings are swept away as flash flood hits Nepal's Budhi Gandaki river

But it's not just tourism. The regions surrounding the Bhotekoshi and Trishuli rivers have seen a significant rise in the construction of hydropower plants.

"As river valleys become increasingly populated, development decisions being made today will shape disaster risk for decades to come," warns the study's lead author, University of Leeds glaciologist C Scott Watson.

And even more buildings may be under threat than those identified by the researchers.

The study categorised buildings at risk as all those that were within 50 vertical metres (164ft) of a river or glacial lake's surface.

But during the catastrophic flooding on 26 August, locals in Rasuwa district say the river level had risen by 70m (230ft) at some points - so even buildings that were relatively far from the river banks were hit and buried by debris-sludge.

Two months after the devastating August floods, these buildings in Trishuli Bazar remain devastated

The risks in all of these areas are largely driven by warming in the Himalayan region, which scientists say is happening at twice the global rate. This has sped up the melting of glaciers, icefields, snowpacks, and ground that's normally frozen all year.

This can, in turn, destabilize mountain slopes which are also frequently shaken by earthquakes, unleashing devastating disasters downstream.

The rapid melting is also filling up and expanding glacial lakes until they burst, causing deadly floods. Nepal alone has documented around 30 of these events since 1940.

Another issue is that rising temperatures cause rain to reach high altitudes where it used to only snow, leading to slope failures, rock and ice falls, and other disasters.

Nepal's Prime Minister Balendra Shah raised the dangers of climate change at his recent UN General Assembly speech, where he said his country was paying a "colossal price" for a crisis it did not create.

"Every major disaster threatens to erase years of development. And it forces a poor country to borrow money to repair damage it did not cause."

Alton Byers, a mountain geographer at the University of Colorado who was not involved in the study, says it should serve as a "springboard" to bring about more effective early-warning systems and other precautionary measures.

But achieving that will be a challenge. Campaigners say building regulations aren't implemented on the ground and through the years, development has been haphazard in too many places.

Roads, hydropower projects, hotels and market towns have expanded on the low, flat land along rivers, which is accessible and buildable, says Prof Surya Raj Acharya, an urban planning expert and registrar at the University of Nepal.

Those responsible have often ignored safeguards including keeping off the river bank, he adds.

"A fragile ecosystem like the Himalaya demands stricter planning and building regulations than elsewhere, yet these rules have been weak and poorly enforced."

Authorities in Nepal say they are working to address these issues. Settlements within 15m (49ft) of the riverbank along the recently devastated Rasuwa-Bhotekoshi corridor will soon be banned, according to the national disaster agency. Similar rules are being introduced in other river corridors soon.

"It is a challenging job because people in many places have sentimental attachments with their land and property but we cannot ignore the increasing risks," says chief executive Dharam Raj Uprety.

There has been "rampant construction" in the Indian state of Uttarakhand in areas at risk from flooding, activists say

A similar story has unfolded across Nepal's western border in India's Uttarakhand state, where maximum tourism limits and environmental regulations have been bypassed in the name of economic growth, claims activist Anoop Nautiyal.

On paper there are zoning rules prohibiting the construction of houses and infrastructure too close to river banks, "but on the ground we see no implementation of such rules.

"You can see rampant construction all over."

The Uttarakhand government on 12 October approved new floodplain zoning rules to regulate land use and development. "Its objective is to protect life, property and important infrastructure from possible flood damage while maintaining the natural flow of flood waters," the state's cabinet decision read.

But Basanta Raj Adhikari, director of the Centre for Disaster Studies at Tribuvan University, thinks it will not be possible to mitigate such threats completely.

Insurance grades, he suggests, could be another way to encourage people to build in less risky areas. If authorities were to map places as high, moderate and low risk, he believes insurance coverage would "follow accordingly" ensuring people who build homes in safer areas get better protection.

But for now, the pace of construction across the region shows no signs of slowing.
