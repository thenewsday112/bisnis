#2914. Back to the Backwoods

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

I first heard about the “Fun Scale” when I was on the PCT in 2021: somewhere in southern California, a fellow SOBO described the four types of fun. Most websites condense that list to three, and I’m going to reduce it to a simple set of circumstances.

Usually in an outdoor setting, “type two fun” gets the most attention. As a thru-hiker, you’re often going to be hot, cold, wet, thirsty, hungry, tired, and sore. If personal growth comes from discomfort, you’ll grow a lot. With time to reflect, you’ll also (hopefully) be able to look back fondly at the uncomfortable days. Beyond that, if you consistently have a type three experience, quitting is probably the right choice.

In terms of fun, every job I’ve ever had was structured two, one, three. The thickness of the sandwich varied a lot, and getting through stage three required perseverance. During the final stage of my last job, reading The Trek posts each lunchtime was a good way to escape the daily grind. If you can relate, and you’re planning a thru-hike, consider becoming a Trek blogger. In a virtual world increasingly overrun by chatbots, your efforts will be appreciated.

Before starting the PCT in 2021, I intended to blog in real time. I soon discovered, however, that gazing at the scenery was preferable to tapping away on my phone. In town, I had neither the time nor the willpower to devote to writing. I admire the bloggers who make time. Their stories contain type two fun complete with all of its rough edges.

As usual, I’m posting post-trail, using my paper-and-pencil journal for reference. The downside of the delay is that time has already started to smooth those rough edges. It might, possibly, lead me to describe a rosier version of events than if I’d blogged in real time. I’m only mentioning it because I don’t want to inadvertently set unrealistic expectations for prospective thru-hikers.

So, with my long-winded disclaimer out of the way, let’s get this show on the road. Or more precisely, in the air.

I needed to get to the western terminus of the Ice Age Trail (IAT), so in the last week of July, I flew from Boise to Denver to Minneapolis. The flights were on time, my backpack arrived safely, but Frontier ended up being no cheaper than a non-budget airline.

The Frontier employees I spoke to all seemed to dislike their job and/or customers, which wasn’t surprising. With every interaction, I was informed of extra costs; sometimes optional, sometimes not. Assigned seats were $25 per flight. I declined.

On my second flight, my row had four empty seats. A few rows ahead, a guy asked if he could change seats, and the flight attendant said no. I waited to move to the window until she went to the back of the plane. The guy on the other side of the aisle moved to his window. The middle-seat occupants in the row behind me moved to the aisle seats in my row. When the flight attendant returned to give her safety demonstration, she knew people had switched seats. We looked innocent. We said nothing. She didn’t have the time or energy to argue, but she clearly wasn’t pleased. I won’t be flying Frontier Airlines again.

I started the next day with a one mile walk, doubling the number of miles on my new boots. My bus left just after midday, and dropped me about 30 miles north of the city in a commuter town called Forest Lake. I bought a five day resupply at the nearby Walmart, shouldered my pack, and walked another mile to offload my duffel bag at the post office. All I had to do now was find my way to the start of the IAT. At first I considered hitching, but couldn’t find a place I liked: somewhere with a pull-out, and slower vehicles that were obviously going in the direction I needed to go. While I was searching for a potential location, a passerby sized me up and took an educated guess.

“Weed store’s right there,” he pointed over his shoulder.

After that, I decided to take an Uber. The car picked me up about five minutes later, and my day’s journey ended at Interstate State Park.

At eight o’clock the next morning, I walked my final Minnesota mile. If not for the rush-hour traffic on Highway 8, the trail along the St Croix River would have been peaceful. I crossed the bridge into Wisconsin, walked a few hundred yards, and put a hand on the rock marking the IAT’s western terminus. Two trail-miles later, I celebrated with two donuts and a cold Mountain Dew. Six miles after that, I did my first tick-check. I found the first of many that I’d have to scrape off my skin in the coming weeks.

At the end of day one, at the edge of a small town called Centuria, I made camp. In the morning, I continued north along a rail-to-trail which is part of the State Trails Network. I stopped for a late breakfast at the second of two small towns, then disappeared into the backcountry for a few days. Here’s what I learned.

Day four, my phone informed me of a heat advisory. Temperature: 90℉. Humidity: 90%. That afternoon, during the road-walk to Grassy Lake Segment, my brain overheated. It suddenly occurred to me that the world needs a hiking-based romance novel. Here’s the first page of my masterpiece.

It was a torrid, sultry, steamy night amidst the swamps of western Wisconsin. The day’s heat advisory wouldn’t expire until midnight, still three hours away. In the corner of a remote trailhead parking lot, in the twilight, stood a hiker. He paused to look down, for a moment, with a certain satisfaction. Naked, but now fully erect, his tent was a monument to self-confidence. Without its fly, it announced to the world that there would be no thunderstorms tonight.

“Oooh, that’s hot,” he murmured, as he slowly pulled the tent zipper down. Then, he eased himself inside. Things were about to get sticky.

He immediately stuck to his sleeping pad. Its rubbery texture resisted the advances of his moist skin, issuing a series of squeaks and moans as he wriggled into position. He lay there for a minute, listening to the crickets and mosquitoes.

He removed his shorts. Not his compression shorts, just his actual shorts. “I’m not Donald Duck,” he thought. The corner of his mouth twitched into a half-smile at the memory.

It was pretty tight in there, and peeling off his t-shirt required real effort. As he squirmed and maneuvered, the sleeping pad complained again, but less convincingly. It was more amenable now that it was better lubricated.

The night air was still and stultifying. Beads of sweat trickled down his torso. Salt stung his legs. They still bore the marks of his previous tryst: a relationship that had started well, but ended badly.

He mopped his brow with the t-shirt. All he could do now was wait. The temperature and humidty would relent soon, he hoped, and he’d sink into a peaceful, restorative sleep…

#3420. 6 Subtle Things I’ve Noticed My Favorite Yoga Teachers Do That Make All the Difference

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Nicole Fabian-Weber is a writer and 500-hour certified yoga teacher who is inspired by the subtle yet powerful habits that make yoga instructors more effective. She currently teaches at Home Power Yoga in Cranford, New Jersey.

My closest friends don’t take my yoga classes. Not because they don’t like my teaching or me (or at least, that’s what they tell me). It’s because of Mindy. For the past decade, twice weekly, my friends have cleared their busy schedules to take a 75-minute vinyasa class with their most beloved yoga teacher, Mindy.

I don’t take it personally. Whether someone has been practicing yoga for a few months or 20 years, they likely have a favorite teacher. As a yoga teacher, I have my own Mindys for whom I have no problem rearranging my schedule or dragging myself out of bed before sunrise so I can take their class.

But what exactly is it about our favorite yoga teachers that gives that can’t-put-your-finger-on-it magic to their classes?

When I asked my friends, at first they were vague in explaining Mindy’s allure as a teacher. “She’s got good energy,” they said. “She’s calm and grounding.” But then one of my friends got right to the heart of it. “I wind up doing things in her class I didn’t know I was capable of—without even realizing it,” she explained. That resonated for me, because my favorite yoga teachers have the same subtle yet powerful impact.

The more I investigated the Mindys in my orbit, the more I realized that they all make similar choices when it comes to their presence, style, approach, and demeanor. And they’re the exact habits that I started to incorporate into my own teaching so that my students feel empowered and inspired when they get on their mats.

These are some of the habits that my favorite yoga teachers do that make all the difference in class. Whenever I’m in need of inspiration or find myself in a teaching rut, these are the tools I return to again and again.

Silence can feel awkward—including for teachers who are just starting out. So it can be difficult to resist the urge to fill the space with cues, advice, and reminders to breathe.

Yet my favorite yoga teachers seem comfortable with periods of silence in class. It’s a skill to hold space in a room by intentionally and wordlessly letting moments linger so that students can sit with sensations, embrace the present moment, and turn inward to find what they need. These teachers can still command the space with their presence, but in a quiet, supportive, “I’m here for you” kind of way.

The teachers I never snooze on are the ones who meet students where they are during early morning classes—exhausted. Instead of willing us awake with pep, their energy is calm and steady, and without fail, they motivate us to ignite our own inner fire. It’s kind of like waking up with tea versus an energy drink. It’s a slower, more sustainable build—and, in my opinion, it’s way more satisfying.

Similarly, during evening classes, students are often coming straight from work or ducking out of a busy house. The teachers that I find the most effective recognize and honor when students might be carrying a lot onto the mat. Perhaps they gauge the level of intensity during class, or share a few extra moments of quiet at the outset for everyone to land.

During my 300-hour training, we were tasked with filming ourselves teaching in order to observe what was working and what wasn’t. When I watched the playback of my class, which was themed around equanimity or calm in the face of change, I was somewhat aghast to see that I paced around the room the entire time in an almost chaotic manner. Calm? Hardly. Turbulent? Yes.

On the other hand, in one of the best yoga classes I’ve ever taken, the teacher spoke about ahimsa (non-violence). The kind tone and moderate volume of her voice, along with how peacefully she moved throughout the space, encapsulated the message perfectly.

The teachers I keep coming back to generally don’t pad their instructions with extra verbiage—they’re comfortable getting their point across with, say, five words instead of 20. By doing this, they’re not only trusting students to take or leave what they’re saying, they’re leaving their cues undiluted so they can land with more impact.

Having a commanding presence while being completely authentic about their own humanity and messiness is a balance many experienced teachers manage to strike. When they inevitably mix up right and left or skip a side, they acknowledge it, maybe even laugh it off, and move on.

A quick self-aware joke or well-timed laugh can be exactly the reminder everyone needs not to take themselves too seriously and to recall that the main goal, in all ways, is to unite.

When I take class with any of my favorite teachers, I and the other students are the main characters. The teachers facilitate the experience, but they don’t claim it as their own. My go-to teachers read the room to try to understand what might be the most helpful tool they can offer—perhaps giving guidance, sharing an anecdote, or stepping back and letting the practice speak for itself. They’re also familiar with straying from their planned sequence if the room is calling for something different.

Put another way, my favorite yoga teachers know that often the most powerful approach is to offer a bit of guidance and then let students take it from there.

Artra’s Incredible New Watch Looks Like a Rolleiflex TLR

Lens and watchmaker Artra is teaming up with the legendary camera brand, Rollei, on a limited-edition Rolleiflex-inspired watch.

Designed to celebrate 100 years of Rollei cameras, the new Artra Camera Dual: Rolleiflex watch looks remarkably like a classic Rolleiflex twin-lens reflex camera. It is extremely stylish, very cool, and coming soon.

As Digital Camera World reports, Artra and Rolleiflex haven’t announced availability yet, but the timepiece is far too cool not to show off, even without all the details.

“We condense the soul of the classic TLR camera onto a wrist-worn timepiece,” Artra writes. “Dual dials faithfully recreate the twin-lens layout of Rolleiflex. Every rotating disc and engraved badge pays tribute to the mechanical romance of film photography.”

The watch will be limited to just 100 pieces worldwide, one for each year since Rolleiflex’s founding. Technically, Rollei didn’t launch the TLR camera that inspires the watch until 1929, but the company’s first camera, the wild Rolleidoscop, launched in 1926.

Watch blog, Watchik, writes that the Artra Camera Dual Rolleiflex made its public debut at the 45th Hong Kong Watch & Clock Fair. Watchik notes that the watch features a Miyota automatic movement, dual sapphire crystals, and six enamel color options: white, green, black, red, gold, and blue. However, Digital Camera World claims the watch instead features a Seiko NH05 automatic movement.

While Artra hasn’t disclosed pricing on its social media channels or shown the new watch on its website, Digital Camera World also reports the watch is priced at HK$13,800, about $1,760 at current exchange rates. Artra itself says the official release, including pricing, is coming in “late October,” so it should arrive soon.

Interestingly, this isn’t the first Rollei-inspired timepiece to hit the market in recent years. Tacs released the ATL Rollei, a $980 watch that puts the Rollei’s iconic TLR look entirely inside a traditional circular watch face. While it’s out of stock, it is still a cool piece. It’s definitely not as Rollei-like as Artra’s new watch, though.

In any event, this is one to keep a close eye on. Artra’s previous photography-inspired watches have been extremely stylish and very detail-oriented. The company has clearly found an interesting niche beyond making photographic lenses.

#4006. What's Really at the Root of Defensiveness?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Defensiveness is one of the biggest problems we face in close relationships. It’s really hard for many of us to hear anything negative about ourselves, something we said or did, or a way we behaved that hurt, angered, upset, disappointed, or failed someone else. We’re really bad at being told that we did something "wrong." It’s staggering, really, the degree to which we humans will battle to the death to prove that we’ve done nothing wrong and someone else’s experience is not true.

Often, when someone close to us tells us we’ve upset them in some way, almost immediately, we brace ourselves and go into an internal state of code red. We stop listening, disconnect from what they’re saying, and switch into a kind of fight-or-flight response. We're not able to hear their words or empathize with their experience because, consciously or unconsciously, we've determined that we're in grave danger and our self-esteem is at risk. It's at this point that our survival instinct kicks in and kidnaps the more evolved and wiser part of our mind.

For every situation we encounter in life, we may write a narrative, a story that answers three important questions: what is actually happening, why is it happening, and what it means. And specifically, what it means about (and for) ourselves. We experience everything through the lens of our personal “self,” a “me” that we've constructed and that must be continually maintained, shored up, and protected.

It’s not, however, because we’re all raging narcissists that we look at every moment from a self-centered perspective, to gauge whether it will serve or harm us, validate our self-image or destroy it. This ever-present question by which we live, “Does this moment feel good, does it make me feel good about myself?” is really all about survival. Our survival instinct is constantly waging a battle with life—vigilantly trying to ensure that our self-image, our story of “me,” doesn’t get harmed or destroyed. The problem, however, is that we imagine that the narrative we’ve constructed about ourselves, our “I’m somebody who” story, is who we fundamentally are. Anything, therefore, that goes against it holds the power to annihilate us at an existential level.

How this plays out in everyday life is that if someone tells me I’ve hurt them, whether intentionally or not, I can't listen, try to understand, or feel openhearted towards them. All I know is that I’ve done something wrong, which means that I am wrong and hurtful at my core, which, through the lens of my survival instinct, means that I will be cast out of the herd and left behind to die. All I can hear in that moment are deafening alarm bells; all I can feel is danger. Their experience puts my “me” story, namely, that I am a kind person, in jeopardy. As a result, their experience must be invalidated; their perception of me proven wrong. Because my "me" story doesn’t feel like just a story but feels like my very existence, I must get them to agree that I am not who they think I am, not the hurtful person they're portraying me to be. Once I can get this confirmation, then (and only then) can I return safely to my story of “me.” With the other's narrative proven wrong, I can relax, knowing that I am once again a kind person. Still, even with this current threat deactivated, I must remain hyper-vigilant and on watch for anyone's experience that might threaten my “me.”

Our sense of who we are is the sum of every experience we’ve ever lived, every compliment, judgment, and perception of us that’s ever been levied for or against us. Our self-image is therefore highly unsafe, transitory, and fragile: if we say or do something “wrong” (in our eyes—or someone else’s eyes), then who we are is fundamentally “wrong.” Our self feels dependent on and collapsed into the perceptions of our actions. This puts us in a terribly vulnerable position when it comes to other people’s experience, as well as all the other uncontrollable parts of life. After all, other people experience the world — and us — through the lens of their own stories, not ours. The value, meaning, and intention assigned to our behavior vary infinitely depending on the lens through which it’s being viewed. And yet, we award their perceptions with the power to confirm or deny who we are and can be.

Other people and life in general consistently force us to confront parts of ourselves and face perceptions of us that don’t align with our curated narrative on “who we are.” The way other people experience and perceive us, through their conditioning, beliefs, wounds, and everything else, is often inconsistent with the story of “me” we’ve constructed in our mind. And so we fight with this fundamental reality: the person we’re perceived as, how we’re experienced from the outside, is different from how we experience ourselves from the inside. Different and perhaps—also valid.

In part two of this series, I'll address the ways we can start to heal defensiveness and shift our relationship with other people and their experience. Then, we don’t have to be so defensive, self-protective and self-focused; we can truly meet other people, be curious, and empathize with other people's experience. We can stop feeling like everyone on earth is a potential threat to who we are. Ultimately, we build a sense of self that can tolerate the full range of realities we encounter in the world. Stay tuned.

We were travelling in one of those old cars with a driver’s seat and a boot… and apparently no particular consideration for passenger comfort. We had to experiment with various contortions to fit ourselves, our backpacks and whatever remained of our legs into the available space.

A minor detail. The ride promised to be an epic one.

We picked up Juliette on the way and, by noon, we were back at the trailhead. I was aiming for 10–12 miles that day, to give my knee some time to adjust, especially with a full resupply on my back. Another hot day, with water sources few and far between.

Juliette and I walked together for most of the afternoon. When we bumped into Grace, whom I had not seen since Etna, our little team of two suddenly became a group of three.

My mood, however, was still not quite there.

I could rationally explain what was happening to myself, but I was still tired and, somehow, not particularly excited about the days ahead. It was an odd feeling, perhaps the first time since I had started this whole adventure. I knew the fatigue would catch up with me eventually. I had never seriously considered quitting. Not an option. I’m not a quitter. Or at least, rarely!

But if I’m being honest, this feeling had been brewing for a few weeks already. Limping around for days and being unable to squat or climb without negotiating with my knee hardly makes for an exciting start to the morning.

Cereal bars and trail mix. Tortillas with tuna and chips. Instead, I was beginning to realise that I still had two entire states to cross. I was worried my body might not hold up, and, for once, I was even doubting my ability to make it all the way to the sacrosanct Canadian border.

More tortillas. More tuna. More ramen. More Clif Bars. More water sources. More disrupted nights. And of course, more miles. Good grief. Canada suddenly sounded like it was on the other side of the world. But no quitting. Not yet.

And if it couldn’t give me a brand-new body and a completely refreshed mindset, at least it could provide some company to help me get back in the game. Juliette and Grace were great company, with very different personalities but equally fun and motivating. As the afternoon went on, we spotted a potential campsite on FarOut, conveniently located near a pit toilet.

Sometimes, it’s all about embracing the suck, as the saying goes. But sometimes, the only thing you need to cheer you up is a bloody pit toilet. No squatting. No falling over. No digging a cat hole. And actual toilet paper!

I don’t think I had ever been that excited about a toilet in my life. But let’s face it: after more than three months on the trail, avoiding the cat-hole routine and sparing my still-painful knee a few awkward movements had become legitimate priorities. I had even heard of hikers who would go out of their way to reach a pit toilet and then… hold it for the rest of the time.

This particular icon represented Hyatt Lake Resort, a tiny hotel in a remote location with hot meals and a small shop. It was a bit of a detour. But it took the three of us approximately three seconds to agree on a plan. Hyatt Lake Resort, here we come.

What a glorious morning! The night had been quiet, and I had barely woken up. The three of us got up and packed at roughly the same time, then set off after a light breakfast.

We were saving room for the feast awaiting us at Hyatt Lake Resort.

By that point, my appetite had got completely out of control. After a quick scan of the menu, we all opted for the monstrous pancakes with bacon and eggs. Add coffee, and we were all set. Breakfast of champions. Or, more accurately, breakfast of people who had already been walking for hours and were still planning to walk for several more.

The resort itself was fairly simple: a main building housing the restaurant and a tiny shop, surrounded by a few cabins. Across the road was a peaceful lake with kayaks available for rent. It was probably a popular spot earlier in the season, although it was rather quiet when we visited.

With our stomachs full, we left in excellent spirits and decided to walk around the lake rather than retrace our steps to the trail. In hindsight, this was perhaps not our brightest idea.

The scenery was pleasant enough, and the quiet, deserted lake offered some lovely views here and there. But after about 3km of road walking that felt suspiciously like 15, we were beginning to question our decision.

Then we spotted a truck slowing down. A man got out and waved at us. Juliette and I looked at each other. Was this guy taking pity on us and about to offer us a ride? Nah. Probably not.

We hurried towards him to find out. His name was Randy, and he had indeed spotted us at the resort and taken pity on our little expedition. Although the area was perfectly safe, he said he would feel better driving the three of us back to the trailhead.

Of course, we could have declined on principle. There are rules to this hiking business, after all, including the sacred principle of not hitchhiking around a boring section just because you can’t be bothered to walk it. But then there was the heavy pack, the post-pancake digestion process, and the prospect of walking an unknown distance on a road. Principles are important. Pancakes are also important. And humans are weak…

We climbed into the back of the truck, where Randy’s dogs were absolutely delighted to have some company.

As it turned out, the road was much longer than we had expected. Randy had saved us a fair amount of unnecessary walking, and we were very grateful. Back at the trailhead, we thanked him and resumed our journey, mostly uphill through the forest, which rewarded us with some spectacular views. I was happy to discover that my knee was finally behaving again. I tried to take it easy, and, thankfully, there were no major blowdowns to negotiate. Around 6pm, we decided to call it a day and camp together in an area scattered with fallen trees.

The day had been full of little adventures and plenty of laughter. I was grateful to be hiking again with a group. I know I needed that. We were all pretty independent and tended to walk at our own pace, but having people around after the fatigue of Northern California had given me a new burst of energy and motivation.

Over dinner, we chatted and exchanged random gossip while doing what had become our daily ritual: checking the next day’s mileage, water sources and elevation profile. And, naturally, we spotted another side quest. Fish Lake Resort offered yet another opportunity for burgers, fries and a beer.

The hiking gods were definitely on our side.

We left camp around 7am. As usual, the three of us soon split up, each walking at her own pace. After about five hours, we reached the main road where we planned to hitchhike to Fish Lake Resort. I had been restraining my appetite all morning. By almost 1pm, I could quite literally have eaten a whole cow.

Juliette had just arrived, and we stood together by the road, trying to come up with facial expressions and jokes that might convince passing drivers to stop. Or perhaps inspire pity. We were open to either approach. A few other hikers joined us, but for a while, nothing worked.

Then a car coming from the opposite direction slowed down. The occupants offered to take us back to the resort they had just left.

We thanked them profusely and jumped in. Katie and Dwayne, sitting in the front, explained that they had originally been heading the other way but had decided to help us because it was only a short detour.

The moment we arrived at the restaurant, we spotted Fred and Anna leaving to get back on the trail. We had about five minutes to catch up before they disappeared again.

Then came the usual routine: finding electrical outlets to recharge every device we owned, studying the menu and contemplating the burgers with barely disguised enthusiasm.

Somehow, my Dr Pepper consumption capacity had increased significantly since leaving Mexico. I was beginning to suspect that, by the end of the trail, my blood might be partially carbonated.

We settled down to enjoy the comfort of civilization. The burgers arrived, and Grace soon joined us. She had been behind us but had texted to say we should go ahead with hitchhiking. She had reached the road later and, as luck would have it, found a ride too.

We also bumped into a French couple hiking SOBO, exchanged some information about trail conditions, particularly in Washington, and shared what we knew about Northern California.

Three hours after arriving, we were finally ready to leave.

When we asked the manager about the best way to return to the trail, he casually informed us that the resort offered a free shuttle service. Of course it did. We could have saved ourselves the entire hitchhiking operation. But where would the adventure have been in that?

At 4:15pm, we were back on the trail, fully hydrated, thoroughly fed and ready for another 8.5 miles. Total mileage for the day: 25 miles, including our luxurious side quest. Victory!

Grace and I walked at a good pace through the forest and eventually found a lovely, empty meadow. It looked like a perfect place to camp.

Grace decided to cowboy camp, while I pitched my mesh tent.

We were having dinner when we suddenly heard some strange noises.

The sound was somewhere between a cow and a wild animal. It came again, then again, seemingly closer and from different directions. Now, we had been living in the wilderness for more than three months. We were both perfectly rational human beings. And yet, somehow, we were both getting a little nervous. We considered the possibilities. Bears? Unlikely. Zombies? Technically, no evidence either way.

After a thorough process of elimination based on absolutely no scientific knowledge whatsoever, we settled on bucks. Deer. Probably. That explanation seemed reassuring enough, so we decided to believe it. There was no point letting facts get in the way of a good night’s sleep.

Kelly, a hiker from Boston whom I had met at my hostel in Ashland, arrived around 8:30pm and joined us for dinner before setting up camp nearby.

The meadow was lovely. The wildlife was… mysterious.

The same strange animal noises woke me up at 5am.

I stayed firmly inside my sleeping bag. It was chilly, and absolutely nobody around me seemed to be moving. Not Kelly. Not Grace.

I saw no reason to be the first to leave the warmth of my sleeping bag and investigate the potential presence of whatever had been making those noises. Let the wildlife have its morning. I would have mine later.

As I lay there, I thought about the fatigue that had been creeping up on me over the past few weeks. I was glad that the worst of that feeling seemed to have faded. But would I manage to keep my motivation up? It was a question I had not really asked myself before.

The day itself unfolded much like any other on the PCT. Checking mileage. Checking elevation. Checking water sources. Making random small talk with other hikers. And before I knew it, I had covered 25 miles. Somewhere along the way, I had lost Grace, so I ended up camping alone in a meadow in the forest. I hoped for a quiet, uneventful night. Ideally, one without any unidentified wildlife concerts.

I crossed another burned area, reminiscent of Lassen National Park in Norcal, with a wide-open plain dotted with dead trees, their magnificent white trunks still reaching towards the blue morning sky.

But life was slowly reclaiming the landscape. Tiny pine trees were emerging, adding splashes of green to the otherwise desolate scenery. There was something moving about it: a reminder that even after destruction, things find a way to grow again.

The trail itself was, as so often in Oregon, smooth, rolling and forgiving. Of course, there were still blowdowns. I had been dreading the section between Fish Lake Resort and Crater Lake, which had a reputation for forcing hikers to scramble over or around more than 200 fallen trees. Luckily, a trail maintenance crew seemed to have passed through recently. I counted no more than 20 blowdowns, most of them easy enough to walk around. My IT band was grateful. So was I.

After the rest days in Ashland, my knee had improved considerably, and I was beginning to feel more confident about what lay ahead.

My goal for the day was Mazama Village, below Crater Lake, one of Oregon’s major highlights. I was excited about it. The plan was to arrive around lunchtime, enjoy some decent food, take a shower, collect my resupply box sent from Dunsmuir, wash my clothes and catch up with friends who were either ahead of me or still somewhere behind. In other words, a highly ambitious programme of civilization-related activities.

A long stretch of forest followed the burned area, and I started getting a little bored. Time for a podcast. I opted for a programme about Farinelli, one of the most famous Italian castrati, who had travelled across Europe and enchanted kings and queens with his voice.

And then something quite magical happened.

Listening to opera music in the middle of the forest was unexpectedly moving. The grandiose voices seemed to fill the trees, transforming an otherwise ordinary stretch of trail into something almost otherworldly. For a while, the music and the surrounding landscape seemed perfectly in tune. The forest became a concert hall, and I had a front-row seat. No tickets required. No dress code. And, best of all, no one checking whether I had showered recently.

I arrived at Mazama around 12:30pm, had a burger, called home and headed to the store. And who did I bump into? Corinna, whom I had not seen since Burney Falls! Pack Rat was there too, followed by Grace and then Juliette. Suddenly, the afternoon turned into a series of catch-ups, conversations, showers and laundry. I even managed to hand-wash my clothes and get everything dry within a couple of hours, thanks to the hot, sunny weather.

Perfect conditions for another essential trail activity: ice cream.

It is funny, because I hardly ever crave ice cream at home. But out here, somehow, it becomes a perfectly reasonable reward for walking around in the wilderness for months. Inspired by Grace, I treated myself to a scoop.

My resupply box was waiting for me, so I only needed to buy a few extra items. Other hikers began arriving gradually, until the entrance to the general store was practically taken over by hikers. Many of them had attended Trail Days, and the gathering had turned into a kind of travelling reunion. People were catching up on the latest news, swapping stories and comparing plans. The PCT bubble had caught up with us.

The campsite for PCT hikers was cheap, but located at the far end of the campground, among huge RVs and weekend visitors who had come to see one of the deepest crater lakes in the world. By the time I arrived, the place was full. I eventually found a tiny patch of ground just large enough for my tent.

Corinna, a few other hikers and I had dinner together. I ordered a huge pizza. It was so enormous that I barely managed half of it and had to take the rest away. Pizza on trail: another experiment in backcountry cuisine.

I headed back to the campsite early and chatted over a beer with a friend before we returned to our respective tents.

Would I manage to get up at 3:30am, pack my things and hike the climb to Crater Lake’s rim in time for sunrise?

This had been on my PCT bucket list for a long time. But getting there would require an early start and a climb of around four to five miles. Sunrise, inconveniently, was not going to wait for me.

I was also feeling a little demotivated at the thought of doing it alone.

So I decided to leave the decision until the morning.

And went to sleep with absolutely no faith in myself whatsoever.

Although we typically eat it in small quantities, basil contains vitamins, minerals, and antioxidant compounds that researchers have investigated for their potential health effects. Most such studies, however, use concentrated extracts rather than amounts typically eaten in food.

Native to tropical Asia and widely cultivated across the Mediterranean basin, basil (Ocimum basilicum) belongs to the Lamiaceae (mint) family. Naturally low in calories and made up largely of water, basil also contains a range of nutrients, though a typical garnish provides only small amounts of most. While research into basil’s potential health effects is promising, evidence on the benefits of eating it in typical culinary amounts remains limited. For most people, basil is perfectly safe when consumed in the quantities typically used in cooking. However, as with many herbs, concentrated extracts and essential oils are a different matter.

#589. Quantum particles never completely forget where they came from

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A quantum birthmark keeps a chaotic particle at least twice as likely to return to its starting state, even after a very long time.

Stir a drop of milk into a cup of tea, and within seconds there’s no telling where the drop first landed.

Chaotic mixing like that erases any record of a starting point. In the quantum world of atoms and electrons, new research suggests, the erasing never finishes.

Physicists at Tampere University in Finland, Harvard University and TU Dresden in Germany call what’s left behind a quantum birthmark.

Even a particle bouncing around chaotically stays at least twice as likely to be found back in its starting condition as in any comparable one.

That holds when its motion is averaged over a very long time. The work is theory, built on math and computer simulations rather than a lab experiment.

Joonas Keski-Rahkonen, a co-author, works in the Quantum Control and Dynamics group at Tampere’s Computational Physics Laboratory.

“In the everyday world, chaos wipes the slate clean. What we found is that quantum systems can’t hide their origin, even in the middle of chaos,” he said.

The team worked with one of physics’ oldest test cases: a ball bouncing forever around a billiard table with rounded ends.

Physicists call that shape a stadium, and waves in oddly shaped spaces like it tend to turn chaotic.

A real ball on that table bounces off the curved walls in a new direction every time. Track it long enough and it visits every spot equally, with no hint left of where it began.

Particles as small as electrons don’t behave like billiard balls.

They spread out like ripples on water, and the most a physicist can say is how likely a particle is to be found in one spot rather than another.

A small, bunched-up ripple of that kind is called a wave packet.

The researchers simulated wave packets inside a stadium with soft walls the particle could seep into slightly, closer to what electrons meet in nanoscale devices.

Within a few bounces, each wave packet looked like random noise spread across the whole stadium.

At any single moment, that noise looked featureless. The memory showed up only when the team averaged the motion over a very long stretch of time.

Averaged that way, a wave packet stayed at least twice as likely to be found back in its starting state as in a typical state with the same energy.

For some kinds of starting states, the minimum rises to three times. The advantage didn’t belong to the first moment alone.

Every shape the wave packet passed through on its way kept it too, and the result held no matter when the team began averaging.

Classical physics predicts no advantage at all. By the team’s math, every sealed-off quantum system that doesn’t begin in a settled state carries a birthmark of its own.

To see what makes a birthmark stronger, the team launched four wave packets.

Three left the center of the stadium along paths that repeat: straight up and down, straight side to side, and a bow-tie shape. The fourth started off-center on an ordinary chaotic path.

The ordinary launch came out close to the minimum, at about 1.8 times. The team noted that a single wave packet can land slightly below two, since two is an average.

The up-and-down launch, which kept bouncing back to where it began, reached about 6.6 times, and about 8 in a stadium with hard walls.

Early bounces mattered most. Ripple patterns from the wave packet’s second hit on the wall still showed up in the long-time average.

Even the most ordinary wave packet reached only about half of the combinations of position and direction open to it. A classical ball would eventually have covered them all.

In 1984, Eric Heller, a Harvard physicist and co-author of the new study, found that some quantum states keep sharp imprints along repeating paths, a pattern called quantum scarring.

Scars were long treated as a rare exception to chaos. The team now treats them as one case of something broader: a scar is a birthmark made stronger by a path the particle keeps returning to.

Keski-Rahkonen tied the result to a larger question.

“That matters beyond textbook physics. It speaks to how quantum systems settle into equilibrium, and how our familiar classical world emerges from the quantum-mechanical rules,” he said.

He also pointed to a practical side. “For instance, it matters that a system which quietly remembers its starting point behaves differently from one that forgets,” Keski-Rahkonen said.

In tiny electronic devices holding even thousands of electrons, several statistical measures commonly run three or more times higher than random behavior would predict.

The authors read that as a sign of a birthmark. The American Physical Society’s magazine Physics highlighted the study in a feature article.

The math assumes a quantum system sealed off from its surroundings. Once such a system leaks information into the world around it, the universal part of the birthmark fades.

The particle then starts behaving more like a classical ball, though marks left by early bounces may still remain.

So far, the team hasn’t tested the idea in a lab. How strongly a birthmark shows up also depends on what physicists choose to measure, and the researchers call that an important question for future work.

They suggest looking with quantum simulators, machines related to quantum computers that are built from carefully controlled atoms.

In those machines, physicists can set how many states a system has room for, so they could test whether a birthmark holds as the system grows.

For now, the birthmark exists on paper and in simulations. The next step is measuring one in a real device kept isolated well enough to hold on to it.

The full study was published in the journal Physical Review X.

#919. Jay-Z Bets on Himself for the Super Bowl Halftime Show

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

This is an edition of the weekly newsletter Tap In, GQ senior associate editor Frazier Tharpe’s final word on the most heated online discourse about music, movies, and TV. Sign up here to get it free.

In 2008, when Washington Wizards guard Deshawn Stevenson called LeBron James overrated, Jay-Z intervened on LeBron’s behalf with “Blow the Whistle,” a loose, low-stakes freestyle over Bay Area legend Too Short’s instant classic of the same name. The track is all about being too big to respond to petty swipes. The most enduring line goes: “We let the money do the talkin—as you can see, we be talkin rather often.”

Fitting, then, that Jay opened his concert on Saturday night—the second of back-to-back shows at Los Angeles’ SoFi Stadium—with “Blow the Whistle.” In the midst of similar noise, he let the music do the talking.

The pair of shows marked the finale of Jay’s year-long Jay-Z30 project, celebrating the 30th anniversary of his debut album with a trio of sold-out concerts at Yankee Stadium this past summer and subsequent dates across London and Paris. Incidentally, the grand finale arrived at a moment when Jay is at the center of not one but two divisive debates. The first, currently raging on Rap Twitter and recently aggravated by 50 Cent, concerns his place in the rap canon (was he ever truly the undisputed “dominant” rapper in the game?) And then, with the announcement that he’s chosen himself to be the 2027 Super Bowl Halftime Show headliner, some are calling his relevance into question, or snidely remarking that he’ll need to lean heavily on his Rolodex to actually pull it off.

Both debates are ridiculous and reductive. So Jay used the occasion of two (more) sold-out stadium shows to prove why, running through a roughly 30-song setlist deftly curated with crowd-pleasers, B-Side gems(“The Watcher 2”!!), and hit singles—just him, a 10-piece band, and the occasional light show. Across both shows, there were only four guest moments: the aforementioned Short Dawg, John Legend & DJ Khaled for “God Did,” Scarface for what may have been the first-ever performance of their heralded collab “This Can’t Be Life,” and an extended medley from Dr. Dre and Snoop Dogg. All were sparing, intentional and worked in service of the overarching theme celebrating Jay’s lore. (After all, he did write “Still D.R.E.” Imagine if he came out with them to essentially perform his reference track.)

Jay’s fixation on his naysayers felt especially pointed, sometimes to a fault. While “God Did” is great, it made for an awkward encore on night one, though the stamina flex to do a three-minute verse right after doing a two-hour set was admirable. The obsession with tour cameos in general has gotten a little out of hand. Sure, it’s fun as hell when performers show off how deep their collaborative bag is and how influential their reach is—like, say, night two of Kanye’s Chicago homecoming or night three of Jay in Yankee Stadium. But reinforcing that you can move the crowd by yourself—without even the help of a backing track—is a flex of equal, if not greater, import. Having hits is paramount, but at what point is the effect diluted if you’re just having the track play and letting the audience do most of the work? Exhibiting athletic breath control, Jay tore through one anthem after another, from the lyrical gymnastics of “Hovi Baby” to a track that invites audience participation like “Beach Is Better”—or a song that requires both, like “N-gga What, N-gga Who,” breaking occasionally just to change fits.

On one hand it would’ve been even better if Jay was back on home turf instead of playing to an audience that inevitably consisted of some casuals. But in some regards, winning those folks over is an even more impressive display of endurance and reach. The “was Jay ever the guy?” discourse is amusing because even some of the people coming to his defense kind of get it wrong, relying too heavily on the idea that longevity trounces a few years of white-hot flash. That’s true, but it doesn’t tell the full story. I saw tweets that compared him to the turtle that beat the hare, or a reliable Honda Accord, both misguided, backhanded compliments that don’t really account for the magnitude of Hov’s imperial phase, with “Hard Knock Life,” “Jigga My N-gga,” “Big Pimpin,” “I Just Wanna Love You,” “Izzo,” “Excuse Me Miss,” “Dirt Off Your Shoulder,” and “99 Problems” being released in successive summers.

The Super Bowl thing is especially weird. Jay could’ve anointed himself the first rapper to do the Halftime Show, but instead he happily ceded that honor to Kendrick Lamar. In fact, he could’ve pegged himself for the gig at any time in the eight years he’s presided over the show. If Jay’s music just isn’t your thing, that’s your prerogative (and a skill issue). But the narratives feel forced. The notion that he has no appeal beyond New Yorkers and hip-hop fans of a certain age is a bizarre stance to take on the guy who headlined Coachella and Glastonbury, becoming the first rapper to do both. Moreover, the notion that he isn’t relevant or that his hits are “old” is coming from the same people who were excited to see Usher perform classics from 20-plus-year-old albums. Picking an artist who appeals to Gen-Z or Gen-Alpha has hardly been a major concern in years past. On their podcast, Joe Budden and his co-host Ice said they couldn’t get excited at the prospect of Jay having to perform some of his most pop and broadly appealing hits. But didn’t they chastise Lamar for a Halftime Show setlist mostly comprised of then-three-month-old GNX tracks? Is it “Oochie-wally” or is it “One Mic?”

To that last point, I’d argue that Jay’s recent shows—even going back to his Roots Picnic one-off in May—have been more thoughtfully and intentionally sequenced (backed by his longtime engineer, Young Guru, and longtime band, 1500 or Nothin) than even his classic tours of yore, weaving in a little something for everyone (like the way he mixes “N-ggas in Paris” with “Big Pimpin,” serving two different generations of fan at once). He didn’t just play the hits; he played the cool hits. Quieter songs got beefed up with string quartets, or an assist from Jay’s daughter on the keys. He used Watch the Throne beats to get off classic radio freestyle verses. Each tour stop had a moment designed to amp up the locals, like rhyming over a Giggs staple in “London” or mixing in “Last Time That I Checc’d” in L.A. He has a showman’s knack for routine and on-the-fly innovation.

There’s no way he makes a Halftime Show full of concessions to the masses and zero nods to the fans. I’d argue he’s brazen enough to do something like kicking things off with “Young, Gifted and Black”—“I’m America’s worst nightmare/I’m young, Black and holding my nuts like chea”—which would be a fair trade-off should he include something poppier, like “Young Forever.” As for the staples: does anybody truly think “Hard Knock Life” or “I Just Wanna Love You” are too old to have crossover appeal with a Super Bowl audience? Do you really have to be able to go bar-for-bar on “U Don’t Know” to appreciate Adam Blackstone absolutely shredding that bass outro? I don’t know how anyone looks at the list of tracks from either LA show and doesn’t think Jay’s only problem will be whittling his Super Bowl set down to a tight 13 minutes. (For what it’s worth, he described Kendrick’s decision to highlight mostly new songs as “brave” when I interviewed him earlier this year.)

Jay indirectly addressed some of the narratives in his closing remarks on Saturday night, alluding to bot accounts and smear campaigns that alleged half-empty crowds, lackluster audience engagement, and middling reception. He urged the audience, instead, to look around, use their own eyes, and preach the truth. A fine sentiment, but it was the last line that got me: “They’ll tell you Jigga ain’t Jigga… Hov is motherfucking Hov!”

All this talk on his legacy has Jay referring to himself in the third person, and once again sporting an offensively ostentatious new chain. It makes me wonder if the smoke, doubt, and disparagement has woken up the shit-talking flexer who made “So Ghetto” or “It’s Hot”—Jigga Man, as it were. As everyone anticipates all of this activity building to a new solo album, most (including the man himself) have wondered what he might have to say, what themes could span 10 or so tracks the same way he did on 4:44. Despite being outside this year, he hasn’t rapped much—the only official new verse we got was a remix he did with his wife that sounded more like someone playing around in the studio and clearing the cobwebs.

Before that though, there was Roots Picnic, the comeback show that set the tone for this year, where he paused the celebration to heat check all of his enemies and detractors with a lacerating new freestyle. He ended that verse with a warning: “Not too much on my name/Don’t forget who I am.” At the Halftime Show in February, he’s got a chance to make them remember.
