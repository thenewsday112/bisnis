#3643. Run/Walkers Can Do Speedwork Too. Here’s How Adding Intervals to Training Cut 10 Minutes Off My Half Marathon Time

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A run/walker’s guide to making speedwork feel less intimidating and more effective.

While I was training for my first half marathon during the summer of 2025, I’d clock an average 16:00 run/walk pace. It felt humbling. I wanted to go faster, but I wasn’t physically prepared to accomplish a quicker pace.

So I followed the Runner’s World Beginner Run/Walk plan and racked up miles. By focusing on consistency and going longer, I worked up to an average 13:51 pace come race day. But I still wanted to pick it up.

I signed up for another race last spring, following the Runner’s World break 2:30 plan, which introduced me to some speed workouts: half marathon pace sessions scheduled every week.

I struggled to keep up with those runs, sometimes falling off pace during sessions and other times skipping them entirely. The quicker pace felt intimidating and I typically dreaded the workouts. But I couldn’t deny they led to improvement in my next half, as I finished in 2:48:54, a more than 10-minute PR.

Chasing after the same goal and with my eye on the Copenhagen Half Marathon, I jumped at a chance to train with Runna, an app that creates personalized training plans that adjust to your schedule.

After answering a few prompts, like estimated finish time and how many days a week to run, the app initially spit out a training plan with rolling 800s in the first week. Thinking back to my previous speedwork experience, I felt too nervous to commit. I scrapped the plan and retook the survey hoping I’d get one with less speedwork.

After several tries, I finally got a program with speed sessions every other week—a good compromise for my goal of getting faster without dreading my runs.

As a run/walker, tackling speed was challenging because I had to factor in how long I could run before walking, and it didn’t always align with the length of the running interval, which made the workouts feel out of reach. But I figured out a way to harness the power of the run/walk method and got into a biweekly groove of zeroing in on faster paces. It led me to a 2:38:03 finish, cutting another 10 minutes off my previous time.

Here’s how I overcame my fears of regular speedwork and the strategies you can steal to do the same. Plus, how to strategically add speed to your training without getting overwhelmed.

Even for the most experienced runners, speed workouts are intimidating, Danielle Hirt, NASM-certified personal trainer and RRCA-certified run coach, tells me. The easiest way to get over these fears is to forget about pace, and focus on running at three speeds: easy, medium, and hard. “Most people don’t know their level of difficulty because they’re not willing to explore that discomfort,” she says. This approach helps you do that.

Getting comfortable with being uncomfortable—and hitting that hard effort—is the first step to overcoming fears around dedicated speed sessions. Training with Runna helped me better handle that because I could see the full workouts ahead of time and get audio cues about when to change intensities. This made the work more seamless, and when paces felt doable, it gave me confidence to keep going.

If you’re completely new to running or just getting started with speed, it’s smart to start with strides—short accelerations that usually last about 20 to 30 seconds—during the middle or end of an easy run. These short bursts can help you connect effort to form, Hirt says.

She recommends you add at least three strides to easy runs, then work up to about five strides over the course of a few weeks. From there you can build with hill strides (performing the same accelerations but on an incline), before progressing to other workouts.

For runners who have a strong fitness foundation and experience with speed training, but get nervous ahead of structured workouts, Stephanie Kessell, elite marathoner and head coach at Runna, recommends doing the first rep of your session at a pace that’s about 10 seconds slower than your target. You can build up to your target pace in the following reps, she says.

For example, if the goal is to hit 8:50 pace, do the first interval rep at 9:00. If you feel strong, pick up the pace on the next rep. This gives you a more approachable starting point, and helps you understand what you need to do to get through the session.

“I think a lot of people, who are new to intervals, give themselves a hard time because they’re maybe falling off the paces—faster or slower—and they just want to feel that they can get the pace right,” Kessell says, emphasizing that this easing-in approach helps. “The more pace work you do, the more confident you’ll be at executing a certain pace.”

Often, when runners think about speedwork, they think of it as a tool to get faster for a shorter distance, like a 5K or 10K. But it’s valuable for a half or full marathon too—even if you’re a run/walker.

Doing speed during a half marathon training block can improve your coordination, control, and strength. The more speed workouts you do, the better your body gets at coordinating and controlling your stride, Kessell says. (Strength training, mobility, and form drills help with that, too, she emphasizes.)

Running faster will also help you build strength because it places greater demands on your tendons, helping to propel you forward, Kessell says. “Tendons act a bit like springs, storing energy when your foot lands and releasing it as you push off. Running faster increases the forces passing through these structures, providing a stimulus for adaptation,” she explains.

With the right balance between training and recovery, your tendons should get stronger and better at handling these loads, allowing you to continue picking up the pace.

You can add speed to your training in a few different ways, but the key is following a balanced plan—not running super hard every workout, as that will increase your risk of overtraining and injury.

The training plan I followed mixed in intervals (for example, 800-meter repeats and a mix of 1K and 200-meter repeats), tempos, and long runs with miles at race pace or faster every other week. This helped improve my pace range, while also building speed endurance so I could hold faster paces for longer, Kessell says.

The sessions helped improve my form, too, and that’s important because when you run farther, your form starts to break down. Usually, I notice tension in my shoulders as I run farther, but now my posture feels stronger and more relaxed, even when I push pace.

Short speed sessions help runners harness as much strength as possible, so we are more resilient to the breakdown in longer races, Hirt says. It’s the logic behind the phrase: fast before far.

Doing speed workouts made me feel more confident as my training progressed, and I no longer dreaded them. I started to see these harder sessions as another tool to help me get closer to my goal, and that made it easier for me to keep showing up.

Hirt recommends having different run/walk ratios for different purposes. For example, I run for three minutes and walk for one on easy days, but for runs at a faster pace—like 1K repeats and long runs with race pace miles—I run for 90 seconds and walk for 30. (I adjust my approach based on how I feel on that day, taking a longer walk break if needed.)

In addition to switching up your ratios, you can alternate between running slightly faster than your pace target and brisker walks to hit your overall average goal pace.

No matter how you approach speedwork, Kessell recommends keeping your intervals as symmetrical as possible. For example, if you have 800-meter repeats (about half a mile), but you can’t run that far, break it into smaller chunks. Run 400 meters (about a quarter of a mile), walk at a fast pace for 30 seconds, then do the last 400 meters and keep the original rest breaks as prescribed.

Just remember for speed workouts especially, a walk interval is an opportunity to reset form and breathing—it’s not a break, Hirt says. If you’re struggling to hold a faster walk pace, that’s a sign you’re running too fast, which can lead to form mistakes, like overstriding, and potentially increase risk of injury, she adds.

Run/walkers can also train based on the rate of perceived exertion to make speed sessions more doable (rather than focusing on a specific pace) or train to run a full mile before taking a walk break, so you can carry out a workout like 800-meter repeats as is.

The Nazis were far from completing a working reactor for their atomic bomb program, despite claims by the project's leader that it was almost ready, a new study finds.

The German physicist Werner Heisenberg wrote in 1947 that the reactor for the "Nazi bomb" project ‪— which culminated in Germany's B8 nuclear reactor ‪— was almost finished at the end of World War II.

Heisenberg, who led the reactor's development, claimed the reactor was "just insufficient" to create a self-sustaining fission reaction and that it lacked only a "relatively small amount of uranium" to work.

If it had worked, the B8 reactor would have confirmed that a fission chain reaction was possible, marking a key step toward the German development of an atomic bomb. It could have also spurred the development of specialized fission reactors used to create weapons-grade plutonium from uranium. Instead, the atomic bomb was developed first by the United States and was used to force the surrender of Japan in August 1945. (Germany had already surrendered in May 1945.)

The new study, published Sept. 29 in the journal PNAS Nexus, used historical documents and forensic investigations of two of the project's surviving uranium cubes to refute Heisenberg's claim. The new research indicates that the German B8 reactor had less than half the amount of "heavy water" and about half the amount of uranium it would have needed for a continuous chain reaction.

"My results argue that Heisenberg was off by roughly a factor of two," said first author Patrick Park, a doctoral student at Princeton University who co-wrote the study while at Columbia University.

Park said Heisenberg's mistake was understandable: His calculations were only approximate, and the project was relocated to a rock cellar in the town of Haigerloch, Germany, after Berlin was bombed.

"People were dying or starving and so these reactor calculations were not his first priority," while many details of neutron physics were unknown at that time, Park told Live Science in an email. "Given these circumstances, I think he did the best he could."

To answer the question of how far the reactor for the Nazi bomb project got, the scientists began by tracing archival records of hundreds of uranium cubes that had been used by German scientists. The B8 reactor consisted of five concentric circles of uranium cubes suspended on chains within the heavy water tank. The German scientists planned to place a radium-beryllium neutron source into the reactor's core to kick-start the chain reaction with a flood of neutrons that would split the uranium nuclei and release more neutrons, furthering the chain reaction.

Heavy water is a form of water that contains the neutron-heavy hydrogen isotope deuterium. Like the mainly-graphite blocks and rods used in many reactors, it can be used to "moderate" or slow down fast neutrons created in a fission reaction so they trigger fission — and more neutrons — in nearby uranium atoms. The Germans also used small amounts of graphite in their reactor, but as a coating to reflect neutrons back into the chamber.

A cube of natural uranium from the German atomic bomb project.

By combining the records of the cubes, heavy water and graphite used by the German scientists with measurements of the two surviving cubes, the study authors created a mathematical model of the reactor that agreed closely with historical measurements. This enabled them to establish that the heavy water reactor was far from achieving a stable reaction. The findings also helped them examine the suggestion that the Germans should have instead built a graphite-based reactor like the nuclear pile developed at the University of Chicago to build the American bomb.

But the study authors determined that a graphite-moderated reactor was unfeasible in wartime Germany because the local supply of coal available to create graphite contained high levels of neutron-absorbing boron, making it useless.

Instead, the heavy water used in the B8 reactor was produced at a single factory in Norway, which was occupied by Germany from April 1940 until May 1945. However, the reactor contained only about 370 gallons (1,400 liters) of heavy water, instead of the more than 780 gallons (roughly 3,000 liters) it would have needed, the researchers determined.

Heavy water makes up a tiny fraction of a percent of ordinary water, and extracting it by electrolysis in the Norwegian factory took extraordinary effort. The factory was producing as much heavy water as it could, but the B8 reactor would have needed more than twice the amount it produced during the entire war, the researchers wrote.

Another finding is that the B8 reactor had about half the amount of uranium it would have needed to sustain a fission chain reaction. The reactor contained a total of 664 cubes, each weighing about 5.11 pounds (2.32 kilograms), for a total of 3,390 pounds (1,540 kilograms) of uranium. Similar cubes were distributed among other experimental groups, giving a total inventory in wartime Germany of about 1,100 cubes of uranium.

But the researchers determined that the B8 reactor would have needed at least 1,200 cubes to work. Germany, therefore, was not about to develop a reactor for an atomic bomb in 1945. Contrary to Heisenberg's claims, the heavy-water reactor was not nearly ready, and a working graphite reactor was impossible.

"We conclude Germany could not have built heavy water nor graphite reactors during World War II," the researchers wrote.

Mark Walker, a historian at Union College in Schenectady, New York, who wasn't involved in the study, said the findings explain Germany's decision to develop a heavy-water reactor, since a graphite reactor was not feasible and they didn't know at the time how much heavy water would be needed.

Walker is the author of "Hitler's Atomic Bomb: History, Legend, and the Twin Legacies of Auschwitz and Hiroshima" (Cambridge University Press, 2024) and a noted expert on Germany's wartime nuclear efforts. He agreed that Germany could not have made a graphite reactor during World War II.

"This paper argues that if the Germans had done something they did not do, then this would have failed," he told Live Science in an email. "While this is counterfactual history, it is an interesting engineering analysis."

#3912. Comment | Seabed heritage is facing catastrophic destruction on multiple fronts

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new study has noted that only 0.2% of shipwrecks worldwide lie in areas classified as fully protected, where mining would be prohibited

In 2011, the E/V Nautilus expedition discovered an ancient merchant ship at the bottom of the Black Sea, west of Ereğli, Turkey. Sunk more than 2,000 years ago, it might have been in better condition if bottom-trawling fishing boats had not repeatedly scraped their nets along the seafloor, right through the site. In their wake, the ship’s ancient amphorae lay damaged and the hull timbers had been dragged from where they lay. Unfortunately, this is not an isolated incident.

In a recent study, Rory Quinn of Ulster University, Northern Ireland, and colleagues compiled data on known shipwrecks—some 150,000 records—and the movements of fishing vessels to reveal which underwater heritage sites are most at risk globally from deep-sea mining and trawling. Writing in The International Journal of Nautical Archaeology, the team note that few shipwrecks lie in zones that could soon be exploited for deep-sea mineral mining—an industry in its early stages—but also that only 0.2% of wrecks worldwide lie in areas classified as fully protected, where mining would be prohibited.

More alarmingly, 95% of wrecks are found in shallower waters, without protection, where bottom-trawling nets can scour sites, both known and unknown. Although the vast majority of trawling occurs away from wrecks, the danger remains. One way to combat the threat, the researchers write, is to make fishing crews’ existing informal behaviour of avoiding shipwrecks—which can damage their equipment—a legal requirement.

Good luck with that. Underwater cultural heritage has long been a weak spot in heritage legislation. While Unesco’s 2001 Convention on the Protection of the Underwater Cultural Heritage binds parties to not disturb or damage submerged heritage, key states—including China, the UK and the US—have not ratified it. Some have chosen to adopt their own legislation instead, but this leaves the high seas vulnerable. Meanwhile, the 1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea barely mentions archaeology.

It’s enough to make you wish for the release of a culture-conscious kraken. After all, a wealth of understudied heritage lies beneath the waves, from shipwrecks, harbours and sculptures, to trade goods, ceremonial sites and sunken cities. Each is a tale waiting to be told. And it is not just fishing and mining—there is also pollution of various kinds (some underwater sites are so blanketed in garbage they have become difficult to survey and monitor), treasure hunting and uncontrolled tourism.

And that’s before we mention climate change. Writing in the journal Communications Earth & Environment, Luigi Germinario of the University of Padua, Italy, and colleagues have recently researched how increasing ocean acidity impacts ancient stone. They found that with rising carbon dioxide emissions, there could be an “exponential surge” in the coming decades, causing irreplaceable ancient remains to effectively dissolve.

The team subjected a digital recreation of a marble sculpture (found underwater at the sunken Roman city of Baiae) to three different computer-simulated scenarios. Each reflected how different ocean acidity levels would affect the submerged object over the course of 500 years. At pre-industriallevels of acidity, the statue’s surface remained virtually unchanged. At the acidity levels predicted by the end of this century, the statue lost more than 1mm of its surface, its finer details removed. At the most extreme level tested, more than 1cm of surface vanished, leaving the statue heavily eroded. Exposed to these same extreme acidity levels, less durable stones, such as porous limestone, could suffer “catastrophic erosion” over the course of a century—an outcome that under current conditions would take thousands of years.

On top of all this, creating detailed 3D records of heritage sites at sea requires scarce and complex equipment, often available only in prototype. Luckily, this may be about to change. Investigating a partially buried wreck in the Baltic, Dennis Wilken of Kiel University, Germany, and colleagues found a way to make high-resolution 3D scans of its features quickly and cheaply. Their test, described in the journal Archaeological Prospection, covered two hectares in just two hours with adapted equipment that research vessels already usually carry on board. For experts wanting to discover or monitor archaeological sites beneath the seafloor, it could be a game-changer. And with underwater heritage facing an uncertain future, innovations like this will be needed to turn the tide.

We moved a copy of 99.99999% of GitHub’s repositories to Azure. Here’s how per-reference quorums will bring over the rest, and how object storage lets reads scale independently.

Over the past year, while Git traffic grew exponentially, 99.99999% of the Git repositories on GitHub began living in two places at once: our own data centers and Azure. The rest are among the busiest on the platform, and they’re held back by just one thing: a 25ms round trip.

In an earlier blog post, we laid out the two principles behind our next-generation storage architecture:

In this post, we’ll look at the immediate bridge between our old design and our new one. We’ll talk about how a change to the way our quorum serializes writes gets the busiest repositories into Azure, and how that same step opens the door to replicas that keep no persistent state of their own.

The timing and shape of the move were driven by the agentic shift: rapidly growing traffic, a hard ceiling on the power capacity of our data centers and the surrounding geographical region, and physical supply chain constraints. At the beginning of our migration, the remaining capacity with all possible expansion gave us enough time to migrate safely, but not enough to do it slowly.

While we had prototyped new architectural approaches for scaling Git on cloud infrastructure, changing the storage architecture and the hosting platform at the same time, under that growth, carried too much risk. Given our scale and how critical the service is to the community, we needed to sequence the work carefully: move the data first, then evolve the architecture once it arrived.

Git operations are a Tier 0 service: they’re a core function of GitHub, and nearly everything in GitHub depends on the repository data. That meant the data, services, and every dependency had to move first. The first copy of a repository landed in Azure’s Central US region in August 2025. Today, most repositories have two of their five replicas in Azure, and Azure serves about half of Git traffic. For most of that time, Azure absorbed our growth while our own data centers held steady.

During the migration, traffic follows the data. Most repositories have five replicas to balance durability, availability, and scale. To minimize read latency, Spokes uses preferential routing to direct traffic to a replica in the same region and availability zone as the request. As replicas move to Azure, the share of the traffic that can be served from Azure grows with each one, until the last replica arrives and the old region stops receiving requests entirely. So, what about the remaining 0.00001% of repositories?

In our current architecture, a repository hosted in more than one region has a lower write throughput ceiling. Spokes’ quorum only needs a majority to commit a write, but it waits for every healthy replica to respond (up to a timeout) before moving on, so slow replicas delay every write rather than falling behind. In practice, that means the slowest healthy replica sets the latency of every write, and because each write holds a repository-wide lock until it commits, it also sets the throughput ceiling. Once a replica lives in the target Azure region, it’s about 1,000 miles from our data centers, making each network round trip around 25ms, and a write requires multiple round trips. A repository already near its throughput ceiling can’t absorb that on every serialized write; it would fall further behind with every push.

This is where our first principle, minimizing coordination, comes into play. Specifically for this case: don’t serialize what doesn’t need to be. Git’s consistency requirements apply to each reference individually. Once the server accepts an update to a reference, no client should see an older value afterward, only that value or a newer one. That holds whether the update is a fast-forward, a force push, or a delete. But in most cases, updates to different references don’t depend on each other and can be applied independently.

So, we’re changing the scope of the quorum from the whole repository to individual references.

Holding a repository-wide lock for the entire reference update, round trips included, turns the quorum into a single, unordered request queue with one write in flight at a time. Each write in that queue takes as long as the slowest healthy replica. As the write rate grows, so does the queue, and each write waits longer than the one before it.

With per-reference serialization, only writes that contend on a shared reference must wait for each other. In practice, most writes to a repository touch different references—for example, agents and developers pushing to their own branches—so they no longer wait on each other. Each write still experiences the cross-region latency but writes to different references can be in flight at the same time. That removes the single bottleneck on the repository. These changes raise a repository’s write throughput ceiling roughly in proportion to how many references are being written at once, and get the last remaining repositories into Azure. In internal testing with realistic traffic, writes to different references consistently saw 3–4x higher throughput at lower latency.

Most repositories are heavily read-skewed, with somewhere between 100 and 1,000 reads for every write. Today, reads and writes hit the same replicas, so they compete for the same resources and can’t be scaled independently.

Decoupling storage from compute changes that. Once repository data is durably stored in Azure Blob Storage and updated with each incoming write, we can begin to decouple read traffic from the write quorum, and independently scale the compute needed for each. For example, reads would benefit from far more in-memory cache than we give them today, but that memory is also needed for compaction. Once they’re separated, each gets its own configuration.

The first step is making object storage a member of the quorum, but a special one that we call a virtual replica. The compute that represents it is our first fully ephemeral node. It serves as a bridge to object storage, streaming incoming writes and serving no reads. Because it’s a member of the quorum, a write isn’t acknowledged until it’s durable in object storage.

That adds a little latency to each write, but just like the cross-region round trip, per-reference serialization keeps it from becoming a throughput bottleneck. The virtual write replica also coalesces transactions on disjoint references into a single set of network I/Os to object storage, so a busy repository pays for one round trip across many concurrent writes instead of one per write. For now, the virtual write replica also handles compaction, and read replicas inherit the compacted state.

With the virtual write replica in place, we can scale out as many virtual read replicas as a repository needs, on demand. Each read replica warms its cache from Azure Blob Storage and starts taking read traffic without joining the quorum, using object storage as the source of truth and reconciling its local state against it in the background.

This step unlocks significant read scale. In internal testing, our distributed load testing harness routinely found ceilings in the current architecture. Against virtual read replicas, the harness hit its own limits first. It also comes with a few challenges that are solved in subsequent steps:

How can we push write throughput further?

The GitHub of today was built on the Spokes quorum, and over the years Spokes has become much more than a quorum coordinator. It’s also an intelligent routing and load balancing layer, a unified API that internal clients use to access and transact repository data at well over 100 billion operations per day, and a collection of background systems we call “sweepers” that handle repository placement, repair, and rebalancing between storage nodes.

The vast majority of what lives under the Spokes service umbrella applies just as well to the new architecture as it does to the existing one. But we also recognize that the commit protocol that has taken GitHub to a billion repositories won’t take it to the next billion and beyond.

We’re not done yet. With these steps in place, the next limit is write throughput. Every write still waits on replicas that each hold a full copy of the repository, yet very little of a write actually needs to be ordered. Git objects are immutable and named by their contents, so they can be stored without anyone agreeing on them. What’s left is small, and the hard part is ordering it as fast as possible without moving the bottleneck somewhere else. Designing for that on a whiteboard is one thing. Doing it underneath a billion repositories that developers and agents are writing to around the clock is another. Getting there forced us to rethink the second-order effects too, like where durable reference state lives and how compaction and garbage collection scale.

Agent traffic isn’t slowing down, and each of these steps gives repositories more headroom as it lands, without waiting for the end state.

Here’s what talking to 1,500 adolescents about what they want in their entertainment content illuminated for researchers, from AI hesitancy to a desire for more stories centering platonic relationships.

Every year, the Center for Scholars & Storytellers (CSS) at UCLA performs a study to find out what tweens and teens want to see in the TV shows, movies, and media made for and about them. Researchers interviewed more than 1,500 adolescents ages 10-24 “about their perceptions, opinions, and beliefs regarding popular media, including TV shows, movies, video games, AI, and social media,” a press release about the report reads.

The high-level takeaway: While there are more and more ways to consume stories today, teens still want to consume “traditional” media. However, they want more diverse perspectives in their TV show and movie options.

Among Gen Xers and Millennials, a “story” typically means a TV show or a movie. For the surveyed adolescents, YouTube videos, video games, and 30-second videos also count as storytelling platforms.

“YouTube ranked as the top medium for storytelling, with nearly four out of five respondents saying that short-form videos can tell a story. TV shows, movies, and video games followed closely behind. TikTok scored the highest among social media platforms,” the press release reads.

We tend to think of kids and teens as hapless would-be victims of AI (no one would willingly watch brain rot, right?). But the same optimism and fear adults seem to feel about the technology were also present among the study group. Participants’ ages tended to be the best predictor of how they felt about AI.

Forty-four percent of 10- to 13-year-olds described AI-generated programs as “fascinating,” while only about 10% used the word “doom” in their description (ominous). That split was completely reversed in the 19-year-old group, where doom was the No. 1 feeling participants had about AI (27.4% of them).

In contrast to last year’s results, live-action content took the top spot this year as the preferred choice for adolescent and young adult audiences compared to animation (58% to 42%). In a world where AI is beginning to touch everything, we’re all craving things we know are real.

According to the CSS’s report, only 2.8% of speaking or named characters in the top 100 movies of 2025 had a disability. Younger audiences say that’s simply not enough — they “overwhelmingly” want to see more neurodivergent characters in their media, the study found.

“A whopping 55 percent of all adolescents and young adults surveyed feel this way, with that percentage increasing to well into the 70s among youth who identify as LGBTGIA+ or have a disability themselves,” says the report. “Additionally, only 22.7% of respondents believe that current media portrayals of neurodivergence ‘ring true to real life’ most of the time.”
