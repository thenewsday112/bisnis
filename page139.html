#3824. Alison Kuo pays homage to Cantonese opera at the New Museum

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Alison Kuo (right) and Mei Brunette (left) Photo: Tommy Kha, courtesy the New Museum, New York

After Alison Kuo’s grandmother died, the New York-based artist inherited an unusual object—a costume beard her grandmother had worn during her time as a Cantonese opera singer in Hong Kong. “She would always perform the role of a military general,” says Kuo, adding that while gender-bending is common in traditional opera, the character of the general was often reserved for men.

In the years since she first came across the beard (alongside photographs of her grandmother in full stage getup), Kuo has been studying Cantonese opera—from its singing and staging to the costumes and set design. This weekend (22-24 October), her years of research and training culminate in a performance at the New Museum of a unique take on the 400-year-old tradition.

Titled A flame latches to a bit of straw 薪火相傳, Kuo’s take on Cantonese opera is named for a Chinese idiom similar in meaning to “passing the torch”. Channelling her grandmother, Kuo takes on the role of a general.

The other performers on stage include the artist’s cousin Anna Kuo, her next-door neighbour and former go-go dancer Mark Bolling, the teenage performer Mei Brunette and the Queens-based dancer and textile artist Annika Cheng. Also on stage will be local musicians playing traditional Chinese instruments and three Cantonese opera singers who regularly perform in Manhattan’s Chinatown—MeeMee Chin (Kuo’s mentor), Wai Wah Law and Ying Ying Zhu.

The story begins with the Kuo cousins looking through their grandmother’s photos and admiring her beard, before being whisked away to the world of Cantonese opera. The performance is a melding of traditional and contemporary cultures, represented not only in the cast but also in the music, staging and visual components. Kuo created the sets and several of the costumes herself; others she borrowed from local Cantonese opera performers. She even uses a few of her grandmother’s old dresses.

Although Kuo has been working on the performance for more than a year, it is a collaborative effort. Cheng helped design some of the costumes. And during rehearsals, the entire cast was encouraged to improvise and provide feedback, shaping the final result.

Alison Kuo Photo: Da Ping Luo, courtesy the New Museum, New York

Kuo’s work both as an artist and a community organiser has always been rooted in collaboration—with a particular focus on the Asian American experience, whether her own family’s or that of others. “I’ve been reading a lot of memoirs by Asian American women,” she says, “and books about what was happening at the end of the Qing dynasty.” The fall of China’s last imperial dynasty in 1912 marked the beginning of a decades-long wave of emigration and displacement, creating the rich diaspora communities that still exist today.

Although Kuo lives in Brooklyn, she often finds herself in Manhattan’s Chinatown. She remembers attending one of the very first events at the W.O.W. Project, an arts and anti-gentrification organisation founded in 2016, based out of the oldest continuously operating store in Chinatown. “I started getting involved from there,” Kuo says. Over the years, she has made a lot of valuable connections through the W.O.W. Project. She even attended Cantonese opera workshops there. A couple of her teachers join her on stage this weekend.

In 2021—while working full-time running artist residencies at the International Studio & Curatorial Program—Kuo co-founded Sisters in Self-Defense, a Chinatown-based self-defense and support group for Asian Americans. The other co-founder was the writer Ava Chin, whose daughter (Mei Brunette) features in Kuo’s performance.

Given Kuo’s proclivity for all things Chinatown, she was thrilled to become an artist-in-residence at the New Museum just a few blocks away. Since June, she has been working out of a 730-sq.-ft studio space on the museum’s sixth floor. In addition to setting up her practice there, she brought in bookshelves packed with research materials and family heirlooms. She set up a couch facing a giant window and often invites people to the space.

“It’s good to have friends in the neighbourhood,” she says. “We’ve even had Mahjong parties here in the studio.” Kuo, who helps teach art classes at a Chinatown school, even brought her young students in to see it. She says they asked her if she was a famous artist. Previously, Kuo had shared a studio in Brooklyn with her husband, who is also an artist. “I needed to be in Chinatown, I realised,” she says.

At the end of A flame latches to a bit of straw 薪火相傳, “there’s a transformation”, Kuo says of the character she plays (a version of herself). “I’m more confident, celebrating taking part in an art form that has endured for hundreds of years.” The torch has passed from Kuo’s grandmother and Chinatown’s Cantonese opera singers onto Kuo and her cousin, her friends and the teenage Brunette. A centuries-old tradition continues.

#1320. Where Did Harry Houdini Live? Inside His Life at Home

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A century after his death, a look at the escape artist’s homes—from his Wisconsin childhood house to his longtime Harlem brownstone.

For all the attention paid to Harry Houdini’s escapes, the places he lived are less familiar. The Budapest-born magician grew up in Wisconsin and spent his early New York years in rented rooms before international success brought him to a Harlem brownstone. There, he assembled a substantial library, displayed mementos of his career, and shared a home with his wife and performing partner, Wilhelmina Beatrice “Bess.” The house was both a family residence and a working headquarters.

This Halloween, a century will have passed since Houdini’s death at 52. To mark the anniversary, we’re looking beyond the stage to the homes he occupied along the way.

Houdini was born Erik Weisz in Budapest on March 24, 1874. His family lived in Terézváros, a district on the Pest side of the Danube, before immigrating to the United States in 1878, when he was four. According to the House of Houdini museum, the building in which he was born no longer exists.

The city’s principal destination for learning about him is now the House of Houdini at 11 Dísz Square, in the Buda Castle district. Despite its name, this museum was not his residence. Founded by escape artist and collector David Merlini, the museum houses a permanent collection that includes Houdini’s handcuffs and personal letters, alongside props from the Adrien Brody television production Houdini. A small theater hosts live magic performances.

The Weiss family settled in Appleton in 1878, where Houdini’s father was a rabbi. They lived on Appleton Street, in the downtown area now associated with Houdini Plaza, where a bronze bust of Houdini was installed in 2015. Although the family stayed only about four years, Houdini continued to regard Appleton as his hometown throughout his life.

A full account of Houdini’s life is on view at the History Museum at the Castle. Its permanent exhibition, AKA Houdini, follows his childhood, rise to fame, and legacy.

The family moved to Milwaukee in the early 1880s after Houdini’s father lost his job in Appleton. Their circumstances deteriorated, and the young Houdini helped support the household by selling newspapers and shining shoes. They occupied several addresses before he left for New York with his father in 1887.

Milwaukee was also where Houdini began performing. At nine, he appeared on a trapeze in a neighborhood circus under the name “Ehrich, The Prince of the Air.”

When Houdini and his father arrived in New York in 1887, they stayed at a boarding house at 244 East 79th Street. The building survives, and bears commemorative plaques detailing its connection to Houdini.

The family later lived in an apartment at 205 East 68th Street. During this period, Houdini began developing his professional act, performing with Jacob Hyman as the Brothers Houdini in 1891.

In 1904, Houdini purchased 278 West 113th Street for $25,000. The brownstone became his primary residence for the next 22 years, shared with his wife and performing partner, Bess. The house was also his professional headquarters, with space for secretaries, correspondence, research, and the growing collections that occupied much of his life offstage.

His office space extended across several floors. An upstairs room accommodated secretaries and the cataloging of his extensive library—Houdini worked at a desk in the library itself, at the rear of the parlor floor.

Today, the brownstone remains privately owned, but its owners offer guided visits with advance arrangements.

In 1904, Houdini bought a summer property on Webb’s Hill Road in North Stamford. The seven-acre farm gave him a country residence in addition to his Manhattan home, although he owned it only until 1905.

The house Houdini occupied was an 1830 saltbox at 286 Webb’s Hill Road. It was destroyed by a fire in the 1960s, but a replacement house was subsequently built on the site in the same saltbox style.

#3785. Facade of Mexican university building draws on Gothic architecture

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Photography by Recording Architecture (unless stated otherwise)

Mexican studio Jonathan Corral Arquitectos has completed an educational building called the UAD Alamedas Tower, which features a faceted facade that takes cues from stained-glass windows found in Gothic cathedrals.

The building is part of the Universidad Durango Santander, or UAD, a private institution with campuses in several Mexican states.

For its Durango location, the university acquired a site overlooking Las Alamedas, a prominent, tree-lined promenade that stretches through the heart of the city.

Responding to this context was a key concern for the building’s designer, local studio Jonathan Corral Arquitectos.

“The project establishes its relationship with this setting through an intermediate condition – an architecture with a clear urban presence, whose scale and materiality engage with the existing landscape,” said the team.

Triangular in plan, the 7,987-square-metre building contains eight levels and a basement.

A variety of spaces are found in the building, including classrooms, studios, meeting rooms, auditoriums, offices, a cafeteria and a courtroom for mock trials, among other spaces. The school offers courses in law, architecture, medicine, sciences, communication technologies and gastronomy.

On the exterior, the building appears as multiple structures given the use of varying facade treatments.

Along the main elevation, which faces north, one portion is clad in grey fibre-cement panels, while another is lined with a black metal screen.

The central portion features a glazed elevator and a faceted curtain wall with dark mullions and triangular panes of glass – a design that draws upon Gothic architecture.

“The large glazed surface of the facade draws on the compositional logic of the Gothic stained-glass window, translating it into a contemporary urban scale,” the team said.

While the front elevation is meant to be transparent, the rear wall is more opaque. It has grey cladding and short, horizontal windows.

The main entrance is fronted by a broad stone staircase that doubles as a plaza. The building is elevated above grade to help protect from potential flooding, the team said.

Visitors enter a full-height atrium, ringed by walkways with frosted glass railings. The atrium is meant to establish “visual continuity” between the interior spaces and the adjacent promenade.

“From within, the urban landscape is framed by different levels and circulation routes, keeping the context a constant presence throughout the building experience,” the team said.

The lobby features a sunken plaza wrapped in black marble and a suspended staircase, which acts as a sculptural element.

The building’s structural elements were left exposed in many areas.

The building’s basement – which contains digital research and consultation space – has exposed concrete walls and flooring.

On the top level – which holds three auditoriums – a pine-lattice ceiling is meant to be “in dialogue with exposed structures and ducts, as well as steel railings”.

“Rather than concealing the logic of construction, the solution allows structure and program to share a common architectural expression,” the team said.

The building is topped with a rooftop terrace, where people can gather and take in views of the city.

Other university projects in Mexico include a business school by a team of architects in the city of Aguascalientes, which features courtyards and lush gardens, and a Tatiana Bilbao-designed biotechnology building in Mexico City that is composed of irregularly stacked glass boxes.

#3987. Studiogusto Unveils the Michelangelus Typeface

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Michelangelus typeface decodes Michelangelo's handwriting into a humanist serif, commissioned by the Vatican and shipping with Microsoft 365.

The family reads like the Renaissance itself, hand-inked and set down four centuries late. Lowercase letters keep the quill's logic: a wide-countered o that billows, an ink drop pooled in the bowl of the b, and every terminal ending in a gestural sweep instead of a hard stop. The capitals belong to another hand entirely, the chisel's. Studiogusto drew the uprights from Roman letterforms Michelangelo carved into stone, the sash of the PietÃ , the inscriptions of St. Peter's Basilica, the Sistine Chapel. They run narrower than a Garamond without ever reading condensed, which keeps the two halves of the family in quiet tension: the scribe's hand below, the mason's above.

Grid first, always. Studiogusto's boldest decision was its first: do not imitate Michelangelo's handwriting. "Our first decision, probably the most important one, was not to emulate and perfectly imitate Michelangelo's handwriting," said founder and creative director Stefano Caliandro. Instead the studio decoded the hidden order inside it, the invisible grid the architect in Michelangelo laid under every line, and rebuilt the letters as modular elements with clear boundaries. The build ran italic-first, the way the period actually wrote, since no complete alphabet survived and the lowercase g alone took a week, a double-stacked regular set against an italic drawn straight from the master's signature. In the specimens, blue letterforms sit inside pink circles and slanted guidelines: B, D and E carry internal spatial pressure, the T's crossbar spreads like wings, and the vertex of the N cuts through the baseline.

As a tool, Michelangelus wants to be everywhere. It is free to download now and ships with Microsoft 365 at year's end, which puts a Vatican-commissioned serif into Word documents and presentation decks by the millions. The studio's own posters show where the family shines: a magazine cover set in orange and forest green, a fragrance campaign drawn in outlined script, an airline ad stretched across an airplane window. Marble and manuscript sit behind all of it, the PietÃ 's stone and the Vatican's archives, and the typeface holds its own in both registers, equally at home in the everyday and the monumental.

#3555. How to Run Your Fastest Times After 50, According to Those Who Have Done It

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Vying for a lifetime PR after 50? These tips helped runners level up their training.

Every runner knows a PR doesn’t happen overnight; it can take years to accomplish. But with those years of experience comes better endurance and time to learn, test, and fine-tune training habits, allowing you to get faster as you get older.

“All the previous years of running have developed the psychological capacity to endure, not just the race, but the training to get to the race,” says Mike McKnight, founder of RunningWorkx and a run coach who specializes in working with older athletes. “Sometimes runners who started running late in life and established a baseline, but then learn, get more experience, or even get a coach,” have dramatic cutdowns in time, he says.

To help you run your fastest times at any age, we gathered six tips from athletes who scored a PR after 50. Here’s what transformed their training.

At 68, Gwen Jacobson holds an all-time marathon PR of 3:16:04, earned a World Marathon Major six-star and eight-star medal on two separate occasions, and ran marathons in all 50 states and on all seven continents. The reason Jacobson was able to earn such an outstanding list of accolades is because she’s constantly setting goals for herself and reaching them.

For Jacobson, setting goals is a natural habit that gives her something specific to work toward and makes everyday training feel more purposeful. Actually achieving those milestones also boosts her sense of accomplishment and confidence, and encourages her to set another benchmark to reach. (She’s currently chasing a 50-miler and wants to complete a half Ironman before she turns 70.)

McKnight explains that runners over 50 don’t have to limit their goals, but it helps to break bigger, long-term goals down into shorter steps to check off along the way. “Short-term goals basically lead you like a blueprint to your goals. This provides you with the confidence to go after and chase that ultimate long-term goal,” says McKnight.

Setting big goals is important, but taking planned steps toward those goals actually gets you to accomplish them. Mike Uelses, 60, got back into running during the pandemic, running a 3:22:31 for his first marathon in 2022. He continued getting faster every year since by closely examining his race data and setting incremental goals.

More specifically, Uelses examines his performance data from Garmin, Strava, and Runalyze, looking at his weekly mileage, heart rate, training readiness, sleep, stress, VO₂ max, marathon shape, endurance score, and race predictions. This gives him a full picture of what he’s capable of accomplishing. That way, he doesn’t try to progress too quickly or risk getting hurt. In doing so, he ran his most recent PR: a 2:48:16 at the 2026 Boston Marathon.

McKnight tells his runners to keep a workout journal or training log to reflect on how they feel and what their training looks like as they progress. (Tracking via tech as Uelses does works, too!) This helps build communication between him and his athletes, but it’s also helpful for runners without a coach to stay honest with themselves, he says.

Nora Cary, a 70-year-old record-setting American masters runner, doesn’t follow a traditional training plan or work with a run coach to achieve results. “At this point, I don’t think I would be coachable,” she previously told Runner’s World.

Instead, Cary follows a 10-day or even two-week cycle that balances long runs, hillwork, speedwork, and averages anywhere between 35 and 55 miles, depending on her upcoming race distance.

Many coaches recommend a nine- or 10-day training cycle, as opposed to the traditional seven-day cycle, especially for older runners. This allows for more time to recover between hard sessions, McKnight adds.

One major pivot in Jacobson’s running journey was the day she picked up strength training. Her marathon times improved from regularly running in the 3:30 to 3:40 range in the marathon to breaking 3:30. She even credits strength training for eliminating fatigue. It’s also helped her sidestep the injuries that used to plague her training years ago (she’s been injury-free for four years now!).

Jacobson goes to her local gym at least four times a week, working on total-body strength across 10 different stationary weight machines, aiming for five sets of 10 repetitions.

Uelses rarely prioritized strength training when he was younger, but after seeking physical therapy, he treats it like an absolute necessity, prioritizing it twice a week, and credits it with preventing injuries.

Of course, not every runner’s strength routine will look the same, and it doesn’t have to look complicated or extensive. McKnight recommends lifting for runners over 50 to dodge injury and build durability. He specifically recommends a few easy go-to movements for his runners who don’t like spending too much time on strength, including squats, calf raises, planks, side planks, and push-ups.

Few runners think about their cadence (how many steps you take per minute) on a regular basis, but Sue Redding, 58, credits training for optimal cadence as the factor that finally helped her break five hours in the marathon.

Cadence was something Redding dismissed until she received a copy of the book Born to Run, in which author Christopher McDougall explained cadence in a way that clicked for her. The biggest thing that stuck was how McDougall asks runners to try running to the beat of “Rock Lobster” by the B-52s without kicking the wall, she previously told Runner’s World. “It’s really hard to get used to it, your foot touching on each of those beats,” she says. “But once you get it, it’s amazing.”

Redding practiced this technique to the beat of the song during training runs, measuring her cadence with her Garmin watch. Once she tested it out in a half marathon, she felt it was a game changer and a major confidence booster and used it to pace a marathon. Now she’s running faster than ever, most recently finishing the 2025 Marine Corps Marathon in 4:55:54.

One of the things you can do to work on your cadence is improve your posture through form drills, like fast feet, staircase running, or pogo hops, which McKnight recommends implementing into your training plan every week.

If you wait for the right moment to get your workout in, you might end up waiting forever. Take it from Andrea Teague, a 53-year-old runner who recently broke 3:15 in the marathon. Teague told Runner’s World that she sometimes wonders about what her running might look like if her busy schedule allowed for more time to train and recover more, but she doesn’t let that stop her from chasing her goals.

Balancing a demanding job and still running 40-mile weeks, Teague often turns to the treadmill at 10 p.m. to log her miles. Her advice: If you want to get faster, simply start now and figure it out as you go.

McKnight says that no matter what life stage you’re in, your schedule can (and likely will) disrupt your training. The important thing, though, is finding a consistent routine. “Most runners that come to me have been intermittent for a few years because life interrupts things, then they get back into it,” he says.

Whenever you work out—no matter when or for how long—you’re building more experience, improving endurance, and shaking off the rust, he adds. You may run every other day at first, or run for just five minutes a day before hopping back into race training, but every bit counts.

Carrion crows made decisions about their visual environments significantly faster than humans and primates tend to, according to a comparison in a new study.

Crows are intelligent animals that can use tools, hold grudges and even host funerals. Now, new research suggests these birds can make certain decisions faster than humans and monkeys — another potential sign of their smarts, the researchers argue.

In a study published Thursday (Oct. 22) in the journal Science, researchers found that two carrion crows (Corvus corone) could distinguish the direction of moving dots on a screen in an average of 235 milliseconds, or less than a quarter of a second. By contrast, historical studies of humans suggest they make decisions in similar tasks six seconds as fast.

The birds were highly accurate, and recordings of their brain activity suggested that their decision-making process resembled that seen in primates and humans.

Given birds and mammals have evolved along separate paths for more than 310 million years, "this suggests that evolution may have independently developed similar solutions to the same computational problem," study co-author Andreas Nieder, a professor of animal physiology and director of the Institute of Neurobiology at the University of Tübingen in Germany, told Live Science in an email.

To understand how crows make decisions using cues in their environments, the researchers trained two carrion crows to complete a visual task on a touchscreen. The birds watched a cloud of white dots moving against a black background. Some dots moved to the left or right while others moved randomly. The crows had to determine the predominant direction of the dots' movement and peck the corresponding target on the screen to earn a mealworm reward.

By changing the proportion of dots moving together, the researchers could make the decision easier or harder for the crow. When most dots moved in the same direction, the answer was fairly obvious for the birds. But when only a small percentage moved together, the birds had weaker visual evidence to base their decisions on.

One of the carrion crows used in the study completes a decision-making task.

The crows reached nearly 100% accuracy when many dots were involved and the motion was clear. But even when just 3% of the dots moved in concert, both birds performed significantly better than just guessing, getting the answer right 50% of the time.

The birds maintained high accuracy in different rounds of the experiment, suggesting they were not sacrificing judgement for quickness.

The study did not directly compare crows to humans and monkeys performing the exact same task. However, previous studies suggest that humans and monkeys take roughly twice as long on comparable, self-paced visual motion tasks.

To understand how the birds reached their decisions so quickly, the researchers recorded activity from 248 neurons in the nidopallium caudolaterale (NCL) — a region of the bird brain that's considered functionally comparable to the prefrontal cortex in humans and primates. Birds lack the layered cerebral cortex found in mammalian brains, but the NCL performs similarly advanced functions.

In primates, neurons accumulate sensory information and their activity increases until it hits a critical threshold and the brain reaches a decision. The new findings suggest that crows use a comparable process, the researchers concluded.

"The bigger implication is that a brain does not need a mammalian neocortex to perform sophisticated computations," Nieder said.

The team could use neural activity to predict the crows' eventual choice "as early as 70 milliseconds after" the dots appeared, Nieder said. That was roughly 170 milliseconds before the crows' behavioral responses, meaning their pecks at the screen.

The speediness of the crows' decision-making may reflect the demands of the birds' daily lives.

"Flight places enormous demands on visual processing and sensorimotor [sensation and movement] coordination," Nieder said. "Birds must navigate three-dimensional environments, avoid obstacles, and react quickly to moving objects. Finding food and capturing prey can also require rapid, precise decisions. These pressures may have favoured fast information processing."

But for now, scientists don't know why crows seem to outpace primates in this particular type of decision-making. The study included only two birds and no direct comparisons to primates, so more work could be done to confirm the results and explore that question further.

For Nieder, observing the faster decision-making process in crows raises a broader question about how scientists define intelligence.

"Rather than assuming that the mammalian brain is the blueprint for intelligent behaviour," he said, "we can study different species to discover which computational principles are shared across animals and which reflect adaptations to their particular environments."

Every year, trendsetters crown a new color as the “it” shade, and usually there is one hue that pulls ahead of the rest. This year it’s purple, specifically plum. From the fashion week runways to interior design firms, experts say this color is not only taking over homes right now, but will also continue to be a dominating force in 2027.

“At Interior Define, orders for purple and plum furniture grew consecutively every quarter from Q3 2025 through Q3 2026, representing a 34 percent relative increase,” says Havenly’s Chief Commercial Officer Lee Anne Blake. “Plum and jewel-toned decor are surging in tandem with upholstery. Havenly designers sourced 53 percent more plum and jewel-toned decor items year over year.”

Blake also adds that customers shopping from Havenly’s brands are requesting white, cream, and beige pieces far less often. As customer design preferences shift to embracing color and moody interiors, especially with Art Deco trending right now, a deep shade of plum falls neatly into the desired vibe. “Because plum carries warm brown and red undertones, it reads more like an earthy, grounded neutral than an intimidating bright color. It offers the same versatility as navy or forest green while feeling fresh, layered, and inviting,” says Blake.

For design enthusiasts ready to dive in, purple paint, wallpaper, or sizable sectionals are a great way to embrace the shade, while for the noncommitted, a rug or smaller accent piece can be a nice way to add color without detracting from your existing home’s palette. An even easier approach is to start with fresh flowers. “Try styling plum or berry-colored dahlias in a glass vase. It’s such an easy way to see how a bolder color works with your existing palette, lighting, and decor before bringing in anything more permanent,” says Taniya Nayak, Marshalls and T.J. Maxx home expert.

Ahead, we spoke with several interior designers on how to incorporate purple into the home tastefully, if you’re ready to embrace this color with open arms.

While purple is considered by many to be a controversial color choice, plum is much more of a crowd favorite. The color is moody, darker, and leans more reddish-brown.

“I would stay away from grape purples, which can carry a lot of cultural associations with children's characters and products,” says designer Kamber Mason. “Choose a purple with complexity. I tend to gravitate toward plums with brown, red, or gray undertones rather than anything overly bright or blue-purple,” adds designer Isabel Clune. “Then ground it with materials that have character—aged brass, stone, patterned textiles, rich woods, or handmade tile.” A plum with real depth of color adds sophistication to your space.

Your first instinct when using a bold color in your home may be just adding a splash, but every designer we spoke with about plum says to just lean into it.

“Don't treat plum like a guest that needs to be carefully introduced to the room. Give it something equally interesting to play off of! I love pairing rich colors with other unexpected tones, whether that's a muddy olive green, a tobacco brown, or even a soft, dusty pink,” says Lindsay Angyus, principal of KSA Design Group. “The trick is to create a relationship between the colors rather than letting one bold piece sit awkwardly in an otherwise neutral room.”

Low-stakes rooms in which to color-drench or try out plum are your powder rooms, a library, or even a primary bathroom. “These are the spaces where you can be the most fearless with color, and fearless is exactly the energy plum requires. Do not dip a toe in; commit,” adds interior designer Amy Kartheiser. “Paint all four walls and the ceiling too if you are brave enough.” These smaller, intimate areas let you explore an aesthetic without it visually overtaking your home like a purple-forward kitchen or primary bedroom would.

Commitment-phobes who aren’t ready for a room dedicated to purple can still ease into the look by thoughtfully incorporating accent pieces that nod to the shade.

“A beautiful plum velvet pillow, upholstered accent chair, or piece of artwork can instantly add warmth and interest to a neutral space. It's a wonderful way to experiment with color without completely changing your existing design,” says Lynn Berney, cofounder of LTA Interiors.

However, a few designers did warn that plum could look underwhelming in a space or feel disjointed from the rest of the room if the piece doesn’t play well with existing decor.

“Consider which side of the ratio feels like it's taking over, and perhaps that's what's throwing off the balance,” says Erika Johnston, founder of Tiny Lane Interiors. “It doesn't have to be 50/50, but sometimes [plum] needs to have a bigger part in the conversation. You can also introduce a third color into the color story to give it more versatility and make it feel more cohesive.”

Don’t just paint a room purple and call it a day. You have to incorporate other colors, textures, and metals into the same space to make the color feel sophisticated and purposeful. According to designer Cindy McKay, plum pairs well with jade and a warm cognac brown, as the space pictured above demonstrates.

“This great wallpaper [in a recent project of ours] held all of these colors so I could easily pull them out in various spots in the space,” says McKay. “Plum pairs equally well with warm whites and blacks and is a great way to add a little color to an otherwise more neutral space if you aren’t ready to go all in!”

Meanwhile, Johnston says she likes marrying muddy greens with plum to give it a garden-inspired quality. She also thinks this type of purple plays well with smaller doses of red and pink. A third designer we spoke with also mentioned a color in the green family to style with plum: olive green! Take this as your cue to go for a verdant pairing.

“There is something so rich and unexpected about both combinations. Plum has so much depth on its own, and pairing it with these earthy, grounded tones creates a really sophisticated palette,” says Heather Weisz. “Add in some aged brass, warm wood, or even a little unexpected chartreuse, and suddenly you have something incredibly interesting.” If you’re worried this is too much color for you, Berney says warm neutrals such as ivory, taupe, and camel work well too for keeping a space feeling sophisticated and balanced.

If you’ve decided to commit to plum-colored cabinetry or a room swathed in this shade, not only do you need to work in some complementary colors, but you also need to pay attention to the metals you use.

“Warm antique brass and aged gold are plum's most natural companions. The warmth of the metal pulls the red undertones out of the purple, creating something genuinely luxurious. Think unlacquered brass fixtures, aged gold hardware, amber glass,” says designer Amy Kartheiser.

You can even lean into the Art Deco-ness of it all with the metals and jewel tones. “Plum already has this regal quality, so pairing it with touches of gold, silver, or even marble instantly gives a room that collected, Art Deco-inspired feel,” says Nayak. Don’t be afraid to have a little fun with it! The key is letting plum be the star and allowing the pieces you already have to support it rather than compete with it.

Materiality can make color look different thanks to the weave, fiber chemistry, and how light-reflecting properties interact with dyes. For instance, smooth surfaces reflect light evenly in a single direction, so it can make colors appear brighter, deeper, and more saturated. Meanwhile, textured or rough surfaces scatter light in many different directions, so the colors can appear more muted.

“Plum in velvet, mohair, or a beautiful woven fabric feels much more luxurious and intentional,” says Berney. Blake echoes this, saying, “Plum looks best in rich velvets, bouclé, chenille, or linen so the fabric catches the light naturally and brings out the warm undertones.” Kartheiser adds, “A deep plum wall with rough linen drapery and a worn marble counter does not look tacky for a single second. It looks like somewhere you would never want to leave.”

#1433. I Don’t Want Any of These Guys to Play James Bond

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The latest rumors name Callum Turner, Jack Lowden, Josh O’Connor, Jack O’Connell, and Jacob Elordi.

I’m prepared to eat my words for this opinion. I know very well that the critics before me bemoaned Daniel Craig getting the gig only to watch Casino Royale in theaters and swiftly swallow their pride. But when I look at the field of potential James Bond actors today, I can’t say that I’m too excited.

According to The Hollywood Reporter, the race to star in Amazon’s rebooted franchise is down to just two names: Callum Turner (One Night Only) and Jack Lowden (Slow Horses). Both actors are reportedly “in the mix for screen tests, which are taking place in London this weekend.” Other well-known actors rumored to be competing for the role include Josh O’Connor (Disclosure Day), Jack O’Connell (28 Years Later: The Bone Temple), and Jacob Elordi (The Dog Stars), but they are not part of this round of testing, per THR. Maybe that’s for the best.

With this list, it’s clear that Amazon is looking for a big-name star who can bring people to the theater. Daniel Craig turned the franchise from roughly $500-million-grossing films into around $1-billion-grossing ones, and I’m certain that Amazon wants to keep it that way. Plus, whoever takes up the 007 license next will likely need the calendar space to sign up for four to five movies over the next ten years. And they’ll need the acting chops to uphold a significant legacy, stepping into a role that is arguably more scrutinized than any part that requires donning a cape and tights onscreen.

Turner, though he certainly looks the part, has his pros and cons. He has the range and the experience for the role. He’s a heartthrob already, and his marriage to singer Dua Lipa sets up a nice one-two punch for Amazon to potentially cast both the actor and the songwriter in one move.

At the same time, Turner hasn’t been a part of a film that took in more than $100 million at the box office since he was a part of the larger Fantastic Beasts ensemble. He was great in Masters of the Air, the Apple TV limited series about World War II pilots costarring Austin Butler and Barry Keoghan. But critics panned three of his latest films: The Boys in the Boat, Eternity, and One Night Only.

Lowden, meanwhile, has spent the better part of the past four years on Apple TV’s Slow Horses. The British crime drama starring Gary Oldman is an award-season mainstay that was also just renewed for two more seasons. Outside of Horses—and his role as Mr. Darcy on Netflix’s Pride and Prejudice miniseries—there’s little to go on as far as his potential action chops. And while Slow Horses may be an incredibly popular drama series, there’s no telling if his name alone would translate to box-office sales.

As for the other three names, Jack O’Connell is the most exciting actor of the bunch, so much so that I wouldn’t want him to cancel more interesting opportunities just to become shackled to the 007 franchise. His roles in Sinners and 28 Years Later proved that he has a knack for wacky, disturbed characters, and I’d love to see him continue to explore that path. Telling Bugs Bunny to play cool and collected just sounds like the opposite of what we’re going for.

Josh O’Connor was fantastic in Challengers, but I can’t say the same for Disclosure Day. And while Jacob Elordi may be able to sell his star power following leading roles in Wuthering Heights and Frankenstein, The Dog Stars’s failure to launch certainly gives me pause.

Meanwhile, the 007 franchise has let a lot of promising actors slip through its fingers. Idris Elba was heavily campaigned for by fans, as was Bridgerton’s Regé-Jean Page. Aaron Pierre (Lanterns) spent some time in the rumor mill, as did Harry Golding (Crazy Rich Asians) and No Time to Die’s Lashana Lynch. But looking at the latest five to be considered, the chances of James Bond being a person of color or a woman are slim—even if they would have been great steps forward for the franchise.

I’m not the first to say this, but Amazon might be better off with a complete unknown. Instead of trying to replicate the tenures of Daniel Craig or Pierce Brosnan, I’d point the team in the direction of what Spider-Man did for Tom Holland. Find a young, undiscovered talent. You want a guy who is free for the next ten years, who is wholly invested in smashing it out of the park because the future of their career rests on it. They need to live, breathe, and become James Bond. And when the next generation pictures the character, you want them to see that actor’s face in their mind.

Is that person Turner? Or Lowden? Is it Elordi, O’Connell, O’Connor, O’Anyone? If I had it my way, it would be someone we haven’t even met yet.

Did you know that Wisconsin has more than 15,000 lakes? Or that it has over 460,000 acres of state parks and natural areas? What about the fact that the Ice Age ended so recently here that glaciologists consider it the global textbook example of the last major glacial period?

When Backpacker arrived in Wisconsin in September to hike the Ice Age Trail, we had a lot to learn about the state. As we cruised along the eskers, skirted glacial lakes, climbed over moraines, and wound through the autumnal forests, Wisconsin’s magic dawned on us. Even those who had spent a lot of time in the Midwest found new things to appreciate.

The Backpacker team hiked a 25-ish-mile section from Jerry Lake to Mondeaux Flowage. We witnessed wildlife, glacial geography, and forest flora so thick we couldn’t see past the trail’s next bend. We all came away with a new appreciation for the state, and a better understanding of this iconic Midwest long trail.

Beginner backpackers and advanced backpackers will both find something to appreciate on this trail.

Associate Editor Will Brendza here with the Backpacker team, and we are hiking the Ice Age Trail in Wisconsin. A beautiful trail, it winds and wanders through tons of woods. A lot of it’s flat, so it’s a very approachable trail for a lot of different levels of backpackers.

As far as elevation gain goes, you’re not going to find too many steep climbs on the Ice Age Trail. It’s relatively flat, but it still keeps your mind active. There are so many rocks and roots to watch out for, and small animals like frogs and snakes to see. Your brain’s still working while you’re hiking.

For advanced backpackers who want to cover a lot of miles and get distance, this trail offers a lot of nice rolling hills, esker walks, and the trees are so thick you could easily get into a nice flow state while you’re hiking.

Beginner backpackers, the miles are fairly easy. If you’re trying to approach a trail that you can just tick off bits and bits by day, you’ll love this one.

You should expect some road walking on this trail. We’ve run across several pieces, and we just did a small bit of the trail, so don’t think you’re going to be in the woods the whole time.

We’ve been hiking along these long eskers, which are basically these ridges that were carved out by glaciers. A lot of this trail is deep in the woods. You don’t really get the big, stunning views that you’re going to see in places like Colorado or Wyoming. But you pop out on lakes like the one that we’re camping on right now, and they are just stunningly gorgeous. Thick woods, lots of trees, squirrels—we’ve seen snakes, we’ve seen foxes, we’ve seen deer. There’s some really interesting bird life.

We’ve been popping out of the woods and finding some really gorgeous lakes along the way. But throughout the trail, you kind of walk along these boardwalks. Clearly, there’s a lot of water at certain times of year. We haven’t really crossed a ton of creeks or rivers. So when there are opportunities to fill your water or filter, we’ve been taking them for sure.

This national forest is kind of unlike any national forest that I’ve really been in before. It’s a lot of birch trees and oak trees, and this time of year, the colors are just exploding. It’s stunning in a truly Wisconsin kind of way.

The largest news publishing trade association in the United States is calling on Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg to suspend the implementation of the company’s new commercial terms of service, set to go into effect on October 30.

In an open letter to Zuckerberg published Thursday, News/Media Alliance’s president and CEO Danielle Coffey wrote that Meta should “withdraw these terms and should hold discussions around future changes with NMA and publishers.”

The terms, first reported by Nieman Lab on October 15, would allow for the mass scraping of websites “associated with” business accounts on Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, and Threads. There is currently no option to opt out. On October 16, Meta removed the word “train” from its terms and limited the use of future scraped content to “advertising and business products.” But legal experts told Nieman Lab the revised terms would still permit mass scraping.

(i) the specific Meta Company Products and uses for which Meta would like to use publishers’ scraped content;

We are the survivors of immense and minute events — violent cosmic collisions and subtle genetic mutations, the deaths of innumerable suns and the births of innumerable cells, the splitting of continents and the splitting of atoms. Out of it all, we emerged as creatures muzzled by a consciousness that demands we give meaning to our survival. It will not come like alms dropped from the unfeeling hand of the universe. It cannot be found ready-made in the great books and the great teachers, or bought at the price of an Ivy League tuition, or sold by Silicon Valley in a ChatGPT query. That meaning is not something we find but something we make, that it is intimate as love and subjective as the reasons for it, may be the great gift and the great onus of being alive.

Ursula K. Le Guin (October 21, 1929–January 22, 2018) leavens the onus and magnifies the gift in a wonderful passage from her 1975 essay “Science Fiction and Mrs. Brown,” later included in her altogether magnificent collection The Language of the Night: Essays on Writing, Science Fiction, and Fantasy (public library).

With an eye to the human subject as the beating heart of the reckoning with life we call art, she writes:

What good are all the objects in the universe, if there is no subject? It isn’t that mankind is all that important. I don’t think that Man is the measure of all things, or even of very many things. I don’t think Man is the end or culmination of anything, and certainly not the center of anything. What we are, who we are, and where we are going, I do not know, nor do I believe anybody who says he knows, except, possibly, Beethoven, in the last movement of the last symphony. All I know is that we are here, and that we are aware of the fact, and that it behooves us to be aware — to pay heed. For we are not objects. That is essential… And with us, nature, the great Object, its tirelessly burning suns, its turning galaxies and planets, its rocks, seas, fish and ferns and fir trees and little furry animals, all have become, also, subjects. As we are part of them, so they are part of us. Bone of our bone, flesh of our flesh. We are their consciousness.

In this sense, the meaning of life is less like a postulate than like a poem. Half a lifetime later, Le Guin would make a beautiful distinction between how science explicates the universe and poetry implicates it. Knowing that we are implicated in the universe and implicate it in ourselves, touching that knowledge, holding it at the center of our lives, may be the full stop beyond the question of meaning.

“One can’t write directly about the soul,” Virginia Woolf wrote. “Looked at, it vanishes.” This is true of any soul — our own, that of another, that of the world. It vanishes because whenever we look, we see not as reality is but as we are. We see the rest of nature — including each other — through eyes gauzed with preconception, our distracted vision blurred by the thousand thoughts that come alive before the mind’s eye at any given moment, more vivid than the living reality before us.

A lovely recipe for how to see the world more clearly comes from Nathaniel Hawthorne (July 4, 1804–May 19, 1864) — perhaps a lesser novelist than Woolf, but a greater one than Melville in Melville’s own estimation, lovesick as he was, and a far greater observer of nature in his novels than all the journaling Transcendentalists combined. When Thoreau looked at nature, he saw only metaphor and parable; when Hawthorne looked, he saw nature on its own terms, letting it mirror back the fractal of itself that is human nature as the light of unfiltered awareness fell on it. In that respect, he was more a Buddhist than a Transcendentalist, more a scientist than a novelist, and always a poet of reality.

In the high summer of 1851 — a year after The Scarlet Letter interrupted Hawthorne’s long obscurity to catapult the middle-aged author into celebrity — he took his five-year-old son Julian to the lake near the little red shanty they had rented in the Berkshires. Sitting at the water’s edge, Hawthorne wrote in a journal entry later included in Julian’s tender two-volume biography of his parents, Nathaniel Hawthorne and His Wife (public library | public domain):

The best way to get a vivid impression and feeling of a landscape, is to sit down before it and read, or become otherwise absorbed in thought; for then, when your eyes happen to be attracted to the landscape, you seem to catch Nature unawares, and see her before she has time to change her aspect. Like the gardens themselves, the designs are meant to live alongside one another. Here are five of our favorites, along with the patterns to pair them with, that capture Highgrove’s varied landscapes.
