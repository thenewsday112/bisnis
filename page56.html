#1201. I Own 65 Houseplants—These Are the Ones I Think Are Way Too High-Maintenance

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Ever since I was a kid, I’ve been fascinated by houseplants. One of my very first was a zebra plant that lasted all of about a week (probably drowned by my overzealous watering!). Eventually, I got better at nurturing my plants. My gram, who encouraged my efforts, often gave me cuttings to start my own new baby plants.

These days, digging outside in the garden is my passion, but my collection of houseplants also brings me joy, especially in the colder months. Indoor gardening gives me hope that it will eventually be spring again, and nothing makes a room feel more cozy than greenery.

My houseplant collection ranges from colorful new pothos and philodendron varieties to a huge bird of paradise that takes up a whole corner of the room to old-school standards such as snake plants. In fact, I don’t think there’s a “bad” houseplant. But after decades of plant parenting, I’ve also learned that some plants simply aren’t worth my time.

The truth is that certain plants require more babying than others, and that’s not for me. I’m done with all those finicky houseplants that are prone to spider mites or drop their leaves at the slightest change in temperature. If a plant doesn’t bring me joy, it’ll end up in the compost bin before too long.

Some of the plants on my list might thrive in your home—so kudos to you! And it’s always fun to experiment with a new variety that catches your eye. But if it doesn’t go well, please don’t blame yourself. I’ve learned it’s fine to give yourself permission to toss a plant that struggles or doesn’t make you happy. After all, life’s too short for fussy houseplants. Ahead, here are the plants that are just too high-maintenance for me now.

Oh, orange tree. I grew one like you years ago, until I moved and had to gift it. I wanted to love you this time around, but you broke my heart again and again. First, you needed an LED grow light to flourish indoors, and I even set up a humidifier near you to counteract the dry indoor air and prevent the inevitable spider mite invasion, come winter. But after too many winters of battling bugs, I had to sacrifice you because you only brought me angst.

What I grow instead: Olive tree. Its silvery leaves and elegant form look good all year and has never once developed pest issues.

I know I should love you, peace lily, with your shiny leaves and pretty white spathes. But you’re a pain in the behind because you prefer to remain consistently moist, and you wilt if you’re not getting what you want. Yes, you’re sturdy, easy-to-grow and always bounce back, but you don’t bloom as well as I’d like in my house. Because my space is limited, you had to go because you’re just so meh.

What I grow instead: Anthurium, which has shiny heart-shaped leaves and pretty red or white flowers that last for months.

For years, I would try to overwinter my Boston ferns indoors because they were so lush and glorious by summer’s end. But once indoors, all they did was drop fronds. And drop some more. And then some more. That’s because, like most ferns, they need very bright light and high humidity levels (50 percent or higher), which isn’t realistic indoors in winter. After the billionth time of sweeping up leaves, I gave up.

What I grow instead: Bird’s nest fern has glossy, thick leaves that are more lance-shaped than fern-like, and it’s not as fussy about light, humidity, and watering.

There are so many stunning, colorful varieties of alocasia with splashes of color and various textures, and I’ve grown many of them. But the truth is they never live up to my expectations and eventually start to look sad and scraggly. Because alocasia has very specific needs—lots of very bright light, consistent moisture, high humidity (50 percent or higher)—I never found them easy to care for.

What I grow instead: Colorful philodendrons, such as Moonlight, are not as flashy but they are much more forgiving of lower light and humidity levels and inconsistent watering.

You may have been the darling of social media for the last decade or two, but I don’t love you. Fiddle leaf fig is not an easy plant to grow. It loves direct sunlight, which means it needs a plant light; it is Goldilocks-like in its need for just the right amount of water; and it is happiest in high humidity (50 percent or higher). This makes it too finicky for me.

What I grow instead: Bird of paradise is just as dramatic as a floor plant, plus it’s much more forgiving of lighting conditions and dry indoor air. Mine is about nine feet tall.

Purported to be a symbol of good luck and prosperity (and who doesn’t need more of that?), I wanted to adore you. But you felt more weedy than houseplant-y to me, especially after a growth spurt. I trimmed you back a few more times, and you just kept sprouting awkwardly. Honestly, you did nothing for me. I just wasn’t wowed by your braided trunk and weird-looking leaves.

What I grow instead: I love my snake plant. It’s tough, tolerates low light, and has interesting rounded, spiky foliage instead of the usual flat sword-like foliage.

With cute fuzzy leaves and frilly or scalloped flowers of white, pink, purple, or bicolor, African violets have been popular for generations. However, they need constant moisture and lots of indirect bright light. Given the right conditions, they’ll bloom almost continuously. But no matter what I did, they never performed as I hoped, blooming sparsely and getting leggy. Without flowers, they’re boring plants, so I gave up on them (though it is hard to resist their dainty look every time I see them at the store!).

What I grow instead: Holiday cacti bloom reliably and require much less fussing. I have grow lights for them, and as long as I fertilize regularly, they bloom pretty much year-round, especially at the holidays.

The gorgeous, autumnal-colored foliage of these plants is appealing, but they’re not happiest indoors. The biggest issue is that they like lots of bright light, which is hard to replicate in even the sunniest window indoors. They also prefer higher humidity levels (45 percent or more), so it can be iffy to keep them contented indoors.

What I grow instead: Aglaonema is just as colorful and tolerates all light levels, even low light. It tolerates drying out, too, so you don’t have to hover.

It’s not that I don’t think these succulents are adorable: they resemble tiny trees, and how cute is that? But they grow ever so slowly—it takes years to see new growth—and are often victims of overwatering. While they’re actually easy to care for (if you don’t water too often), I don’t love their slow growth rate, which feels uninspiring.

What I grow instead: I like haworthia as a succulent that’s much more colorful and interesting. It still grows slowly but boasts textures and colors that jade plant doesn’t offer.

You’ll typically only find this plant for sale around the holidays. The fern-like foliage is dusted lightly with silvery-white, which gives it the common name—though it’s actually a type of moss. As such, it requires constant moisture and consistent bright, indirect light with humidity levels of 70 percent or more (not happening indoors!).

What I grow instead: Here’s a guilty admission—even though this plant never lasts more than a month or two for me, I still buy it every year. It’s so different from other holiday plants, such as poinsettia and amaryllis, that I just can’t give up. I’ve learned to treat it as a short-lived indulgence—much as you would treat cut flowers.

An experimental medical implant that removes excess sugar from the blood has shown promise in treating diabetes and obesity in tests with lab mice.

Coupled with a continuous glucose monitor, which tracks blood sugar levels in real time, the tiny device is designed to adjust blood sugar levels according to a patient's needs. It does so using a chemical reaction powered by electricity. In the mouse experiments, it prevented sudden insulin spikes and promoted fat loss.

The implant, described Oct. 22 in the journal Science, could offer a completely new way to treat diabetes and obesity, study co-author Martin Fussenegger, a professor of biotechnology and bioengineering at ETH Zurich, told Live Science in an email. His team is now working toward testing the device in additional lab animals, and eventually, humans.

About 790 million people have type 2 diabetes globally, and over 1 billion have obesity. People with obesity often have insulin resistance, in which cells don't respond to insulin well and can't take in the sugar they need from the blood. At first, the body makes more insulin to compensate, but eventually, it can't keep up; this sets the stage for prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. About 90% of people with type 2 diabetes also have obesity or are overweight.

At the same time, chemical reactions in the liver convert the extra glucose in the blood into fat stores, contributing to further weight gain.

Current treatments for these conditions focus on either lifestyle changes, such as diet and exercise, or long-term medications such as metformin, which lowers blood sugar, and GLP-1s, which reduce appetite to promote weight loss. But Fussenegger and colleagues wondered if they could target the glucose directly.

They designed an inch-long reactor that converts glucose into a harmless byproduct, called gluconate, through a controlled chemical reaction triggered by tiny amounts of electricity. This transformation takes place at an electrode coated with gold and platinum nanoparticles embedded within a graphite felt.

Crucially, the reaction occurs only when the device receives sufficient electricity, so the glucose removal can be switched on and off by controlling how much power is supplied to the implant.

The device is wireless and is both powered and controlled from an ordinary smartphone. The phone emits low-level radio frequency energy, which the implant's electronics then convert to a stable DC current to activate the reaction. Combined with a glucose monitor, the device detects high blood sugar and then sends feedback to the smartphone, which then adjusts the amount of power transferred to the implant.

"In principle, this enables the device to remove more glucose when [blood sugar] levels are elevated and reduce its activity when glucose returns towards the desired range," Fussenegger said. "The objective is to create an autonomous system that continuously adapts to the patient's metabolic needs."

In the mouse study, the implant was combined with a continuous glucose monitor, which provided updates on blood sugar levels to a phone that controlled the device.

The team tested this self-regulating medical device in mice with both diabetes and obesity. Over six weeks, the mice fitted with the implant had much more stable blood sugar levels than the untreated mice did. They also lost an average of 10% of their body weight.

Because the mice's glucose was consistently maintained within normal levels, the body's metabolic chemistry shifted toward fat burning, while the on-off regulation of the glucose-removal reaction prevented the mice's sugar levels from falling below safe limits.

It's an exciting proof of concept, Fussenegger thinks. However, "one important challenge is demonstrating that the device can convert sufficient quantities of glucose in a human while maintaining long-term safety and catalytic efficiency," he said. In other words, they still need to show that the chemical reaction works at the larger scale required to treat a human patient.

In a separate test, the team introduced the device to a large beaker filled with a sugary solution, to see if it could cope with larger volumes of glucose. The amount, about 1.3 gallons (5 liters), was similar to a person's total blood volume. The device still effectively controlled sugar levels in this larger system.

The test is not a substitute for research in larger animals and human patients, though, Fussenegger said. Those studies are the team's next priority.

Importantly, "our device is not intended to replace healthy nutrition, physical activity, or preventative medicine" for the treatment of diabetes and obesity, Fussenegger said.

"Obesity and type 2 diabetes are complex conditions involving much more than elevated glucose levels," he added. "We see it as a potentially powerful addition to the therapeutic toolbox, one that could eventually be combined with established treatments and lifestyle interventions."

#4209. I Broke a Browser Agent on Purpose. Spotting the Fault Was Not the Same as Fixing It

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I replayed correct model decisions through four broken browser actions. A simple page check missed half of the wrong clicks, and raised a false alarm on every click that worked inside an embedded page.

WebFovea, a paper by Jiangang Han, reports a disquieting browser agent failure. A mismatch between screenshot and browser coordinates placed clicks at three quarters of their intended position. A click meant for a point 400 pixels across landed 300 pixels across.

The mismatch came from the service that runs the model, which shrank each screenshot before the model looked at it. The paper estimates that a 1920 by 1080 screenshot reached the model as about 1440 by 810, which is three quarters of the size. Anthropic's vision documentation describes the same behavior for its own models. It says large images are scaled down to cap their cost, and that the model answers in positions on the resized picture. The paper's software used those positions as if they were measured on the full size screen, so every click landed at three quarters of the intended spot.

The agent in the paper is a browser agent, a program that lets an artificial intelligence model inspect a web page and choose actions such as clicking a button. Coordinates, the numbers in the click example above, are positions on the screen, given in pixels.

Besides the click error, WebFovea reports two more failures in the software between the model and the page. Hidden end of message markers from the model were typed into search boxes. A click inside a page embedded in another page worked, but was reported as having no effect.

The paper fixed failures like these across four submissions to a 2026 contest on live websites. The agent's score rose from 31.0 to 57.0 out of 100 with the same model. Each submission bundled several changes, and the live websites could vary, so the score alone does not show which fix mattered most. That is the gap I tried to look at on a small scale.

To look at that gap, I tested what happens after the model makes a correct choice. I built a small browser site with four short tasks, such as searching for a record and clicking a button. I asked Claude Haiku 5.5, a small, low cost model from Anthropic, to complete them.

I saved 80 of its decisions that worked, then replayed each through browser code that I had deliberately broken. A check is a small piece of code that looks at the page after an action and decides whether the action worked. I measured whether checks spotted the fault and whether the agent, meaning the model plus the code that drives the browser, still finished the task.

Spotting a fault and getting past it turned out to be different problems. The faults matter to anyone who asks an AI tool to click through a website, including web developers and people who build sites by describing them to an AI tool. The WebFovea paper and its code motivated my setup. The results below come from my own browser pages and saved step by step logs.

The code, step by step logs, figures, and recording are public. The first test, where I ran the checks on 40 versions of each page, and the final agent runs support the counts below.

One of the 80 saved decisions shows the problem clearly. The test page shows a grid of numbered buttons, Item 0 to Item 24. The agent saw a 900 pixel wide screenshot of it and was asked to click Item 15. It answered with the image point (150, 413), meaning 150 pixels across and 413 down from the top left corner. The browser's visible area, called the viewport, was 1200 pixels wide.

I made the screenshot smaller on purpose, to copy the problem in the paper. That means the executor, the code that sends an action to the browser, must scale each image coordinate up to browser size. Here the browser is 1200 pixels wide and the image is 900, so the scale is four thirds. The working executor multiplied 150 by four thirds to get 200, and 413 by four thirds to get 550.67. It clicked (200, 550.67) in browser pixels and selected Item 15.

The broken executor skipped that scaling and sent (150, 413) directly as browser pixels. That point fell on Item 10, one row above Item 15, so Item 10 was selected. I saved the model's answer and ran it twice, once through each executor. The screenshot, instruction, and saved answer were identical both times.

To choose what to break, I used the paper's way of describing a browser action. The WebFovea paper treats each one as a round trip. The action travels from the model to the page, and the result travels back. The paper splits that trip into four steps.

A parser turns the model's reply into an action. An executor sends the action to the page. Feedback tells the model what happened. Observation is what the model is shown afterwards, usually a new screenshot.

Three terms in the table need a quick explanation. An embedded frame, or iframe, is a page inside another page. A tooltip is the small label that appears beside a chart mark when the pointer rests on it. The text [im_end] in the table below stands in for an end of message marker that some AI models use. I added one deliberate fault at each step.

Append [im_end] to the text entered in the field

Treat image coordinates as browser coordinates

Say “No detectable change” after the click succeeds

Capture the image before the tooltip appears

In the Feedback row, my embedded frame comes from the same site as its parent page, which is easier because the page can read its own frame directly. The paper's frame example involved a harder case across sites.

To see whether the checks caught these faults, I generated 40 layouts for each task, with different target positions or values. A layout is one arrangement of the same page. Every check was applied to 40 faulty actions and 40 working actions. The working actions matter because a check that always warns would catch every fault and also interrupt every correct action.

To know whether a check was right, the page kept its own record. It logged the text entered, the button clicked, the frame row selected, and the tooltip visible when the screenshot was taken. That record was the answer key for counting detected faults. The checks could not read it while deciding whether to warn.

I compared four policies, meaning four ways of checking an action. Unverified accepted the message the browser tool sent back to the model, such as “No detectable change”, as true. Generic looked for changes in the web address or the main page's state. Pixel compared screenshots. Outcome check compared the page with the specific outcome requested, such as the exact field text, the selected button, the row inside the frame, or whether the chart value appeared.

The simple checks failed in different ways. Generic missed 20 of 40 wrong clicks. In those, the click hit a different button, which turned green, so the page looked as if it had responded. The other 20 clicks hit empty space, and Generic caught those. It also warned on all 40 correct frame clicks because the main page did not change when the embedded page did.

Pixel had a different weakness. It found only three corrupted searches and warned on all 40 working searches, because the page looks different after every search. A change in pixels does not prove that an exact query survived parsing.

By contrast, Outcome check found all 40 faults in each task and made no false alarms on the matching working actions. I wrote both the checks and the page code used as the answer key. The checks were built for these faults, so perfect detection here proves little about a different website. A mistake in my thinking could therefore sit in both.

Spotting a fault was only half of the question. I used the first 20 of the 40 layouts for a second experiment, and fixed that choice before any model call. Haiku first made one decision on a working page for each of the 80 combinations of task and layout, four tasks times 20 layouts. Every one of those decisions worked, and I kept all 80.

I replayed each recorded decision with the fault enabled, then let the model see the result, as each policy would report it, and respond. An episode is one attempt to complete a task. The model could stop, retry, or wait, meaning pause so the page could finish changing. It had up to three browser actions.

This comparison used Unverified, Generic, and Outcome check. Pixel was evaluated for fault detection but not for task completion, so this experiment cannot say whether it would have helped the agent finish. Each policy ran three times on the same 20 layouts for each of four tasks, yielding 4 × 20 × 3 × 3 = 720 final episodes, from four tasks, 20 layouts, three policies, and three runs. I scored an episode as successful when the requested action reached its target, or when the chart answer came from a screenshot that actually showed the value.

One choice shapes the results below. I left the search parser and click executor broken on every retry, because fixing code is not something the model can do. This shows what better information alone can give it. A check could describe their errors, but another action through the same code would make the same mistake.

That persistence matters when reading the zeros. The zeros were expected, and they show what a diagnosis does and does not give the model. On one search, Outcome check read Record-10[im_end] back from the field and told the model it differed from Record-10. A retry still went through the same parser.

The click episodes show something less obvious. In all 60 Outcome check episodes, the check reported the item hit and supplied the target's correct browser position. The instructions still told the model to answer in image coordinates, and it repeated its original point all three times. The unchanged executor therefore sent three wrong clicks in every episode.

On the frame task the result was the opposite. The browser tool said “No detectable change,” yet the page selected the correct row. All three policies completed all 60 episodes.

Generic told the model nothing had changed, because it watched only the main page, and in 4 of 60 episodes the model clicked a second time for no reason. Outcome check read the frame and needed no extra click. The browser tool's message and the page disagreed, but the screenshot showed the row selected, so the model could finish.

The chart task, where the screenshot came before the tooltip appeared, was the only one with a completion difference. Unverified succeeded 56 times out of 60. In four episodes it stopped before a screenshot showed the value. Either check led the model to hover again or wait, then take a new screenshot, and it succeeded in all 60.

The gain is four of 60 episodes, or 6.7 percentage points. To see how much the gain could vary, I redrew the 20 layouts many times, keeping each layout's three runs together. The middle 95 percent of the redrawn gains ran from 0 to 15 points. Zero gain is still possible, so this small result does not establish a reliable advantage. Generic matched Outcome check on completion, so the simpler check was enough on this task.

These changes and limits matter when comparing the final counts with earlier runs or applying them to other browser agents.

A development run showed that some model replies were cut off before the action text was complete. I switched to a fixed reply format before the final results. I also stopped the first attempt, which ran one episode after another, after 13 episodes because completing all 720 that way would take too long. Those 13 search episodes had zero successes and are archived, then excluded from the final table.

I reran the full set in four separate groups of 180 episodes. The prompts, faults, checks, scoring rules, and 20 selected layouts stayed the same across that schedule change.

Beyond that schedule change, the study has limits. The 20 layouts in the full run were a subset of the 40 layouts in the first test. The study used one simple site, one model and prompt, deliberately injected faults, and a frame from the same site. Each final episode began with a decision already known to work on a clean page. These counts measure recovery on this setup, not the frequency of such failures on public websites or accuracy on harder browsing tasks.

The experiment was cheap to run. The final loop used about 24 US cents in logged model calls. All logged model work for this article, including development and the stopped first attempt, totaled about 27 US cents.

The test was small, but the WebFovea paper reports all four kinds of fault on live websites. That makes them worth knowing about if you ask an AI tool to click through a site. This applies to web developers who test pages this way, and to people who build sites by describing them to an AI tool, sometimes called vibe coding. These are suggestions from one small test, so check them against your own site and tools. One idea goes beyond what I measured. If you build a site, showing an important value in the page text, and not only on hover, may make it easier for a screenshot based tool to read. I did not test that, so treat it as a guess.

A last suggestion is for anyone who builds or checks a site with an AI tool. If the tool says it clicked a button but the page looks the same, ask it to read the page back and say what changed. That is what the Outcome check did in this experiment.

These faults all happened between the model's answer and the page. So I would save the model's request, the image and browser dimensions, and the action actually sent to the browser. I would also record the element or frame that received it and the observation shown to the model.

The click conversion above makes one step explicit, the move from image position to browser position. An exact search also needs the text read back from the search box. A frame click needs the state of that frame, and a chart answer needs a screenshot that contains its value.

Checks can reveal a mismatch and can help when another observation supplies missing evidence. They cannot correct a persistent parser or executor fault unless the agent or its surrounding code changes how the next action is sent. When an agent appears to reason badly, inspect the action's path through the browser before changing the prompt.

After a failed click, would your logs show the coordinates the model asked for, the point the browser actually received, or only the final screenshot?

#635. AI chatbots are giving medical advice, but no one is tracking the harm

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers argue that AI health advice can harm people and leave no record for doctors or regulators, and they lay out what should change.

After reading about the harms of table salt, a 60-year-old man asked ChatGPT how to cut chloride out of his diet.

He subsequently replaced his salt with sodium bromide and ended up in the hospital with paranoia and hallucinations.

Only after days of treatment did his doctors learn that a chatbot was involved, and they never saw the conversation.

When medical treatment goes wrong, doctors and hospitals have systems for documenting and investigating what happened.

When a chatbot gives harmful medical advice, however, there may be no record available to doctors, regulators or independent researchers.

Without that information, dangerous patterns can go undetected.

Matthew DeVerna of Stanford University led the work, with Harry Yaojun Yan of Texas A&M University, Kai-Cheng Yang, an assistant professor at Binghamton University, and Filippo Menczer of Indiana University.

Their paper is a perspective piece, an argument from published evidence rather than a new experiment.

“We’re getting medical advice from these chatbots, but nobody – literally no one – is looking at this. If there’s an error or an issue, there’s just no way for people to know,” Yang said.

By March 2026, about one in three U.S. adults had used AI for health information or advice, according to a KFF poll.

Among them, 65% said a major reason was wanting an answer right away. Another 36% said they felt more comfortable asking health questions privately.

The authors don’t dismiss those reasons. A chatbot can turn a page written for physicians into plain language, and it answers at any hour.

For someone who speaks little English or has no doctor nearby, it may be the only affordable medical advice available.

The researchers are concerned about the lack of consistent oversight.

AI tools used in hospitals may face medical regulations and institutional safeguards, while general-purpose consumer chatbots often operate without comparable protections.

“At present, a question about chest pain is handled like a question about restaurant hours,” the authors wrote.

Other kinds of medical harm leave records. A drug that hurts people generates an adverse-event report, and those reports accumulate until a pattern emerges.

A chatbot conversation is different in two ways, the authors wrote.

The exchange is visible only to the company that runs the model, and AI companies give outside researchers no way to study those exchanges.

And a fluent, confident answer gives the user no sign that anything went wrong. Someone wrongly told their symptoms are harmless may never connect a late diagnosis to the chat.

The bromide poisoning case illustrates this problem. Because the doctors never saw the original chatbot conversation, they could not determine exactly what advice the man had received.

The authors listed the failures. Chatbots produce fluent statements that are factually false, a failure researchers call hallucination.

And people can plant false claims on websites that a model later absorbs during training, a practice known as data poisoning.

Quality also varies from one chatbot to another, and even with how a question is phrased.

Whether a person gets sound medical advice depends partly on which system they happened to open.

Replies in languages other than English are substantially less accurate and less consistent, and the people who rely on them most have the fewest alternatives.

Grok, the AI built into X, answers users’ questions about posts in public feeds. Its replies about vaccine safety are visible to everyone scrolling past, whether or not they asked.

Some accounts now consist largely of AI-generated text, images or video, and their authoritative-sounding health posts reach millions of people.

“Sometimes I’m not even looking for health information, but just by browsing my social media feeds, it’s there,” Yang said.

“It just shows up, and we believe that could have undesirable outcomes, especially if there’s medical misinformation or state actors trying to manipulate the online discussion.”

Coordinated groups of AI agents can make one health claim look like many independent voices agreeing on it.

The authors pointed to anti-vaccine messaging, which measurably reduced vaccination during the COVID-19 pandemic, as a campaign that can now run at scale on social media.

Researchers can’t measure the exposure, because platforms withdrew the data access that once let them study how content spreads.

First, the authors wrote, people should have a right to see their own health conversations with an AI system.

That way, a harmful exchange could be reconstructed and shown to a clinician. Those exchanges should then be reported and investigated the way adverse drug events are.

Every major AI company, they added, already has the technical means to build such a system. A company reporting on itself decides what counts as harm, though.

So the authors also call for independent researchers to have ongoing access to deployed systems, in every language those systems serve.

Platforms, they wrote, should label AI-generated health content and hold it back until experts have vetted it.

And chatbots should treat health questions as a higher-stakes category, answered only from sources vetted by medical societies.

The final recommendation concerns legal accountability. The authors argue that malpractice-style liability should apply to chatbots offering medical guidance.

They also call for changes to Section 230, a U.S. law that generally protects online platforms from liability for certain third-party content.

“We do not think this is something we should rely on the companies to do, because their incentive is always to make more money,” Yang said.

“Building such a system goes against that incentive. So we have to have some kind of third-party monitoring system, an independent evaluation.”

Today no one can say how often AI health advice is wrong, or whether its failures fall hardest on particular groups of people. Only the companies hold the data that would answer those questions.

Changing the law could take years, but the researchers argue that AI companies and social media platforms could introduce many of these safeguards immediately.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Health.

#574. Unexpected Discovery of Abnormal Antibodies Could Help Explain Childhood Cancer

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Laboratory experiments show that antibodies associated with childhood cancer can enter cells and bind DNA repair proteins, suggesting a possible route by which the immune system could weaken genetic safeguards.

Years before some children were diagnosed with cancer, their blood already contained antibodies directed against proteins that normally repair damaged DNA. Researchers in Norway have found that these antibodies were much more common among children who later developed cancer than among those who did not, raising the possibility that the immune system itself may interfere with a cell’s ability to protect its genetic material.

Childhood cancers, including leukemia, myeloma, neuroblastoma, and osteosarcoma, differ from adult cancers in their causes, locations, and responses to treatment. Children have had far less time to accumulate DNA damage from environmental exposures, lifestyle factors, and aging. Their tumors therefore tend to contain fewer mutations and often arise from immature cells, such as stem cells or progenitor cells that can develop into more specialized cell types.

Cells rely on hundreds of proteins to identify and repair damage or errors in their DNA. The Norwegian researchers suspect that this protective machinery may sometimes be disrupted by auto-antibodies, antibodies that mistakenly target the body’s own proteins rather than foreign threats. If these antibodies interfere with DNA repair, genetic damage could accumulate even without inherited defects in the genes responsible for maintaining the genome.

To investigate this possibility, Serguienko, Dr Ola Myklebost, and their colleagues analyzed the proteome, or collection of expressed proteins, in tumor and blood samples from 175 adults and children with sarcoma or acute lymphoblastic leukemia. The samples were collected between 2014 and 2017 through the NoSarC Norwegian Sarcoma Study, the Norwegian Childhood Cancer Biobank, and the Oslo University Hospital Cancer Research Biobank. The team compared them with samples from anonymized healthy individuals, including blood donors, children participating in the Bergen Growth Study, and children visiting a clinic in Bergen.

Children with cancer between the ages of two and 19 had substantially more circulating IgG antibodies targeting DNA repair proteins than young adults or older individuals. These antibodies primarily recognized proteins associated with six genes: RAD52, NUMA1, ALKBH7, APBB1, UNG, and MGMT. Although these genes are closely involved in DNA repair, they were not significantly mutated in the cancers studied. This raised the possibility that the proteins could be disrupted by the immune system even when the genes encoding them remained largely intact.

Finding these antibodies in children who already had cancer, however, could not establish whether they appeared before the disease or developed in response to it. The researchers therefore examined blood samples collected years earlier from 59 school-age children who participated in two long-term British studies, the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children and Born in Bradford.

Roughly half of these children subsequently developed cancer before turning 30, while the others did not. Comparing the two groups allowed the researchers to investigate whether antibodies targeting DNA repair proteins were present before cancer was diagnosed.

“Although this cohort is small, it was the best we could find internationally,” said Myklebost, an emeritus professor of molecular oncology at the University of Bergen. “Even so, we were able to show that indeed, children who later got cancer (on average six years after blood sampling) were about eight times more likely than their healthy peers to have antibodies to at least one core antigen involved in DNA repair.”

The findings, published in Frontiers in Immunology, suggest that these antibodies can appear well before cancer develops. Their presence alone does not establish that they cause the disease, however. To explore whether they might actually interfere with DNA repair, the scientists conducted additional experiments using living cells.

Those experiments showed that the auto-antibodies could enter cells, particularly during the stage when cells finish growing and prepare to divide. Once inside, the antibodies appeared to concentrate around a cellular structure called the TIGER organelle, where transcription factors and other short-lived but essential proteins are produced and modified.

“We showed that many children with various types of cancer have antibodies against some of their own DNA repair proteins. We also demonstrated that such ‘auto-antibodies’ can enter live cells and bind to – and probably inhibit – the activity of these essential proteins,” said corresponding author Myklebost.

The researchers propose that antibodies entering a cell or its nucleus could attach to DNA repair proteins and prevent them from working normally. By binding to these proteins, the antibodies might immobilize them or directly interfere with their activity, leaving the cell less capable of correcting genetic damage.

Such interference could produce effects resembling those caused by inherited defects in DNA repair genes that increase cancer susceptibility. The difference is that the repair machinery would be impaired by antibodies rather than by mutations in the genes encoding its components.

“[These results] together provide a plausible mechanism for increased cellular damage that could lead to cancer development,” concluded the authors.

An important question remains unanswered: Why would a child’s immune system begin producing antibodies against proteins needed for DNA repair?

“They could be triggered by infections, by crossreacting to some microbial proteins or overstimulation of the immune response, or by some imbalance in immune tolerance,” hypothesized Myklebost.

These possibilities remain unconfirmed, but the findings suggest a potential approach to identifying children who may face an increased risk of cancer. If further research establishes that the antibodies contribute to cancer development, screening for them could eventually help identify susceptible children years before diagnosis, potentially allowing earlier detection or even preventive interventions.

For now, the researchers are working to determine whether the antibodies directly impair DNA repair and whether that interference can cause cancer. Demonstrating those effects would provide much stronger evidence for the proposed mechanism.

“At present we are investigating the effect of patient antibodies on the repair of induced mutations in cultured cells. Ultimately, we hope to clone white blood cells that secrete such auto-antibodies, and test for any cancer-causing effects in mice to definitively prove our hypothesis,” said Myklebost.

#644. What should you really eat? Scientists unveil a new food pyramid

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Scientists have developed a new international food pyramid that brings together nutrition advice from around the world, with some surprising differences.

Nutrition advice has a way of making dinner complicated. Between arguments over protein, warnings about sugar and questions about carbs, it’s easy to lose sight of what a healthy meal is supposed to look like.

The official food guides from several parts of the world are much less divided.

An international team has pulled their shared advice into a new International Food Guide Pyramid, with plenty of vegetables, fruit and whole grains, and less room for red meat, sugary drinks, and white bread.

Karen Della Corte, a nutrition scientist at Brigham Young University, led the work.

“Despite what people often hear, there’s actually substantial agreement in nutrition science about the foundations of healthy eating,” said Della Corte.

She worked with 22 colleagues from North America, Europe and Australia.

The team reviewed existing research and guidelines to build a guide for generally healthy people aged two and older; they didn’t run a new diet experiment.

The researchers compared official advice from Canada, Germany, the Netherlands, China, Australia and the Nordic countries.

They also read the scientific advisers’ report for the newest US guidelines, looking for agreement across countries rather than giving one country’s advice the final say.

All the guides put vegetables and fruit first, with whole grains, beans, nuts and soy also favored.

They preferred unsaturated fats to saturated ones and recommended limiting red and processed meat, added sugar and salt.

The team sized the layers using food intake ranges from the EAT-Lancet Commission, which considers human and planetary health.

Its ranges assume an average adult eating about 2,400 calories daily, so the pyramid shows proportions rather than fixed servings.

The paper draws on nearly 300 published sources. Readers can choose between an upright pyramid and an upside-down version, both carrying the same advice.

Vegetables and fruit fill the biggest layer, together with whole grains and traditional grain staples.

Beans, lentils, nuts, seeds and soy make up the main protein foods in the next layer. Potatoes go there too, as do plant oils such as olive and canola.

The guide includes up to about two daily servings of animal foods: one of dairy, preferably yogurt or another fermented option. The other is fish, poultry, eggs or lean red meat.

Red meat gets about half an ounce (15 grams) a day, or roughly one weekly serving.

The recommendation is supported by long-term research involving three large groups of US adults. Over more than 30 years, researchers recorded over 22,000 new diabetes cases.

Diabetes risk was lowest among people who ate little or no red meat. The more they ate, the higher their risk tended to be.

Beef, lamb, and pork sit together in the guide’s health categories.

On land use and greenhouse gases, though, pork generally has a smaller footprint than beef or lamb.

David Jenkins is a co-author and nutrition researcher at the University of Toronto.

“Our diet has to be not only for our own health but also for the health of our planet,” Jenkins said.

The pyramid looks beyond the usual whole-grain versus refined-grain distinction.

It also uses the glycemic index, first developed by Jenkins’s team, to measure how much carbohydrate-containing foods raise blood sugar compared with a reference food.

Durum wheat pasta can lose its bran and still score low on the index because its coarse grain cells stay largely intact.

Grind grain down to fine flour, though, and those cells break open, which helps explain why white and whole-wheat bread both raise blood sugar more than intact grains do.

At least half the grains should be whole, the guide says, while traditional staples with a low glycemic index can make up the rest.

White bread goes at the top with sweets and chips, a separation most national guides don’t make because they show grains as one group.

“A healthy diet won’t look exactly the same for everyone around the world,” Della Corte said.

“Durum pasta, basmati rice, tortillas, rice noodles, bulgur and many other staples with a low to moderate glycemic index can all fit into a healthy diet.”

Much of the research behind the familiar sugar limit concerns sugary drinks, the authors argue.

That limit, used in many guidelines, asks people to get less than 10% of their daily calories from sugar, but the team questions treating every source alike.

Della Corte previously led an analysis combining long-term studies: each additional serving of a sugary drink was linked to a 25% higher risk of type 2 diabetes.

For fruit juice, each serving was linked to a 5% higher risk, yet total sugar intake was linked to lower risk.

That last result doesn’t mean sugar prevents diabetes. The authors point to what comes with it: whole fruit and yogurt offer fiber, protein or other nutrients, while soda contains sugar and little else.

The authors also warn that diet surveys don’t always capture how much sugar people eat, because people tend to report too little.

That may have skewed the results, but the pyramid still gives sugary drinks their own spot at the top.

The authors say the newest US guidelines call for about 30% to 50% more protein, or up to twice the standard requirement, while emphasizing meat and dairy.

In their view, the standard amount of 0.36 grams per pound of body weight (0.8 grams per kilogram) is enough for most adults.

One large combined analysis linked eating more protein to lower death rates, but plant protein accounted for that link. A modest increase in protein may still help older adults and people losing weight keep muscle, the authors say.

The US advisers’ calculations also favored a swap: less meat, poultry and eggs, and more beans, lentils, nuts and soy.

That added fiber, folate, magnesium and vitamin E without new nutrient shortfalls, although more iron came in a form the body absorbs less easily.

Butter gets different treatment, too. The authors disagree with the US pyramid’s grouping of butter and other foods high in saturated fat with plant oils.

Feeding trials show that replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat lowers LDL cholesterol, which is linked to heart disease.

This pyramid is a proposal, and its authors reached agreement by revising drafts, without a formal vote.

The guidelines they selected weren’t meant to represent the whole world, and they haven’t tested whether seeing the pyramid changes anyone’s eating habits.

Several authors reported financial ties to foods it favors: two received research support from pasta maker Barilla, others from groups promoting nuts, soybeans and pulses.

One runs a university glycemic index testing service, and the authors reported no funding for the paper itself.

Study co-author Anette Buyken is a professor at Paderborn University in Germany.

“Knowing what is healthy and being motivated to eat healthily is not enough when these choices are difficult or expensive,” Buyken said.

“Nutrition policies need to support this through measures on food marketing, access and prices.”

The authors want to develop local versions using each region’s staple foods.

The study is published in the journal Advances in Nutrition.

#1060. The Influencers Telling Young Women Not to Vote

As the midterms approach, an alarming online trend is gaining traction

In the spring of 2026, at a three-day gathering of young Christian conservative women in San Antonio, Texas, a 21-year-old Florida influencer named Savanna Faith Stone took to the stage and told her audience something that made headlines for days afterward. Women, she declared, shouldn’t vote. Not because they lack the intelligence or the standing—Stone is careful to position herself as no one’s subordinate—but because the vote itself is a con: a gift from elites who never had women’s interests at heart, wrapped in the language of liberation and sold as progress. Her preferred alternative: “household voting,” or one ballot per family, cast by the husband, because husband and wife are “one flesh and one political unit.”

The clips spread quickly. At the Turning Point USA Women’s Leadership Summit, an event designed, with some irony, to recruit young women into conservative politics, several attendees told journalists that they would happily surrender their individual votes if it meant a more conservative country.

“If my husband’s the head of the household,” one woman told CBC, “I would imagine if he was voting on behalf of our household, I would be fine with that.”

What followed was a familiar media cycle: shock, mockery, a wave of think pieces positioning Stone as a fringe eccentric. And then, a week or so later, the story moved on. But the impulse to laugh and scroll past is precisely what the rhetorical strategy Stone deployed is designed to invite.

“These are reactionary statements that tend to do really well online,” says Jess Britvich, a former social worker who is now a content creator. Britvich focuses on the different ways the right uses the internet to promote anti-feminism and now anti-suffragism. “Inflammatory and emotionally provocative content does really well on the algorithm. This is why the right is so much stronger in online messaging,” Britvich explains. “They’re able to spread misinformation.”

This doesn’t always look dangerous. “It starts with women looking as though they are living these perfect, ‘traditional’ lives, in prairie-style dresses and playing with their kids,” Britvich continues, “and then later on, once that audience is captive, that is when they are able to convince women to go into these ideas.”

The podcast Culture Apothecary, hosted by Alex Clark, is funded by Turning Point USA. At the beginning of September, they launched their series Wife School, which gained wider attention, with their first episode promoting the idea that women should submit to their husbands, especially when it comes to sex. The guest on that episode, Tilly Dillehay, actually sells a course in becoming a “good” wife to a “godly” man, and Clark argues that a healthy marriage should not treat sex as optional.

The type of content that makes up the “womanosphere”—the term coined to describe the online ecosystem of antifeminist media and content creators—is outrageous enough to generate attention and just memorable enough that attention rarely hardens into analysis. But the claim that women shouldn’t vote is made not only by influencers like Stone, but also powerful people like the pastor Doug Wilson, who has deep ties to the Trump administration and who has told NPR that under a theocratic, patriarchal America, the 19th Amendment would be repealed. Last year, sociologists Paul Djupe and Brooklyn Walker polled a group of Americans about what they thought of women’s political rights. Of the respondents, 20.5 percent agreed or strongly agreed with this proposal for a household vote. There was a gender gap, however; 24.2 percent of men and 16.9 percent of women agreed.

Anti-suffragism and anti-feminism have strong and long links to each other, and ironically, women have been at the forefront of both movements. Savanna Stone has argued that the suffrage movement forced the vote upon women, many of whom, particularly those from working-class backgrounds, did not want it. Another anti-feminist, Rachel Wilson, has made a similar argument, adding that the true purpose of women’s suffrage was to get women to pay more taxes. None of this is supported by the historical record around suffrage movements, which included working-class, middle-class, and upper-class women. So why do these arguments resonate? Focusing on class divisions speaks to larger anxieties around affordability, the large number of women who left the American workforce post-Covid, and the shrinking of educational institutions, hospital workforces, and federal workforces—industries that traditionally provided women with not just jobs but the potential for career advancement and real power.

“At times of social change, you can often see this clawing back of a form of ‘normal’ or, rather, of power,” says Katie Gaddini, author of Esther’s Army: The Christian Women Who Power the American Right. “After the Civil Rights Movement and the ascension of women and people of color, the religious right organized against desegregation until they realized they were unpopular and focused on gender and abortion. We can see parallels to now, in which over the past 10 to 20 years, there is this expansion of rights again for minorities, but also the pandemic caused massive amounts of inequality and instability, and this ideology is another way of clawing back some control.”

Kristy Campion, an Australian researcher into extremism, sees numerous reasons why this messaging is currently gaining traction. On getting rid of the 19th Amendment, she says, “I think this is a fairly divisive idea among women on the far right. Some women who exhibit far right-wing ideologies generally connect women’s-liberation advances to active plots or conspiracies broadly designed to ‘defeminize’ women and ‘emasculate’ men as a way of undermining Western civilization. Others cast their decision not to vote as a uniquely liberating (and contradictory feminist) choice.” Campion continues, “Others, however, yield political authority to their husbands, framing politics as a uniquely male domain.… This can at times be a position interwoven with a romanticized historical nostalgia.”

The lifestyle presented in these reactionary accounts leans heavily on that feeling of nostalgia—a longing for a past that didn’t exist for most people. What’s worrying is how well it can resonate with young women of all sorts living through a rapidly shifting economic landscape.

Young women in America and Britain are navigating an economy that has made the dual-income household not a choice but a necessity, a romantic landscape shaped by apps that commodify intimacy, and a cultural landscape that has multiplied new demands on women without reducing any of the older ones. For years, women have been pressured to “have it all,” to pump out increased economic productivity despite a wage gap, and to still be the best mothers in the world. Oh, and they’re supposed to do it while maintaining their looks and being well-read and extremely fit and providing a world of emotional and political labor. The feminist promise—that equality would mean freedom—has delivered, for many women, equality of overwork. Which can make the idea of turning your back on politics alarmingly appealing.

Thea, 29, based in Durham in the U.K., has found herself drawn to the “aestheticized, sanitized picture of feminine fragility that is so popular on social media.” She says, “I am drawn to the idea that I can do the things I love—dress pretty, bake, read, scrapbook, tidy the house—and be rewarded for them.”

She assures me that she enjoys her work and has no interest in giving up her right to vote—“It’s nonsense! Women all over the world have gone through so much to be allowed a voice”—but she feels disillusioned around the constant toil, working for so little and being tired all the time, and she knows that so many young women feel the same.

“I think the corporate grind and the modern job market have become such a soul-sucking experience—not just for women, but for everyone—that there is something appealing in the idea that you can just stay at home instead of working yourself to the bone for the bare minimum of survival,” Thea says. “Of course, if we live the trad life the way our grandmothers and great-grandmothers did, it would be a different type of exhaustion, but the fruits of that labor would be much easier to observe.”

In that context, where young women feel pessimistic about the future, influencers like Wilson and Stone offer something that sounds, perversely, like relief: not the freedom to do everything, but permission to do less. Not a program for political change, but a vision of settled domesticity in which someone else handles the civic and economic burden. For the women of the right who advocate such ideas, the appeal is not to the worst instincts of their audience, but, in some sense, to their weariness. Which is part of what makes it so dangerous.

“No one dreams of labor,” Britvich agrees, “but we also know that women should not give up financial autonomy because that can impact your safety at the end of the day. And that’s why all these people pushing this messaging about how women belong in the home are so irresponsible. Especially as these are the same people who are voting against things like paid parental leave, who are voting against anything that would actually help you stay in the home and get paid for that labor that you’re providing.

“If we can actually pay women for their labor,” Britvich continues, “if we could start paying the people who are caring for our society better—teachers, nurses, health care workers—and also provide paid parental leave and universal health care, there would be actual choice and autonomy in these choices. That would be great, but that’s not what this messaging is trying to push toward. Convincing women to opt out of politics does not help women.”

The vote is far from being repealed, but it’s worrying to witness these ideas spreading not just within the Christian sphere, but outside of it, too. The question worth asking is not “How could any woman believe this?” but “What conditions make this a viable solution?”

As the midterms approach, women’s votes becomes crucial once more; turnout can often be low, especially among younger voters, and the ideological divide between young men and women has grown, with polls indicating a continuing split by gender. According to the latest polls by Pew Research and YouGov/The Economist, women are more likely to vote Democrat than men. Combatting anti-suffrage messaging does not mean ignoring it but rather engaging in politics and community. The answer to solving issues like the cost of living and access to health care lies not in surrendering the vote or nationalism, but in building societies and economies that actually work for everyone.
