Fall is a fab time to begin a productive veggie patch in a raised planter, and these small-space varieties will get your vegetable growing journey off to a great start.

Fall is a great time to start a vegetable garden if you’ve never grown your own before, and these compact crops will bring an abundant harvest in a small space. Sow in October and growing vegetables is so easy as you don’t have to worry about pests or remembering to water your plants every day, and you can still raise plenty of tasty crops at this time of the year. You won’t have to wait long to enjoy your very first harvest, either, and just wait till you taste the difference between homegrown and shop-bought!

The secret to start growing your own vegetables in October is to use a raised bed or planter. Gardening in raised beds is so much more straightforward than growing veggies in the ground as it doesn’t matter a jot what your soil is like as you can instantly create perfect conditions with a tailored potting mix like Miracle-Gro Organic Raised Bed Soil, available from Lowe's.

Another big advantage to growing fall-sown veggies in a raised bed is that you can position the planter in a sheltered, sunny spot to help plants grow well in shorter days and cooler temperatures. You could even place it right up against a house wall to take advantage of your home's heat, and this also makes it simple to throw a layer of horticultural fleece over the top if you need to keep young seedlings cozy through a cold snap.

There are plenty of small-space veggies that thrive in a container, but here's my pick of the best vegetables to sow in October – all compact enough to flourish in a raised bed – if you want to grow your own this fall. So don’t keep dreaming about doing it done day – do it today!

All these vegetable varieties are cold-tolerant, but tailor your choice to your zone. Position your raised bed in a warm, sheltered area of your garden and you can absolutely cheat zone recommendations to successfully grow winter crops. I would happily grow all these varieties in Zone 7 and above, and if you live in a colder zone simply choose a covered raised bed (you'll find my recommendations below) or be ready with a duvet of horticultural fleece (ditto!) when temperatures plummet, and give it a go: for the price of a packet of seeds, it’s well worth the risk.

Think you can’t grow peas through winter? Think again! ‘Meteor’ is an exceptionally hardy variety that’s been bred to withstand freezing temperatures so is one of the best vegetables to sow in fall, with seeds that are smooth and round so resist rot in cool wet soil far better than traditional wrinkled types. What’s more, this variety only grows to a bushy 18 inches high (rather than the wild tangle of 6-feet-high stems of many pea cultivars) so it’s a great choice for a small space. Just push some twiggy sticks into the soil when you sow and the plants will look after themselves.

While you’ll have to wait until May to enjoy the fresh peapods, the plants will get busy through fall growing a strong root system, ready to burst into growth in early spring for an early harvest. And the big advantage of a fall sowing is that you won’t have to net your plants against the pea moth, a pest notorious for ruining summer pea crops. Meteor pea seeds are available from Mr Fothergills USA.

Carrot crops are normally sown in spring and summer but ‘Atomic Red’ is a cool-season specialist that flourishes in low temperatures. And just look at that color! Atomic Red is fully frost-tolerant and fast to grow so in milder zones you could be harvesting around 80 days after an October sowing. In colder climates, plants won’t crop until spring but the harvest will be well worth the wait: cool temperatures make the plant convert its starches into sugars to act as a protective antifreeze, so the carrots taste beautifully sweet.

As well as great flavor, Atomic Red carrots also keep their striking coral-scarlet color through cooking. With roots growing up to 11 inches long, this variety is the perfect size for a raised bed, where the loose soil will favour long, straight carrots. A fall sowing means there’s zero threat from pesky carrot flies, too. Atomic Red carrot seeds are available from Botanical Interests.

The name tells you everything you need to know about this small but hardy veggie! While standard kale varieties can reach up to 3 feet tall, Dwarf Siberian kale stays at a tidy 15 inches high so it’s a smart choice for a raised planter. It survives freezing temperatures and is super-tasty too, staying tender and mild (unlike some of its curly-kale cousins that can grow tough and bitter) and becoming sweetener when exposed to fall frosts.

Young leaves can be eaten raw in salads and smoothies and if you sow in October you'll be enjoying these by late November or December – or wait a few more weeks for mature leaves to sauté with a little garlic and oil. Just be sure to only harvest the outer leaves, leaving the central crown intact, so the plants keep producing as a cut-and-come-again crop. Dwarf Siberian kale seeds are available from Eden Brothers.

Homegrown garlic is a delight so you should definitely find room in your raised bed to plant some cloves. Softneck garlic is best in a small space and the incredibly reliable ‘California Early’ is a perfect choice if you're at the start of your veggie-growing journey. It's very easy to grow and has a highly compact, vertical habit so you can pack plenty into your bed. California Early matures around June, too, clearing your veggie bed in time for mid-summer sowings of other crops.

Because it’s a softneck variety, California Early garlic doesn’t need the long period of freezing weather that hardneck varieties do to form cloves, which means it’ll still form plump bulbs in the warmer conditions of a sheltered raised bed. A raised bed also means you can plant a little deeper than usual, which is the expert secret to growing garlic with the plumpest cloves, even if this your first time growing this crop. California Early garlic bulbs are available from Walmart.

Being able to pick fresh greens for a winter salad is such a joy, and the taste is a far cry from the disinfected leaves sold in many grocery stores. Also known as lambs lettuce and mache, corn salad is the easiest to grow through fall and winter and ‘Vit’ is my pick for glossy, tender leaves with a gourmet nutty flavor.

This French variety is so easy to grow as it’s vigorous and naturally resistant to mildew and, as it only grows to 6 inches high in upright rosettes, it’s a great crop to squeeze in between bigger plants. Vit is incredibly resilient in cold temperatures and quick to crop too, ready to eat in around 50 days. As well as eating mache raw, you can cook the leaves as you would spinach. Organic Vit corn salad seeds are available from Burpee.

Before you brush off the idea of growing radishes, hold up! Have you ever tasted a homegrown radish? Because it’s nothing short of amazing! ‘Purple Plum’ is my pick for an October sowing because this cool-season specialist crops incredibly quickly and, in a warm, sunny spot, you could be harvesting these little heirloom beauties within a month.

Purple Plum radishes are a super choice for a small space because their roots reach peak crunch when they’re just 1½ inches wide. As they’re so quick to crop, you can squeeze them in around slower-maturing crops, too. Another good reason to choose Purple Plum for an October sowing is that – unlike most other radish varieties – they don’t become woody or pithy in damp fall soil if you don’t get round to picking them at the perfect moment.

This is another vegetable that gets sweeter when touched by fall frosts, too, and you can also eat the radish tops, raw in salads or tossed into winter stir fries. Purple Plum radish seeds are available from Botanical Interests.

Another must for a fall sowing is arugula. One packet of seeds will provide a wealth of peppery leaves for salads and garnishes, or try making a zingy spring-is-coming pesto by swapping out basil for arugula. ‘Astro’ is the variety to reach for when sowing in October as it still germinates quickly in cool fall soil and, unlike many other arugula, keeps producing its flavorful green leaves once daylight hours drop below 10 hours a day. It tastes good, too, with a smooth, nutty flavor and a mild peppery bite.

While Astro arugula can tolerate light frosts, growth will slow and speed as temperatures lower and rise, or you can keep a steady supply coming by popping a clear plastic cloche or horticultural fleece over your crop. While you can leave foliage to grow up to 18 inches high, a big advantage to growing your own is that you can pick oh-so tender and tasty leaves when they’re around 4 inches high, and the plant will quickly grow more of this cut-and-come-again crop. Western states that were thought to be resistant to prolonged drought may be more vulnerable than scientists previously believed, a new analysis of groundwater reserves in the Colorado River Basin finds.

Researchers revealed the four states that together make up the Upper Basin — Colorado, New Mexico, Utah and Wyoming — lost almost twice as much groundwater as the Lower Basin did during an intense drought in the southwestern U.S. between 2020 and 2022. The finding was unexpected and might signal a turning point for the region, because the Upper Basin has long been seen as more resilient to dry spells than the Lower Basin, said Esther Oyedele, a doctoral student in geosciences at Virginia Tech and the study's first author.

"The Upper Colorado River Basin has historically been considered more resilient because it receives more precipitation, particularly snowfall in the Rocky Mountains," Oyedele told Live Science in an email. "I think the important takeaway is that we should not assume that the Upper Basin will remain relatively resilient simply because it has historically received more precipitation and snowmelt."

The Lower Basin comprises parts of Nevada, California and Arizona. Less rain and snowfall means the Lower Basin naturally has less groundwater recharge, or less replenishment from other water sources, than the Upper Basin. On top of that, the Lower Basin generally has higher evaporation rates and substantial water demands, given that roughly 30 million of the 40 million people that depend on the Colorado River for water live in the Lower Basin, Oyedele said.

In the new study, published Oct. 13 in the journal Water Resources Research, researchers used satellite and hydrological data to estimate groundwater depletion across the Colorado River Basin between 2004 and 2023. The data included measurements of surface water, soil moisture and snow-water storage, as well as observations from the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment mission and its successor, called the Follow-On.

The results showed that groundwater depletion has been a persistent problem in the Colorado River Basin over the past two decades, but losses accelerated in 2019. The three-year drought that began in 2020 is the principal cause of this, because it shrank the snowpack in the Rocky Mountains, increased evaporation and boosted water demand across the basin, Oyedele said.

During the drought between 2020 and 2022, the Upper Basin lost around 4.2 cubic miles (17.5 cubic kilometers) of groundwater, while the Lower Basin lost about 2.3 cubic miles (9.4 cubic km). In total, the Colorado River Basin lost 14 cubic miles (58.3 cubic km) of groundwater ‪—‬ equivalent to 25 million Olympic-size swimming pools' worth of water ‪—‬ during the study period, between 2004 and 2023.

It's unclear why the deficit is so much higher in the Upper Basin than in the Lower Basin, but the Upper Basin's proximity to and dependence on the mountains for aquifer recharge probably didn't help, Oyedele said. As snow melts, some of it replenishes groundwater, so a reduction in snowfall between 2020 and 2022 would have affected the Upper Basin particularly badly, she noted.

The Upper Basin also recorded more aquifer recharge during wet periods before the drought than the Lower Basin did, so it's possible that the pronounced decline during the drought was linked to a higher initial amount of groundwater in the Upper Basin, Oyedele said.

The Upper Basin includes parts of Colorado, Wyoming, Utah and New Mexico, while the Lower Basin comprises parts of Nevada, California and Arizona.

Next, the researchers used advanced statistical methods to explore the relationship between groundwater and surface water in the Colorado River Basin. Due to global warming and the overallocation of the Colorado River's water in a 1922 document, the river's flow is now 35% less than it was on average in the 20th century. Water scarcity in the basin has reached alarming levels, according to the study, and Lake Mead and Lake Powell, the two largest reservoirs in the U.S., dropped to record lows in August.

The team found that in the Upper Basin, groundwater levels evolve in the same direction as surface water, but with a slight delay. This suggests groundwater losses occur due to a lack of recharge and more aquifer pumping during periods of drought, when surface water levels drop. In the Lower Basin, however, this link is weaker and more complex. The results showed that groundwater losses sometimes preceded surface water declines during the study period, suggesting that groundwater depletion is not simply tied to less surface water trickling into the ground or to humans extracting groundwater when there's less surface water available. Instead, groundwater is consistently being overused, Oyedele said.

"This distinction is important because it suggests that groundwater depletion is not simply a direct response to surface-water shortages," she said. "Human water use, particularly sustained groundwater extraction, may also play a significant role."

Looking ahead, it's hard to tell if the Upper Basin will continue to lose more groundwater than the Lower Basin and what that would mean for the region, Oyedele said. What seems clear, however, is that the Upper Basin is more vulnerable to drought than experts had thought.

"Continued warming, changes in snowpack, prolonged drought, and increasing water demand could place additional pressure on groundwater resources across both basins," Oyedele said. However, future groundwater responses will depend on several factors, including precipitation, snowmelt, water use and management decisions, she added.

On the management side, Oyedele suggested tackling groundwater and surface water together rather than as two separate resources. Groundwater helps to sustain river flows, and rivers can recharge aquifers ‪—‬ so the two are interconnected. Groundwater often serves as a backup during drought, but it shouldn't be treated as an unlimited reserve, she said.

#2220. Uganda: Ensure Justice for Killing of Rwandan Dissident

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image The funeral of of Sgt. Maj. Robert Kabera on October 21, 2026, in Kamengo, Uganda. © Private

(Nairobi) – Ugandan authorities’ obligation to conduct an effective investigation into the brutal killing of a Rwandan dissident requires examining all evidence that points to a possible politically motivated killing, Human Rights Watch said today. Ugandan authorities should ensure a prompt, impartial, credible investigation that leads to accountability.

The body of Sgt. Maj. Robert Kabera, a singer, former Rwanda Defence Force member, and outspoken critic of the Rwandan government, was found on October 17, 2026, in Luwala village near Kampala, where he was constructing a house. He had fled Rwanda in November 2020 and claimed asylum on political persecution grounds. He had reported to Human Rights Watch multiple times that he had been threatened and attacked in Uganda. In a September 2022 interview with Human Rights Watch, Kabera said: “They will kill me. They know where I am … I will die before I get resettled.”

“It was clear from our conversations that Robert Kabera knew his criticism of the Rwandan government made him a target, and that he feared for his life,” said Clémentine de Montjoye, senior Great Lakes researcher at Human Rights Watch. “Ugandan authorities should investigate all possible motives for the killing and take immediate steps to protect Rwandan dissidents and refugees in the country.” 

Kabera’s body was found beheaded and with other injuries, according to a relative and a photograph and three videos analyzed by Human Rights Watch. A photograph published by local media on October 18 shows Kabera’s decapitated naked body in a wooded area with blood on the ground next to it. The police reported that Kabera was found dead in a eucalyptus plantation on October 17. 

Witnesses provided three videos that showed Kabera’s body being transferred from a vehicle onto a trolley in a hospital. A forensic medical expert told Human Rights Watch that Kabera’s body was likely mutilated after he died, the unnatural position of his shoulders seen in the images and the ligature marks on his wrists suggested that his hands were tied behind his back when he died. According to a document seen by Human Rights Watch, the Ugandan police requested a postmortem medical examination, the results of which are not yet available. 

There are many reasons to treat Kabera’s death as suspicious, Human Rights Watch said, and foremost that he was an outspoken critic of the Rwandan government. In a video published on YouTube days before his death, titled “Kill Us if You Want,” he spoke about the Rwandan ruling party’s alleged crimes, as well as the threats and persecution he had faced since fleeing to Uganda.

A relative told Human Rights Watch that a man speaking Kinyarwanda, who presented himself as a political opponent in need of assistance, had traveled to the construction site with Kabera on the afternoon of October 16. “[Kabera] called me around 11 p.m., and after that no longer picked up the phone,” the relative said. “The man he was with gave me a fake number to call him. The next day, when I couldn’t reach [Kabera], his number didn’t go through.”  

On November 23, 2020, a news release by the Rwanda Defence Force Military Prosecution Department announced an investigation into “defilement allegations” against Kabera, and alleged that the crime was committed on November 21, 2020, two days after Kabera said he left the country.

In a May 2023 letter to Human Rights Watch, the Ugandan police inspector general confirmed that Kabera was granted asylum in Uganda on the basis of his claims of political persecution, was being provided with additional security by the police and security forces, and was in the final stages of resettlement to a third country. 

Kabera faced arrest and multiple physical attacks in Uganda. Those looking for a new router for woodworking projects have likely noticed a wide selection of styles. DIYers can choose between a fixed-base or plunge-style router, and some combo kits (with both options) are also available. Get to know the pros and cons of each type to choose the best one.

Fixed-base routers are the most basic models. They have a solid exterior casing, but users can adjust the depth of the router bit to create a variety of design options. They can adjust the bit’s depth by loosening the collar and twisting a dial. A fixed-base router is easy to set up and use on the outside edge of a board or countertop, but it can be challenging to start accurately in a workpiece’s field (middle). That’s because the router bit extends below the base plate, and the user has to tip the router carefully into position while the bit is spinning—which increases the odds of being slightly off the mark.

Plunge routers have a spring-loaded base that allows users to lower the spinning router bit into the field of a workpiece without the need to tip the router to the side. Like fixed-base routers, plunge routers come with depth gauges that let the user select the desired penetration of the cut. Plunge routers are the top pick for cutting details in the center of a board, but they’ll also work for edge profiling, making them versatile for both types of design cuts.

This type of router is a favorite of professional woodworker Richard Louise: “a plunge router is normally my go-to router (routers come with a fixed base or a plunge base). But the plunge version can do both, while the fixed base really can’t safely plunge.”

Alternatively, router packages are available that allow the user to swap exterior cases, providing fixed-base and plunge-base capabilities in the same kit. Though these combo kits are versatile and handy, they can be more expensive than a single router. Users don’t have to store two separate routers, but swapping bases frequently can slow the workflow.

When it comes to routers, intended use is a determining factor. If shoppers are looking for a good general-use router, they will want to stick with a fixed-base model. These models are easy to set, and users can build jigs (custom patterns) that allow for identical, repeated designs. A fixed-base router can be used on a router table for added stability. A fixed-base router’s primary use is to cut a shaped edge (called a “profile”) on boards and countertops.

Users who like to make mortise-and-tenon (interlocking) joints will want a plunge router since they’re more precise when making center-field cuts. The downside is that they’re not compatible with router tables.

The amount of power a router has helps determine how well it handles large router bits and dense hardwoods, such as ash or oak. While users can make multiple passes with any router—shaving off just a little at a time with each pass—a powerful router can shave off an entire edge design in one pass. When time is of the essence, a powerful router can make quick work of creating designs, but routing is usually more about precision than speed.

Most wood routers have horsepower ratings of 1.25 to 1.75, while some hot-rod versions have more than 2 hp. In most cases, the lower range works quite well, but 2-hp routers make short work of dense materials, such as hardwoods or Formica countertops.

For cordless models, power relates to battery size. Today’s rechargeable lithium-ion batteries are rated in amp hours and range from 1.5 up to 6Ah. The greater the amp hours, the more power the battery has. A battery with 3 to 5Ah hours is sufficient for most cordless routers.

One of the challenging aspects of creating perfect edge profiles is choosing the correct speed to get the job done quickly without burning the wood. No magic speed exists, as boards (even among boards of the same species) differ in density. It comes down to experience and the right touch.

The good news is that a variable-speed router can be tweaked until users find just the right speed for a specific type of wood. They often have small dials to help select their speed. For a workable range, look for a router with speeds between 9,000 and 20,000 rpm.

Some routers have constant-speed sensors that monitor how quickly a bit is spinning and adjust it accordingly to maintain the ideal speed. For example, if woodworkers are using a router at 10,000 rpm but come across a particularly dense section of wood, the router can slow down and cause inconsistent results. With a constant-speed sensor, the router boosts its power output to compensate for the increase in density, so the bit continues to spin at the same speed.

Routers are powerful tools, and users need a safe grip on them to ensure proper control. A comfortable grip helps users maintain a firm grasp.

Fixed-base routers and plunge routers often have two handles to provide better control. Depending on the manufacturer, they can be rubberized or made from polished wood. Some models even have handle-mounted switches, making it easy to turn the tools on and off without releasing a handle.

Palm routers fit in a single hand, so the body essentially becomes the handle. Many have rubberized moldings and contours meant for comfortable use.

Routers make a lot of dust. The high-speed cutting/shaving process removes tiny bits of wood and sends them flying through the air, creating a mess and a potential breathing hazard. To avoid a lengthy cleanup, look for a router with a dust collection port that attaches to a shop vac or dust collection system.

Some router’s dust collection ports work better than others; higher-end models usually work best. These brands are popular among pros who work in custom woodworking shops where effective dust collection is necessary.

The bit size a router accepts is contingent on the size of the router’s collet—the adjustable holder that secures the bit in place. Bits come in two standard sizes: ¼ inch and ½ inch. The smaller (¼-inch) bit works well for light-duty jobs like edge profiling and milling hinge mortises in doors. However, it might not work as well for heavy-duty work, such as routing dense hardwoods, which requires the larger ½-inch bit.

Some of today’s smallest palm routers come with a collet that only accepts ¼-inch router bits, but the majority of routers come with interchangeable collets that accept bits of both sizes.

Doing so is significantly more difficult with a fixed-base router, as it requires tipping the router into position, which can reduce precision.

A fixed-base router works for most projects. If creating intricate joinery, however, look for a plunge-base router or use a fixed-base router with a table for added precision and stability.

She felt as if they were giving up on her. With the diagnosis, Montgomery-Haga not only learned that SPS existed, she says, “I was told it’s treatable.”

Current treatments include muscle relaxers, anti-seizure medications and Botox injections in body parts that feel especially stiff. Although these off-label therapies can address people’s symptoms, they’re “doing nothing to treat the underlying autoimmune disease,” Piquet says. And some can come with potent side effects, like mental fog.

Zier, who has tried multiple combinations of different meds, remembers frequently losing her train of thought. She has since tapered off all but one drug, a low dose of Valium. “The trade-off,” she says, “is pain.”

People with SPS can also receive intravenous infusions of healthy antibodies, a treatment called IVIG, to decrease muscle spasms and stiffness. Montgomery-Haga found it helpful but time-consuming. She’d go to the clinic every three weeks for an infusion, which could take up to six hours.

“The bottom line is there are no FDA-approved therapies for this disease,” Piquet says. Now, the one most likely to cross the finish line first is something no one would have expected even a few years ago, CAR T pioneer June says. If you told doctors that CAR T cell therapy was going to be FDA approved for an autoimmune disease, he says, “people would have laughed.”

June may be best known for his work in cancer, but he’s been keenly following the progress in auto-immune therapy. His daughter, now in her 30s, was diagnosed with juvenile rheumatoid arthritis when she was 9 years old. For her, that’s meant over 20 years of treatment with drugs that bring a bundle of side effects, such as fatigue and risk of infections. “It would be much better to be cured,” he says.

In autoimmune diseases like rheumatoid arthritis, lupus and SPS, a person’s own antibodies attack tissues throughout the body, from blood vessels to cartilage to neurons. Wiping out these rogue antibodies can stop the attacks. But CAR T cell therapy doesn’t act on the antibodies themselves. It goes straight to the source, snuffing out the cellular factories that manufacture them.

Those antibody factories are known as B cells. To eliminate them, doctors recruit T cells, which act as the body’s security system and work together to hunt down and destroy problematic cells. In the lab, scientists fine-tune these hunters, outfitting them with a molecular homing device called a chimeric antigen receptor, or CAR. When infused back into patients’ bodies, the newly-engineered T cells can target the antibody factories directly — like a saboteur told exactly what to destroy.

The CAR T cell therapy Lynn Montgomery-Haga received wipes out B cells, including the ones likely driving her disease. Doctors hope this therapy “resets” her immune system, getting rid of harmful B cells and letting healthy new ones take their place.

“The secret behind CAR T cell therapy is that you completely deplete B cells in the body,” says Georg Schett, a rheumatologist at Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg in Germany. Temporarily wiping out a person’s B cells lets the body replace them with fresh, unproblematic ones, Schett says. He calls it an “immune reset.”

CAR T cell therapy got its start in the 2010s, when researchers used engineered T cells to target cancer. In 2017, the FDA approved the first CAR T cell therapies for two blood cancers, acute lymphoblastic leukemia and large B cell lymphoma. Soon after, scientists saw the first tantalizing hints that it might work for autoimmune diseases, too. In some cases, cancer patients with autoimmune disorders reported improvements in both conditions after treatment.

But most researchers didn’t think it could be that easy, Schett says. After all, autoimmune diseases were driven by a complex network of cell types. How could targeting just one of them work?

That thinking underwent a paradigm shift in 2021 when Schett and his team published the first major paper reporting a successful treatment with the therapy. The patient was a 20-year-old woman with a severe case of lupus, a complex, sometimes-fatal illness that can damage the kidneys and other organs. Within five weeks of CAR T cell therapy treatment, signs of her disease began to vanish.

Results like these were “unheard of,” says rheumatologist Maximilian Konig of Johns Hopkins Medicine in Baltimore. Lupus is extremely difficult to treat, and for decades, most drugs tested against it failed. No one had ever seen the kind of improvements that Schett’s patient experienced.

After the paper came out, “everybody was talking about it,” Konig says. Today, more than five years later, the woman’s lupus hasn’t returned, Schett says. “We consider this patient cured,” he says.

The woman’s response was no fluke. The following year, Schett’s team reported similar results in five more lupus patients. All went into remission after CAR T cell therapy.

This was the true tipping point, June says. “It’s rare, very rare, that you get such striking results.”

What followed was an explosion of research around the world. Scientists are now testing CAR T cell therapy in nearly a dozen autoimmune diseases linked to harmful B cells, including lupus, rheumatoid arthritis and a connective-tissue disease known as systemic sclerosis.

Kyverna, the California-based company behind the SPS trial, is also running trials for neurological autoimmune conditions including generalized myasthenia gravis and progressive multiple sclerosis, or MS.

One reason using CAR T cell therapy for neurological conditions excites researchers is because of the cells’ potential to slip past the blood-brain barrier. That means they might be able to access pockets of smoldering inflammation inaccessible to other drugs, says neurologist Rebecca Farber of Columbia University.

That could be a huge help in diseases like MS, in which a person’s antibodies attack the protective sheaths around nerve cells, causing numbness, brain fog and difficulty walking. Still, Farber doubts that CAR T cell therapy could fix existing damage. “We’re not expecting it to repair the nervous system,” she says. But it’s possible the treatment could freeze the disease’s progress. Patients are already asking her team about future treatments.

For Piquet, who cares for roughly 100 people with SPS, Kyverna’s trial offered a compelling opportunity to help her patients. Montgomery-Haga cried when Piquet told her about the trial at one of their regular Zoom appointments. She recalls wondering, is this the treatment that “might help me be normal again?”

Though Montgomery-Haga leapt at the chance to try Kyverna’s treatment, called miv-cel, CAR T cell therapy is no walk in the park. Before starting, doctors sit patients down to “talk about the good, the bad and the potentially ugly,” Piquet says.

That includes the rigorous prep work needed before people receive their engineered T cells. Patients must first get low-dose chemotherapy, which flushes T cells from the body and makes space for the new ones. Chemo can ambush the body with symptoms including hair thinning, nausea, vomiting and diarrhea.

Treatment itself also carries risks. It destroys most B cells in the body — not just the ones that cause disease. That can lead to fever, inflammation and potentially risky infections. In Kyverna’s trial, for example, 15 percent of participants had severely low white blood cell counts after treatment. Such symptoms were expected, Piquet says, and were easily treated.

Other CAR T treatments seem to have fared far worse. In August, the pharmaceutical company Novartis paused clinical trials of its therapy, rap-cel, across a spectrum of autoimmune diseases after three patients died. Bristol Myers Squibb has also paused similar studies after some patients experienced neurological complications, according to the Wall Street Journal. Details of the incidents were slow to emerge and have set the field on edge. “It’s a tragedy,” Konig says. He calls such outcomes an important wake-up call.

But red flags in one trial don’t mean all CAR T products are dangerous, Konig says. That’s because many different designs and manufacturing processes exist, and scientists can’t always predict what will make one product toxic and another safe. Kyverna’s miv-cel, for instance, has been consistently well-tolerated in the more than 100 patients treated, a spokesperson for the company says.

CAR T cell therapies are transformative, Konig says. “They’re going to change the field — but there are risks.”

Another risk is that the once-vanquished disease will return. Doctors don’t know all the genetic and environmental conditions that trigger autoimmune disease. Some researchers wonder whether vulnerable individuals remain vulnerable, even after treatment. But because it often takes multiple insults for someone to tip into a state of disease, Scherlinger says, CAR T cell therapy could buy patients lots of time.

After 10 days in the hospital for her treatment, Montgomery-Haga returned to recover at home. In the following months, she started to notice some changes. It was easier getting around the house, for one. And she felt her balance and stability improving. She can now kneel down to garden and clean without the days of recovery it used to require. “I can vacuum my house,” she says. “It seems so little, but now I can vacuum!”

Montgomery-Haga can even sit down “crisscross applesauce,” she says. “I couldn’t do that for, like, 10 years.”

Four months after the treatment, Piquet’s team tested Montgomery-Haga’s walking ability. In a hallway at University of Colorado Anschutz’s research building, the team marked out 25 feet with little orange cones. At one end stood Montgomery-Haga. At the other, Piquet watched behind a video camera. This time, Montgomery-Haga’s gait was smooth and her arms were down, not hovering out into the air to catch herself if she fell. “You could see that she was walking with a lot more confidence,” Piquet says.

The team saw similar results in other SPS patients. Some who had difficulty walking before treatment now wanted to run, Piquet says. “It’s incredible to just see those patients speeding down the hallway.” She remembers smiles, high fives and a sense of celebration in the air. Eight of 12 people who had used a walking aid for the initial test didn’t need one for the follow-up, Piquet reported at the American Academy of Neurology meeting in April.

In September, Kyverna reported the trial’s one-year results: Patients maintained mobility improvements, with a third completing their 25-foot walk about as fast as a healthy adult. Additionally, nearly all patients no longer rely on immuno-suppressive drugs to tamp down harmful immune activity. That’s good news because such drugs can bring all sorts of nasty side effects, like vomiting, bleeding and infections.

The results with SPS are “really something to celebrate,” June says. But only time and many more treatments will reveal how safe CAR T really is, he says.

That’s a concern for Zier, who says she wouldn’t feel comfortable trying the therapy at this point. Zier doesn’t know how she’d respond. She’s a single mother with two kids; even a rare side effect could disrupt her life in a way she may not be able to manage. And these days, she’s managing as well as she can. Zier is the founder and CEO of The Stiff Person Syndrome Research Foundation, which supports research for SPS treatments.

Kyverna is in the process of submitting miv-cel for FDA approval. The company is seeking priority review and hopes the treatment might be available to patients in 2027. If SPS is the first autoimmune disease to have an FDA-approved CAR T cell therapy, myasthenia gravis could be the second, says Warner Biddle, Kyverna’s CEO. What’s more, miv-cel also appeared to improve symptoms in six patients with severe cases of rheumatoid arthritis, researchers reported in August in Nature Medicine.

As for what’s next, the door is wide open. Some scientists are working to make off-the-shelf forms of CAR T that wouldn’t require personalized engineering. Others are trying to reprogram T cells directly inside people’s bodies, rather than in the lab. Still others are trying to make T cells that target only the B cells responsible for disease. In the future, doctors might use such drugs to help patients avoid the cumbersome treatment process that Montgomery-Haga endured.

Montgomery-Haga’s own future looks far different than what she had imagined a few years ago. In 2026, she took a trip to Washington, D.C., to see the monuments. Now she’d like to visit Paris, Scotland and Ireland, too.

For so many years, Montgomery-Haga has looked at trips and activities — and even household chores — that most people take for granted, thinking, “Man, I wish I could do that,” she says. “Now I can.”

#4214. Body doubling: what it is, how it works, and tips to try

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

If you’ve ever opened your laptop to begin your workday from home or checked your mountain of a to-do list and felt zero motivation to tackle it, you’re in good company. On days that you just can’t find the energy or focus to get started, it can help to have a little boost of accountability.

Body doubling, a phrase that means working alongside another person, has gained popularity over the years and is especially popular among people with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Body doubling (either in person or virtually) is said to help motivate people to stay focused and accountable for the tasks at hand. And while it isn’t a formal treatment or backed by robust clinical research, plenty of people have spoken to its effectiveness.

So, what does body doubling involve, and why do so many people swear by it? More importantly, will it work for you? Pull up a seat and let’s explore what you need to know about the technique, plus a few simple ways to try it out for yourself.

Body doubling is the practice of working alongside another person to stay focused and complete a task, either in the same space or virtually. According to the ADDA, the term “body doubling” was coined in the late 1990s by ADHD coach Linda Anderson.

The rationale behind body doubling is that having someone else present and engaged in their own task can make it easier to start and stay on track with yours. To be clear, there’s no coaching or collaboration involved. You’re just working next to each other, because a shared presence can create a sense of structure and accountability.

If you sit at your kitchen table while your roommate does homework, join a silent Zoom session where everyone works on their own projects, or even fold laundry while a friend cleans their space on FaceTime, those are all examples of body doubling.

Many people feel that body doubling helps them break through procrastination and maintain momentum,  especially during overwhelming tasks.

Getting started: Sometimes the hardest part is just beginning. Having someone else nearby can help create a sense of gentle urgency.

Staying on task: When another person is focused, it can be easier for you to stay engaged with your own work.

Feeling less isolated: This practice can reduce the emotional friction that builds when you’re feeling alone in your head.

Creating structure: Knowing someone will be present at a specific time gives your day a natural rhythm. Plus, it can add a layer of accountability.

This technique has gained popularity within the ADHD community. While it’s important to note that this isn’t a clinically recommended treatment—and there’s limited research to support it—many people with ADHD do say that it helps them function more consistently. The idea is that having someone nearby provides external structure and co-regulation.

When your executive functioning is impaired, simple tasks can feel overwhelming. But body doubling creates a soft sense of accountability, which can make it easier to begin and stay engaged in tasks.

With that said, some people find it distracting or feel pressure to “perform,” especially if their partner’s energy doesn’t match theirs. Additionally, if your body double doesn’t understand ADHD, their presence can sometimes feel more frustrating than supportive.

If you’re navigating ADHD, body doubling could be another great tool to add to your toolkit. But only if it feels natural to you.

#3827. “Hydrofeminism” and leaky saints feature in Everything Moist Seems Alive installation

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Photography by Hanneke Wetzer and Nikola Lamburov

The technical minimalism of artist Boris Acket and the maximalism of fake fashion house Maison the Faux come together in the installation Everything Moist Seems Alive at MU Hybrid Art House during Dutch Design Week.

The multidisciplinary creatives worked with MU to devise the large-scale installation, which fills a dark, cavernous room with kinetic water structures connected by clear pipes and running on a continuous cycle of containment and deluge.

They are overlooked by towering video installations of glistening, oozing humanoid figures called the “leaky saints”.

These are surrounded by marshy candlelit altars and wishing wells, and, several times a day, brought to life by ritualistic choral performances from the Dutch National Opera and Ballet.

Everything Moist Seems Alive was the product of two years of discussion between the creatives on the topic of water, during which they were particularly drawn to cultural theorist Astrida Neimanis’ concept of “hydrofeminism”.

In her 2012 essay Hydrofeminism: Or, On Becoming a Body of Water, Neimanis considered the philosophical, ecological and sociological implications of conceptualising one’s human body primarily as water.

“In this hydrofeminist approach in the sense, we are all bodies of water,” said MU director Angelique Spaninks. “The world is made out of water, and so are we, for at least 60 per cent of our body.”

“Water is like: we need it. We tend to hide it. We want to control it. We use it as something that we need instead of thinking that we are part of it,” she continued. “And all these ideas have culminated into this total installation.”

The leaky saints serve as the most overt visual embodiment of Neimanis’ theory, dripping with shimmering fluid from their eyes, pores and other orifices.

“We lactate. We sweat. We have blood,” Spaninks summarises.

Greek myths and their powerful rivers also influenced the design of Everything Moist Seems Alive, which is broken up into five water structures.

Dubbed “water bodies” by the creatives, they each represent a different form of or interaction with water. There’s The Well, The Womb, The Whirl, The Wake and The Waterfall.

The Womb is a large circular tub in the centre of the space, and represents how everything “gets born in water, some way or another”, said Spaninks – an image that is evocatively conjured in the performance.

The Well is a series of three pumps that draws water from the tub and guides it into the rest of the system, in preparation for a climax that sees the usually dripping Waterfall, representing a dam, turn into a torrent.

The Whirl holds a white cloth that spins and dances with the movement of water, and the Wake slowly drips water onto a hot plate, emitting bursts of steam.

The pipes connecting the structures nod to the sanitation systems that underlie our daily interactions with water, while the gothic 3D-printed metallic ornaments on the pipes were inspired by Paris’ gargoyles, traditionally used to redirect rainwater flow away from buildings.

“We are big believers of ornamentation and the beauty of ‘a lot’ to give some sort of a reaction to mundane bareness,” Maison the Faux co-founder Joris Suk told Dezeen.

“What I like [in Everything Moist Feels Alive] is that it’s a collision of aesthetics. Studio Boris Acket is well known for its quite technical and stylish and minimalistic visual approach to things. We are very maximalist. So just bringing those two worlds together has created a lot of the aesthetics that you see here.”

Acket is best known for his mechanical fabric canopy Einder, which featured on the world tour of music producer Fred Again.

The Everything Moist Seems Alive installation is accompanied by a water bar at the entrance, where visitors can gather and sample three types of water – or choose to feed it to the installation.

The darkened water bar also features a suspended mixed-material sculpture by the artist Roni(a) Johansson that resembles abstracted flesh, with dozens of moist “egg sacs” setting the tone for the installation.

Maison the Faux was founded in 2012 by Suk and Tessa de Boer, both fashion design graduates who have gone on to build a cross-disciplinary practice involving performance art, fashion and costume design and set and interior design.

The conceit at the heart of their practice is that they are “a creative studio posing as a grand couture fashion house”, playing with the aesthetics of fashion and the boundaries between the real and the fake.
