#694. New evidence suggests spinosaurus spent most of its life underwater

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For millions of years, evolution helped animals make the transition from life in the ocean to life on land. But the journey did not always move in one direction. Many species eventually returned to the water, developing new adaptations that allowed them to thrive in aquatic environments.

Dozens of major groups of mammals and reptiles made this remarkable evolutionary reversal. Now, a new study from Yale University is shedding light on when and how these transitions occurred, as well as which ancient animals became fully aquatic and which continued to depend on land.

The findings were published in the journal Current Biology.

"These secondarily aquatic groups adapted in strikingly similar ways to their new aquatic home -- evolving flippers and a suite of other features that made them better swimmers," said lead author Caleb Gordon, who completed his doctorate at Yale's Graduate School of Arts and Sciences (GSAS) earlier this year and is now a postdoctoral researcher at the Florida Museum of Natural History. "As a result, they've become textbook examples of convergent evolution, which can tell us a lot about the processes driving and constraining adaptive change in response to similar environmental cues."

Using Ancient Fossils and AI to Reconstruct Evolution

To investigate these evolutionary changes, the researchers examined hundreds of fossil and modern specimens housed at the Yale Peabody Museum and dozens of other institutions worldwide. They collected extensive new data, including measurements (more than 11,000), photographs, and CT scans.

The team combined traditional paleontological techniques with machine learning models that account for evolutionary relationships. They also incorporated a statistical approach originally used by naval forces during World War II. Together, these methods offered a new way to investigate one of the most remarkable transitions in vertebrate evolution.

"Reconstructing the lives of extinct life forms in a scientifically rigorous way, as opposed to just telling stories, is a precise and delicate undertaking," said Bhart-Anjan Bhullar, senior author of the study. Bhullar is an associate professor of Earth and planetary sciences in Yale's Faculty of Arts and Sciences (FAS) and an associate curator of vertebrate paleontology and vertebrate zoology at the Peabody Museum.

"It requires a careful interweaving of data from modern organisms, of which our understanding is necessarily far deeper, and knowledge of these living organisms' genealogy relative to fossil forms," he said. "Caleb's work accomplishes all of this and more, and on a massive evolutionary scale."

For decades, scientists have disagreed about when particular groups of prehistoric animals became fully adapted to life underwater. The fossil record often provides incomplete or conflicting clues, making it difficult to determine whether an extinct species primarily lived on land, in water, or in both environments.

Some skeletal features may indicate an aquatic lifestyle, while others suggest that the same animal remained capable of moving and feeding on land. These contradictions have led researchers to different conclusions about how ancient species behaved.

"In these cases, paleontologists are often stuck, as different lines of evidence disagree about what the ancient animal was like," Gordon said.

To address this uncertainty, Gordon developed a method using relatively simple but effective machine learning models trained on living animals. By identifying relationships between skeletal proportions and known lifestyles, the models can estimate how extinct species used aquatic environments. They can also predict soft tissue adaptations, including swimming structures that rarely survive in fossils.

Spinosaurus May Have Spent Most of Its Life Underwater

One of the most striking findings concerns Spinosaurus, a giant prehistoric dinosaur whose relationship with water has generated considerable controversy.

Spinosaurus lived approximately 113 million to 94 million years ago in what is now northern Africa. Although scientists generally agree that it was associated with aquatic environments, exactly how it moved, hunted, and spent its time remains a subject of debate.

Some evidence points to an animal that frequently submerged itself and pursued prey underwater, much like seals or penguins. Other findings suggest a lifestyle closer to that of a modern heron, with Spinosaurus walking and searching for food along shorelines and in shallow water.

The Yale team's analysis provides new support for the more aquatic interpretation.

"Our results shed new light on how much time Spinosaurus spent submerged, which could support the underwater hunting view," Gordon said. "We confidently recovered highly aquatic habits for Spinosaurus, indicating that it spent the vast majority of its time submerged in the water."

Although the findings do not settle every question about how Spinosaurus hunted, they strengthen the case that this enormous predator spent much more of its life underwater than on land.

Ancient Marine Reptiles That Never Fully Left Land

The researchers reached a different conclusion about mesosaurs, small marine reptiles that lived between 290 million and 274 million years ago in regions now forming parts of South Africa and eastern South America.

Mesosaurs have long interested paleontologists because they gave birth to live young, a characteristic some researchers have interpreted as evidence of a fully aquatic existence. However, the new analysis suggests that their limbs were not adapted for a life spent entirely in water.

Instead, their skeletal proportions indicate that mesosaurs regularly moved between aquatic and terrestrial environments.

"They spent a lot of time on land, like a modern-day alligator or platypus does," Gordon said. "Mesosaurs didn't completely leave the land behind."

The findings also suggest a broader pattern among the earliest marine reptiles. According to the researchers, all Paleozoic marine reptiles (all marine reptiles around before the dinosaurs appeared) continued to return to land regularly. None appeared to have evolved a completely aquatic lifestyle, with even the most water adapted species remaining amphibious.

The researchers found that forelimb proportions were particularly useful for distinguishing these lifestyles. Using this information, their models could predict with more than 90% accuracy whether an animal possessed soft tissue flippers and whether it had highly or fully aquatic habits.

A World War II Technique Helps Solve an Evolutionary Mystery

An especially unusual part of the research involved adapting a statistical method developed during the 1940s.

"Caleb resurrected a statistical technique from a bygone era," said co-author Jacques Gauthier, a professor of Earth and planetary sciences at Yale and curator-in-charge for vertebrate paleontology at the Peabody Museum. "It was developed during WWII to estimate the probability that an approaching blip on the radar screen was an enemy plane. Caleb cleverly used it to get at whether or not an extinct animal had limbs modified for swimming."

By repurposing this approach, the researchers could assess the likelihood that an extinct species possessed specialized swimming adaptations, even when direct evidence of those features was missing.

The method could eventually help scientists investigate other major changes in evolutionary history. Potential applications include studying when human ancestors developed the ability to walk on two legs and how the dinosaur ancestors of birds evolved the ability to fly.

Because most prehistoric vertebrates are known primarily through their fossilized skeletons, reconstructing their behavior is one of paleontology's greatest challenges. Bones preserve valuable information, but they rarely provide a complete picture of how an animal lived.

Traditionally, scientists have interpreted particular skeletal features to make arguments about the lifestyles of extinct species. Those interpretations can differ significantly, especially when fossils are incomplete or several anatomical characteristics point toward different conclusions.

The new approach offers a more systematic way to evaluate that evidence, using patterns observed in living species to make testable predictions about animals that disappeared millions of years ago.

"Bones are all that is left to us of the vast majority of vertebrate animals that have lived on Earth," Bhullar said. "We want to know so very much about these creatures and the succession of lost worlds they inhabited, but we have so little on which to base our conclusions. From skeletal remains, people have tried to discern life habits, generally making an argument on the basis of one preferred set of features or another. Caleb's method removes the subjectivity from this process and allows the data to speak for themselves."

The study's additional co-authors are Lisa Freisem, a doctoral student at Yale's Graduate School of Arts and Sciences, and Christopher Griffin, an assistant professor of geosciences at Princeton University who previously worked as a postdoctoral fellow at Yale.

If you’ve got snakes in your garden then I don’t need to tell you that it’s not the snakes themselves that are the biggest problem, but the constant worry that you might be about to encounter one. Only a quarter of native snake species in the USA are venomous and most – like garter snakes and corn snakes – are helpful garden visitors, eating rodents as well as slugs and bugs. But even if you’re happy to tolerate them as part of a healthy garden ecosystem, it’s smart to set some boundaries and deter snakes from yard areas you regularly use.

October is the very best time of the year to make your yard inhospitable to snakes, if you don’t fancy your garden becoming their permanent winter home. As fall temperatures drop to around 60°F, reptiles will start scouting out a frost-free space to brumate (like hibernating, but not as deep), so now is the time to take action. By making these areas scarce in your garden, snakes will likely move out of your yard to find somewhere to stay. Snakes are also exceptionally hungry in October as they need to store body fat to survive months of winter brumation, so removing food sources will also drive them out of your garden.

And if you need any more motivation to keep snakes out of your garden this month, know that they frequently breed in the exact same space they brumate. A typical female produces 10-30 babies, so if you don’t want a heap more slithering around your garden next summer, here’s your 8-step plan that explains how to deter snakes this fall.

Snakes like cool, damp, dark and undisturbed areas so take a look around your garden to identify probable hiding spots. If you’ve got a large yard and like to share your yard with wildlife, then by all means leave shelter in unused corners. However, in areas of the garden you frequent, clear yard clutter, trim vegetation and bag up fallen leaves. If you have a firewood stack or compost pile, consider moving them further away from the house.

When you’re clearing an area that may already be providing cover for snakes, always use tools rather than your hands. Don’t think that even leather gauntlet gardening gloves will protect you, either, as the single layer of fabric is easily pierced by a snake fang. Gloves are a smart move, but you’ll need animal-handling gloves such as these from Amazon for any worthwhile protection.

Snakes hate spending time in open spaces where they’re visible from the sky and could become easy prey. Especially in October when snakes are highly mobile, keep all grass mown to a length of 3 inches or less and you’ll strongly deter snakes from slithering across it. Be sure to trim transitional zones too, where a mower can’t reach, as these can become favored travelling routes for snakes. A pair of shears with a 90° blade action such as these lightweight Hand Grass Shears from Walmart make short work of trimming lawn edges, or use cordless clippers like this Rechargeable Grass and Hedge Trimmer, also from Walmart.

Dense shrubs provide welcome cover for snakes but it’s an easy task to trim off lower branches to expose the ground and make them less inviting. You can also prune a shrub into the shape of a small tree. Use a pair of pruners – and do sharpen them first with a tool such as a Sharpal, available from Amazon, as cuts made with sharp blades heal much quicker – to cut unwanted branches flush with the main trunk. For bigger branches, a pruning saw like Fiskar’s Power Tooth Pruning Saw, available from The Home Depot, is a better bet.

We often mulch our garden beds in fall but a cosy, damp layer of pine straw or chunky bark chips offers snakes the ideal spot to regulate their body temperature. Use fine compost to mulch beds and borders instead, which snakes will find far less appealing. If you make your own compost then a rotary sifter such as the Mikiotto Soil Sifter, available from Lowe’s, makes short work of sieving the larger chunks out. Otherwise use a pre-made manure-free compost such as Back to the Roots Organic Compost, stocked at Walmart, as a mulch.

Bird feeders are a prime reason why snakes hang around in gardens. While they don’t eat the dropped seed itself, the reptiles are drawn to the plentiful supply of mice, voles and insects it attracts. And how many of us resume feeding garden birds in fall, just as snakes reach peak appetite?

Getting rid of this easy food source is a key part of the plan to keep snakes out of a garden. The solution is to switch to a waste-free bird food such as Kaytee No Mess Wild Bird Seed, available from Amazon. Hook a seed catcher like this hanging tray from Walmart to the base of hanging bird feeders too, to catch any discarded seed husks.

Fix any gaps that give snake access to a shed, summerhouse or your home. If you need a temporary closure then steel wool is the best way to create a sturdy, weatherproof but non-permanent barrier and bulk packs are available cheaply, such as this steel wool from Amazon. In October, snakes are highly mobile and need the heat of the sun to be mobile, so you’re far more likely to block them out of a hole than seal them in. Do your repairs in the middle of the day when the sun is at its warmest and you can be confident a snake is out basking, hunting or scouting for winter digs.

There are plenty of urban myths that promise mothballs, crushed eggshells and even marigolds deter snakes, but extensive research has proven otherwise. What a study by the USDA National Wildlife Research Centre does show to be an effective snake deterrent is essential oils that are rich in eugenol, namely cinnamon and clove. The research found that this compound irritates the snake’s scent receptor in its mouth and triggers its escape response. So if you're wondering how to keep snakes out of gaps that can't or shouldn't be blocked, here's your solution.

Add 15-20 drops of pure clove oil and the same of pure cinnamon oil to a quart of water in a spray bottle. Add a teaspoon of dish soap to help the oil bind with the water and shake well to mix. Spray this blend around ground vents, woodpiles and decking or porch voids, or anywhere else that gives access to a dark space that can’t be blocked.

Outdoors, oil disperses quickly so reapply after heavy rain or every 2-3 weeks. Once temperatures fall consistently below 50°F, it’s safe to stop as snakes will be holed up in their winter dens.

Snakes are terrified of birds of prey, and one of the easiest ways to deter snakes is to attract owls into your garden. Whether you DIY an owl box or opt for a ready-made design like the Aoibox Large Owl House from Lowe's, October is a prime time to invite owls into your yard as they're actively looking for nesting sites between September and March.

#714. Scientists discover how pancreatic cancer rewrites its DNA to escape treatment

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists have uncovered new details about how pancreatic cancer constantly changes its genetic makeup, allowing it to become more aggressive, spread throughout the body, and resist treatment. By examining nearly half a million individual cancer cells, researchers identified genetic changes that could reveal new ways to fight one of the deadliest forms of cancer.

The study, conducted by scientists at NYU Langone Health and its Perlmutter Cancer Center, focused on pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, a particularly difficult cancer to treat. Nearly 85 percent of patients die within five years of being diagnosed.

Although researchers have already linked more than half a dozen genes to the disease, much remains unknown about how pancreatic cancer develops and adapts. By examining tumor cells individually rather than studying them in large groups, the team gained a clearer understanding of how different populations of cancer cells change their DNA as the disease progresses.

How Pancreatic Cancer Evolves To Escape Treatment

The researchers discovered that pancreatic tumors contain a variety of distinct cell populations known as clones. Each clone consists of cancer cells with similar genetic characteristics, but their behavior can differ dramatically.

Some clones develop changes that help them withstand cancer treatments, while others become better equipped to invade surrounding tissues and spread to other parts of the body. These findings suggest that the cancer's ability to continually reorganize its genetic material plays a central role in both treatment resistance and metastasis.

Previous studies generally divided pancreatic cancer cells into two main categories: classical (less aggressive) and basal (more aggressive). However, because those studies examined large collections of cells together, scientists could not fully explain how cancer cells move from one category to the other.

The latest findings help explain this process. Genetic changes occurring within individual tumors allow cancer cells to shift between these two states, potentially making the disease more dangerous as it develops.

Scientists Identify a Gene That Could Make Pancreatic Cancer Less Aggressive

Published on October 13 in Nature Genetics, the study identified several genetic changes associated with pancreatic cancer progression. One particularly important finding involved MYC, a gene that showed heightened activity in some of the most aggressive cancer cell populations.

In additional laboratory experiments, the researchers blocked MYC activity in aggressive basal pancreatic cancer cells. Remarkably, the cells shifted back toward the less aggressive classical state.

The change also made the cancer cells more exposed to immune cells, suggesting they could become easier for the immune system to recognize and attack. Although further research is needed, the results point to MYC as a promising target for future pancreatic cancer treatments.

"Our study provides the most granular view to date of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, revealing how ongoing genetic reshaping drives tumor evolution over the course of disease," said study lead investigator Despoina Kalfakakou, PhD, a postdoctoral research fellow at NYU Grossman School of Medicine and Perlmutter Cancer Center. "These findings suggest that targeting the mechanisms underlying tumor evolution, including MYC, may help restrain the cancer's rapid progression."

"Our research also suggests that single-cell clonal analysis offers a better approach to understanding complex cancers, like pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, as it develops over time, by mapping how tumors evolve and adapt at each stage of disease progression," said study co-senior investigator Aristotelis Tsirigos, PhD, a professor in the Departments of Medicine and Pathology at NYU Grossman School of Medicine and a member of Perlmutter Cancer Center.

"More broadly, our study results highlight the critical role of clonal diversity, genetic plasticity, and interactions with the tumor's microenvironment in shaping human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma biology," said Dr. Tsirigos, who is also co-director of the Division of Precision Medicine and director of NYU Langone's Applied Bioinformatics Laboratories.

Why Pancreatic Cancer Can Evade the Immune System

The investigation also provided important clues about pancreatic cancer's ability to hide from the immune system, a characteristic that may help explain why the disease often responds poorly to immunotherapy.

Researchers discovered that certain cancer cell clones showed increased activity of the TIGIT gene, which is associated with proteins that help tumors escape immune detection.

At the same time, these clones exhibited reduced activity of the PD1 gene. This combination may help explain why immunotherapies designed to target PD1 have shown limited effectiveness against pancreatic cancer.

Understanding these differences between cancer cell populations could help scientists develop treatments that interfere with the specific mechanisms tumors use to avoid immune attack.

According to Dr. Tsirigos, clinical trials investigating drugs that inhibit MYC or TIGIT are already underway for pancreatic ductal carcinoma and other cancers. He said the latest findings underscore the need for larger studies examining the different states cancer cells can adopt and how treatments might be tailored to target them.

Nearly Half a Million Cancer Cells Reveal Hidden Tumor Diversity

To investigate how pancreatic cancer changes over time, the scientists examined gene activity (using transcriptomics) in 443,451 individual cells collected from 62 tumors belonging to 56 men and women diagnosed with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.

Six of these patients also provided tumor samples both before and after chemotherapy, allowing researchers to examine changes associated with treatment.

Unlike traditional approaches that measure changes in gene activity across large groups of cells, the new analysis examined individual cells. This provided a much more detailed picture of the different cancer cell populations living within the same tumor.

The results showed that individual tumors contained approximately two to 11 distinct clones, consisting of varying combinations of classical and basal cancer cells.

By tracing the genetic origins of these clones and comparing patterns of gene activity, the researchers connected differences in DNA copy numbers with the types of cells present in each tumor.

This approach allowed them to identify which genes, including MYC and TIGIT, were particularly active at different points during cancer progression.

Together, the findings provide a more detailed understanding of how pancreatic tumors evolve, resist treatment, and spread. They also suggest that interfering with the genetic mechanisms driving these changes could offer new opportunities to combat a disease that remains exceptionally difficult to treat.

#3655. Arup creates arrow-shaped viewpoint for Fehmarnbelt Tunnel project

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A zigzagging path tops the demountable Pilen viewpoint in Denmark, created by engineering firm Arup to give the public views of the construction of the vast Fehmarnbelt Tunnel.

Fehmarnbelt Tunnel is an 18-kilometre-long road-and-rail tunnel being built underwater between Rødbyhavn in Denmark and Puttgarden in Germany to improve links between continental Europe and Scandinavia.

Pilen, whose name means “the arrow” in Danish, offers views of its construction site to help the public gain an understanding of its scale.

According to Arup, its design is intended to symbolise travel, evoking an arrow or compass needle, and it points to the site where the tunnel emerges in Germany to orient users.

The ramped structure measures 217 metres in length, reflecting the size of each of the 89 individual concrete elements that will form the tunnel, which will be the longest of its kind.

“The arrow-shaped design points towards the location where the Fehmarnbelt tunnel emerges on the German side, acting as a large compass needle for visitors,” project architect Kristian Winther told Dezeen.

“At 217 metres long, the viewpoint also reflects the length of one of the tunnel elements,” Winther continued.

“We hope the viewpoint will create a closer connection between the tunnel project, the local community, and visitors, giving people an opportunity to experience the scale of the project more directly.”

Pilen was designed for contractors Femern, which is overseeing the tunnel’s construction and wanted to open up the construction site to the public.

The site was chosen for its proximity to the tunnel portal, maximising views of construction work, but also for the expansive views of the coastal landscape beyond.

Pilen’s structure is built from landfill timber and steel and designed to be demounted once the tunnel is complete.

To facilitate this, all the components, including the foundations, are engineered to be easily reused in other projects, “helping to give the materials a life beyond the viewpoint itself”, Winther said.

The main deck is formed from timber and landscaped with greenery and local vegetation, through which a fully accessible landscaped walkway zigzags as it rises 24 metres above sea level.

Fehmarnbelt tunnel is scheduled for completion in 2029. It is expected to reduce journey times between Hamburg and Copenhagen to 2.5 hours by train.

It’s the biggest lesson I’ve learned in more than 30 years of studying and hunting whitetail deer across North America: Don’t overthink it. You don’t need a grand plan to kill a buck, especially when the rut is on in November. Just go out and have fun, hunt every hour that you can, and use these time-proven pointers to your advantage. I’ve hunted the whitetail rut for decades all around the country for my show (BigDeerTV) and for various articles and writing assignments. Below are the best hunting tips I’ve learned and developed through the years.

1) Check the record books, and you’ll see that more giant bucks have been shot November 7 to 12 than any other days. Make sure to plan some all-day sits during this window.

2) A bunch of antler-mangled saplings or cedar branches are sign of an aggressive buck—the kind of beast you want to hunt because he’s apt to move in daylight.

3) “In many areas, the first scrapes pop up in old logging roads, slip along and scout those first.” – Jim Crumley, Virginia bowhunter and creator of Trebark camouflage.

4) “Big trees with scarring from years of rubbing are signposts. Our observations suggest older bucks deposit pheromones on these rubs, and that plays an important role in the dominance ladder of a herd. All deer, does and bucks, interact with signposts—they smell and rub them—but only mature bucks make them. They act as communal scent wicks and are located in areas with high deer traffic.” –Dr. Grant Woods, Missouri biologist and hunter

5) November 10 a few years ago, Illinois: The wind was blowing 20 miles per hour. “I wasn’t going out,” says bowhunter Gary Sulcer, “but I did.” He’d been in the woods only minutes when a doe tipped by with a bruiser on her heels. Gary’s arrow was true; the main-frame, 8-pointer scored 160. Lesson: You only have so many days to hunt the rut, go when you can, no matter the conditions.

6) “We didn’t have any trail camera photos of that buck, and I had never seen him,” says Gary Sulcer. “But I knew there was a big one in the area, he was rubbing trees as big around as your thigh.”

7) Killer bow setup: Tree stand on an edge where pines or cedars meet hardwoods. Bucks rub, scrape, and prowl for does on these break lines.

8) Best scrapes to watch: In brushy cover with doe trails nearby.

9) Watch active scrapes 50 to 75 yards from dense bedding cover. You might catch a mature buck milling around scrapes closest to his sanctuary at first or last light.

10) In hill country, look for scrapes dug 70 yards or so below a brushy hilltop. Bucks like to bed on a ridge or bench, watching for does and scent-checking scrapes below.

11) University of Georgia research: Multiple bucks hit one set of scrapes while other scrapes 100 yards away go dormant. Scout, move to, and hunt the hottest sign.

12) Set up too close to scrapes, and deer will see, smell, or sense your presence. Back off 100 yards or so, even when bowhunting, and watch for a buck circling into scrapes.

13) Wear rubber gloves and clip a mangled licking branch (which holds the forehead and saliva scent of several bucks and does) from atop an active scrape and wire it over a mock scrape near your stand.

14) Best rut stand #2: Downwind of the “X” where two trampled doe trails cross.

15) “Set tree stands between 17 and 20 feet. When a buck comes by at 20 to 30 yards, you’ll see plenty of lungs, the perfect shooting angle.” – Terry Drury, Midwest bowhunter and outdoor TV personality.

16) Best rut stand #3: Thirty yards back in the woods off the corner of a field, along a main trail. Does will come from several directions, converge in the corner, and make their way back into the timber. Bucks prowl a corner where they can see and scent-check many does.

17) In Indiana one November, bowhunter Brent Ireland got a single cam picture of a double-drop giant. He hung a stand on the ridge near his camera and killed the 199-inch buck two days later. Lesson: Get one image of a monster on his feet in daylight, move in, and hunt him.

18) Set trail cameras on different sets of scrapes, and you’ll get images of most of the bucks on your land, both the locals and the passers-through.

19) A doe that flits around with her tail erect is ready to breed or almost ready. A buck or bucks will be close.

20) Best rut stand #4: Hogback ridge flanked by crops and CRP, swamp or similar thick cover on the other side.

21) One humid Texas morning, we rattled up 15 bucks. Guide Clay told me, “With moisture thick in the air, horns have a high-pitched tinkling, bucks like that.”

22) Science out of Texas: Halloween to Nov. 8 and Thanksgiving to Dec. 8 are the best times to rattle up mature bucks.

23) Rattling bags and boxes work, but nothing beats the real thing. A solid set of 140-inch antlers are the bomb, with just the right tone and volume.

24) After rattling at a buck a good ways off, don’t let your guard down, even if he shows little interest. Sometimes a buck will walk 50 more yards, get curious and circle back to check you out.

25) Best time to rattle: Sunrise to 10 a.m. I don’t carry horns in the afternoon.

26) Hold one rattling antler tight to your body and bang and grind on it with the other antler to cut down on hand movement.

27) “If your arms and hands aren’t tired after a rattling sequence, you’re not doing it hard enough.” – Texas rattling expert Gary Roberson

28) Screw a rubber-coated hook ($2 at Home Depot) into a tree close to your stand. Hang rattling horns within easy reach.

29) A shooter trots by 100 yards out—try a quick burst of rattling to stop him.

30) Research from Texas shows ground rattlers don’t see half the bucks that come in. Elevate and rattle from high ground, or a tree stand or tripod, to spot more bucks.

31) Best-sounding grunt call for my money: Ridge Runner from Quaker Boy.

32) When a buck hears your rattles or grunts and is interested, he begins to hunt you. Set up with thick cover to your sides and behind you. Make a buck commit close as he searches for the “deer” he hears.

33) A stud chasing or tending a doe makes loud, weird sounds. Blow “urrrp, urrrp” or “urrrg, urrrg” on your grunter to mimic it.

34) Blowing a sequence of 10 to 12 grunts on your call is most realistic.

35) When a buck rolls in out of nowhere, don’t reach for your call. Bleat or grunt loudly with your voice—”baaahhh” or “eckkkkk”—he’ll stop.

36) Remember to draw your bow or aim your muzzleloader before you grunt or bleat to stop a buck. Obvious, but it’s easy to forget when a big-racked bruiser is close!

37) “The grunt-snort-wheeze is the most aggressive rut call. It can bring in a dominant deer, but it can also scare off young bucks.”—Georgia biologist Dr. Karl Miller

38) Good rattling/grunting setup: Wind in your face and rock bluff or stream at your back. A buck can’t circle downwind and bust you.

39) A deer’s eyes are oriented to pick up predator movement on or just below the horizon. You can get away with a bit more movement in a tree stand, but still, be careful.

40) Favorite muzzleloader setup: Sitting on a hillside, looking across to an adjacent hillside where I can cover 2 or 3 thickets. Bucks move all day from one cover to the next checking for does.

41) Best rut stand #5: A creek or shallow river crossing pocked with fresh tracks in brushy woods. The waterway dictates and funnels most all deer movement through the area.

42) Deep, splayed tracks 2.5 to 3.5 inches long in the mud were likely made by a buck, though the size of his rack is anybody’s guess.

43) Position a stand or blind so the sun rises or sets behind you. You’ll have an extra 10 minutes of shooting light at dawn and dusk. To hide better overall, understand deer vision and what colors deer can see.

44) “On land that has been managed for years, 5- and 6-year-old bucks can’t compete with the aggressive 3- and 4-year-olds anymore, or don’t want to. Some old giants travel across the fences and take up residence where there’s less competition for does. If that happens to be where you hunt, great.” –Iowa bowhunter Don Kisky

45) Best tip for hunting public land: Hang a tree stand in thick, secluded cover and sit in it all day. You might catch a buck rutting a doe on natural movement, or another hunter might inadvertently drive a 10-pointer to you. Either way, you win.

46) Multi-year data from a property in South Carolina showed that 95 percent of the 3.5-year-old and older bucks harvested were killed within 100 yards of a well-defined sanctuary, such as a pine stand, brushy clear-cut, or cedar thicket.

47) Around Nov. 10, break out the hot-doe scent like Tink’s #69.

48) Reach up and hang scent wicks 6 feet or higher so the wind and thermals swirl and carry the scent.

49) Texas study: Bucks split time equally at scrapes juiced with doe or buck urine. Might as well dump both into a real or mock scrape you’re watching.

50) “The weekend of Nov. 8 to 10 should be excellent this year, especially if it’s cool. I think your best shot to see a mature buck is 1 p.m. to 5 p.m. Hunt your best food source.” – Mark Drury, Iowa expert and TV personality

51) Douse a doe decoy with estrus scent and set her in a clearing where a buck can see her and approach comfortably from downwind.

52) In an abundance of caution to prevent the spread of chronic wasting disease, 12 states prohibit the use of natural deer urine and permit only synthetic scents. Check your regulations. Don’t worry, synthetics smell like deer and work fine.

53) Cut tarsal glands off a doe or buck your buddy shot, and hang them near your stand both as an attractant and cover scent. Black hocks from a buck that has rutted for weeks reek the best.

54) Did you know? You can store deer hocks in a bag in the freezer and use them again next November.

55) Watch a doe trail trodden to dirt or mud long enough, and a good buck will prowl by.

56) When a big rutting buck comes in, focus on what you need to do to execute a good shot, not how big the deer is. Read more about how to effectively shoot a big buck and avoid buck fever.

57) If 10 hours on stand are too much, sleep in, sneak to your spot at 9 a.m., and hunt out the rest of the day. A North Carolina State University study found that during a full moon, deer don’t move much in the morning, but the midday hours and early afternoon are good.

58) Good post for a mid- to late-November afternoon: 70 to 100 yards back in timber and along the thickest, nastiest ditch that leads out to crops.

59) Don’t waste precious rut-hunting time. If you sit in a spot for three days but don’t see much, move.

60) Dilemma: Pass or shoot the first buck that chases a doe past your stand because a bigger buck might be coming? Advice: If you’re happy with the first rack, take him.

61) Studies of GPS-collared deer from Maryland to Texas show that most all bucks make “doe excursions” out of their core area during peak rut. Some bucks might wander 5 to 8 miles or more, while others move 2 miles or less.

62) Oklahoma study: When bucks embark on doe excursions, they move in linear patterns. By traveling for miles in straight lines, they maximize their chances of contacting hot does. Set several stands along a linear creek bottom or ridge to intercept a buck.

63) When gun season opens, monitor where people park their trucks and enter the woods. Hunt a secluded spot that others overlook. It might be 2 miles deep in the woods, or 200 yards off a back road.

64) You’re still-hunting and a doe jumps out of a ditch. Find the nearest rest and ready your rifle, a buck is apt to roll up out of there next.

65) Wyoming, Nov. 18 a few years ago: It was 70 degrees and windy, and bucks rutted all day. I shot a 150-incher dogging a doe at 3 p.m. Lesson: Hunt hard, the pull of peak rut overrides the weather.

66) If you plan to hunt afternoons after work this season, do it Nov. 19 to 24. North Carolina State University researchers found that deer movement can be excellent the last hour of daylight during the last-quarter phase of the moon.

67) Post-rut hunting is better than you think! Research from Maryland shows that 20 to 40 percent of mature bucks continue to make doe excursions into December. Keep grinding until you get one.

68) One December in Kansas, my friend Jim Riley saw three bucks hassling a small doe. The next day he went back and saw five bucks dogging her, and he shot the biggest 8-pointer. Lesson: Find the last hot doe and stick with her.

69) Killer gun stand: Narrow weed strip between two blocks of woods. Bucks run the “point-to-point” for does.

70) First week of December, lay a doe-in-heat trail into your stand. It can actually work better now to bring in a buck because there are fewer receptive does misting the woods.

71) Studies of hunter behavior consistently show that most people hunt only a half-mile or so from a road, field or another easy area. For more elbow room and fun, hike deeper.

72) Aging a buck before peak rut: Check stomach girth; the older a buck gets, the bigger his belly gets. If his stomach sags lower than the bottom of his brisket, the buck is 4.5 years old or older.

72) Aging a buck after peak rut: Forget girth because a buck can lose 25 percent of his body weight chasing and breeding does. Glass a buck’s tarsal glands. As a buck matures, his hocks appear bigger and blacker.

74) A ditch on a ridge; a fence corner 20 yards back in the woods off a bean field; a tiny creek bend or crossing… Little “inner terrains” funnel the movements of bucks as they prowl from one doe unit to the next. Hang a stand downwind of one.

75) Expert bowhunter Kevin Robinson kills big deer in the suburbs of Missoula, Montana, but never in October. “Not hunting early for four or five weeks is hard to do, but I know how good the hunting will be as the rut comes on,” Kevin says. In November, Kevin sets tree stands low in mountain draws, often fairly close to houses, roads, and developments. “Bucks that live up in the hills start moving down and looking for does, and it’s game on.”

76) When bowhunting, always pack an extra release in your backpack (you’ll thank me later). – Alex Robinson

77) Deer are most likely to “jump the string” at 30 to 35 yards. Hold slightly lower when shooting deer at these ranges. —A.R.

78) Keep shooting your bow through the entire hunting season. During the late-season, when the temps are frigid and you’re wearing an extra layer or two of clothes, it makes sense to turn down your draw weight a bit. Just be sure to re-zero. —A.R.

79) If you’re going to bump deer while hanging a new set, follow Mike Hunsucker’s tip and use a four-wheeler and other loud equipment to make the disturbance. Deer will likely not blow out of the area when they hear loud equipment. —A.R.

80) Always wear a safety harness in your treestand. But even better, pair it with a lifeline so you are always strapped in. Most treestand accidents occur when hunters are getting into or out of the stand. —A.R.

81) Upgrade that old riflescope for better shooting at first and last light. There are now a ton of great and affordable riflescopes for deer hunters that offer much better performance than grandaddy’s old scope. —A.R.

82) Days with consistent wind (direction and speed) give the hunter the advantage. Shifting and variable winds give the deer the advantage. —A.R.

83) Don’t get hung up on “the best caliber for deer”. Bullet selection and shot placement are far more important than the cartridge you decide to shoot. —A.R.

84) When hunting public land, your first job is to get away from all other hunters. This could mean trekking deep into the backcountry or finding an overlooked spot near the road. Regardless, find the place where other hunters aren’t and you’ll find deer. —A.R.

85) If you hunt from a box blind, you should make sure to have a good shooting bag. Long range competition shooters use them religiously. You should too, because they will help make 100- and 200-yard shots easy. —A.R.

86) Once you hit that target buck, watch how he reacts. Carefully track his path of escape and his body language. Use an digital mapping app to mark the last place you saw him. Then, wait. That advice your grandpa gave you about waiting one hour before going to look for blood is still true today. —A.R.

87) If the blood trail doesn’t look good, consider calling in a tracking dog. This new, free app pairs hunters with tracking dog owners. —A.R.

88) During the lockdown phase of the rut, consider hunting where you can see into old overgrown fields. Deer feel protected in this kind of cover, but hunters can still see them moving through it. —A.R.

89) Don’t fight the heavy acorn crop (if there is one in your area). Just get in the woods with the deer and hunt the acorns. —A.R.

90) If you’re going to hang a stand in a bedding area, consider doing it at night when you know the deer are out feeding and chasing. Just be careful hanging that stand in the dark. —A.R.

91) Don’t get discouraged by “nocturnal bucks”. Expert deer hunters agree that even that “nocturnal buck” will be moving somewhere during daylight hours. You just have to find out where. —A.R.

92) A good way to hunt a nocturnal buck is to try to back track him from the area you got trail camera photos of him at night. Move cameras around to likely bedding areas (thick cover) until you are able to capture daytime photos of him (or see him from your stand during the day). —A.R.

93) Some of the best deer shooting advice came from my dad, a lifelong Northwoods hunter. It goes something like this: “Keep shooting until the buck is down or you can’t see him anymore.” —A.R.

94) Always keep your scope dialed to the lowest power during the rut. It’s possible that buck could come charging in at 25 yards, and if you have your scope dialed to 12X, it will be difficult to get on him quickly. —A.R.

95) Everyone likes to talk about accessing a stand without bumping deer, but few people actually do it. That’s because it takes real effort to figure out how (and when) to get into setups without spooking deer. But, it’s worth the effort. —A.R.

96) Cellular trail cameras are worth their weight in gold. Upgrade your cams if they are legal in your area, you might be surprised how affordable the new cell cams are. —A.R.

97) Listen carefully and that approaching buck will give himself away. Rutting bucks have a marching cadence that sounds different than a doe or squirrel. Once you hear it, turn toward that direction and get ready. —A.R.

98) Visualize the different buck-approach scenarios that could play out. Think about how you’ll react during each scenario. Think of specific details (like how you’ll move your feet). And listen to archery coach John Dudley on other ways to beat buck fever. —A.R.

99) Remember that big bucks like to search for does while walking into the wind. We all want to hunt downwind, but sometimes hunting a steady cross wind is more productive. —A.R.

100) Don’t overthink moon phases or barometric pressure or even weather. If you have a day to hunt during the heat of the rut, just go hunting. —A.R.

101) Keep deer hunting fun. When it seems like every one of your hunting buddies has tagged out and every social media influencer has killed a booner, just remember you get to keep hunting while they’re at home posting online. Every day in the woods is a good one (as long as you have the right perspective). —A.R.

#656. Low-dose aspirin linked to 70% lower dementia risk, but there's a catch

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A new analysis from Monash University suggests that low-dose aspirin may be linked to a dramatically lower risk of dementia in older adults with certain genetic characteristics. Researchers found that people with a specific genetic profile who took aspirin had a 70 percent lower relative risk of developing dementia, raising the possibility that genetic testing could eventually help identify people who might benefit from preventive treatments.

The findings, published in Alzheimer's & Dementia: The Journal of the Alzheimer's Association, were based on genetic data from more than 13,500 participants in the landmark ASPREE Trial (Aspirin in Reducing Events in the Elderly). Scientists examined whether differences in participants' genes could help explain why aspirin might affect dementia risk differently from one person to another.

Aspirin Linked to 70% Lower Dementia Risk in Certain People

The most striking results involved genes associated with platelet count. Platelets are small components of the blood that help form clots, and researchers wanted to determine whether genetic variations related to platelet levels could influence aspirin's potential effects on dementia.

After assigning participants genetic scores associated with platelet count, the team compared dementia rates among those who received aspirin and those given a placebo.

Among people in the highest 20 percent of platelet count genetic scores, just 1 percent of aspirin users developed dementia, compared with 3.3 percent of participants taking a placebo. That difference translates to approximately a 70 percent lower relative risk of dementia among those receiving aspirin.

However, the apparent benefit was accompanied by a significant safety concern. In the same genetic group, 4.4 percent of aspirin users experienced major bleeding, compared with 2.1 percent of those receiving a placebo.

A Genetic Clue Could Change Dementia Prevention

Lead author Dr. Peter Fransquet, a Research Fellow at Monash's School of Public Health and Preventive Medicine, said the results suggest that a person's genetic makeup could eventually play an important role in determining which dementia prevention strategies are most appropriate.

"Previous trials found aspirin didn't prevent dementia when everyone was considered together," Dr. Fransquet said.

"Our findings suggest there may be more to the story.

"In people with this particular genetic profile, we saw substantially fewer cases of dementia among those taking aspirin.

"It raises the possibility that genetics could one day help us identify who may benefit from a preventive treatment, rather than taking a one-size-fits-all approach."

The possibility of more targeted prevention is especially important as dementia becomes increasingly common. Dementia Australia estimates that approximately 446,500 people in Australia are living with the condition in 2026. That number is expected to exceed one million by 2065.

Blood Platelets Reveal an Unexpected Dementia Connection

Previous research has connected dementia with platelet activation and aggregation, the processes through which platelets become active and stick together. However, scientists have had a less complete understanding of how a person's overall platelet count might relate to dementia.

The new findings point toward platelet biology as a potentially important area of investigation. Notably, the genetic association identified by researchers did not involve the well-established genetic risk factors typically linked to Alzheimer's disease and other forms of dementia.

"What's particularly interesting is that the signal wasn't linked to the established genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's disease and dementia," Dr. Fransquet said.

"Instead, it's pointing us towards platelet biology and gives us a new avenue to investigate."

Why More Research Is Needed Before Taking Aspirin

Although the findings suggest that aspirin could have different effects depending on a person's genes, researchers emphasize that the results must be confirmed before they can guide medical recommendations.

The next step is to investigate whether the association appears in other populations. Scientists would then need to conduct a clinical trial specifically involving people with the genetic profile identified in the analysis.

"We now need to confirm the finding in other studies and ultimately test it in a trial designed specifically for people with this genetic profile.

"People shouldn't start taking aspirin to prevent dementia on the basis of this study without consulting with their doctor, particularly given the increased risk of serious bleeding.

"If it holds up, the fact that aspirin is already cheap and widely available could make personalized dementia prevention a real possibility."

Scale insects look more like bumps than bugs, making them easy to miss. A careful inspection and timely treatment can prevent them from spreading indoors.

Almost every year in my house, when the porch plants come inside in October, scale insects often come in with them. Adults don't move. Each one sits on a stem or the back of a leaf under a hard or waxy cover, and most people mistake them for rough spots on the bark until the leaves start to yellow.

The best time for controlling scale insects is before the pots come inside. Outdoors, cold nights slow scale down, and lady beetles and tiny wasps eat a share of it. Indoors, the pests have nothing to stop them. They just keep breeding through the winter on whatever plant they came in on.

An adult's waxy shell keeps most sprays from reaching it, making scale harder to get rid of than aphids or spider mites. Newly hatched crawlers have no shell yet. They're the stage sprays work best on, and new ones keep hatching, so treatments have to be repeated.

All scale insects feed through a thin, straw-like mouthpart sunk into a stem or leaf. Brown soft scale (Coccus hesperidum) is rounded or slightly domed, and its covering is part of its body. Armored types such as fern scale (Pinnaspis aspidistrae) are smaller and flatter. Pry one up with a fingernail and the cover usually comes away by itself, leaving the insect behind.

Only the young get around. Crawlers are about the size of a speck of dust, usually yellow or orange, and they spend a day or two wandering before they settle down to feed. Indoors, they cross from pot to pot wherever leaves touch and ride along on hands and pruners. There isn't one hatch to wait out – new ones keep turning up all winter.

Look for small bumps on stems, along leaf veins, and on the undersides of leaves. They can be brown, tan, white, or black. Black scale (Saissetia oleae) is dark, while oleander scale (Aspidiotus nerii) has a pale, nearly round cover. Use a magnifying glass to check plants properly; Carson's TriView folding loupe, available on Amazon, magnifies up to 15x.

Soft scale leaves sticky honeydew on the leaves and on the table under the pot. Black sooty mold, a group of fungi that includes Capnodium species, often grows on it. Badly infested plants show yellowing leaves, leaf drop, and stunted growth. Dead scale can stay stuck for months – press a bump with a toothpick; if it leaves a wet smear, it's alive.

October's moon reaches 100% illumination at 12:12 a.m. EDT (0412 GMT) on Oct. 26, as the lunar disk shines opposite the sun in Earth's sky. In the 17th and 18th centuries, the fear of vampires reached extraordinarily heights. People believed corpses could reanimate and wander around, terrorizing villages and spreading disease. They went to extreme lengths to prevent a vampire from rising. Some chopped the deceased person's head off, others placed sickles across their necks. Many were placed face down in their graves. In one grave in Poland, the remains of a boy were found with an iron padlock around his foot.

In this excerpt from "Killing the Dead: Vampire Epidemics from Mesopotamia to the New World" (Princeton University Press, 2025), author and historian John Blair reveals the impact the printed press had on the spread of vampiric lore — and the grizzly purported practice of ingesting the blood and organs of a vampire to prevent other people becoming one.

With the invention of printing, the circulation of European stories about restless corpses moved up several gears. Occasional early references were followed by a clutch of theological and philosophical discussions during c. 1580-1610, and then — from the 1670s — a steadily growing volume of printed academic and historical debate and commentary, reaching a climax in 1732.

Another landmark came in 1746 when the Benedictine scholar Dom Augustin Calmet published his survey of supernatural beings, which was quickly reissued in several editions, including an English one of 1759. This book's section on vampires was the most influential academic work on the subject ever published. Its straightforwardly evidence-based approach was a breath of fresh air after a long series of increasingly derivative works.

It achieved its remarkable popularity because it entered the market at the right point: saturated with so much fragmented and repetitive discussion of "vampires" (still a relatively new word), the reading public wanted a textbook. Calmet inspired a huge progeny of followers and popularisers, bringing the stories to a much wider readership; a good example of the genre is the Histoire des vampires et des spectres malfaisants [History of Vampires and Malevolent Specters] of 1820, a pocketbook that re-packages Calmet for the mass market.

Calmet's book remains the ultimate source of much modern vampire literature in Europe and America, both fi­ctional and supposedly historical, and was transformative in bringing the topic to a wider reading public. There and elsewhere, stories were now frozen on the printed page, where later generations could read them. But they were stories still: not necessarily more reliable, and by no means stable in transmission.

The tangled story of consuming blood from vampire corpses starts in a different and very unexpected place.

The complexity of interactions between learned discourse and popular practice is illustrated by one particularly bizarre and repellant (not to say dangerous) strand in the belief-system. This is the practice of ingesting blood or organs from a supposed vampire corpse, or ashes derived from burning them, as a remedy for vampire-induced illness.

Although not wholly dissimilar from the idea that victims will recover as they watch the smoke rise from burning vampire organs — already known in 11th-century England — its shocking physicality sets it apart. Did people in pre-modern Europe really do this?

A complicating factor is the medicinal use of human blood and bodyparts, in accordance with the theories of the physician, philosopher, and alchemist "Paracelsus" (Theophrastus von Hohenheim, c. 1493–1541). A brilliant experimenter, Paracelsus believed that substances healed through occult 'influences' or 'spirits,' the most powerful being those derived from what they were meant to cure: the human body.

The fact that medicine now rejects these hypotheses should not make us view them patronizingly: in the early modern period, ingesting or anointing with substances from Egyptian mummies or the blood of executed criminals seemed as rational as doing the same with any other substance. These Paracelsian ideas entered popular practice: at a beheading in Vienna in 1668/9, a man ran forward and caught the spouting blood to cure his epilepsy.

An illustration of an execution of a vampire.

The tangled story of consuming blood from vampire corpses starts in a different and very unexpected place. In 1687, a major ethnographical account of parts of West Africa by the Capuchin missionary Giovanni Antonio Cavazzi was published in Bologna. In it, Cavazzi describes magical and medical practices among the Mbundu of Angola, including a method of obtaining vital curative fluid from undecayed corpses:

When they are in a fresh and intact state when unearthed, their heads are cut from their bodies, from which it is said that blood flows out (which may happen through some illusion or deceit). It is collected and made into poultices for the sick, or mixed into their food, which infallibly guarantees health. Thereafter the dead person, having lost his strength, will no longer be able to harass them.

From Angola to Paris: in May 1693, a story appeared in the society magazine Mercure Galant. The author was the courtier and polymath Pierre des Noyers (1607–93), who spent several years in Poland in the entourage of Queen Louise-Marie. Much earlier, in 1639, he had written to a friend about the being known as upior (Ruthenian) or striga (Polish): born with teeth; eating its shroud and then its hands and arms in the coffi­n; causing successive deaths of family members; and extinguished by decapitation and release of pent-up blood.

This story is rather highly coloured (eating the hands and arms is unusual), but it does probably originate in genuine oral accounts of the shroud-chewer belief.

Very different is des Noyers’s account from 1693. Now the striges and upierz have become corpse-hijacking demons: 'It is said that the demon takes this blood from the body of a living person, or from certain animals, and carries it into a dead body; for it is alleged that the demon goes out from this corpse during a certain period, between noon and midnight, after which it returns and stores there the blood that it has gathered.'

They denied cutting out the heart, mixing the blood into flour, and baking cakes with it, but the judges were sceptical and made them swear an oath.

The blood comes out of the mouth, nose, and ears of the corpse, the corpse swims in blood, and it starts to eat its shroud. The demon especially troubles former associates and relatives of the dead 'host,' appearing to them in the guise of parents or friends, and does not leave until all the family members have died in succession. The remedy is to exhume the corpse, which is found soft, flexible, bloated, and red:

When they are found in that state, having the likeness of those who had appeared in dreams, their heads are cut off and their hearts are opened, and a quantity of blood flows out. It is collected and kneaded into flour, which is made into bread, which is a certain remedy to guarantee against such a terrible affliction. After the heads [of the dead people] have been cut off, those whom the Spirit torments at night are no longer troubled by it, and are well thereafter.

This narrative has problems. The idea of the demon using the corpse as a safe-deposit for its hoard of blood seems to be unique; so, probably, is the time-slot between noon and midnight. These read like clerical rationalizations, perhaps fed to des Noyers by some Polish priest and overlaid on his earlier, more authentic observations. But more fundamentally, it seems obvious that des Noyers’s account of Poland shamelessly re-works Cavazzi's account of Angola, published eight years previously.

It might not be quite so simple. An earlier common source, still unidenti­fied, remains possible. The descriptions of mixing blood into food and poultices look remarkably close to Paracelsian ideas, extended from ordinary corpses to undead ones: was even Cavazzi's account distorted by his own medical learning? But in any case des Noyers’s account is a piece of unprincipled plagiarism, worthless as evidence for Polish folk-belief.

Even so, it has bounced down through the echo-chambers of vampire literature ever since. The following year, a lawyer called Marigner built on the narrative — which he took at face value — with long philosophical musings on the nature of vampires. Inevitably, in 1746, it was republished in Calmet's book, which made it permanently available to vampire-aware readers.

While otherwise faithful to des Noyers, Calmet's version irons out the motif of demonic corpse-possession by calling this predator a vampire 'or a demon in its form.' This process of moulding to a more familiar template culminates in a recent version that presents the story as straightforward ethnographic reporting, omitting the demonic motif completely.

So far, this has just been a morality tale of plagiarism and uncritical copying. But in 1713, at Dąbrówka (now western Ukraine), some villagers were tried in the Uniate episcopal court for exhuming a woman's corpse and mutilating it in inventive ways. They admitted to the exhumation (because the dead woman had been walking around and attacking people), and to drinking the corpse's blood mixed with vodka. They denied cutting out the heart, mixing the blood into flour, and baking cakes with it, but the judges were sceptical and made them swear an oath.

This will sound very familiar to readers of early modern witch trials, where suspects were regularly bullied into admitting bizarre fantasies formulated by the persecutors. In this case, it seems clear that at least the idea of the blood-cakes was suggested by the judges; the same could be true of the blood-drinking, even though the accused were induced to confess that. It is distinctly possible, not to say likely, that an ordinary corpse-killing was embellished with luridly fi­ctitious detail by judges who knew des Noyers's report from twenty years earlier.

At nearly 10 feet (3 meters) tall and around 500 pounds (230 kilograms), Gigantopithecus was the largest primate that ever lived.

Foraging across the dense tropical forests of Southeast Asia between 2 million and 200,000 years ago, the giant ape may have shared the habitat with new arrivals — hominins such as Homo erectus. (Hominins are members of the group that includes modern humans, their ancestors and closely related species, after the evolutionary split from great apes.)

Despite their immense size Gigantopithecus mainly consumed plants and is closely related to modern-day orangutans.

So what would have happened when Gigantopithecus encountered these human ancestors?

Experts suggested a variety of possible scenarios, including that Gigantopithecus and human ancestors didn't encounter each other at all.

It's "very difficult to say with any certainty, as we have no direct evidence documenting interactions between Gigantopithecus and human ancestors," Renaud Joannes-Boyau, director of higher-degree research training at Southern Cross University in Australia and an archaeologist who has conducted extensive research into human evolution, told Live Science in an email.

"That said, if we look at the broader pattern of human evolution, our lineage [Homo] has consistently behaved as an apex predator," Joannes-Boyau said. "Humans and our ancestors have shown a remarkable tendency to explore, exploit and, at times, eliminate large animals that shared their environment. I think it is reasonable to imagine that those interactions may have involved a mixture of indifference, curiosity and violence, depending on the circumstances."

Friedemann Schrenk, a professor of paleobiology at Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz in Germany, thinks Gigantopithecus would have likely tried to avoid humans. "Humans were and are the most aggressive primates ever on earth using tools and weapons and were probably encroaching on Gigantopithecus habitats," he told Live Science in an email. Homo erectus made use of hand axes and possibly hafted spears.

Schrenk envisions the hypothetical interactions as similar to those between modern-day humans and orangutans, which has resulted in humans encroaching on orangutan habitats and the apes' populations declining. "After all, Gigantopithecus was a gigantic orangutan," he said, noting that if there was hostility in a direct encounter, early humans would have been the aggressors.

Yet it's also possible that Gigantopithecus and hominins lived in different environments in Southeast Asia, which would have minimized the number of interactions they had, said Hervé Bocherens, a professor of biogeology at the University of Tübingen in Germany.

Fossilized Gigantopithecus jawbones suggest that the giant ape's diet was plant-based.

The "carbon stable isotopic ratios measured on some teeth of both taxa suggest a different habitat, closed forests for Gigantopithecus and open savannah for Homo erectus," Bocherens told Live Science in an email. However, because of limited data, "we cannot exclude that some hominins were also present in more forested environments," he added. Grinding patterns in their teeth suggest that Gigantopithecus' diet was essentially plant-based and may have included bamboo.

In a 2024 study, scientists investigated the demise of Gigantopithecus and found no evidence that hominins contributed to Gigantopithecus' extinction. Instead, they determined that the main cause was environmental change that resulted in a reduction in the forested environment where Gigantopithecus lived and foraged. In fact, hominins and Gigantopithecus may not have encountered each other at all, Yingqi Zhang, a researcher at the Chinese Academy of Sciences and first author of the paper, told Live Science in an email.

We can only imagine what hominins would have thought when they glimpsed these gigantic creatures. It’s not clear how hominins and Gigantopithecus would have interacted, if they ever did, said Frido Welker, a professor of biomolecular paleoanthropology at the University of Copenhagen. He thinks they would have avoided each other but they may have "been in awe when they did happen to run into each other."
