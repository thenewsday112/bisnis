Would it be practical if I said, “I want to find out the average marks of Class 12 students in the Non-Medical stream all over India”?

I mean, who is going to visit every school in India, across every state, every district, and every village? That is next to impossible. And even if we somehow managed to collect all that data, it would require an enormous amount of time, money, and effort, with plenty of opportunities for errors and inconsistencies along the way.

We can solve it using one of the most important concepts in statistics: Population vs. Sample.

As shown above, a subset of a population is known as a sample.

When the data is too large to collect or compute, we often use sample data instead. But there is one important condition: the sample should be representative of the population and should not introduce systematic bias. This means that we should not select only a particular group of people in a way that could easily alter the outcome.

For example, if I only consider students who have scored 90% or above, the average marks will naturally be very high. But if we consider students with all kinds of marks, ranging from low to high, the sample is more likely to represent the actual population, and therefore, the results are more likely to be closer to the true population value.

The size of the population is denoted by N, while the size of the sample is denoted by n.

Here, a question may arise — why, in a sample, are we dividing by (n-1) and not by (n)?

To clear up this confusion, we need to learn something important.

Did you know that the sum of deviations from the mean is always 0?

After expanding, we will get something like this, which means the sum of the deviations of each sample point from the mean:

(x₁ − x̄) + (x₂ − x̄) + ⋯ + (xₙ − x̄) = 0

Now, according to this, let’s notice something.

From the other two, I can easily say that the third one has to be (+2). That’s the only value that will make the sum equal to 0.

So, out of these 3 deviations, we can independently choose only 2. The last one is automatically determined by the other two.

Therefore, in general, if we have (n) deviations from the sample mean, only (n-1) of them can vary independently because their sum must be 0.

And this is where we get our first intuition for Degrees of Freedom.

When we calculate sample variance, we are using the sample mean instead of the true population mean. Because of this, dividing by (n) would systematically make our estimate of variance a little too small.

Dividing by (n-1) corrects this bias and gives us an unbiased estimate of the population variance.

And that’s how, unknowingly, you became familiar with the concept of Degrees of Freedom.

Now you might be wondering: why did we take such a long route through populations, samples, and degrees of freedom before even talking about skewness?

Because skewness and kurtosis are statistical measures, and to properly understand their formulas, we need to understand where population and sample formulas come from.

Before we move on to our main topic, I want us to learn one more important concept first, Moments.

For this blog, we will take a deep dive into the Third and Fourth Central Moments and see how, after standardization, they lead to measures of Skewness and Kurtosis.

As we can see above, I have mentioned it as Related to Skewness, not Skewness itself. And that perfectly leads us to an important question: Why are we standardizing it here?

We just need to standardize it a little bit so that the resulting measure becomes independent of the scale of the data.

Here, we divide the Third Central Moment by the cube of the Standard Deviation.

The Third Central Moment has a cubed unit, such as (cm³), (km³), etc. But we don’t want our skewness value to depend on the unit in which our data is measured.

So, by dividing it by the cube of the Standard Deviation, we cancel out the units and get a unitless (dimensionless) value.

Now, one more important thing to mention, the formulation we saw above is for Population data.

But in most real-world problems, we rarely have access to the entire population. We usually work with a Sample.

Therefore, we need to adjust the population formulation when calculating skewness from sample data.

This adjustment leads us to the Fisher–Pearson sample skewness coefficient.

g₁ = [(1/n) Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (xᵢ − x̄)³] / [(1/n) Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (xᵢ − x̄)²]^(3/2)

On looking at the formula for the first time, it might look scary, but it is actually quite simple.

It is basically the third sample central moment divided by the (3/2)-power of the second sample central moment.

- The Third Sample Central Moment tells us about the asymmetric behaviour of the data.

- The Second Sample Central Moment tells us about the spread or scale of the data.

So, by dividing the third moment by the (3/2) power of the second moment, we are essentially asking,

How much asymmetry is there relative to the scale of the data?

This standardization is important because we don’t want our skewness value to depend on the units or scale of the data.

But even this estimator has a limitation: it can be biased, particularly for small sample sizes.

Think about it this way, if we repeatedly take different samples from the same population and calculate the skewness of each sample, the values will fluctuate. If we take the average of these sample skewness estimates over many repeated samples, it may systematically differ from the actual population skewness.

We wouldn’t simply call this difference a mistake. When an estimator systematically tends to overestimate or underestimate the quantity we are trying to estimate, this systematic difference is called Bias.

So, we need a correction factor, let’s call it C(n).

This correction factor is derived from the expected behaviour of the sample skewness estimator under repeated sampling.

This leads us to the Adjusted Fisher–Pearson Skewness Coefficient.

The adjustment slightly changes the estimate to compensate for the systematic bias present in the unadjusted sample skewness.

And as the sample size becomes very large, the correction factor approaches (1), so:

In other words, for large samples, the difference between the adjusted and unadjusted skewness becomes very small.

Here, as we can see, the correction factor only depends on (n). But why not something else?

→ We don’t want to calculate a different correction based on whether the data is measured in ₹100, ₹10,000, heights, salaries, etc.

The problem we are correcting is primarily a finite-sample problem.

So, the correction should depend on the Sample Size rather than the actual values or units of the data.

Now, the last thing we need to discuss is the value of skewness we will get.

How will we classify whether our data is highly skewed or mildly skewed?

There isn’t a universal mathematical rule for this because the interpretation of skewness depends heavily on the context and the data.

- If Skewness ≈ 0 → the data is approximately symmetric.

- If Skewness is positive → the distribution is Right-Skewed.

- If Skewness is negative → the distribution is Left-Skewed.

- If |Skewness| is large → the distribution shows a stronger degree of asymmetry.

A skewness value of 1 might be perfectly acceptable in one context and problematic in another. There is no single cutoff that can tell us whether skewness is “good” or “bad” for every dataset.

But before getting there, I want us to learn one more important concept, Excess Kurtosis.

Before going further, another question may arise here:

As we wanted to know the direction in Skewness, we used the 3rd power, which preserves the sign of the deviation.

But here, we want deviations on both sides of the mean to contribute positively, while also giving much greater weight to large deviations. The 4th power does exactly that.

So, both positive and negative deviations contribute positively.

But that’s not the only reason we use the 4th power.

The fourth power also gives extreme values a disproportionately large weight.

This means that as an observation gets farther away from the mean, its contribution to the Fourth Central Moment increases very rapidly.

So, Kurtosis doesn’t just care that an observation is far away, the fourth power makes it care much more as the observation gets farther away.

Now, as we are clear about Excess Kurtosis, we are actually one step closer to our Sample Data formula for Kurtosis.

Here, (g₂) represents the sample excess kurtosis.

As we learned earlier, we obtain excess kurtosis by dividing the Fourth Central Moment by the square of the Second Central Moment and then subtracting 3, the kurtosis of a Normal Distribution.

But just like we saw with Skewness, this estimator has a small-sample bias.

Therefore, we apply a correction to obtain the Bias-Corrected Sample Excess Kurtosis, also known as Fisher’s correction.

G₂ = [(n − 1) / ((n − 2)(n − 3))] · [(n + 1)(m₄/m₂² − 3) + 6]

This is the Bias-Corrected Sample Excess Kurtosis (Fisher’s correction).

One last important thing to keep in mind before we move on.

As I mentioned before, people often associate Kurtosis with peakedness. But this is not always true and should not be the centre of its definition.

Two distributions can have similar peaks but very different tail behaviour.

Kurtosis is strongly related to tail behaviour and the influence of extreme observations, rather than simply being a measure of peak height.

And with that, I will end this section with a Memory Map for you all.

A distribution can have High Skewness but Low Kurtosis, or Low Skewness but High Kurtosis.

Because Skewness and Kurtosis measure two different aspects of a distribution:

- Skewness → tells us about the asymmetry and direction of the distribution.

- Kurtosis → tells us about the behaviour of extreme values and the tails.

So, a distribution can be almost perfectly symmetric while still having very heavy or very light tails.

A key thing to remember is that symmetry does not tell us anything about the behaviour of extreme values.

#2000. The Ten Commandments, classroom walls, and the establishment clause

The Supreme Court will soon consider whether to review the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit’s full panel decision upholding a Texas law requiring a poster of the Ten Commandments to hang in public school classrooms (as well as a somewhat similar case in Louisiana). The Supreme Court need not take this case, but it was rightly decided. The 5th Circuit used the correct “historical practices and understandings” test to analyze this establishment clause challenge and reached the right result that the Texas law is constitutional. I want to focus on one part of its ruling in particular: That a text with religious origins can over time develop an additional nonreligious message which public schools can acknowledge with passive displays in classrooms. Here, Texas’ decision to hang the Ten Commandments in its classrooms does not promote religion but acknowledges its foundational role in American law.

In its 2022 decision of Kennedy v. Bremerton School District, the Supreme Court announced a new test for the establishment clause. There, the majority discarded what is known as the “Lemon Test” (named for the 1971 decision of Lemon v. Kurtzman) to determine whether state action violates the establishment clause, which had asked courts to consider whether such action had a “secular purpose”; whether it had the “principal or primary effect” of “advanc[ing]” religion; and whether the action contributed to “excessive government entanglement” with religion. In Kennedy, the court replaced this convoluted test with a more originalist one that looked at “the historical practices and understandings” of what established churches actually did under law as governmental entities.

The 5th Circuit applied that new test in the challenge to S.B. 10, the Texas law enacted in June 2025, requiring public elementary and secondary schools to display in each classroom a “durable poster or framed copy of the Ten Commandments.” The display must be at least 16x20 inches, easily readable, displayed in a conspicuous place, and must only include the text of the Ten Commandments and nothing else. The schools can acquire these displays either by accepting compliant, privately donated displays, or purchasing compliant displays using district funds.

Shortly after its passage, numerous parents of varying religious backgrounds sued several Texas school districts claiming S.B. 10 violated the establishment clause and the free exercise clause of the First Amendment. In Nathan v. Alamo Heights Independent School District, the 5th Circuit, sitting en banc, upheld Texas’ law. Specifically, the 5th Circuit found that S.B. 10 did not violate the establishment clause because it punishes no one who rejects the Ten Commandments, requires no religious exercise or observance, and neither catechizes students on the Ten Commandments nor encourages them to adopt them. The 5th Circuit reasoned that, while “[p]laintiffs have sincere religious disagreement with its content . . . that does not transform the poster into a summons to prayer.”

In upholding the Texas law, the 5th Circuit considered the Supreme Court’s 1980 decision in Stone v. Graham, where the high court ruled unconstitutional a Kentucky law that required posting of the Ten Commandments in public schools. Because Stone relied on the Lemon test to rule against the Ten Commandments in the Kentucky classrooms, the 5th Circuit followed the Supreme Court's later directive in Kennedy that it was abandoning the Lemon test and its progeny. “With Lemon extracted, there is nothing left of Stone,” the 5th Circuit concluded.

The Nathan court’s opinion demonstrates how to adhere to the Supreme Court’s ruling in Kennedy, which asks courts to “reference … historical practices and understandings” in order to interpret the establishment clause so as to “faithfully reflect[] the understanding of the Founding Fathers.” As the 5th Circuit recognized, this requires asking “a question rooted in the past: does the law at issue resemble a founding-era religious establishment?”

When Congress and the state legislatures enacted the First Amendment’s establishment clause in 1791, people generally understood what an established church did given their presence in many of the states and European countries. At its core, the establishment clause forbade Congress (and later the states, with the passage of the 14th Amendment in 1868) from enacting laws that formally coerced people to participate in the activities of a particular church.

Those challenging the Texas law argue that the religious origins of the Ten Commandments mean it is unconstitutional to place them in a public school that impressionable youth are required by state law to attend, and doing so thus at least implicitly “coerces” these students into religious participation.

This argument has several flaws (as pointed out by the 5th Circuit), but I believe its most fundamental error is to assume that the Ten Commandments can only have one meaning and that meaning is always religious. Indeed, this flies in the face of the Supreme Court’s own statement that a religious symbol like the Ten Commandments can acquire over time an additional nonreligious meaning.

In the 2019 case of American Legion v. American Atheist Association (which was brought by the First Liberty Institute), the court considered whether Maryland violated the establishment clause by maintaining a memorial to deceased World War I veterans that was in the shape of a cross. Obviously, a cross signifies the crucifixion of Jesus Christ. Nevertheless, the court, in an opinion by Justice Samuel Alito, decided that the memorial could stand, as over time the cross had gained additional nonreligious meanings of marking where someone had died in battle so as to honor their ultimate sacrifice in war. The mothers who erected the cross did not do so to promote Christianity, but to honor those from Prince George’s County, Maryland, who died while serving in World War I.

To bolster its ruling, the Supreme Court pointed to the Ten Commandments as another example of a religious symbol that has acquired an additional nonreligious meaning over time:

As the court recognized, its very courtroom has a huge carved figure of Moses holding the Ten Commandments, with brass latticed screens on both sides of the courtroom filled with small imprints of the Ten Commandments. This is not because the Supreme Court desires to promote (or coerce others into adopting) the religious message of the Ten Commandments; rather, this is to acknowledge that the Ten Commandments serve as a foundation for the Anglo-American legal system.

Others have long echoed Alito’s words that the Ten Commandments “have historical significance as one of the foundations of our legal system.” John Quincy Adams wrote to his son that “[t]he law given from Sinai was a civil and municipal as well as a moral and religious code; it contained many statutes adapted to that time only, … but many others were of universal application—laws essential to the existence of men in society, and most of which have been enacted by every nation which ever professed any code of laws.” William Blackstone wrote in his Commentaries on the Laws of England that “all human laws” depend on “natural law and divine law.” Blackstone defined divine law to be precepts “found only in the holy scriptures.” He then gave as an example “the case of murder” which he said “is expressly forbidden by the divine.”

In 1997, the Senate and the House of Representatives passed concurrent resolutions that stated in part that “the Ten Commandments have had a significant impact on the development of the fundamental legal principles of Western Civilization.” And during oral argument in the American Legion case, even Monica Miller, the attorney for the American Humanist Association challenging the World War I memorial, conceded that “the Ten Commandments … [is] basically shorthand for law itself,” because of its “dual secular meaning.”

Those challenging the Ten Commandments posters in the classrooms assume that the religious content of the Ten Commandments renders them constitutionally toxic, while refusing to recognize their additional nonreligious message.

Nor is such a dual message uncommon in public schools. Think of a public school teaching a unit on the Civil War. A teacher hangs on the wall Abraham Lincoln’s famous words, “a house divided against itself cannot stand.” But wait! Lincoln was quoting the words of Jesus from Mark 3:25. Also during that Civil War unit, say the teacher hangs the lyrics of the Battle Hymn of the Republic, adopted by Union supporters and opponents of slavery as their rallying song. It started as a Christian hymn: “Mine eyes have seen the glory of the coming of the Lord.” It would be constitutional overkill for a court to order a public school studying the Civil War to remove from the walls Lincoln’s quote and the Battle Hymn of the Republic just because they have religious origins.

Of course, that is not all. If religious materials in public schools inherently violate the establishment clause because it is irrelevant that they have an additional meaning, how could teachers ever have students study the anti-slavery speeches of Frederick Douglass, or Martin Luther King Jr.’s Letter from the Birmingham Jail, or view Renaissance art, or sing African-American spirituals or Handel’s Messiah? It would also be questionable for the school to hang posters on the classroom walls telling students to love their enemies, or to go the extra mile to help someone, or to do unto others as you would have them do unto you, all words expressed by Jesus in the Bible.

In the cross case, the Supreme Court gave another example of words that began with religious meaning and over time added a nonreligious meaning: the names of U.S. cities, like Bethlehem, Pennsylvania; Las Cruces, New Mexico; Providence, Rhode Island; Corpus Christi, Texas, etc. As the Supreme Court made clear, those city names do not violate the establishment clause – and it would be absurd to think otherwise. But under the establishment clause theory of those challenging the Ten Commandments law, it is hard to see how one could draw a line here and hang the name of their school district in classrooms without violating the First Amendment. Either words can have two meanings, or else the St. Paul (Minnesota) Public Schools better start sandblasting its name from the schoolhouse walls.

One common reaction might be that the Ten Commandments not only address universal moral principles but also certain tenets more specific to Judaism and Christianity, such as having no other gods before the one God or a prohibition of graven images of Him. But those commandments do not cancel out the fact that the Ten Commandments collectively have served as a foundation for the Western legal tradition. Texas also designed its law so that public schools cannot use the posters to proselytize or coerce students to believe them, or to punish those who do not. A Hindu student, for example, could read the Ten Commandments and conclude that her family lives in a nation where most people believe in monotheism, not the polytheism of her family, and also realize that the Founding Fathers’ religious beliefs helped form a Constitution that protects all Americans, including her. That Constitution forbids any religious tests for people seeking public office, prohibits a state church that compels belief or punishes dissent, and protects everyone’s right to freedom of speech, belief, and conscience.

All of this is to say, passive displays with religious origins have not historically been viewed as establishment clause violations, especially when the display has a second, nonreligious meaning. The same can be said about S.B. 10.

Much has been written about the current period of restraint facing the cultural sector. Public funding is under pressure, operating costs continue to rise and many cultural organisations are navigating an increasingly complex political environment, while being asked to do more with fewer resources. One consequence of this moment is that institutional programming can be shaped by financial constraints and fundraising imperatives, narrowing the space for experimental projects that require significant investments. Another is that the financial burden can be transferred onto artists themselves, who are asked to absorb production costs that institutions once carried. It is becoming more common for artists to subsidise aspects of their own projects simply to ensure that an exhibition or work can move forward. If this trajectory continues, the cultural ecosystem itself will become less able to sustain the kinds of work that challenge and expand public discourse.

This moment calls for new models of support to reinforce the ecosystem and allow artists and institutions to remain ambitious, even in periods of constraint. The question is not simply where additional funding might come from, but how support might be structured differently.

It is with this question that I started to consider other modes of philanthropy. I wanted to create a dedicated model that could provide sustained support for artists’ film and video, media that are notoriously difficult to fund and have struggled to attract sustained philanthropic attention. This grew from a belief in the intrinsic value of the arts and a recognition of a structural gap in how ambitious artistic production is supported. I wanted to work alongside institutions, not against or in spite of them. For me, a private foundation offered a way to complement the work of museums and public funders while recognising the distinct role each plays within a healthy ecosystem.

In 2022, I founded The Vega Foundation with a specific focus on artists’ film and video. This type of work occupies a unique position within contemporary art, and I have long felt connected to it. Moving image works often demand lengthy research periods, large crews, specialised equipment and expertise, and complex installations, all of which contribute to making these projects more challenging for public institutions to support on their own. Since establishing Vega, we have worked with more than 20 artists whose projects frequently require years of development and collaboration across multiple organisations. Our curatorial programme is shaped by the conviction that artists working in moving image are leading some of the most vital and exciting conversations happening in contemporary art. At Vega, we choose to support public institutions rather than work in isolation because we believe that existing arts infrastructure plays a vital role in carrying institutional history, providing balanced governance structures and sustaining long-term relationships with audiences.

We begin supporting artists and their most ambitious ideas long before a work reaches exhibition. We spend time in artists’ studios, discuss production challenges and help assemble networks of institutional and financial support. We work as commissioner, advocate and convener, helping artists navigate the period before institutional support is fully assembled. Sustaining ambitious artistic production depends not on any single organisation, but on collaborative networks capable of sharing resources, expertise and risk. But collaboration is not simply a financial strategy. Rather, it allows institutions with different strengths—collections, production expertise, commissioning experience, public programming, conservation—to contribute where they are most effective.

Foundations are particularly well positioned to respond to conditions of institutional restraint precisely because they operate differently from public institutions. They are often able to make earlier and quicker decisions, support an artist’s work before formal exhibition commitments are in place, and bring together institutional partners from varying contexts. This flexibility allows foundations to absorb a measure of the uncertainty that is inherent to ambitious artistic production, and early support can create the conditions for artistic risk-taking because it alleviates the stress of fundraising and allows the artist to focus on their work. There is no established handbook for this approach. There are many foundations that are developing creative models in real time, responding to changing conditions while trying to remain focused on artists’ needs. Foundations offer a distinct type of support and are uniquely positioned to be the catalysts to meet this moment.

The challenges facing the cultural sector are unlikely to disappear soon. But periods of uncertainty can also create opportunities to rethink how support is structured. If we want ambitious art to continue being made and seen, we will need deeper partnerships. The future of cultural production will not be sustained by any one type of institution alone. It will depend on a broader ecosystem, one in which different organisations contribute according to their strengths. Foundations have an important role to play within that ecosystem: not as substitutes for public support, but as catalysts that help artists imagine, produce and present work. Ultimately, the goal is to ensure that artists continue to have the freedom, resources and support to take risks and create work that expands how we understand the world around us.

On June 25th, 2007, English city Kingston upon Hull, located southeast of York, received 10 inches of rain in just a few hours. Within a day, a month’s worth of rain inundated the region, overwhelming the city’s pump-only drainage system. Some 10,000 properties were damaged, including every one of the region’s 98 schools, so it’s only fitting that, nearly 20 years later, scientists at the University of Hull found a cheap solution to prevent future damage as flooding becomes more and more of a concern in an era of climate change.

“For decades, sandbags have been portrayed as the go-to emergency response to flooding. But sandbags have practical limitations,” University of Hull’s Stuart McLelland, who worked on the study as part of the U.K. government’s FloodReady Review that called for practical flood prevention, writes for The Conversation. “They are heavy to transport, labour-intensive to deploy and difficult to dispose of after contact with contaminated floodwater. And of course people don’t keep a supply of sandbags ready to deploy.”

Duct tape, on the other hand, is exactly the opposite: It’s light, affordable, and likely sitting in your junk drawer right now. Similar to the floods that impacted the area two decades ago, the researchers focused on low-depth flooding with a maximum water level of roughly two feet. Testing sandbags (including synthetic versions) and various types of tape, including duct tape, aluminum tape, and flexible synthetic rubber, the researchers attached the materials to gaps around six doors made from commonly used materials (they also tested in-swing and out-swing doors). While flexible synthetic rubber performed the best by reducing water leakage by an incredible 99 percent, duct tape came in a close second at an impressive 97 to 98 percent. Sandbags, on the other hand, were outperformed by even the least effective taping strategy and provided only 70 percent leakage protection.

“Its effectiveness will depend on the condition of the door and frame, the surfaces to which the tape is applied, the quality of the seal and flood conditions,” McLelland writes. “For properties at significant flood risk, professionally assessed protection remains important.”

Of course, this protection only applies to low-depth flooding. For torrential disasters that have residents fleeing to their roofs for rescue, water leaking through the front door is the least of your worries. Intense floods will cause immense pressure on brickwork and masonry while water speeds through windowsills and drainage pipes.

Sandbags serve purposes beyond blocking a doorframe—they can also reinforce structures that redirect floodwater entirely. Still, as research from the University of Hull suggests, the cheapest and most easily deployed defense against an unexpected downpour may be the tried-and-true adhesive that’s served DIYers for decades: good old duct tape.

#2006. Chief Justice John Roberts’ long game on executive power

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

One of Chief Justice John Roberts’ most enduring commitments has been to expanding presidential power. In that regard, Roberts has long opposed Congress’ limits on the president’s authority to remove the officers heading the nation’s executive agencies. Indeed, as far back as 1983, Roberts, serving in the Reagan administration, wrote a memo to the White House counsel that “the time is ripe to reconsider the constitutional anomaly of independent agencies.”

This summer, Roberts was able to turn his long-held goal into reality. Writing for the majority in Trump v. Slaughter, the court ruled that the president may unilaterally remove those officers leading nearly all of the nation’s federal independent agencies. Much has been written on this decision. But much less has been said on how Roberts carefully, and then all at once, guided the court to its decision in Slaughter. This not only provides insight into Roberts’ core conservative commitments but how he has wielded his power on a fractured court.

Central to this tale is the chipping away, and eventual reversal, of Humphrey’s Executor v. United States, a unanimous Supreme Court decision from 1935. In Humphrey’s, the court upheld congressional restrictions on President Franklin Roosevelt’s ability to remove a Federal Trade Commission commissioner, William Humphrey, who had been appointed by FDR’s predecessor, President Herbert Hoover.

Although Congress had specified that an FTC commissioner could be removed only for “inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office,” Roosevelt did not invoke those grounds when firing Humphrey. Instead, as the president explained to him, “I do not feel that your mind and my mind go along together on either the policies or the administering of the Federal Trade Commission.” Humphrey sued for reinstatement and ultimately prevailed before the Supreme Court, although not before passing away in 1934 (hence, Humphrey’s executor).

Throughout the 20th century, Humphrey’s sat alongside Myers v. United States, decided in 1926, in which the Supreme Court rejected the claim by a postmaster that he could not be removed from his position by the president. Myers relied on an 1867 law known as the Tenure of Office Act that required the president to obtain Senate consent before removing certain executive officers. In rejecting Myers’ claim, the court held that the postmaster was a subordinate of the president and could be removed unilaterally at will. In an opinion written by Chief Justice (and former President) William Howard Taft, the court reasoned that the president has “the exclusive power of removal” so that the president can “take care that the laws [are] faithfully executed.”

The court in Humphrey’s distinguished between “purely executive” officials (such as the postmaster in Myers) and agency officials (such as FTC commissioners) authorized by Congress to perform “quasi-judicial” and “quasi-legislative” duties. The court described this distinction as between officials assisting the president in executing the laws, on the one hand, and officials responsible for carrying “into effect legislative policies” and performing “other specified duties as a legislative or as a judicial aid.” As Professor Nathaniel Wald Donahue has explained, “Progressive Era lawyers used the categories of ‘quasi-judicial’ and ‘quasi-legislative’ to refer to administrative bodies that were understood to be executive-branch auxiliaries to the judiciary and legislature.”

In 1988, the Supreme Court applied Humphrey’s in the case of Morrison v. Olson, when it upheld the “good cause” restriction on the attorney general’s authority to remove an independent counsel appointed “to investigate and, if appropriate, prosecute certain high ranking Government officials for violations of federal criminal laws.” The court not only again credited the “distinction between ‘purely executive’ officials and officials who exercise ‘quasi-legislative’ and ‘quasi-judicial’ powers,” but rejected the argument that the removal restriction should not apply to the independent counsel engaged in the “purely executive” task of prosecution. (The lone dissenter in Morrison was Justice Antonin Scalia, who lost that battle but now has decisively won the removal power war.)

Accordingly, when Roberts became chief justice in 2005, Humphrey’s was a valid, longstanding precedent. Over the course of 16 years, through a “removal trilogy,” Roberts made sure this was no longer the case.

2010’s Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board, the first case of the trilogy, involved the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board, established by Congress in 2002 to regulate the accounting industry. Essentially the PCAOB was supervised by the Securities and Exchange Commission. The SEC appointed PCAOB members and could remove members, but only for “good cause shown.” By a 5-4 vote, in an opinion written by the chief justice, the court held that this dual level of protection by Congress from removal for board members violated the constitutional separation of powers.

In reaching this conclusion, Roberts employed three strategies. First, he elevated certain presidential powers over those of Congress. In particular, Roberts invoked Article II’s vesting clause, or that “the executive Power shall be vested in a President of the United States of America,” and combined this with the executive’s constitutional prerogative to “take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed.” In the chief’s words, “the President cannot ‘take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed’ if he cannot oversee the faithfulness of the officers who execute them.”

In reaching this conclusion, Roberts downplayed (1) that Congress created the PCAOB and (2) that the separation-of-powers claim at issue involved a dispute between the law Congress wrote and the president’s claimed removal authority. Roberts acknowledged that “[n]o one doubts Congress’s power to create a vast and varied federal bureaucracy.” Yet he then immediately added, “[b]ut where, in all this, is the role for oversight by an elected President?” Throughout his opinion, Roberts elevated the president’s removal authority while at the same time minimizing Congress’ legislative authority to specifically “set the terms by which” agency heads hold their positions.

The chief justice’s second strategy in Free Enterprise Fund was to apply a formalistic understanding of the separation of powers even though prior cases, including Humphrey’s Executor and Morrison, took a more functionalist approach. A formalist believes there are clear boundaries between the different branches of government and insists upon adherence to those boundaries. For Roberts, the executive branch’s responsibility to execute the laws encompasses what occurs between Congress legislating pursuant to Article I and the judiciary deciding cases under Article III – a vast province under the president’s supervision.

Formalism contrasts with functionalism, which denies the imperative of keeping the branches separate and insists upon the importance of workability in evaluating the relationship between them. In his dissent in Free Enterprise Fund, Justice Stephen Breyer, a quintessential functionalist, pressed the need for governmental flexibility to allow Congress “to respond to future ‘exigencies’” in creating agencies and designing their structure.

The third leg of Roberts’ Free Enterprise Fund opinion was his selective reading of the history of the president’s removal power. While Article II explicitly allows the president to appoint executive officers, it is silent regarding the president’s authority to remove them. Accordingly, history must be consulted. As has been canvassed extensively, the historical record is complicated. And although Breyer made this point in dissent, Roberts downplayed the debate. While the “removal of executive officers was discussed extensively” at the First Congress, Roberts breezily concluded that the “view that ‘prevailed . . .’ was that the executive power included a power to oversee executive officers through removal.”

Hence the three prongs of Roberts’ approach to attacking Humphrey’s: elevating the president’s constitutional authority to take care that the laws are executed over Congress’ authority to legislate; employing a formalistic approach to resolve the separation of powers dispute; and insisting that the historical evidence provides a conclusive answer to the question presented.

Ten years after Free Enterprise Fund, in Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, the Supreme Court addressed a constitutional challenge to the structure of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, established by Congress in response to the financial crisis in 2008. As with the PCAOB, Congress limited removal of its leadership to cause to protect the agency from the intense political pressure attendant to financial regulation. In contrast to other independent agencies led by multiple members, such as the SEC or the Federal Communications Commission, the CFPB had a single director subject to removal by the president only for “inefficiency, neglect, or malfeasance.”

The court sustained the challenge by a 5-4 vote in an opinion written by Roberts that employed essentially the same reasoning and rhetoric as in Free Enterprise Fund. Indeed, the first case cited by Roberts was Free Enterprise Fund itself, for the importance of the president’s removal authority to ensure full accountability “for discharging his own responsibilities.”

The constitutional sin of Congress’ approach in structuring the CFPB was, according to Roberts, its novelty. Leaning in even more to the formalistic, anti-innovation approach to separation of powers taken in Free Enterprise Fund, Roberts cast Myers as controlling authority and Humphrey’s as the narrow exception, stating, “[o]ur precedents have recognized only two exceptions to the President’s unrestricted removal power”: the first, Humphrey’s, involved an expert agency “led by a group of principal officers,” and the second, illustrated by Morrison, involved an inferior officer with “narrowly defined duties.” According to Roberts, Congress’ “deviat[ion]” from these precedents rendered the director’s for-cause protection from removal unconstitutional.

The Supreme Court decided Seila Law in the summer of 2020. By the time the year ended, Justice Amy Coney Barrett had succeeded Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg, giving conservatives a supermajority of six, and Joe Biden had defeated Donald Trump in the presidential election. Four years later, Trump was elected once more.

From his first day in office in 2025, Trump exercised executive power – including his removal authority – more aggressively than any of his modern predecessors. In March 2025, Trump fired the two Democratic commissioners on the Federal Trade Commission, stating “that their ‘continued service on the FTC [was] inconsistent with [his] Administration’s priorities’ and that they were removed from office ‘pursuant to [his] authority under Article II of the Constitution.’”

One of the fired commissioners, Rebecca Slaughter, filed suit. The Trump administration could not have asked for a better case to argue for overruling Humphrey’s: Slaughter had been terminated from the same agency, the FTC, that was at issue in that 1935 decision.

The issue before the court in Slaughter was broader and more consequential than the questions presented in Free Enterprise Fund and Seila Law. In those earlier cases, the court evaluated the removal restrictions without addressing whether Humphrey’s should be overruled. The novelty of the agency structure designed by Congress was sufficient to strike down those restrictions. Now, if the court agreed with the administration, it would have to explain why, as a matter of stare decisis, Humphrey’s should be discarded.

With the wind of five additional votes at his back, Roberts demolished Humphrey’s. His opinion began with a historical paean to the unitary executive. The Framers vested the executive power in “a President” and insisted that there would be a single person who would sit atop the executive branch hierarchy, he wrote. Roberts acknowledged, quoting Alexander Hamilton, that the “Chief Magistrate” would have “assistants or deputies.” But to ensure accountability, those officers “must be removable by the President.”

Roberts then surveyed the governing case law – including his own. He described Myers as “scholarly” and “seminal,” rhetorically elevating this precedent. Perhaps most importantly, Roberts applied his broad understanding of executive power to repudiate Humphrey’s. He explained that the current FTC on which Slaughter served is different from the agency described by the court in 1935. According to Roberts, since “its creation in 1914, the FTC has accumulated vast rulemaking, enforcement, and adjudicatory powers” that involve it in the execution of the law. Because the FTC is now engaged in the “President’s constitutional role,” the agency must be “controlled by the Chief Executive” and commissioners must be removable at will. “If anything more is left of Humphrey’s,” Roberts wrote, “we overrule it.”

In doing so, Roberts finally subordinated the vast majority of executive agencies to the president’s will.

So, what exactly, does this tell us about the chief justice?

First, Roberts knows how to play the long game, having done so for years on the court and more than four decades as a lawyer. To be sure, Roberts had help along the way. Justice Brett Kavanaugh, who worked in the White House when George W. Bush was president, played a role in developing the court’s jurisprudence in this area. As a judge on the U.S. Courts of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, Kavanaugh wrote dissenting opinions that ultimately were upheld in Free Enterprise Fund and Seila Law. (As a justice, Kavanaugh voted with the majority in both Seila Law and Slaughter.) Trump is, of course, a critical figure in this story as well – most notably through his executive actions and his appointment of three justices (Justice Neil Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Justice Amy Coney Barrett) sympathetic to Roberts’ position on presidential power.

Second, there are limits to even Roberts’ commitment to executive authority over the independent agencies. He also wrote the court’s opinion in Trump v. Cook, decided the same day as Slaughter, in which the court made it clear that the statutory restriction on removing a Federal Reserve governor remained valid. Roberts justified this exception for the Federal Reserve on the basis of “history and tradition.” As Roberts wrote in Cook, “the Government concedes, Congress limited the President’s power to remove Governors for good reason – ‘[t]o preserve the independence of the Federal Reserve’ and to continue the ‘long tradition’ of ‘monetary policy . . . exercised independent of . . . executive influence.’” Accordingly, Roberts elaborated, any “change in that scheme must come from Congress, not the courts.” (Justice Clarence Thomas vigorously challenged Roberts’ historical account and reasoning.)

Finally, and relatedly, the removal trilogy illuminates the chief justice’s careful dance between his professed institutionalism and his core conservative commitments. In the first part of the trilogy, Roberts confined Humphrey’s but did not squarely question its validity. Subsequently, with the benefit of his own precedent and a supermajority, Roberts did not need to check his fidelity to the president’s unfettered removal authority. Indeed, Roberts may have figured out how to square this circle, taking an institutionalist approach to lead the court in its adoption of fundamental conservative principles. As the justices consider cases this term involving climate change, assault weapons, and religious freedom, we will see how fully – and at what pace – Roberts continues to embrace this strategy in his leadership of the court.

#898. My Garlic Turned Blue—Is It Still Safe to Eat?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When your garlic suddenly changes color, suspicion is a perfectly reasonable response.

There are few smells more enticing than that of fresh garlic hitting a hot pan. When raw, it’s sharp enough to announce itself from the cutting board; give it some heat, and it becomes the beginning of roughly a thousand delicious garlic dishes I want to eat immediately. Garlic is so familiar to me that I don’t expect much in the way of surprises.

So when a perfectly ordinary clove suddenly starts looking decidedly less ordinary, it’s enough to make me suspicious. Under certain conditions, garlic can turn green, turquoise or even startlingly blue. And unless you want your garlic to look like it came from a galaxy far, far away, it’s reasonable to wonder what exactly happened—and whether you should still eat it.

The short answer is sulfur. Garlic is full of sulfur-containing compounds, which are responsible for much of what makes garlic taste and smell like garlic in the first place. When you cut or crush a clove, you break open its cells and allow compounds and enzymes that were previously kept separate to mingle. That’s when the chemistry starts.

Usually, we notice the result as flavor. One of the compounds produced is allicin, which is responsible for much of raw garlic’s sharpness. But introduce the right conditions—like an acid—and that same basic chemistry can take a much more visible turn.

The sulfur compounds can react further with amino acids in the garlic to produce pigments. Some of those pigments are blue or purple, while others are yellow; together, they can leave garlic looking green, turquoise or outright blue. Vinegar is a common trigger, which explains why the phenomenon frequently shows up in pickles and other acidic-heavy preparations.

Not every clove is equally likely to change color. Older garlic—particularly garlic that’s spent some time in cold storage—tends to be more prone. Younger, fresher garlic is less likely to turn blue, which gives you one fairly easy way to avoid it.

Acid is the other piece of the puzzle. If keeping your garlic’s milky color matters for your dish, avoid leaving raw garlic in acidic ingredients longer than necessary. Cooking the garlic first can also help because heat deactivates the enzymes that set the reaction in motion.

Of course, none of this is particularly important if you don’t mind the color. Blue garlic may look a little strange, but there’s no need to throw it away—assuming your garlic was fresh to begin with and your food has been handled and stored properly.

In fact, brightly-hued garlic isn’t always an accident. In northern China, laba garlic is made by soaking cloves in vinegar until they turn a vivid green. It’s traditionally eaten around the Lunar New Year, and the transformation is so prized that researchers have studied how to produce the brightest, most vivid green.

So the chemical reaction that can make you eye your pickle jar with suspicion is one that other cooks actually encourage. Your garlic hasn’t gone bad; you’ve just stumbled into a centuries-old culinary tradition.
