#4551. Worm armed 42 GitHub repos by hiding a payload too big to fail

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Payload blobs measuring 12.9 to 13.7 MB were large enough to evade GitHub code search

A new Miasma-family npm worm, originally believed to be just a test with no victims, secretly armed 42 real GitHub repositories from 11 owners, hijacking accounts as early as July and using a stealthy payload built to stay hidden from GitHub and automated scanners, according to JFrog researchers.

Threat hunters at SafeDep, an open source supply chain security firm, first spotted the “testmetest” npm campaign last week, identifying 18 packages linked to the same operator between October 19 and October 25, including releases containing credential-stealing malware. As with earlier npm worms, the malware is self-replicating, steals credentials, and installs a wiper that deletes the home directory when the victim revokes the stolen GitHub token, the security researchers noted.

The new npm worm shares code with June’s Miasma Phantom Gyp worm, and adds Mini Shai-Hulud’s token-revocation wiper.

As of October 22, however, SafeDep had found no compromised third-party packages or victims. “All observed activity belongs to the operator’s own test accounts,” the team wrote.

As with most happy-ending security stories, that proved too good to be true.

In a Monday report, supply chain security company JFrog said its analysis proved otherwise, and found the testmetest campaign operator “had already armed real repositories belonging to established developers and organizations.”

This isn’t just about timing, either, according to JFrog researchers Guy Korolevski and Yair Benamou. “The payload blobs were large enough to evade the GitHub search and API paths normally used to find this kind of compromise,” they wrote.

The attackers planted execution hooks for AI coding assistants and development tools, including Claude, Gemini, Cursor, and VS Code, alongside polymorphic wrappers measuring 12.9 to 13.7 MB that decode a hidden CI-publisher payload. Separately, the npm worm steals credentials, modifies repositories and CI configurations, and spreads through npm packages.

The malware also uses a compiled native add-on to decrypt and launch a separate payload. Before executing, the loader checks for CI runners, containers, sandboxes, debuggers, and other signs of automated analysis, exiting silently when it detects them to reduce the likelihood of detection by security scanners.

The researchers also found a second build that skips the encrypted file. “It forks, detaches and runs a hard-coded command that pulls a loader from a repository on the operator's own GitHub account,” they wrote. That account – CementBeetleSteeple – hosted 17 campaign repositories in total.

JFrog connected this account to a shellpanel scanner, previously documented by Socket in a large-scale campaign that abused GitHub Actions in compromised repositories to exploit CVE-2026-41940 and steal server credentials.

CVE-2026-41940 is an authentication bypass affecting cPanel and WHM. CISA added it to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog in April, and cPanel has since published patched versions.

“C2 infrastructure were previously reported by Socket; our findings connect them to the testmetest GitHub victim cluster through artifacts and workflows on the same hijacked owners,” the JFrog researchers wrote. They added that while they found 42 armed GitHub repositories and attempted internet-wide exploitation, they did not confirm any downstream cPanel compromises.®

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The deal will make ads on the platform available via a range of third-party exchanges, including those run by Yahoo, Amazon and Google.

Pinterest is making its ad inventory more broadly available via a new programmatic buying partnership that will make Pinterest ad inventory available across a range of third-party ad platforms.

Facilitated by Magnite, Pinterest ads will now be available via multiple ad exchanges, including Yahoo DSP, Amazon Ads DVA+ and Google Display and Video 360. That will see more ads flowing into Pinterest’s system, while also showing more advertisers the results that Pinterest can drive for their business.

As explained by Pinterest: “Programmatic Deals let advertisers buy selected Pinterest display and video inventory alongside the rest of their campaigns through strategic supply-side platform (SSP) and demand-side platform (DSP) partners, bringing Pinterest’s premium inventory and intent into the planning, buying and measurement workflows enterprise marketers already use.”

In other words, programmatic ad buyers can include Pinterest ad placements in their broader ad sets. The system will display their ads in relevant Pinterest placements without the need for Pinterest-specific campaigns.

This could be a valuable expansion for Pinterest, and it also offers an opportunity for the company to demonstrate the platform’s promotional value to potential ad partners.

Pinterest said that Programmatic Deals are currently available in North America, South America and Asia Pacific, and that interested advertisers can learn more about Pinterest Programmatic Deals by contacting a Pinterest sales representative.

#632. Tiny Magnets Implanted in Muscles Are Giving Prosthetic Hands a Remarkable New Ability

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Tiny magnets implanted in muscles are helping a prosthetic hand feel like it is opening and closing naturally.

You can close your eyes, make a fist, or reach for an object without ever needing to look at your hand. That seemingly effortless ability depends on a hidden sense called kinesthesia, which tells the brain how muscles and limbs are moving. After an amputation, this vital stream of sensory information is disrupted, making even simple movements with a prosthetic hand more difficult and less intuitive.

Now, researchers led by the Sant’Anna School of Advanced Studies in Pisa, in collaboration with Cleveland Clinic, have uncovered new clues about how the brain interprets these movement signals. Their findings, published in Science Advances, suggest that the brain perceives finger movements as coordinated patterns rather than isolated actions. The discovery could help scientists develop prosthetic hands that not only move more naturally but also restore the feeling of movement itself.

The team compared data from the world’s only two neural-machine interfaces designed to restore this sense in upper limb prostheses. Despite their different designs, both produced sensations of coordinated finger movements, suggesting that the brain organizes incoming muscle signals into grasp patterns known as “synergies.”

Vibrating a muscle can make someone feel that a limb is moving. But applying vibration from outside the body also stimulates the skin, mixing sensory signals in ways that can complicate feedback from a prosthesis.

Sant’Anna’s myokinetic kinesthetic interface (MKkI) targets the muscles through small magnets implanted in the remaining forearm. The magnets generate vibrations within the muscles without directly stimulating the skin. Researchers connected this bidirectional interface to the Mia Hand, a robotic hand developed by Sant’Anna spin-off company Prensilia.

A 34-year-old Italian participant tested the system for six weeks. He experienced sensations of his hand opening and closing, with fingers appearing to move together in patterns closely resembling natural movement.

“The myokinetic kinesthetic interface is unique because it uses a simple, minimally invasive implant to stimulate muscles without touching the skin. This approach may be the key to better understanding how human motor control works but also how to restore movement sensation after amputation,” says Federico Masiero, Ph.D., the study’s first author. A former Ph.D. student at Sant’Anna, Masiero is now a postdoctoral researcher at the Munich Institute of Robotics and Machine Intelligence (MIRMI) of the Technical University of Munich (TUM).

The sensations reported in the Italian trial resembled those experienced by participants using a kinesthetic feedback system developed at Cleveland Clinic. That system combines surgical nerve redirection with robotics, whereas Sant’Anna’s approach uses implanted magnets. Both selectively vibrate deep muscles to generate movement sensations.

Across the two systems, users felt coordinated finger actions rather than isolated movements. Both teams also observed that some sensory information was processed without users immediately becoming consciously aware of it. The findings suggest that muscle feedback may be organized into coordinated movement patterns at a more subconscious level than previously understood.

“The ability to compare independently generated data from two very different interfaces makes these findings especially compelling,” says Professor Paul Marasco, Ph.D., who coordinated the study at Cleveland Clinic. “It gives us a stronger foundation for designing therapies and devices that work with the nervous system in a more natural way, with the ultimate goal of improving outcomes for patients.”

Understanding that organization could help researchers deliver feedback that better matches how the brain handles movement. Beyond prosthetics, the findings may eventually inform stroke rehabilitation, epilepsy, and pain treatment, although those applications remain possibilities for future research.

The next challenge is to use the implanted magnets for control and sensation at the same time. Building on earlier research, the team plans to track the magnets’ positions to control the prosthesis while simultaneously applying vibrations to the same magnets to provide movement feedback.

The longer-term goal is a permanent implant that can be studied beyond the initial six-week trial. Together with the teams’ previous collaborative studies, this work lays a foundation for combining intuitive control with natural grasp sensations in a new generation of more human-like prosthetic hands.

“Our solution was implemented as a preliminary demonstrator: the implant was designed to last six weeks, a period we considered sufficient for an initial verification of the interface’s usefulness and effectiveness. The results were very promising and prompted us to explore a permanent implantable solution, which will allow us to study the interface over much longer periods and with a larger number of participants,” says Professor Christian Cipriani, who created the interface and coordinated the study.

Reference: “Coordinated hand movement sensation revealed through an implanted magnetic prosthetic kinesthetic interface” by Federico Masiero, Mattia Gentile, Marta Gherardini, Eliana La Frazia, Charles H. Moore, B. Ülgen Kilic, Valerio Ianniciello, Roberta Reho, Tommaso Mori, Flavia Paggetti, Lorenzo Andreani, Simon A. Whitton, Paul D. Marasco and Christian Cipriani, 24 June 2026, Science Advances. DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.adx5046

The study, coordinated by The BioRobotics Institute of the Sant’Anna School of Advanced Studies in Pisa, in collaboration with the Pisa University Hospital (AOUP) and Cleveland Clinic, was supported by European, Italian, and U.S. funding. These include the ERC projects MYKI and MYTI, the Fit For Medical Robotics and PROPRIOUSS projects funded by the Italian Ministry of University and Research, and US data originally collected under awards from the NIH Office of the Director and DARPA. Christopher Wren at basecamp during the 1974 Pamir Expedition. In this photo, he is using a typewriter to draft the New York Times story that revealed the deaths of American climber Gary Ullin and eight Soviet female climbers. (Photo: Courtesy of Jed Williamson)

Christopher Sale Wren served as The New York Times bureau chief in Moscow, Cairo, Beijing, Ottawa, and Johannesburg. His creative life was deeply intertwined with a respect for nature, big expeditions and the many people he met on assignment. He passed at his home in Vermont on February 15, 2026, at age 89.

Wren began climbing as a student at Dartmouth, where he sometimes felt out of place, according to his daughter, Celia. A scholarship student whose parents were actors, he had little interest in fraternity life. In the outdoors, he found a bigger sense of kinship. He learned to climb with the college’s mountaineering club, practicing on campus at Bartlett Tower and on the nearby cliffs of Orford and Owl’s Head. According to Samuel C. Silverstein’s memorial in The American Alpine Journal, he once ended a public-speaking assignment on mountaineering by rappelling out of the classroom’s third-floor window.

In 1962, Wren joined the six-man team that made the first ascent of Denali’s Southeast Spur (Alaska grade 4, 65-degree ice). The expedition became the subject of his first major article for Look magazine, merging his climbing with his reporting into a career he chased around the world.

Twelve years later, while stationed in Moscow for the Times, he joined an American expedition to the Pamirs. On Lenin Peak (23,406ft), Wren and fellow climbers Jock Glidden and Allen Steck encountered members of a Soviet women’s team who had died in a storm. His reporting brought their tragedy to the Times front page, breaking the news of deaths Soviet authorities had kept secret.

For Jed Williamson, who was also on the Pamirs expedition, Wren’s contribution to mountaineering extended to his ability to tell its stories. “What he really brought to the mountaineering scene was his ability to write about it and put it out there,” Williamson said. “He had a really good sense of humor. He could turn a situation that one might not think was so humorous into one that was humorous.”

Even when faced with tragedy, Wren found a way to bring levity to dangerous moments. Celia remembered one story her father told about provisioning the Pamirs expedition. The American climbers wanted peanut butter, which he couldn’t find in Soviet shops. Instead, he packed tins of caviar for protein. “His teammates thought it was disgusting,” she remembers. “But he was a Renaissance man with an incredible sense of humor.”

Wren played guitar and banjo and wrote songs, two of which Johnny Cash recorded: Jesus Was a Carpenter and Gospel Road. “It was a fun thing that he did, and he was good at it,” Williamson said. “But he was never a chest pounder about that kind of thing.”

At home, Wren’s appetite for being outside became part of family life. During the family’s years in the Soviet Union, Celia remembered her father heading out on skis, pulling her and her brother behind him on a sled. He penned numerous books, from a biography of Johnny Cash to Walking to Vermont, an account of his roughly 400-mile walk home from Manhattan after retiring from The New York Times.

Williamson remembers that despite the breadth of his traveling and range of his accomplishments, Wren was down-to-earth.  “He wasn’t aloof or standoffish or anything like that,” he said. “He was happy to share.”

Late into retirement, he continued to ski and ride his mountain bike around the nearby lake. Celia remembered his ability to keep moving ahead. When recalling difficult experiences, he would often say, “Well, you know, that’s all behind us now.”

#2738. How Two Bored Belgians Broke the Backyard Ultra World Record

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Trail Runner contributing photographer Jacob Zocherman drove 15 hours from his home in Sweden to document the Belgian team at the 2026 Backyard Ultra World Team Championship in Retie, Belgium. Unlike a typical race with a predetermined finish and a predictable narrative arc within it, backyard ultras only end when there is one runner left. Zocherman spent the five days and four hours of what ended up being a world-record-breaking run on the course, sleeping in a camper van when he could. He spoke with managing editor Abby Levene during and shortly after the race.

Ivo Steyaert and Merijn Geerts had been running for 98 hours at the 2026 Backyard Ultra World Team Championship, and they were bored. Never mind they had each run over 400 miles on the same 4.167-mile course. Never mind they hadn’t gotten more than a few minutes of sleep at once in over four days. They looked fresh, the nights were long, and blah blah blah, on-the-ground photographer Jacob Zocherman reports. But with the 100th lap on the horizon, something clicked.

“You could see something happen to them, like a spring flower waiting through winter,” Zocherman tells Trail Runner. “Suddenly the sun comes and nothing happens, nothing happens, and then in a specific moment everything happens and the flower comes out in bloom. That’s what I saw in them—suddenly they were happy to be there, cheering with the audience, laughing with each other and joking.”

It was like colleagues going out to the bar on a Friday afternoon: They’ve found their happy place, Zocherman says. Except in this case, their happy place was a park in Retie, Belgium, aka the middle of nowhere.

“It’s very touching to see, because they are so humble and so easy to like,” Zocherman says. “At that point, I felt like the race would go on forever. But it also feels very fragile—when there are only two runners left, there is no room for error.”

Per backyard ultra rules, runners must run the 4.167-mile lap on the hour, every hour, until only one runner remains and is declared the winner. In case that isn’t nutty enough, the Backyard Ultra World Team Championship is an even nuttier spinoff of this format: Each participating country (of which this year there were 67) simultaneously conducts its own backyard race with 15 participants, and the country with the most cumulative laps wins. This backyard team world championship now alternates every other year with the individual championship, held on the original backyard ultra course in mastermind Gary “Lazarus Lake” Cantrell’s backyard in Bell Buckle, Tennessee.

Team USA would go on to win the team world title, with Mark Dowdle leading the stars and stripes to a collective 1,120 laps, also known as “yards,” for its third world title in six years. Australia took second, 51 yards behind, and Belgium took third, 11 yards off of them. While the team competition inherently shaped the dynamic of the race, with runners sacrificing their own best interests to slow down and pull teammates along, Steyaert and Geerts had several additional goals on their minds.

They’d each run over 100 laps multiple times—four times for Steyaert and six for Geerts. But neither had ever run through a fifth night. Belgium’s fourth-place runner Kenneth Vanthuyne was long gone after hallucinating that he was at work when Steyaert and Geerts put on their headlamps for the fifth time.

“That was the critical moment,” Zocherman says. “It was the one thing they had always wanted to do.”

As the sun rose over the dewy fields surrounding the dirt road of the race course, they both looked unscathed.

“I was like, ‘OK, nothing can stop them,” Zocherman says. They turned their attention to the Belgium national backyard ultra record of 115 laps, held by Jan Vandekerckhove. They checked that off and reset the goal to the world record of 119 laps, set by Australia’s Phil Gore at Dead Cow Gully in Queensland last year. That, too, proved to be seemingly no problem.

At 5:00 p.m. on Thursday, October 22—over five days after they started running—Zocherman heard rumors that Steyaert and Geerts were setting out on their final lap.

“I think they could have continued. They looked fresh, they looked really good,” Zocherman says. “But I also know they wanted to finish together. And the longer you go, the smaller the margins are that something will happen and one will finish over the other. And they wanted to avoid that. But they wanted to finish together.”

Sure enough, when the pair returned to the start/finish arch at 5:57 p.m., they did not go back out when the clock reached the top of the hour. Instead, the two friends, standing side-by-side in matching red shirts, grinned to the crowd of cheering onlookers, each raising a hand and joining them together over their heads in the shape of a heart.

Together, they had accomplished what no one had done before: Run 124 laps of a 4.167-mile course every hour, on the hour, for a total of 516.7 miles and a new backyard ultra individual world record.

“They are like brothers and have run many times as the two remaining runners,” Zochermans says. “So to them, it is not a competition that late in the race. They are doing it together. And they want it to remain like that. It’s kind of beautiful. It’s poetry.”

From the start at 2 p.m. local time on Saturday, October 17, until Steyaert and Geerts concluded their record-breaking run five days and four hours later, Zocherman was on the ground to document it all. It’s a stroke of good fortune that of the 67 backyard ultra team competitions happening around the world, Zocherman—who lives in his native Sweden—chose to go to Belgium. It’s also not a coincidence.

In fact, Zocherman almost didn’t make the 15 hour drive to Retie after his mother was unexpectedly diagnosed with an aggressive form of cancer two weeks before the race. The only reason he did go, Zocherman tells Trail Runner, is because that random campground in a foreign country is like home. Zocherman had been at the same course in April for the Legends Backyard Ultra, and the mini-universe environment of these events bring people together in a special way that’s hard to describe.

“It was an emotional rollercoaster and a logistical battle,” Zocherman says. “I wouldn’t have gone if it wasn’t this backyard ultra. It was a personal reason to be among people I know and like. I didn’t think it would be the crazy result that it was.”

Zocherman made the trek with his dog Balou, who he says keeps him grounded at these events with no predetermined end, or even rhythm. (She’s now witnessed three world records and could not be less impressed.) They did two laps of the course every day and got a decent amount of sleep for the first few nights before squeezing in 20 minutes at a time between loops by the end.

“I also know it will be a very long race and I have to drive home for 15 hours after so I can’t be crazy wasted,” he says. “Although you become crazy wasted anyway.”

#718. A Brief-ish History of METI. Part I: Early Ideas and Experiments

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Long before modern astronomy, humans looked to the stars and were absolutely astounded by what they saw. Even by the most ancient reckoning, the night sky offered enough to inspire awe and wonder, make us feel insignificant, fire our imaginations, and foster hope and a sense of connection. Yet, for as long as humans have surveyed the heavens, they have seen no indication that life as we know it exists elsewhere - what has come to be known as the "Great Silence."

For many, the disparity between the assumed prevalence of life in the Universe and the lack of evidence for it raises a troubling incongruity often erroneously referred to as "Fermi's Paradox" (in truth, it was neither Fermi's nor a Paradox). It seems like a valid question when you consider the facts. Given the age, size, and density of the known Universe, shouldn't we have found unambiguous evidence of extraterrestrial life (and intelligence) by now?

Sixty years after the first Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence (SETI) experiment (Project Ozma), scientists are still asking the question: "Are we alone in the Universe?" In addition to listening to the cosmos for radio signals and other signs of technological activity ("technosignatures"), efforts have been mounted to send messages to space for the purpose of making contact with an extraterrestrial intelligence (ETI).

This practice has come to be known as Messaging Extraterrestrial Intelligence (METI) to distinguish it from its more "passive" SETI cousin. Previously, we chronicled the history of SETI by examining some of its greatest milestones. Today, we will begin looking at the brief(-ish) history of METI and how it came to be.

As Universe Today's own Paul Patton addressed, Fermi's Paradox is named for famed Italian-American physicist Enrico Fermi. In 1950, during a lunchtime conversation at the Los Alamos National Laboratory, where he had worked a few years prior on the Manhattan Project, Fermi and his colleagues were discussing the recent spate of UFO sightings. It was here that Fermi posed his big challenge, saying out of the blue: "Where is everybody?"

After some quick calculations, Fermi and his colleagues concluded that, if interstellar travel were feasible and desirable, Earth should have been visited several times already. This led to the Hart-Tipler Conjecture, named after physicists Michael Hart and Frank Tipler. In a series of papers that appeared between 1975 and 1982, they argued that this "paradox" can be resolved only by asserting that extraterrestrial life does not exist.

Since then, many scientists (including Carl Sagan) have challenged this logic, arguing that there are innumerable reasons why humans have not heard from intelligent life yet (yet being the key word). Or as Sagan and William Newman famously put it: "the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence."

From this, the modern definition of the Fermi Paradox emerged. It states that the disparity between the assumed prevalence of life in the Universe and the lack of evidence for it can be resolved by one of two postulates: either extraterrestrials do not exist, or special circumstances exist that prevent us from detecting them. Before we get into the history of this discipline, a little note on nomenclature is necessary. Traditionally, scientists made no distinction between SETI and METI, and pursued both projects concurrently. For instance, Cornell astrophysicist Dr. Frank Drake led the first SETI experiment (Project Ozma) in 1960, and was also vital to the first true METI project roughly fourteen years later (the Arecibo Message). Similarly, famed science communicator Carl Sagan, who became integral to early SETI efforts, later collaborated with Drake to create the Arecibo Message, the Pioneer Plaques, and the Voyager Golden Records.

The term "Active SETI" didn't emerge until 2005 to describe sending messages into space intended for extraterrestrial recipients. The following year, Russian scientist Alexander Zaitsev coined the term "Messaging Extraterrestrial Intelligence" to differentiate SETI and METI. As he wrote in the proposal paper, released in 2006:

Those who propose, or oppose, sending Messages to Extra-Terrestrial Intelligence (METI) must contemplate the Hamlet-like question: "To send or not to send?" The science known as SETI deals with searching for messages from aliens. METI science deals with the creation of messages to aliens. Thus, SETI and METI proponents have quite different perspectives.

SETI scientists are in a position to address only the local question "does Active SETI make sense?â In other words, would it be reasonable, for SETI success, to transmit with the object of attracting ETI's attention? In contrast to Active SETI, METI pursues not a local, but a more global purpose â to overcome the Great Silence in the Universe, bringing to our extraterrestrial neighbors the long-expected annunciation "You are not alone!"

In a follow-up paper released in 2011, "Rationale for METI," Zaitsev laid out METI's mission statement. In it, he quoted famed American radar astronomer Steven Ostro's unpublished paper, which proposed creating a powerful omnidirectional radio beacon on the far side of the Moon. As Oslo said in his paper, "We might conclude that it is better to give than to receive, and that the war on Great Silence must begin at home."

Among METI's benefits, Zaitsev highlighted what he coined the "SETI Paradox": advanced civilizations hear only silence from space because everyone is listening and no one is transmitting. He also noted that previous SETI programs spent hundreds of times longer listening to space than METI experiments did transmitting. But most important were the possible benefits interstellar communication could bring, something that was being avoided with passive SETI efforts. This, he wrote, could very well lead to civilizations living in isolation and eventually going extinct:

Isolationism as a possible cause of extinction of civilizations I do not know definitely, but it seems to me that Sebastian von Hoerner was the first, who in the 1960s indicated that "apathy" or "Loss of Interest" represents real cause of extinction of advanced Civilizations... I think that it is not interesting for inhabitants to live in such en- forced self-isolation, in such lurker-like Civilization! Civilizations, which are forced to hide and tremble because of farfetched fears, is doomed to extinction.

For fans of Liu Cixin's Remembrance of Earth's Past (or The Three-Body Problem) trilogy, these sentiments likely bring to mind the Dark Forest Hypothesis. And they would be correct! Whether it's safe to broadcast is at the heart of the debate over the ethics of METI (more on that later).

The first example of an "active SETI" proposal came from the 19th-century German mathematician and astronomer Carl Friedrich Gauss, though debate continues over who first proposed the idea. In 1820, Gauss proposed drawing a petroglyph representing the Pythagorean theorem (a right triangle and three squares) on the surface of the Earth that would be large enough to be seen from the Moon or Mars. This would signal to extraterrestrial beings using the language of mathematics.

Several experiments were attempted in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, which were heavily inspired by astronomers like Giovanni Schiaparelli (1835-1910). While observing Mars, he noted several faint linear features, which he designed "canali" (Italian for "channels"). He further named these features after ancient seas and rivers derived from Classical Antiquity. The name "canali" was misinterpreted by scientists and the public to mean "canals," fueling belief in a Martian civilization.

Percival Lowell popularized the idea in the late-19th century, building an observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona, to map these supposed canals. Henceforth, people tried to message the civilization they believed was there. As Hugo Gernsback wrote in a 1909 opinion piece published in the journal Modern Electrics:

Every time our neighbor Mars comes in opposition to the Earth, a host of inventors and others begin to turn their attention to the great up-to-date problem, "signaling to Mars." It is safe to say that all the proposed, possible as well as impossible, projects would fill a good-sized volume, especially the ones invented in this country, which by far leads the world in number of projects and "inventions."

That same year, Harvard astronomer Professor William Pickering proposed building a massive $10 million system of motorized, synchronized mirrors in the desert to flash signals to Mars. Known as the Mirror Scheme, the goal was to see whether any sentient life and a Martian civilization existed by looking for return signals. In 1928, Dr. Hugh Mansfield Robinson, a British lawyer who believed he had been telepathically connected to a Martian woman (Oomaruru) since the early 1920s, launched what became known as the Martian Telegram.

The experiment consisted of a telegraph sent from London's Rugby Radio Station at a wavelength of 18,240 meters (ft). The message contained three words: "Opesti, Nipitia, Secomba," which Robinson believed were derived from the Martian language, followed by "Mar la loi de Earth" ("Love to Mars from Earth"). The station also kept a receiver antenna tuned to a wavelength of 30,000 m (ft), which Robinson insisted on, stating it was what the Martians preferred.

Like the earliest SETI efforts, the first METI experiments were humble and aimed at the nearest planets in the Solar System. They also reflected the then-accepted idea that Mars and Venus were inhabited and hosted civilizations. As our knowledge of the cosmos grew and technology improved, METI efforts would expand and direct their messages towards the stars. #2329. The Business of Flower Farming: Bewitching Blooms

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When Laura Grimm bought an 11-acre farm with a once-stately house that hadn’t been updated in decades, what did she renovate first? The land.

She spent two years clearing invasive plants that had swallowed the property during the 50 years since it was last farmed, before tilling its heavy clay soil. Only then could she begin building Bewitching Blooms, her flower farm in Far Hills, New Jersey, which formally opened this year.

Grimm, 63, seems an improbable farmer. She spent years in the white-collar world of finance in New York City, but gardening was always part of her life. She grew plants on city rooftops and terraces, and when she moved to the suburbs, the garden behind her house just kept expanding—until she simply ran out of room.

Enter the farm—and gardening on a whole new scale. Last year, Grimm and her cousin planted 17,000 tulip bulbs.

“Some people don’t realize how much work it is,” Grimm said. “Because it’s flowers, some people think it’s all pretty dresses and hair ribbons, and not the dirty nails and sore feet.”

Grimm started her farm in the middle of a quiet boom. Between 2012 and 2022, the number of U.S. farms reporting cut-flower production increased by roughly 50 percent, according to the USDA Census of Agriculture, and many of the new growers are women building small businesses on relatively modest pieces of land. Unlike many forms of agriculture, flower farming can begin with a small plot and expand as customers—and confidence—grow.

That growth has been accompanied by rising enthusiasm for locally grown flowers. Debra Prinzing, founder of the Slow Flowers Society, an organization that promotes locally and sustainably grown flowers, sees the shift as something more enduring than a passing preference.

“I don’t think it’s a trend,” Prinzing said. “I think it’s a cultural shift—and one that’s here to stay.”

Grimm sees the demand firsthand. In her first year alone, thousands of customers came to the farm, stunning her. They wandered the rows of tulips and dahlias, filling buckets to overflowing. When the flower stand was empty, some even knocked on her front door.

Her son Mark, 27, who helps run the farm’s Instagram account, has learned to brace himself whenever a florist posts a photo of Grimm’s flowers and tags Bewitching Blooms.

“All of our phones will start blowing up with direct messages, and we’re like, ‘Oh God, it’s going to be a long one for Mom,’” he said.

Grimm’s three sons, ages 25 to 29, come home about twice a month to work alongside her, planting and hauling compost. They call every day to check in. Last year, though, they realized their mother needed more help than they could give her. So they bought her a tractor.

“It was amazing because I thought I was in a little over my head,” she said, “and they believed in me enough to do that.”

Days on the farm start at 5:00 a.m. Grimm walks around the beds with her dogs and a cup of coffee, doing a daily assessment of what needs to happen that day and what can wait. She has given up on making a to-do list the night before, because she just never knows what is going to happen.

“You have to learn to let go, and that’s been really hard for me,” she said. “You don’t have control, and you have to work with what you have. Even though you think you’re doing it the right way, Mother Nature kind of wallops you.”

More than anything, though, she is grateful for it all. She finds a sense of peace walking through the farm and loves watching it become a place where other people find that peace, too.

Event planners come not only to buy flowers but to draw inspiration from the colors in the fields. Local residents come to gather bouquets or pick a few flowers for the dinner table. Some simply come to walk the rows.

On a recent day, a family visited the farm with their daughter. Among the tulips, Grimm watched her transform—smiling, taking pictures, and gathering flowers by the armful as her amazed parents looked on.

Grimm loves seeing people experience the farm that way: “Just seeing how much joy people can find just walking through the rows of flowers.”

The house can wait. For now, this is the dream.

“At some point, we’ll get around to having a working bathroom on the second floor,” Grimm said, “so I do not have to go down the hallway to brush my teeth.”
