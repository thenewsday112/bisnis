#15. US forces accused of ‘weaponising’ Somalia’s floods by trapping women and children in the path of El Niño

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Exclusive: Coordinated airstrikes on territory held by al-Shabaab are driving the civilian population to find refuge alongside a river where rising water levels are a threat to safety. Is it a deliberate military strategy?

The water was rising rapidly. It had already reached Cayni Ali’s waist. Her home, five metres from the flooded River Juba in southern Somalia, never stood a chance. There was nowhere to go. Ali’s city of Jilib was under attack. Repeated airstrikes, said the mother of six, were pinning its population beside the rising river.

“The drones come all the time,” said the 27-year-old. “We hear them constantly. They kill animals, civilians. The town is big but destruction is everywhere.”

Asho Omar from Malenda, a village near Jilib, described “constant” airstrikes that had forced people towards the banks of the Juba.

“The airstrikes are everywhere; it is difficult to move. So many people have been killed.”

Their testimony offers a rare glimpse of life inside the stronghold of al-Shabaab, the Islamist militants who control 30% of Somalia. Closed off from the outside world, residents describe a city dealing with significant civilian casualties. Bodies, some said, lay in the streets.

The ongoing conflict is a legally ambiguous, shadowy war coordinated by the United States in east Africa, in which there is little accountability for harm to Somali civilians.

The United States Africa Command (Africom) would not share “operational security” details of a seemingly accelerating campaign that has also raised questions over potential covert US strikes. But there is, perhaps, an even darker dimension to the offensive on al-Shabaab’s de facto capital.

Sources say the military strategy appears to be seeking to drown those living in al-Shabaab territory by “weaponising” a rapidly strengthening El Niño, forecast to intensify Somalia’s rainy season from this month.

“The airstrikes are forcing people to go close to the river. It’s an extremely aggressive offensive, exploiting El Niño as a weapon of war to push the population into flood-prone areas,” said a source monitoring the Juba floodplain in southern Somalia.

Pinning the population – which includes large numbers of women and children – on the river’s floodplain has raised concerns of large-scale deaths in the months ahead.

“The official line is that they are targeting military positions and al-Shabaab, but because al-Shabaab is embedded within the community, it’s difficult to avoid collateral damage,” added the source.

When El Niño last struck Somalia in 2023, it caused the worst floods seen in the country for a century, displacing more than 1.2 million people. Analysts have questioned the ethics of the US military allegedly using mapping data from flood-prone areas – conceived to mitigate civilian casualties – to inflict harm.

Jethro Norman, a senior researcher with the Danish Institute for International Studies, said: “A weaponisation of El Niño is a reversal of process – using humanitarian remote sensing to inflict casualties rather than to mitigate harm. You capture the cumulative impact of a sustained airstrikes campaign in an El Niño flooding context.”

US officials deny exploiting climatic factors. A spokesperson said: “Africom does not target civilians, conduct operations for the purpose of displacing civilian populations or use weather or flooding as a method of warfare. Africom targets lawful military objectives and takes feasible measures to mitigate civilian harm.”

Nevertheless, many wonder if the opportunity to weaken al-Shabaab using the weather was too tempting to ignore, offering a catastrophic drowning event blamed on a supercharged El Niño.

Like many cities, Jilib’s location has been found wanting in an era of extreme weather. On a low-lying plain, the city is chronically vulnerable to flooding. In 2023, vast swathes of farmland were submerged. Halimo Abdikadir from Kamsuuma, south of Jillib, described the destruction of her home, 6 miles (10km) from the river. “The waters came quickly at night – when they left, everything was mud. A lot of animals drowned.”

Arbey Famu from Ahmed Yare, another village outside Jilib, expects this El Niño to be far worse than 2023: her crops of maize and sorghum are underwater.

“In Jilib, there is big flooding already,” said the 70-year-old.

US defence officials will know the early indicators of El Niño’s impact in al-Shabaab’s heartland. UN agencies say the Juba River basin is of “particular concern”. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), meanwhile, foresees “severe flooding”.

Attacking Jilib using ground forces carries much risk. Airstrikes can yield triumphs such as the death of a senior al-Shabaab commander on 1 October, but are unable to overrun a city of 100,000.

Ali Adan Ali, the commissioner for displaced people in Jubaland, is worried. In his Kismayo office, Ali identified 327 regions prone to flooding. By October, El Niño warnings had only been issued to half.

His chief concern is the lack of boats. Without them, he says, thousands in al-Shabaab territory risk being trapped on the east side of the river, unable to reach help in Kismayo, at the mouth of the Juba.

Three years ago, during the last El Niño, Ali says, 19,000 people were stranded for a week without food. “We are running out of time,” he says.

The absence of boats sums up Somalia’s funding woes. In 2023, the country’s aid budget was $2.2bn. Now it stands at about $300,000. Flood protection investment has dwindled.

Compounding concerns is al-Shabaab’s lack of preparedness in the area it controls. Ali has not received a response from the militants regarding El Niño readiness. Cayni Ali said there are no sandbags or flood defences deployed yet in Jilib. Earth walls were erected along the river but have proved insufficient.

“They are not big enough,” she said. The latest flood advisory for the River Juba urges communities to “reinforce” weak riverbanks.

Corroborating testimonies describe a prolonged – and seemingly indiscriminate – campaign of airstrikes against al-Shabaab that appears to have killed many civilians. The US would not share details of potential civilian fatalities “to protect US and partner forces and preserve operational security”.

Farhan Ahmed, who lived in Jilib until fleeing last month, described constant terror. “There were airstrikes every day, all the time.” One strike flattened five homes in his neighbourhood. Ahmed, 35, described bodies littering the city streets.

Fadumo Abdi claimed the airstrikes were largely failing to target militants. “The attacks are mostly killing civilians. Some were buried, but some were not,” she said.

Abdikadir, from Kamsuuma, added: “The drone attacks on Jilib are too much. So many people die.”

Yusuf Siidi, 27, from Araare near Jamame in al-Shabaab territory, witnessed three teenagers killed in one strike. Maryan Salah was at home near Jamame when a drone sent a shard of burning metal 20cm long into her back yard, landing next to her two-year-old daughter.

“It was so close. I am very angry. I’m not part of the conflict.”

Salah’s near miss predates the current offensive. She can recall only that it happened in “mid-March”. The US admits a drone strike near Jamame on 19 March, but no other data is provided.

Although eyewitness accounts detail an extraordinary number of strikes in Jilib, official US records confirm only one airstrike in Jilib during August. Six took place last month. Between the start of August and now, the US says it conducted a total of just 11 strikes.

Allegations of ambiguity increasingly characterise US operations in Somalia. It is an opaque approach that raises questions over whether Africom has authorised covert airstrikes in the region.

Records from the nearest hospital indicate intensive airstrikes or fighting. The ICRC has treated 205 patients with “weapon-related” injuries in Kismayo this year, a 43% increase on last year.

David Sterman, analyst at the New America thinktank, said: “The possibility that we are seeing covert US strikes always looms large when there are strikes without clear attribution.”

The US, however, said it publicised “all airstrikes conducted under its authority in Somalia. We do not maintain an undisclosed or unannounced strike count.”

Turkey has also used drones to target al-Shabaab in Somalia. Operating alongside Africom, Turkey rarely divulges any details of its strikes or military operations in Somalia.

Beyond airstrikes and floods, the primary concern for Somalis is hunger. Those fleeing the Juba valley – local authorities are offering humanitarian help to those who escape – say there is insufficient food to sustain them.

Omar’s family of 13 survives on one fish a day. How big is the fish? Omar held her hands less than 30cm apart.

Habiba Ali, 55, from Jamame, said her family – like many – shares one daily meal of maize. Time in between is spent hiding from conflict. “When the fighting stops, we wait for it start again.”

In Kismayo, too, long-term drought and USAID cuts have brought misery.

Shukri Ahmed, 32, the president of Galbeed refugee camp, estimates that 6,000 might have died in Kismayo’s network of displacement sites since the Trump administration abruptly shut USAID last year. Overnight, two children had died in makeshift shelters from diarrhoea.

El Niño will “deepen [Somalia’s] hunger levels”, warns the UN-backed body responsible for identifying famine.

Zakaria Mohammed, head nurse at Kismayo’s ICRC-supported hospital for five years, says children from al-Shabaab territory arrive every day so severely malnourished or weak they cannot be saved. Cases of child malnutrition rates have risen 40% this year.

Xaawo Nacdoor made it just in time. Al-Shabaab had blocked her leaving Jamame to receive treatment for her baby. But her 14-month-old daughter Faadumo was dying, so Nacdoor fled at night.

For three days, with just a bottle of water, Nacdoor walked with her daughter strapped to her back. On the second day, the toddler lost consciousness.

“She was severely dehydrated, but I kept going.” Arriving at the hospital, Faadumo weighed 4.1kg. Three days later: 4.4kg. She survived.

Most patients here vow never to return home, saying life under al-Shabaab meant no school, education, healthcare or food. For the thousands of families straddling the Juba River – often the poorest – things are about to get much worse.

Archaeologists in Peru have found an elaborate 1,500-year-old tomb that may hold the remains of a high-status toddler from a pre-Inca civilization.

The burial, which is from the Moche culture, also contained vessels depicting prisoners with rope around each of their necks and a sculpture of a human-bird deity.

Moche (also known as Mochica) culture developed along the northern coast of what is now Peru from the third to the ninth century. They created agricultural lands in the region's desert landscape, and their archaeological record includes monumental temples, water canals, pottery, metallurgy, textiles and a distinctive form of art. The Moche didn't have a written language, but they left behind thousands of realistic portrait vessels, which are somewhat similar to the prisoner sculptors found in the toddler's burial.

In the newfound tomb, the team unearthed a small skeleton, which is likely the remains of a toddler who was, at most, 2 years old, Carlos Rengifo-Chunga, director of the University of Trujillo's Huacas de Moche Archaeological Project, told Live Science. The child's sex is unknown.

The child was buried alongside 32 ceramic objects associated with Moche culture.

Rengifo-Chunga and his colleagues also found two copper objects in the burial: a mask-like disk covering the child's face, and a half-moon object on their feet. In addition, the burial contained 32 ceramic objects associated with Moche culture, including those depicting the prisoners and the deity.

The tomb was large compared to the child's small size, and could have easily held an adult, Rengifo-Chunga said. It dates to the sixth or seventh century, he added.

The team unearthed the tomb inside the Uhle Platform at the Huacas de Moche archaeological site in northern Peru. The Uhle Platform is a mausoleum, attached to the Huaca de la Luna temple, with a patio archaeologists call the Patio of the Serpents. Of the more than 80 burials previously uncovered there, the tomb represents the only known child to receive this sort of lavish burial.

The elaborate funerary treatment, including the use of copper, and the location of the tomb all suggest that the child belonged to an elite class of Moche society, Rengifo-Chunga said.

The two prisoner sculptures are associated with the "ritual of combat," a common theme in Moche art. In this sort of imagery, two warriors fight each other, and the loser is stripped of his clothes and weapons, tied around the neck with a rope and offered as a human sacrifice, Rengifo-Chunga said. The prisoners depicted on these two vessels were the losing warriors, and their presence in the burial of a child is rare.

"One would normally presume that these kinds of vessels represent something related to the deceased's life," Rengifo-Chunga said. For example, a vessel like this might signify that the individual took part in combat.

One of the ceramic objects depicts a presumed deity with human and bird features.

An archaeological team discovered a likely elite child burial at the Huacas de Moche archaeological site in Peru.

One of the two ceramics depicting prisoners that archaeologists found in the toddler's burial.

But a toddler couldn't have taken part in such an activity. In general, archaeologists wouldn't expect to see elements of an adult ritual in the burial of a child. "This expands our knowledge on Moche funerary practices," he added.

The sculpture of the presumed deity depicts a human body with bird wings and a bird tail, although for some scholars it might be interpreted as a god in transformation, Rengifo-Chunga said. The deity holds a square shield in one hand and a goblet in the other, and is flanked by two bird "assistants" wearing ceremonial garments.

Researchers will have to investigate whether the deity is a known Moche god, or a newfound addition to the pantheon. Either way, the object joins a contentious debate over whether the Moche pantheon consists of many gods, or just a few that can transform into many different versions, Rengifo-Chunga said.

The artifacts are now in the laboratories of the Santiago Uceda Castillo Museum in Peru, where they will undergo conservation treatment.

#4264. Claude Code vs. Cursor: What a Field Test Shows That the Feature Comparisons Miss

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Publish AI, ML & data-science insights to a global community of data professionals.

One benchmark says Claude Code used 33,000 tokens where Cursor used 188,000. Another says the exact reverse. Here is the field test that actually settles it for your team.

If you search "Claude Code vs Cursor," you get a wall of feature tables. Tab autocomplete versus terminal agents. Multi-model versus Claude-only. A column of green checkmarks on each side, and a conclusion that almost always lands on "it depends, most teams use both."

All of that is true, and almost none of it helps you decide. I noticed it most clearly in the benchmarks. One popular comparison reports that Claude Code completed a task in about 33,000 tokens while Cursor burned through 188,000 on the same work. Another comparison reports the opposite, Cursor at roughly 33,000 and Claude Code at 188,000. Both numbers are real. Both were measured. And the fact that they contradict each other by the same multiple, in opposite directions, tells you everything about why a single benchmark cannot answer this question.

Token cost on a coding task is not a property of the tool. It is a property of the task, the codebase, and how the human drove the agent. Change any of those and the winner flips. So the right way to choose between Claude Code and Cursor is not to read someone else's benchmark. It is to run a small field test on your own work, measuring the thing that actually matters, which is almost never the thing the feature tables measure.

This is how I would run that field test, what it reliably reveals, and how to turn the result into a decision. The methodology is the same one I apply to any agent deployment, which is to measure cost per successful outcome rather than cost per task, and it changes the answer.

Learn this step by step with the interactive AI Engineer roadmap.

Before the field test, the honest shape of the two products, because the difference is architectural and it drives every result you will measure.

Claude Code is agent-first and terminal-native. It runs in the terminal, with IDE extensions and a desktop and web surface on top. The interaction model is that you brief it and it goes and does the work, often across many files, over a long horizon, with hooks and sub-agents for larger jobs. It runs on Claude models only. Its natural habitat is the autonomous task: a multi-file refactor, a migration, a bounded feature you can describe and walk away from, and anything that runs in CI without a human in the loop.

Cursor is IDE-first. It is a fork of VS Code, so the interaction model is that you are in the editor driving, and the agent assists: tab autocomplete, inline edits, a Composer agent for larger changes, and a visual diff you review line by line. It is model-flexible, letting you route to Claude, GPT, Gemini, and its own model depending on the task. Its natural habitat is the interactive loop: front-end and full-stack work where you want to see and steer every change, tight edit-run-review cycles, and the smoothest path from idea to a reviewed pull request.

That is the real fork in the road. One tool assumes the human briefs and the agent executes. The other assumes the human drives and the agent accelerates. Every token-cost number, every "which is faster" claim, and every benchmark is downstream of which of those two modes the task actually wanted. That is why the public numbers contradict each other, and why yours will too until you tie them to your own work.

Three things go wrong in almost every "Claude Code vs Cursor" comparison I read.

They measure cost per task, not cost per shipped change. A tool that is cheap per task but produces changes a human has to rework twice is not cheap. The only unit that means anything is the cost to get one correct, merged change into production, including the human oversight time, and no feature table measures that.

They benchmark one task and generalize. The 33,000-versus-188,000 contradiction is the proof. A single task is a single point on a distribution. An autonomous multi-file refactor and a one-line interactive fix sit at opposite ends of that distribution, and the tools invert depending on where the task lands. A benchmark that is not weighted to your actual task mix is noise dressed as data.

They ignore where each tool fails, which is the expensive part. Cursor's interactive model gets awkward on long-horizon autonomous jobs, where you do not want to babysit every diff. Claude Code's agent-first model gets awkward on tight visual or front-end loops, where seeing and steering each change is the point. The failure modes, not the feature lists, are what cost you time in production.

Here is the field test, and it takes about a week. It is deliberately boring, because boring is what produces a decision you can defend.

1. Sample your real work; do not invent a benchmark. Pull fifteen to twenty recent tickets that represent your actual mix: some interactive front-end changes, some autonomous multi-file refactors, some bug fixes, some migrations. The sample is the field test. If it does not look like your backlog, the result will not predict your backlog.

2. Run each ticket through both tools, with the same engineer. Same person, same ticket, both tools, so the variable is the tool and not the operator. This is tedious, and it is the point. You are building a small, representative distribution instead of borrowing someone else's single data point.

3. Measure cost per successful outcome, in four parts. For each ticket and each tool, record: whether the change was correct and merged without rework, the human oversight time it took, the token or dollar cost, and a one-line note on where the tool struggled. Correctness first. A cheaper tool that ships a wrong change is the most expensive option you have.

4. Read the distribution, not the average. Do not average the tokens into a single number, because that is how you reproduce the contradictory benchmarks. Look at which ticket types each tool won. The pattern that falls out is almost always the same shape, and it is more useful than any single figure.

I am deliberately not going to hand you a table of my own token numbers, because a number from my codebase predicts nothing about yours, and publishing one as if it were a benchmark is exactly the mistake this whole piece is about. The methodology is the deliverable. The number is the thing you have to measure yourself, which is the point. This is the same discipline we apply when we help a client stand up a custom AI development program: the field test is tied to their workload, not to a leaderboard.

Across real use, the distribution has a consistent shape, and it is the opposite of a single winner.

Cursor wins the interactive and line-level tickets. Front-end work, tight edit-run-review loops, anything where seeing and steering each change is the job. The tab autocomplete and inline edits inside a familiar editor are genuinely faster for this, and the visual diff review catches problems before they merge. For a team whose work is mostly in this bucket, Cursor is the lower cost-per-shipped-change, and the multi-model flexibility is a real advantage when you want to route a task to a cheaper or stronger model.

Claude Code wins the autonomous and multi-file tickets. Long-horizon refactors, migrations, bounded features you can brief and leave, and anything you want to run in CI without a human watching every step. The agent-first model and the hooks and sub-agents are built for delegation, and on these tickets the human oversight time drops enough to move the cost-per-outcome decisively in its favor, even when its raw token count is higher.

Which is why the honest answer, the one the feature tables reach by hand-waving and the field test reaches by measurement, is that most serious teams run both and route by task type. The decision you are actually making is not "which tool wins." It is "which task goes to which tool, and what is my default." The field test tells you your default by telling you where the bulk of your distribution sits.

For an individual developer the choice is taste and workflow. For an enterprise it is a security and governance decision, and here the two diverge in ways the field test above does not capture.

Both offer the enterprise table stakes at their top tiers: single sign-on, audit logs, and the controls a security review expects. At the time of writing, Cursor carries the more complete enterprise package and more certifications, which matters in regulated procurement where a certification is a gate, not a nicety. Claude Code leans on deployment routes through the major cloud platforms, which matters when your constraint is where the model runs and under whose contract. Pricing structure differs in a way that affects cost control more than the headline number: Cursor meters against a credit pool drawn down at model prices, while Claude Code meters against usage that resets, so the right plan depends on whether your usage is spiky or steady. Verify the current pricing and certifications directly before you standardize, because both move quarterly.

The deeper enterprise point is the one that applies to any agentic tool in production: the tool is a fraction of the cost, and the governance around it — who can run what, against which repositories, with which model, and how it's logged — is the larger and more durable decision. That is the same lesson that shows up every time we wire AI agents into business automation, where the model and the tool are twenty percent of the work, and the controls and integration are the other eighty.

Turn the field test into a default, not a religious war.

Run the one-week field test on your own tickets. Fifteen to twenty, same engineer, both tools, measured on cost per merged change. Do not skip to someone else's benchmark.

Set your default by where your distribution sits. Mostly interactive front-end and full-stack? Default Cursor. Mostly autonomous refactors, migrations, and CI-oriented work? Default Claude Code.

Keep the other one for the tail. The minority task type still belongs to the other tool. Routing by task beats standardizing on one for everything.

Decide procurement on governance, not features. Certifications, deployment route, audit logging, and cost-control structure, weighted to your constraints.

Is Claude Code or Cursor better in 2026?Neither is better in the abstract, because they optimize for different interaction models. Cursor is better for interactive, editor-centric, line-level work and multi-model flexibility. Claude Code is better for autonomous, terminal-based, multi-file, and CI-oriented work. The right answer for your team comes from a field test on your own task mix, not from a leaderboard.

What is the core difference between Claude Code and Cursor?Architecture. Claude Code is agent-first and terminal-native: you brief it and it executes, often across many files, on Claude models only. Cursor is IDE-first, a VS Code fork where you drive and the agent assists, with the option to route to Claude, GPT, Gemini, or its own model.

Which one uses fewer tokens, or costs less?It depends entirely on the task. Public benchmarks contradict each other because token cost is a property of the task and how the agent is driven, not of the tool. Claude Code tends to cost less on autonomous multi-file work once you count human oversight time; Cursor tends to cost less on tight interactive loops. Measure cost per merged change on your own tickets, not cost per task.

Can you use both Claude Code and Cursor together?Yes, and most serious teams do. The practical pattern is to route by task type: interactive and front-end work to Cursor, autonomous refactors and CI jobs to Claude Code. The decision is which task goes to which tool, with a default set by where the bulk of your work sits.

Which is better for enterprise?Both offer SSO, audit logs, and security controls at their top tiers. At the time of writing Cursor carries more certifications and a fuller enterprise package, while Claude Code offers more deployment routes through major clouds. The deciding factor is usually governance and deployment constraints, not the feature list, and both should be verified directly because they change quarterly.

The "Claude Code vs Cursor" question feels like it should have a winner, and the entire internet is happy to give you one, backed by a benchmark that contradicts the next one you read. The reason is simple: they are measuring cost per task on a single point, and the answer lives in the distribution of your own work, measured as cost per shipped change.

Run the boring field test. Set a default. Route the tail to the other tool. Decide procurement on governance. That takes a week, and it will outlast every feature table, because the tools will keep leapfrogging each other on features, and the thing that actually decides your cost, the shape of your task distribution and the discipline of your field test, will not.

#622. Chinese scientists discover “D-rex,” a giant fungus with roots stretching back 65 million years

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Scientists exploring the forests of Yunnan, China, have discovered an extraordinary giant fungus that is challenging previous assumptions about how large certain fungi can grow. The newly identified species, Dianjunus rex, produces upright reproductive structures that can reach 50 cm (20 inches) in height and weigh as much as 2.2 kg (4.9 pounds). These are the largest known fruiting bodies among fungi belonging to the phylum Ascomycota.

"This remarkable species represents a new fungal family, Dianjunaceae, and provides a rare window into the evolution of extreme fungal body forms," said Dr Indunil C Senanayake, an associate professor at the Germplasm Bank of Wild Species of the Kunming Institute of Botany in China and corresponding author of the study, published in Frontiers in Fungal Biology.

"The discovery illustrates how much fungus biodiversity remains to be discovered in East Asian forests."

Researchers from the Kunming Institute of Botany encountered the unusual fungus while surveying several forest environments in Yunnan. Their fieldwork included temperate coniferous forests near Damaidi and Yipinglang, along with tropical rainforest near Nanbang.

The team spotted unusually large, whitish reproductive structures, known as 'stromata', growing from the woody roots of Chinese cork oak trees. These structures emerged from the rhizosphere, the region of soil surrounding plant roots, and appeared to belong to an unidentified member of the ascomycete fungi.

Ascomycetes make up the largest major group of fungi, with more than 64,000 recognized species. Unlike familiar mushrooms, these fungi do not produce true mushroom structures. Instead, they generate spores within tiny sac-like structures found inside or on the surfaces of their fruiting bodies.

To investigate the discovery, the scientists collected samples and preserved reference specimens in the Kunming Institute of Botany's collections. They also cultivated spores under laboratory conditions to examine the fungus's physical characteristics and sequenced its genome, including its protein-coding genes.

Meet D-Rex, a Record-Breaking Fungal Giant

The genetic analysis, together with a close examination of the fungus's physical features, revealed that the discovery represented much more than a previously unknown species. It was sufficiently different from known fungi to require the creation of a new genus and an entirely new family (Dianjunaceae) within the order Xylariales.

The researchers named the species Dianjunus rex, drawing inspiration from its impressive size and the ancient Dian kingdom. Its nickname, 'D-rex', reflects its exceptional stature among related fungi.

Its closest known relative is Graphostroma platystoma, a fungus that grows on dead hardwood throughout the Northern Hemisphere. That species is the sole representative of its own family, Graphostromataceae.

Members of the order Xylariales are known for producing a variety of specialized chemical compounds and enzymes capable of breaking down complex molecules in plants. Researchers believe similar enzymes may allow 'D-rex' to decompose dead wood and obtain nutrients from it.

A 65-Million-Year Connection to Dinosaur Extinction

One of the most intriguing findings concerns the ancient evolutionary origins of the newly identified fungal family.

Using a technique known as a 'molecular clock', the scientists compared the fungus's DNA with genomic information from hundreds of other fungi whose evolutionary histories have been established. This analysis allowed them to estimate when the Dianjunaceae family first appeared.

Their results suggest that the lineage originated approximately 65 million years ago, roughly one million years after the Cretaceous-Paleogene mass extinction. That catastrophic event eliminated the non-avian dinosaurs along with countless other animal and plant species.

The timing raises the possibility that the enormous environmental changes following the extinction helped create favorable conditions for the ancestors of this unusual fungus.

"This timing raises an intriguing possibility. After the mass extinction, forests experienced dramatic changes, including the sudden availability of large amounts of dead plant material," said Senanayake. "For wood-associated decomposing fungi, such environmental disruption may have created new ecological opportunities."

Another mystery is how and why Dianjunus rex evolved such an extraordinary size.

Before its discovery, the tallest known ascomycete fruiting bodies measured approximately 20 to 30 cm. The newly identified fungus can reach 50 cm, making it substantially taller than the previous record holders.

One possible explanation involves how the fungus releases and spreads its spores. By extending high above the forest floor, its reproductive structures may be better positioned to catch moving air and distribute spores through dense vegetation.

However, growing such a large structure also requires considerable energy. The researchers suggest that this unusual evolutionary strategy would be advantageous only in environments where the benefits of greater spore dispersal outweigh the resources needed for growth.

"A fruiting body which towers above surrounding vegetation and debris could have better dispersal of spores by wind in dense forests. However, such a large structure requires substantial energy investment, suggesting that this strategy likely evolved only where the ecological benefits outweighed the costs," hypothesized Senanayake.

China's Forests May Harbor More Undiscovered Fungi

The discovery also highlights how much remains unknown about fungal diversity in China's subtropical forests.

Researchers believe the region is particularly well suited to the evolution of unusual fungal species because it contains a wide variety of habitats, has experienced relatively stable climatic conditions, and supports intricate relationships among living organisms.

Despite these favorable conditions, many fungi in the region have received little scientific attention. Remote and difficult terrain complicates fieldwork, while the seasonal appearance and often hidden nature of fungal reproductive structures make some species especially difficult to locate. Historically, research has also concentrated heavily on fungi with commercial or practical value.

The scientists expect that continued exploration of these forests will uncover additional species, potentially revealing more unexpected forms and evolutionary histories.

"This region combines exceptional habitat heterogeneity, climatic stability, and complex biotic interactions that promote fungal diversification. Historically, however, fungal diversity in these ecosystems has been underestimated due to logistical difficulties in field sampling, the seasonal and cryptic nature of fungal fruiting, and a historical emphasis on economically important fungi," said Senanayake.

For kidney patients with acid buildup, a decade-long trial suggests fruits and vegetables may offer what a standard treatment did not: fewer hospital visits without higher overall costs.

When kidneys lose their ability to function properly, the consequences can extend far beyond waste buildup. People with chronic kidney disease can also develop excess acid in their blood, a condition known as metabolic acidosis that can contribute to worsening kidney function and other serious complications. Doctors commonly treat this imbalance with sodium bicarbonate, but new research suggests that a surprisingly simple dietary approach could offer greater long-term benefits.

In a decade-long clinical trial, researchers found that patients who received fruits and vegetables to help neutralize excess acid experienced substantially fewer hospitalizations and emergency department visits than those receiving usual care. O

ver 10 years, the 36 patients in the dietary group had just 14 hospitalizations and 20 emergency visits, compared with 64 and 88, respectively, among the 36 patients receiving usual care. Only four patients in the dietary group required dialysis, compared with 16 in the usual-care group, raising intriguing questions about the role of nutrition in protecting kidney health.

The trial enrolled 108 adults with stage 3 chronic kidney disease (CKD) and metabolic acidosis, the medical term for excess acid buildup in the body. Researchers randomly assigned participants to three groups of 36, receiving fruits and vegetables, oral sodium bicarbonate, or usual care. The dietary approach used base-producing fruits and vegetables, which help counter acidity. “These findings support evaluating dietary interventions in chronic kidney disease not only at the individual-patient level but also at the population level, where the cumulative clinical and economic benefits may be substantial. #550. New 3D thermal invisibility cloak makes objects disappear from infrared cameras

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists have developed and tested the first three-dimensional thermal invisibility cloak, a device that can conceal objects from infrared cameras while protecting them from extreme temperatures. The breakthrough could lead to new ways to cool microchips, safeguard delicate electronics, and prevent equipment from being detected through its thermal signature.

The cloak can conceal objects with a wide variety of shapes, regardless of the direction from which heat approaches. Earlier thermal cloaks were limited to two dimensions or worked only when heat traveled in a particular direction. The new design overcomes these restrictions by directing heat around a hidden object rather than simply blocking it. As a result, infrared imaging shows a temperature pattern that makes the object appear to be absent.

The research was conducted by University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign civil and environmental engineering professor Shelly Zhang, postdoctoral researcher Weichen Li, and graduate student Yibo Wang, in collaboration with professor Ole Sigmund of the Technical University of Denmark. Their findings were published in Nature Communications.

"A real thermal cloak should work no matter where the heat comes from," Zhang said. "Our device can hide a complex 3D object in an infinite number of directions while keeping the temperature inside stable and protected."

A New Material Makes 3D Thermal Invisibility Possible

Creating a thermal cloak that functions in three dimensions has been a longstanding challenge. Previous experimental devices could redirect heat only across a flat surface or along one direction of heat flow, leaving them far short of the capabilities needed for a fully three-dimensional cloak.

To overcome these limitations, the researchers revisited the foundational principles of transformation thermotics, a field that explores how heat can be redirected through specially designed materials. They focused on a central question: "What kind of material structure could cover almost all the thermal properties needed for a perfect cloak?"

The solution was a new lattice-based material whose structure can be independently adjusted along three directions. These changes allow the researchers to control how efficiently heat passes through different parts of the cloak. Compared with previous designs, the material can achieve a much broader range of thermal conductivities, closely approaching the properties predicted to be necessary for ideal thermal cloaking.

Importantly, the researchers went beyond computer simulations and constructed a working device. They used 3D metal printing to produce an intricate aluminum lattice, which provides pathways for efficient heat conduction. They then used mold casting to fill the remaining spaces with a rubber-like material that conducts heat poorly. Together, these materials create a structure that can carefully control the movement of heat.

Infrared Camera Tests Confirm the Cloak Works

To evaluate the device, the team positioned it between hot and cold regions, establishing a temperature difference that caused heat to flow through the surrounding environment. They monitored the resulting temperature patterns with an infrared camera to determine whether the cloak could successfully redirect heat around the concealed area.

The experiments showed that the device could guide heat around the hidden region while preserving the surrounding temperature pattern. To an infrared camera, the area appeared much as it would if no object were present. At the same time, temperatures inside the cloak remained uniform, shielding the concealed space from the extreme conditions outside.

The researchers also tested whether the cloak could accommodate unusually complicated objects. They successfully concealed intricate three-dimensional geometries, including detailed shapes resembling human heads. According to the team, earlier experimental thermal cloaks have not demonstrated a comparable combination of geometric complexity and cloaking performance.

Potential Applications in Electronics and Stealth Technology

The ability to control heat in three dimensions could have important implications across several industries. In electronics, the technology could help engineers direct heat away from sensitive components, improve thermal management in microchips, and protect devices operating under demanding conditions.

The researchers also see potential applications in security and defense. Future versions of the technology might help conceal people or equipment from thermal detection systems or shield valuable assets from severe temperature conditions. These uses would build on the device's demonstrated ability to redirect external heat while keeping the protected region thermally stable.

"Any field that needs precise control of heat or needs to protect something from being detected thermally could benefit from this work," Zhang said. "But we also see it more broadly: it's about hiding and protecting information that is carried by heat."

The Next Challenge Is Hiding Objects That Generate Heat

Although the current cloak represents a major advance, the researchers want to develop more sophisticated versions capable of performing additional functions. One particularly important goal is to conceal objects that produce heat themselves, rather than only protecting them from heat originating outside the cloak.

Achieving this would require materials that can actively control heat within the concealed region. Instead of simply redirecting incoming heat, a future cloak would need to concentrate, distribute, or guide thermal energy as conditions change. Such capabilities could expand the technology's usefulness in electronics, thermal protection, and applications involving infrared detection.

"We've shown that a true 3D omnidirectional thermal cloak is possible," Zhang said. "The next step is to make cloaks that don't just hide and protect, but also actively manipulate heat in useful ways."

The research received support from the National Science Foundation, the Villum Foundation, and the Air Force Office of Scientific Research. Zhang is also affiliated with mechanical science and engineering and the National Center for Supercomputing Applications at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign.

#602. Tiny magnetic vortices could help computers work more like your brain

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Tiny magnetic structures called skyrmions could help pave the way for a new generation of computers that process information more like the human brain while using far less electricity. Although most people have never heard of skyrmions, these microscopic magnetic vortices may eventually play an important role in how computers store and process data.

A study published in the journal Nature Nanotechnology provides new insights into how skyrmions behave and how scientists can control them. The findings could help researchers develop smaller, more efficient computing systems and advance emerging quantum technologies.

Most of the research was conducted at the Institute of Physics at Johannes Gutenberg University in Mainz, Germany, with contributions from a professor at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU).

"Skyrmions are small, magnetic vortices that can store information in a completely new way," explained Asle Sudbø, who leads NTNU's Centre for Quantum Spintronics (QuSpin).

Unlike conventional technologies, which require increasing amounts of space and energy to handle growing quantities of data, skyrmions could offer a much more efficient alternative.

"Instead of the current technology, where we constantly need more space and energy to process data, skyrmions could make it possible to build much smaller and more energy-efficient data storage systems," explained Sudbø.

Tiny Magnetic Vortices Could Enable Brain-Like Computers

The potential applications of skyrmions extend well beyond conventional data storage. They are also important to spintronics, which explores ways to use the magnetic properties of electrons in electronic devices, and to research involving topological quantum effects.

One particularly promising possibility is the development of computers that operate in ways similar to biological brains.

"These nanoscale vortices could help develop computers that resemble biological brains," said Sudbø.

Rather than relying on conventional transistor-based computing, such systems could use magnetism to process information. In principle, this approach could dramatically reduce electricity consumption, potentially allowing future computers to perform complex tasks with very little energy.

Turning that possibility into practical technology, however, requires scientists to understand exactly how skyrmions behave and how to manipulate them reliably.

The latest research represents an important advance toward that goal.

Scientists Film Magnetic Skyrmion Lattices Melting for the First Time

Skyrmions do not always exist as isolated magnetic structures. Under certain conditions, they organize themselves into repeating patterns known as lattices, resembling the orderly arrangement of atoms in a crystal, but on a much smaller scale.

Scientists can manipulate these arrangements, a capability that could be essential for using skyrmions in future computing devices.

In the new study, researchers achieved something that had not been demonstrated before. They recorded the melting of a magnetic skyrmion lattice as it happened while simultaneously controlling individual skyrmions.

"For the first time, researchers have managed to film how a magnetic skyrmion lattice melts in real time, while simultaneously being able to manipulate each individual skyrmion directly as they film it. For example, both their shape and size can be changed 'on the fly'," said Sudbø.

The ability to observe this process directly provides a clearer picture of how skyrmions respond when their organized structures break apart. It also allows researchers to study how changing the size and shape of individual magnetic vortices affects their behavior.

A New Step Toward Energy-Efficient Computing

Understanding how skyrmions behave during the breakdown of their lattices could help scientists develop more precise ways to control them. That control is particularly important if these magnetic structures are to become useful components in future computer systems.

The findings therefore offer more than a new way to observe an unusual magnetic phenomenon. They provide information that could help researchers overcome challenges involved in developing practical skyrmion-based technologies.

"The findings give us new insight into how these types of particles behave. This could therefore become important for the development of more energy-efficient computer technology in the future," said Sudbø.

Although substantial development is still needed before skyrmions can be used in everyday computing devices, the research highlights their potential for improving data storage, enabling new computing architectures, and supporting advances in quantum technology.

By learning to observe and manipulate these tiny magnetic vortices in real time, scientists are gaining capabilities that could eventually help reshape the design of computers.

"This could revolutionize the way we think about computers," concluded Sudbø.

NASA is overhauling how it selects and develops science missions, with changes intended to reduce costs, streamline competition and get missions into space faster.

The new approach, called Competition Model 2.0 (CM 2.0), was announced on Oct. 21 in an open letter from Nicky Fox, associate administrator of NASA's Science Mission Directorate (SMD), to the NASA science community. The changes, directed by NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman, could significantly reshape how scientists, universities, NASA centers and commercial companies vie for future missions.

"We have heard you. We have listened. We know that the burden of bringing great ideas forward has been too heavy for too long," Fox stated in the letter.

The new process aims to prioritize scientific merit over unduly complicated engineering and financial planning during initial evaluations, and reduce selection timelines to encourage innovation.

Fox and other top NASA science officials will provide an overview of the reforms during a virtual town hall today (Oct. 26) at 2 p.m. EDT (1800 GMT). A livestream of the event will be available here, with an opportunity for the public to submit questions here.

NASA currently selects many of its science missions through competitive Announcements of Opportunity (AOs), inviting scientists and research institutions to submit proposals. Under the existing two-step process, competing teams must include thorough engineering and financial details as part of their proposals before NASA selects finalists for further study. The process can take almost three years, requiring significant investments of time and resources from teams that may ultimately never get selected.

CM 2.0 aims to reduce that burden by emphasizing scientific distinction during initial evaluations, allowing teams to defer much of the engineering and financial planning until later. Instead, initial proposals will focus on the scientific objectives of a mission and a preliminary plan for how to accomplish them.

NASA organizes its science missions into different classes based on their size, cost and objectives, with selection procedures varying between them. These range from smaller Explorer missions to large, expensive flagships like the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST).

Under CM 2.0, programs including smaller Discovery planetary missions, larger New Frontiers spacecraft and Astro-Probes, a program that focuses on mid-sized astronomy missions, will retain a streamlined version of the existing two-step process, but with nearly half of the material required for initial proposals. "This means that the original 35-months timeline will be reduced to about 19.5 months, all while having a stronger, more mature mission at the time of selection," Fox wrote.

Flagship missions, meanwhile, will continue following their current procedures, which were recently revised based on lessons learned from a study of best practices examining the development of JWST.

Medium-class Explorer (MIDEX) and Earth Explorer missions will open to a wider range of applicants, including universities and commercial organizations, and will have similar changes implemented for their two-step selection process. NASA plans to evaluate prospective mission developers based on their track records with projects of similar size, cost and complexity, and offer principal investigator-led teams the opportunity to request support from NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center or Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) during the first stage of selection.

Smaller, lower-cost science programs will see a more substantial selection process shakeup. "Our smallest missions will be riding the momentum of a maturing commercial space economy and will move even faster," Fox wrote.

Under CM 2.0, these programs will move to a single-stage selection process, eliminating the need for a second round of competing proposals entirely.

These include the Small Explorer (SMEX) and Earth Venture missions, which conduct astronomy and heliophysics research, and targeted studies of Earth and its climate. Missions of Opportunity, which allow researchers to piggyback scientific investigations onto other missions, as well as hosted payloads and rideshares, will fall under the same one-step umbrella, which is expected to drop final selection timelines from approximately 32 months to just 19.

"This means more missions, more often, with more incredible science at a lower cost," Fox wrote.

Beyond shortening competition times, the changes put particular emphasis on the roles of Goddard and JPL for developing NASA's science missions. NASA hopes that involving those centers more directly in mission maturations will reduce competition between the centers while maintaining the expertise of the agency's workforce.

The reforms also come with stricter oversight, with NASA insisting on "rigorous cost and schedule control" across its mission classes. "Breaches of control thresholds will carry real consequences," Fox wrote, adding that such consequences could include mission cancellations in the face of unmet expectations.

Together, the changes represent an attempt to shift NASA's mission selection process to one focused on prioritizing scientific merit. The longer-term outcome of the new approach, the agency hopes, will reduce the financial and technical burden on competing research teams and ultimately get missions from idea to orbit with efficiency and speed.

"This is a moment of real opportunity," Fox wrote. "We have an administrator who is empowering us to move faster and a commercial market that is ready to help. The stunningly detailed gold cape is unique in Europe and may have belonged to a high-ranking woman.

The gold accessory was found in a Bronze Age grave near the town of Mold in Wales.

Where it is from: Bryn yr Ellyllon (Goblins' Hill), Wales

When it was made: Circa 1900 to 1600 B.C.

In 1833, stone quarry workers discovered an ancient burial mound at Goblins' Hill near the town of Mold in Wales. At the center was a stone-lined grave that contained a fragmented skeleton and a gold, cape-like accessory that had been crushed over time. The Mold gold cape has a unique shape and style, and it is one of the finest examples of prehistoric goldwork ever found, according to the British Museum, where the artifact is on display.

The cape dates to Britain's Early Bronze Age (2500 to 1200 B.C.), a time that saw a metalworking revolution as artisans shifted from crafting flint tools to making weapons and jewelry out of gold, copper and bronze.

When the cape was originally unearthed, experts assumed that it had belonged to an ancient warrior, chief or religious leader, due to the spectacular design of the object, Benjamin Roberts, then curator for the European Bronze Age collections at the British Museum, told the BBC in 2010. But after the broken pieces of the cape were put back together, the small size of the artifact suggested it may have been worn by a woman or a child.

The Mold gold cape measures roughly 9.3 inches (23.5 centimeters) tall and 18.3 inches (46.5 cm) wide. It weighs about 1.2 pounds (560 grams) and was probably crafted from a single lump of gold about the size of a ping-pong ball.

The gold ingot was beaten incredibly thin, fashioned into a cape, and then embellished with decorations that mimicked beads or necklaces, according to the British Museum. Additionally, several hundred amber beads were found on the cape in the grave, which likely added to the gilded appearance of the accessory. Perforations around the neck opening suggest the cape originally had a lining made of leather or another perishable material that did not survive.

Because the gold cape, when worn, would restrict a person's upper arm movements, it may have been a ceremonial accessory that conferred religious authority and was worn only on special occasions. But experts are still unsure why the cape was buried in a grave. Nor can they confirm whether the person it was interred with was male or female, as the skeleton was not recovered by the quarry workers in the 19th century.

"The cape is unique — there is really nothing to compare it to in the whole of Europe," Roberts told the BBC. "This makes it more difficult to understand why it was made and what significance it had."

#746. Toasted Orzo Chicken Soup

Chicken noodle soup just got way more interesting. Inspired by Egyptian bird tongue soup, this warming spice-tinged chicken broth is as comforting as it is flavorful. The real star here is the orzo. Toasted in butter until it’s deeply golden and nutty-fragrant, the pasta takes on a new personality. Unlike plain noodles, which quickly go limp in hot soup, well-toasted orzo stays al dente for longer, holding its shape and texture until you’ve doled out every bowl.

Any store-bought chicken broth, stock, or bouillon paste offers an extra boost of flavor to the base that chicken thighs and water alone can’t compete with—for an even more robust option, go for chicken bone broth.

More nourishing soup recipes this way: Curried Yellow Squash Soup; Broken Lasagna Noodle Soup With Spinach; Spicy Cod and Potato Chowder.

Season 1¼ lb. skinless, boneless chicken thighs (4–5) all over with 1 tsp. Diamond Crystal or ½ tsp. Morton kosher salt.

Heat 3 Tbsp. extra-virgin olive oil in a large pot over medium. Add 1 tsp. crushed fennel seeds, ½ tsp. freshly ground pepper, ½ tsp. ground cardamom, and ¼ tsp. ground cloves and cook, stirring, until fragrant, about 20 seconds. Add chicken, one 1" piece ginger, peeled, finely grated, 4 cups low-sodium chicken broth or chicken stock, and 3 cups water. Bring to a simmer over medium-high heat. Partially cover pot, reduce heat to medium-low, and simmer until an instant-read thermometer inserted into thickest part of a thigh registers at least 165°, 15–18 minutes. Transfer chicken to a cutting board and let cool slightly. Reduce heat to low and cover pot. Using 2 forks, shred chicken.

Melt 4 Tbsp. (½ stick) unsalted butter in a large skillet over medium heat. Add 1 cup orzo and cook, stirring often, until golden brown and fragrant, about 5 minutes. #3622. Stefano Boeri Architetti to transform industrial site into residential neighbourhood in Tbilisi

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Italian studio Stefano Boeri Architetti has revealed plans to overhaul a former factory on an industrial site in Tbilisi, Georgia, and add a series of colourful housing blocks alongside it.

The studio recently won the competition to design the Loco Park masterplan, which will add housing, green spaces and community spaces to the Georgian capital.

Aiming to celebrate the site’s industrial heritage, Stefano Boeri Architetti will renovate an existing Soviet-era locomotive factory, rebuilding some of its former architectural features with a contemporary spin.

“The new Loco Park neighbourhood will combine the memory of the large Soviet-era locomotive factory with a vision of a contemporary, vibrant space that is green and open to everyone,” said Stefano Boeri Architetti founder Stefano Boeri.

“The buildings of Loco Park will be islands in an urban archipelago, distinct yet connected by a network of tree-lined pedestrian public spaces for the whole city to enjoy.”

A series of new buildings will be built on the site, providing a mix of homes and community spaces dispersed in an open green space populated with sports facilities, parks and boulevards.

Apartments will occupy the upper levels of the buildings, while shops, cafes and workshops will be located on the ground floors.

Landscaping will connect with the surrounding city, providing public space for the wider community in Tbilisi.

Renders of the Loco Park masterplan show a colourful series of block buildings of different rectangular forms, which Stefano Boeri Architetti said are designed to have their “own identity while remaining integrated into the context”.

Stefano Boeri Architetti carried out colour mapping research in Tbilisi to identify colours commonly found in the city’s architecture, which influenced the brick finishes chosen to clad the buildings, including blue-grey, terracotta, green, taupe and white.

“We set out to capture Loco Park’s identity through a simple, balanced and contemporary design, inspired by Tbilisi’s extraordinarily rich colour palette, local materials and traces of the area’s industrial history,” said Stefano Boeri Architetti partner Pietro Chiodi.

“Colour and brick thus give each building its own identity while bringing them together into a coherent whole.”

The buildings will also be distinguished by varying roofline silhouettes, including vaulted, sawtooth and pitched roofs, designed to recall the shapes of traditional industrial buildings.

Terraced roofs will be topped with planting and, elsewhere in the development, greenhouses and communal spaces will be created as spaces for residents to socialise.

Other projects recently unveiled by Stefano Boeri Architetti include plans to transform a historic transportation depot in Rome into a greenery-covered public space, and designs for a monastery in Milan with a sail-like roof.
