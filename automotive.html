#3579. fiat’s tiny multiplina brings 1950s italian microcar design into the electric age

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

inspired by the 1956 fiat 600 multipla, the electric concept fits four passengers into a retro-inspired body at just under ten feet long.

Fiat has presented its Multiplina concept at the 2026 Paris Motor Show, introducing a tiny electric four-seater inspired by the original 1956 Fiat 600 Multipla. Measuring just over three meters (9.8 feet) long, the new concept revisits an idea that shaped some of the Italian automaker’s most inventive early vehicles: how much space can a car offer while taking up as little room as possible? With its rounded body, cheerful orange paintwork and contrasting cream roof, the Multiplina brings the familiar character of Fiat’s small city cars into a new generation of electric mobility.

The reference is the original Fiat 600 Multipla, which managed to accommodate six passengers within a body measuring only 3.5 meters long. Introduced seventy years ago, the unusual little people carrier challenged conventional automotive proportions by pushing the passenger compartment forward and making efficient use of its compact footprint. The Multiplina follows a similar approach, with an upright windshield and a short front end that give most of the vehicle’s length over to its occupants. Alongside the Multiplina, Fiat also unveiled its Multiplay concept in Paris — check it out here.

The Fiat Multiplina takes the shape of a small, almost continuous volume, with its cream-colored upper section wrapping around the windows and extending toward the front. Below, the orange bodywork has gently inflated surfaces and short overhangs. The wheels sit close to the corners, emphasizing the generous cabin relative to the vehicle’s size. Round, color-coordinated wheel covers reference Fiat’s earlier small cars, while the tiny rectangular headlights introduce a contemporary detail to the otherwise retro appearance.

A few recognizable elements connect the concept more directly to the 1956 Multipla. The horizontal lines across the front recall the original car’s distinctive moustache-like treatment, while similar ribbed detailing appears across the rear hatch. Vertical oval taillights sit at either edge of the body, leaving the rear surface largely uninterrupted. Along the sides, expansive windows reveal striped seating inside, and a rolled fabric roof adds another familiar touch from Fiat’s open-air city cars.

Despite its small proportions, the Multiplina accommodates four passengers across two rows of seats. It measures 1.4 meters (4.6 feet) wide and 1.53 meters (5 feet) high, dimensions that place it close to Fiat’s two-seat Topolino, although the additional length makes room for the rear passengers. The long side windows and forward-positioned windshield reinforce this emphasis on interior space. Even the car’s simple, upright silhouette contributes to the packaging, allowing the cabin to occupy a substantial portion of its overall volume.

The concept uses a fully electric L7 heavy quadricycle architecture, a European vehicle classification that places it between lightweight urban runabouts and conventional passenger cars. Fiat describes the proposed category as a Multi-Purpose Quadricycle, combining the compactness of a microcar with the practicality of four seats. The company has yet to announce the Multiplina’s battery capacity or driving range, although the concept is intended to offer greater versatility than the smaller Topolino.

The Multiplina forms part of Fiat’s broader approach to micromobility, which already includes the Topolino and the three-wheeled Tris. Within that family, the new concept occupies an interesting position. Its four-seat configuration makes room for passengers beyond the driver and a companion, while its dimensions remain suited to narrow city streets and limited parking space. Fiat sees potential for the vehicle in short journeys and urban commuting, where the size of a conventional car can feel excessive.

The Multiplina draws attention to how much can fit inside a small footprint, and how an approachable, slightly eccentric little car can still offer a practical response to urban mobility. For now, it remains a concept, with production details yet to be confirmed. Its proportions, however, make a convincing case for the continued relevance of the microcar.

#4082. After 30 years, scientists finally have a drug for a deadly brain disease

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

The FDA has approved the first drug designed to directly treat Alexander disease, an extremely rare neurological disorder that progressively damages the nervous system and can be fatal. The treatment, called Zanvastro, represents a major advance for patients who previously had no options beyond managing their symptoms.

Alexander disease can interfere with movement, thinking, and automatic bodily functions such as breathing and heart rate. Zanvastro is the first and only approved disease-modifying treatment for the condition, meaning it targets an underlying biological process responsible for the disease rather than simply relieving its effects.

The drug emerged from more than three decades of scientific research at the University of Wisconsin-Madison's School of Veterinary Medicine and Waisman Center. Much of that work took place in the laboratory of Albee Messing, professor emeritus of comparative biosciences and former director of the Waisman Center, whose discoveries helped make the treatment possible. Ionis Pharmaceuticals collaborated in developing the drug.

Clinical trials offered encouraging results. Patients receiving Zanvastro generally experienced stabilization of their motor function, and some showed improvements in their ability to move.

"This is a wonderful and long-awaited day for the Alexander disease community," Messing says.

Alexander Disease: A Rare and Devastating Brain Disorder

Alexander disease affects fewer than one in every million people worldwide. Although exceptionally uncommon, its consequences can be severe, with symptoms that gradually interfere with everyday activities and essential bodily functions.

People with the disorder may develop seizures, lose previously acquired developmental abilities, experience increasing difficulty walking or moving, and struggle with speaking or swallowing. Some also develop dangerously elevated pressure inside the brain.

The disease does not follow a single predictable course. Symptoms may first emerge during infancy, childhood, or adulthood, and their severity and progression can differ depending on when the condition begins.

For decades, doctors could offer supportive care and treatments to ease individual symptoms, but they had no approved medication capable of targeting the disease itself.

An Unexpected Discovery Revealed the Genetic Cause

The breakthrough can be traced to research Messing conducted with Michael Brenner, professor emeritus of neurobiology at the University of Alabama at Birmingham. In the late 1990s, their work identified the genetic changes responsible for Alexander disease, providing scientists with a specific biological target for developing treatments.

The discovery began with an unexpected result in laboratory mice.

Messing and Brenner had genetically engineered mice to produce unusually high amounts of a protein known as glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). This protein is normally found in astrocytes, specialized cells that help support and maintain healthy function in the brain and spinal cord.

To the researchers' surprise, the modified mice developed abnormal protein deposits throughout their brains. These deposits appeared inside astrocytes and closely resembled the distinctive abnormalities seen in people with Alexander disease.

"We had, really without intending to, created a mouse that produced these classic lesions of Alexander disease," Messing says.

The abnormal deposits, called Rosenthal fibers, are a defining characteristic of the disorder. Additional genetic studies involving people diagnosed with Alexander disease confirmed that mutations in the gene responsible for producing GFAP were the underlying cause.

"This provided an obvious and specific strategy for therapy -- the GFAP protein," Messing explains.

Identifying the genetic cause also made diagnosing the condition much simpler. Instead of relying on invasive brain biopsies to look for characteristic tissue abnormalities, doctors could use genetic testing from a blood sample to identify the mutations associated with the disease.

Scientists Develop a Drug That Targets Harmful Protein Buildup

Once researchers understood the role of GFAP, their next challenge was finding a way to reduce its production and prevent damaging levels of the protein from accumulating in brain cells.

Messing joined forces with Tracy Hagemann, an associate research professor at the Waisman Center, to create improved rodent models of Alexander disease. These models more closely reproduced important features of the disorder, giving scientists a way to investigate potential treatments before testing them in people.

The team focused on a straightforward idea: If excessive GFAP contributes to the disease, lowering the amount produced might help protect the nervous system.

In collaboration with Ionis Pharmaceuticals, the researchers pursued a technology known as antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs).

These treatments use short, specially designed pieces of genetic material to interfere with instructions that cells use to manufacture particular proteins. Rather than targeting every protein in a cell, an ASO can be designed to reduce production of a specific one.

For Alexander disease, that target was GFAP.

The experimental treatment produced promising results in rodents, supporting the decision to move forward with human testing. A clinical trial began in the summer of 2021.

Zanvastro uses this same targeted strategy. By limiting GFAP production, the drug aims to reduce the buildup of the protein before it can cause additional damage.

Because the treatment needs to reach the central nervous system, it is administered through an injection into the spinal canal. A trained healthcare professional gives the injection once every three months.

Clinical Trials Show Improvements in Walking and Movement

The human trial provided evidence that lowering GFAP could offer meaningful benefits to people living with Alexander disease.

Ionis Pharmaceuticals conducted the international study across 13 clinical sites, enrolling 54 patients to evaluate the treatment's safety and effectiveness.

After receiving Zanvastro for a little more than a year, treated patients demonstrated significantly better walking speeds than patients who had not received the drug.

The results also indicated that treatment could stabilize motor function, an important outcome for a progressive disorder that can steadily undermine a person's ability to move independently. Some participants experienced improvements in motor performance.

These findings helped establish Zanvastro as the first treatment approved to modify the underlying disease process in Alexander disease, rather than focusing exclusively on symptom relief.

The FDA approval covers people with Alexander disease across the full age spectrum, from infancy through adulthood.

Zanvastro is expected to become available in the United States in the weeks following the approval. Outside the United States, access to the drug will be handled through a licensing agreement between Ionis Pharmaceuticals and Recordati, a major pharmaceutical company headquartered in Italy.

A 30-Year Scientific Journey Offers New Hope

For Messing and his colleagues, the approval represents the culmination of more than 30 years of research, beginning with basic investigations into brain cell biology and eventually leading to a treatment for patients.

What started as an unexpected finding in genetically modified mice helped reveal the cause of an extremely rare disease, transformed how doctors diagnose it, and provided the foundation for developing a drug.

The achievement is also an example of translational research, the process of turning discoveries made in laboratories into treatments that can directly benefit patients.

Throughout that journey, individuals living with Alexander disease and their families played an essential role by supporting the research and participating in studies.

"The whole program of research would not have been possible without the support and participation from the patient and family community, to whom we will always be grateful," Messing says.

#4405. Scientists discover a hidden brain rhythm that could improve Parkinson’s treatment

📅 Aug 12, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) can reduce movement problems caused by Parkinson's disease, and new research is providing a clearer picture of why the treatment works. Scientists have found that its benefits appear to depend on stimulating a specific brain network that communicates primarily through a relatively fast beta rhythm (20 to 35 Hz).

The findings come from an interdisciplinary group of neuroscientists and clinicians at the University Hospitals of Cologne and Düsseldorf, Harvard Medical School and Charité Berlin. Published in the journal Brain, the study, 'The Deep Brain Stimulation Response Network in Parkinson's Disease Operates in the High Beta Band', is the first to bring together two approaches that have largely been studied separately: electrophysiology and brain imaging.

Pinpointing Where and How Brain Stimulation Works

"For the first time, we were able to characterize the DBS response network in Parkinson's disease in terms of space and time, simultaneously," says Professor Dr. Andreas Horn from the University of Cologne, who led the study and specializes in computational neurology. "We show that Parkinson's disease can best be treated if we stimulate a very precisely defined network. This network operates synchronized within a specific frequency band, and offers an explanation for how well patients respond to deep brain stimulation."

Deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus is already an established therapy for easing motor symptoms in people with Parkinson's disease. The treatment uses implanted electrodes to deliver small electrical pulses to areas located deep inside the brain.

Previous research has provided only part of the picture. Brain imaging studies have helped identify the locations where stimulation appears to work most effectively, while electrophysiological research has measured the frequencies of the electrical signals involved. Until now, researchers had not captured both the spatial location and timing of these signals at the same time.

To investigate this connection, the team studied a large multicenter group consisting of fifty patients and one hundred brain hemispheres. The scientists simultaneously recorded brain activity through the implanted DBS electrodes and with magnetoencephalography (MEG).

Using these recordings, they mapped functional connections between regions deep within the brain and areas closer to its surface.

Their analysis revealed that the important network connecting the subthalamic nucleus with frontal areas of the brain communicates largely at a comparatively fast frequency (20-35 Hz). Importantly, the strength of this connection was associated with how much individual patients' motor symptoms improved following electrode implantation.

A Brain Rhythm That May Shape Treatment Response

"These results suggest that a certain rhythm of the brain acts as a communication channel between the subthalamic nucleus and the cerebral cortex and may mediate the therapeutic effects of deep brain stimulation," explains Dr. Bahne Bahners, first author of the study, who works at Düsseldorf University Hospital. "By stimulating regions that are connected to the identified network, we will probably be able to adjust DBS settings more precisely in the future, especially in patients who have not yet benefited optimally from deep brain stimulation."

The findings could therefore provide a foundation for making deep brain stimulation more precisely tailored to an individual patient's brain network, particularly when existing DBS settings do not provide the desired level of symptom relief.

Researchers now plan to investigate more directly how deep brain stimulation causes changes within brain networks. Studies examining these causal effects are currently underway.

The study was largely funded by the Professor Klaus Thiemann Foundation.

Orbital sanders are available in both corded and cordless models. Corded sanders are your go-to for extended shop work. They provide constant power and eliminate the need to worry about battery life or work continuity. That’s why, for more extensive projects such as sanding cabinets or refinishing furniture, corded sanders still have the edge.

Cordless sanders have improved dramatically over the past few years—thanks to more powerful brushless motors and lithium-ion batteries that run longer and deliver more consistent sanding power. These sanders are a great option if you’re working around a house, on ladders, or in any situation where dragging a cord is a hassle.

Just keep in mind that sanding draws quite a bit of power, so batteries tend to drain faster than they do with tools such as drills and drivers. Keep a spare charged battery or two on your person—either in a tool belt or jacket pocket—for quick changes on the go.

Sander speed is measured in orbits per minute (OPM). Most random orbital sanders run somewhere between 7,000 and 13,000 OPM. Higher speeds remove material faster, but they also generate a lot more dust and heat. That’s why sanders include variable-speed controls, allowing you to slow things down for fine finishing or more delicate work as needed.

Some good uses of variable-speed controls include:

Higher speed: paint removal, rough shaping, leveling surfaces

Lower speed: finish sanding, veneer, between coats of finish.

Most sanders use 5-inch sanding discs, which are inexpensive and easy to find. Some larger models use 6-inch pads, which remove material faster and are more common in professional shops. For general DIY work, a 5-inch sander is perfectly capable and usually lighter and easier to handle, while 6-inch sanders are typically favored for those working on larger surfaces such as floors, tabletops, doors, and cabinetry, where the bigger pad speeds up material removal.

Anyone who’s sanded indoors knows that dust control matters. Even a short sanding project can leave a room filled with fine particulate matter. Most orbital sanders include a dust bag or canister that captures debris through holes in the sanding pad that line up with predrilled holes in sanding discs. And while these work reasonably well for small jobs, the bags and canisters fill quickly, and it becomes easy to neglect dumping them as you work.

The best solution is connecting your sander to a shop vac or dust extractor to ensure your own personal safety and a cleaner workshop. An added bonus is that sanding discs last longer as debris is cleared from the abrasive surface with proper extraction, which will save you money over time.

Orbital sanders might not look intimidating, but vibration can add up quickly during long sessions, leading to fast hand and arm fatigue. Sanding sessions on large projects require patience as you cycle through grits, which can quickly become exhausting and tedious due to the repetitive nature of the process. Good orbital sanders, such as the Bosch ROS20VSC, are designed with balanced motors, rubber overmoulds, and improved ergonomics to reduce fatigue.

You’ll notice the difference when using a sander with a comfortable grip and minimal vibration for an hour or more. If you plan on using a sander frequently, these are features we recommend you pay attention to.

Sanders don’t need a lot of care, but you can’t just ignore them either. The best thing you can do for a sander is to blow the dust out of it using compressed air (or a can of compressed air purchased at an office supply store). You can also use a shop vacuum and work over its exterior, especially its air vents.

To protect yourself, most notably your lungs, wear a dust mask when sanding. Better models have a foam strip to help the mask make a better seal to your face. They may also have a vent that reduces moisture buildup under the mask.

Clean up as you work to prevent large piles of talc-like dust from accumulating. And when you’re done sanding and ready to take a break, either brush or vacuum yourself off before going inside the house or other clean area. It’s also a good idea to wear an old shirt, coveralls, or a shop apron and leave that in the sanding area, rather than bringing dust into the house.

Having a floor mat outside the shop to wipe your feet is great, too—better yet, take your work shoes off. This cuts down on tracked dirt, which is more than just a nuisance. Remnants of sanding grit on shoe soles can scratch finished floors, and tracked-in dust can form an eye-watering and throat-scratching irritant as it spreads throughout the house.
