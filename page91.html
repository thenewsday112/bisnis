#1412. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

For more information, go to the Microsoft blog.

There is a dizzying array of thermal scopes on the market, and finding the right one for you typically starts with deciding how you’ll use it. Are you hunting hogs over bait? Or calling in predators in open country?

Those two use cases are on either end of the thermal scope spectrum. You really don’t need to spend $10,000 on a scope with a 1280 sensor for close-range hog hunting. A 640 or even 384 optic with a lower base magnification is plenty for that job. On the other hand, David Stroud, a serious competition predator hunter, once told me, “The best is barely good enough,” for his use case.

But, after testing the ATN Thor 6 -LRF, I found it’s a scope that can do just about anything. It’s not completely overgunned — or overpriced — for hog hunting, and the 640 variant is plenty capable for predator hunting. The added LRF, ballistic calculator, and easy controls make it a solid all-around optic. Here’s what I think of it after initial testing and a detailed comparison of the 13 different Thor 6 models.

The Thor 6 LRF has a 1,000-meter rangefinder built into the scope. There’s a dedicated ranging button that’s easy to find in the dark. If you have the ballistic calculator set up, the adjusted aiming point will automatically appear. This system makes shooting at night much easier because you have zero depth perception through a night optic.

The Thor 6 has the form factor of a traditional scope, which makes it easier to use in the pitch dark. Instead of a series of buttons, you have a combination of knobs and buttons for your critical adjustments. The focus control is where you’ll typically find the parallax knob on a traditional scope. You’ll navigate the menu using the elevation turret. The zoom and ocular focus controls are in the same spot as a standard scope. The buttons to start recording video and range targets are large and easy to reach.

One of the hardest parts of getting a thermal scope into the field is figuring out the menus, zeroing it, and just figuring out how the thing works. I’ve tested dozens of thermals and I still get tripped up by some optics. ATN saves you from thumbing through the owner’s manual and watching YouTube tutorials to figure out your new scope. There’s a built-in AI assistant within the ATN app. You can ask it any questions you have, and it will give you the answers. The app is also where you can review your video recordings, stream a live view, and adjust settings.

Thermal scopes are expensive and they have delicate sensors that can be damaged by sunlight. One of the easiest ways to damage your scope is to leave the objective lens uncovered in direct sunlight. ATN helps you mitigate that by outfitting the scope with a lens cap that’s actually quite good and a neoprene cover. They’re nice accessories to have straight out of the box.

Much is made of a thermal scope’s resolution and image quality. But, I think the big differentiator is the user experience. I want a scope that’s easy to zero, easy to adjust, and that I can use while blindfolded. Fumbling around in the dark for the focus or burning a box of ammo to get zeroed are the experiences I avoid like a coyote with mange.

So I was relieved that the ATN Thor 6 was easy to mount and get zeroed. I set one of the included thermal targets at 50 yards and opened up the zeroing setting. Then I fired a shot and checked its location with a spotting scope. Next, I adjusted the zero by moving the reticle from my aim point to the impact location. It’s a simple process, and you can even freeze the image to make it more precise. My next shot was within a few inches of my aim point, and after a small adjustment, I was dead on with my third shot. I moved the target to 100 yards to get my final zero, which required only two shots. So with five shots, I had the scope zeroed and the chronograph data I needed to set up the ballistic calculator.

After zeroing and setting up the ballistic calculator, I started shooting targets at various ranges. The ballistic calculator worked as I expected, with first round impacts at all distances. I’ve had some scopes that refresh constantly and seriously disrupt your ability to scan for animals. The Thor 6 has long a period between refreshes, which I appreciated. After shooting this scope on the range, I can’t say enough good things about how easy the menu is to navigate and how intuitive the controls are.

I’ve spent the last few years falling down the thermal optics rabbit hole, doing everything from an annual trip to Texas to test the latest in night optics to managing predators on my lease. While I’ve tested a ton of different optics, the one constant has been a Horizon Firearms rifle in .22 Creedmoor. The Horizon rifles are unquestionably accurate, handy, light, and the best turnkey coyote gun I’ve used. My current rifle is a Vandal 2 with an 18-inch barrel and chambered in .22 Creed. It shoots 80-grain Hornady ELD Match at 3,018 fps and easily maintains sub-MOA accuracy. The bare rifle weighs around 7 pounds, which is light enough to be handy but still heavy enough for stability. On the muzzle end, I added a Dead Air Nomad Ti OTB, which is an over-the-barrel suppressor. Its design keeps the gun short because only half the suppressor extends beyond the muzzle. The ATN Thor 6 rounds out the build nicely because it’s a truly versatile optic.

#3593. Paralelo Transversal organises Chilean house under cantilever roof

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Chilean studio Paralelo Transversal has designed a rectangular house with a large hip roof and a terrace that cantilevers over a ravine in Puerto Varas, Chile.

Completed in 2025 on a 1.2-acre site near Lago Llanquihue, the 155-square-metre house, known as Casa Ensenada, was oriented around its roof.

“In southern Chile, rain is not an exception but part of everyday life, so we wanted the roof to become more than a protective element,” architect Sebastián Silva of Paralelo Transversal told Dezeen.

“It creates a place beneath the rain, extending daily life beyond the enclosed rooms while bringing the different parts of the project together under one continuous gesture.”

The roof serves as the organising element for the program, circulation, relationship to the landscape and the home’s structure.

“Its section thickens toward the center while remaining remarkably thin at its perimeter,” Silva said.

“This allows it to be perceived as a light, continuous sheet while providing the structural depth required for long-span trusses capable of resisting both Chile’s seismic conditions and the strong winds of Patagonia.”

The house sits on a sloped site with a stream. Residents and visitors approach along the stream before the access ramp turns towards the house, where they enter at the junction of public and private zones.

The narrower side of the plan contains a large, open kitchen, dining and living room that bleeds onto the cantilevered terrace.

Oriented toward the northeast to capture winter sunlight, the house opens its living spaces to the landscape – the valley of Vicente Pérez Rosales National Park on one side and Calbuco Volcano on the other – through floor-to-ceiling windows with thin black frames.

The other side of the house widens to hold three suites. On either side of the house, the perimeter wall pushes under the roof canopy to form a covered bench and a planted garden.

The warm, contemporary material palette continues across the exterior and interior with exposed concrete slabs, large spans of glazing, tongue-and-groove timber and standing-seam metal roofing.

The concealed joints allowed the roof to be precise while reducing the visual impact of construction tolerances. The team collaborated closely with engineers and local builders to achieve its long spans and thin edge.

“Looking back, we believe the project fulfilled its original ambition of integrating architecture and landscape without compromising comfort, construction logic or budget,” Silva said.

“More importantly, it reinforced our belief that a project becomes stronger when one clear architectural idea is capable of organizing structure, climate, materiality and space simultaneously.”

Previously, Paralelo Transversal created a sail-like skylight that brought sun, shadows and air into a home on the outskirts of Santiago.

Other homes recently completed in Chile include a mass timber forest hideaway by Iragüen Viñuela Arquitectos and a seaside home with stacked terraces by Croxatto and Opazo Architects.

#2269. Bob Woodward Reveals He Spoke With Justices Stewart, Powell, White, Blackmun, and Rehnquist for "The Brethren"

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

Steven Goldberg, one of Brennan's clerks, provided Woodward with the 75-page case history of U.S.v. Nixon.

Bob Woodward is publishing a new book, titled "Secrets," that reveals many of the reporter's sources. The Washington Post has published an in-depth profile.

Of special interest are the sources Woodward relied on with Scott Armstrong for The Brethren (1979).

In the memoir, Woodward clears up another decades-long mystery: Which Supreme Court justices talked to him for the 1979 bestseller "The Brethren"? The exposé of the inner workings of the nation's highest court under Chief Justice Warren Burger electrified Washington.

When the book was published, Woodward and his co-author, Scott Armstrong, disclosed that they had interviewed five of the nine justices on the court, as well as 170 former law clerks, but on the condition that they not be named. Given the court's reputation for maintaining a Sphinxlike silence, "The Brethren" ignited a guessing game in Washington and in legal circles around the country, as well as criticism over the book's heavy reliance on anonymous sources.

Nearly a half-century later, Woodward finally names the five justices who granted interviews: Potter Stewart, Lewis Powell, Byron White, Harry Blackmun and William Rehnquist (all are now deceased). Woodward's memoir explains how he and Armstrong painstakingly cultivated the justices and their former clerks as sources. The authors kept long memos about the interviews, which Woodward draws on to quote the justices at length in his new book.

It is curious that Woodward's sources were all the conservatives on the Court. (During the late 1970s, even after Roe v. Wade, Blackmun was still considered a conservative.) Woodward apparently did not talk with Justices Brennan, Marshall, or Stevens. Indeed, throughout the entire WaPo piece, nearly every one of Woodward's sources were conservatives. (I'll go through more of them below). I wonder what Chief Justice Burger would think if he knew that the other three Nixon appointees were the sources of the leaks.

Yet, even if Brennan did not talk directly to Woodward, one of Brennan's clerks leaked the critical document about the Watergate Tape Case. Steven Goldberg clerked for Brennan during the 1974-75 Term. Goldberg's co-clerk was Judge Marsha Berzon.

Woodward also tells the story of how got his hands on an internal Supreme Court document that was a cornerstone of "The Brethren": a confidential 75-page case history chronicling the justices' tense deliberations in U.S. v. Nixon. In an 8-0 decision, the court ruled in the 1974 landmark case that Nixon had to turn over his secret tape recordings in response to a federal subpoena and that, most importantly, the president is not above the law.

In "Secrets," Woodward writes that he learned about the existence of the case history from Steven P. Goldberg, a former Supreme Court law clerk who died in 2010. But Goldberg was adamant: He couldn't possibly give Woodward a copy.

Woodward wheedled and coaxed and pleaded. Despite Goldberg's repeated protestations, Woodward talked the clerk into letting him pay a visit to his home late that night.

Goldberg still refused to cough up the document, however, so Woodward suggested a compromise: How about you read it aloud while I take notes?

"My plan was to wear him out until I either had enough of it down or he eventually gave it to me," Woodward recalls.

With a sigh, Goldberg started reading from a comfortable living room chair. Woodward smiled and took notes — but he did so very, very slowly and kept asking Goldberg to pause.

After a while, Goldberg fell deeply asleep. Woodward contemplated grabbing the case history from his lap and bolting out the front door, but "that seemed a little too far, even for me," he recalls.

Instead, he walked over to Goldberg, gave him a slight nudge and informed him that he would take the document and bring it back first thing in the morning.

Goldberg stirred with a look of "absolute exhaustion" on his face, according to the memoir. But the former clerk mumbled okay — and that was good enough for Woodward.

"Not wanting to disturb him more, I gently took it. I did not want to run out the door but I moved swiftly," he writes. "If I was on a security camera, I wanted to be behaving properly, with whatever might be left of my dignity."

Woodward scampered home to read the whole thing. The case history proved invaluable, a "raw and unforgiving" and "Holy Grail" account of the court's inner workings.

Before returning it to Goldberg the next day, he stopped at The Post's newsroom to make a photocopy.

One wonders if Justice Brennan tacitly or expressly authorized this leak, so long as the document was not provided. While the Chief Justice would often warn that leakers would have their careers ruined, Goldberg did not suffer at all. He would become a law professor at Georgetown, advocating for a strict separation of church and state. Goldberg died in 2010.

I doubt Josh Gerstein had to take notes while someone read him the Dobbs opinion aloud.

One such leaker was C. Boyden Gray, who served as White House counsel to George H.W. Bush during Bush's presidency and vice presidency. Woodward describes Gray, who died in 2023, as "one of my lifelong secret sources." He also lived just a block and a half away from Woodward in Georgetown, which made passing sensitive documents much easier.

One Friday in February 1987, as the Iran-contra scandal was unfolding, Gray walked over to Woodward's house bearing a gift: a three-page memo labeled "top secret" regarding an Israeli official involved in the sale of U.S. arms to Iran. According to Woodward, the document contradicted the Reagan administration's public assertions about the scope of its contacts with Iran, and even he wondered at first why Gray had given it to him. He said Gray later explained that "he knew the memo would get out" eventually and "he wanted to get ahead of it."

Woodward also reveals that President Nixon's lawyers leaked information about their client.

As Nixon's defense counsel, Buzhardt listened to hundreds of hours of conversations that the president had secretly taped in the White House but resisted turning over to prosecutors until he was finally ordered to do so by the Supreme Court. In public, Buzhardt vigorously defended Nixon and was seen as "the operational man in charge of the Watergate cover-up," according to Woodward. In private, however, his assessment of Nixon was caustic.

Of all the leaks, these bother me especially. Lawyers have a duty of confidentiality. If they no longer could ethically represent a client, their recourse was to withdraw. It is unconscionable for lawyers to defend their client in court while undermining their client in the press. You wonder why Trump relies on lawyers he can trust. Because those he cannot trust are likely to attack him in the media. Not much has changed.

A hypothetical particle could have briefly held more energy than everything else in the infant universe combined, which could help explain how matter won out over antimatter.

A neglected "ghost particle" may have dominated the universe right after the Big Bang ‪—‬ changing how the cosmos expanded in its first moments and reshaping the conditions under which matter won out over antimatter, a new study suggests. In other words, it may explain why anything exists at all.

The particle, a hypothetical "right-handed" neutrino, briefly could have held more energy than everything else in the infant universe combined, which would have sped up cosmic expansion and delayed key particle interactions, the study proposes. The research was published Sept. 8 in the journal Physical Review Letters.

Neutrinos are ghostly particles that rarely interact with anything. A typical neutrino from the sun could pass through a slab of lead a light-year thick — about 5.9 trillion miles (9.5 trillion kilometers) — before striking a single atom. Neutrinos come in three types, or "flavors," and switch between them as they travel, making use of a subtle quantum effect that works only if they have mass. Yet the Standard Model of particle physics predicts they should be massless.

One popular fix involves "right-handed" neutrinos. Particles spin a bit like tiny tops, and physicists call them left- or right-handed depending on how they spin relative to their direction of travel. A right-handed particle rotates like an ordinary screw being driven in, turning clockwise as it moves away from you. Every neutrino detected so far is left-handed and turns counterclockwise as it flies away, like a screw with a reverse thread.

"Right-handed neutrinos are hypothetical particles that do not feel the weak nuclear force, "unlike their left-handed counterparts, which are part of the Standard Model," study co-author Rishav Roshan, a theoretical physicist at the University of Southampton in the U.K., told Live Science via email. "They are characterized by having no electric charge and being much heavier."

"Right-handed neutrinos may mix with left-handed neutrinos, helping to explain the mass of the latter" and why they switch flavors, Roshan said.

The IceCube facility, photographed here beneath the Southern Lights, is Earth's premier neutrino detection facility. The proposed 'right-handed' neutrino has not yet been detected.

In quantum physics, particles that mix can exist as a blend of two identities at once. In a theoretical framework called the seesaw mechanism, each ordinary neutrino carries a faint trace of a heavy right-handed partner, and that trace gives it a tiny mass. The heavier the partner, the fainter the trace and the lighter the ordinary neutrino — hence the name, after the playground ride whose ends move in opposite directions.

The particles could solve other puzzles, too. They "aim to address several issues, including the origin of neutrino masses, the matter-antimatter asymmetry of the universe, and the nature of dark matter," Roshan said.

The Big Bang should have made equal amounts of matter and antimatter, which would have annihilated each other, forbidding the universe as we know it from ever taking shape. Right-handed neutrinos play a crucial role in models' proposals that the "observed matter-antimatter imbalance arose from processes involving these neutrinos in the early universe," Roshan said.

In this picture, decaying heavy neutrinos left behind a slight excess of leptons — the particle family that includes electrons — that later became today's surplus of matter, he explained.

The seesaw framework usually includes three right-handed neutrinos, but because two are enough to explain the data, physicists often ignore the third, assuming it is too heavy or interacts too feebly with matter.

In the new study, Roshan and Sudipta Show, a physicist at the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur, examined what this neglected particle — which, in the team's main example, was about 100 trillion times as heavy as a proton — would have done after the Big Bang.

At first, the particle would have zipped around at nearly light speed, so its energy would have faded like that of light, which would have both spread out and stretched to weaker wavelengths as space expanded. But as the cosmos cooled, the particle would have slowed down and most of its energy would have become locked in its mass, which would have been only spread out. Its share of the cosmic energy therefore would have grown until it dominated, and the long-lived particle would have stayed in charge until it decayed into other particles, according to the team's computations.

The study reveals that the neglected right-handed neutrino "significantly impacts the early universe's expansion dynamics, driving a period of early matter domination," Roshan said. This change "affects various Standard Model interactions"‬ that could help to explain the matter-antimatter asymmetry, Roshan added.

Each interaction would have switched on only once it outpaced the expansion of space. Because a universe dominated by the heavy neutrino would have expanded faster, the interactions would have had to wait until it cooled further.

These interactions would have let the early universe distinguish lepton flavors, which would have affected how the matter surplus formed. Thanks to the delay, the team found, a lighter right-handed neutrino about 100 billion times as heavy as a proton could have produced the observed amount of matter before the flavors could be told apart — even after the heavy neutrino's decay diluted part of it.

The heavy neutrino is far too massive to create in any particle accelerator, so tests must be indirect.

Gravitational waves — ripples in space-time — offer one such route. An era dominated by the heavy neutrino would have left a telltale kink in the background of early-universe gravitational waves, such as those from "cosmic strings" ‪—‬ hypothetical thread-like defects in space. The results can be tested through forthcoming facilities such as the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA) and the Square Kilometer Array Observatory (SKAO), Roshan noted.

LISA, a space-based European Space Agency mission, is scheduled to launch around 2035, while SKAO, a giant radio telescope project being built in Australia and South Africa, could catch such waves by timing distant pulsars. Tests could come "as these facilities come online and begin data collection," Roshan said. A faint hum from the hot primordial plasma would carry the era's imprint too, but at frequencies beyond what existing detectors could pick up.

#4411. Will the real Anthropic please stand up

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The company has been masterful at storytelling for years. Will the IPO reveal the company behind the mask or lean deeper into the myth-making?

This story originally appeared in The Algorithm, our weekly newsletter on AI. To get stories like this in your inbox first, sign up here.

After weeks of frenzied speculation, Anthropic has filed its IPO prospectus. The 335-page document (100 pages or so are focused on risks) lays out the company’s unique view of the technology it is building and the ways it believes it will change the world.

It also warns that there’s a fair chance that its tech could “erode trust, harm society and destabilize the geopolitical order” and, if things don’t go to plan, “pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity.”

“We encourage you, as a prospective owner of our stock, not to think of your purchase in purely transactional terms,” the company writes. “We hope you will see it instead as an opportunity to be part of something greater. The advent of machine intelligence is one of the seminal moments in human history.”

This kind of public messaging is a master class in narrative capture. But Anthropic has been honing its storytelling skills for years. Earlier this month, Jack Clark, the company’s cofounder and head of policy, gave a speech at the IMF meeting in Bangkok in which he announced to the assembled economists, bankers, and lawmakers that the singularity is already here.

Introduced by the science fiction writer and futurist Vernor Vinge around 40 years ago (and long debated on the nerdier fringes of the internet), the singularity is supposed to be the point at which technology gets so good that it outstrips the abilities of its makers.

Humans are left in the dust, the idea goes, no longer able to compete with—or even understand—the fruits of their former creation.

And this fictional world that many of us have imagined since we were kids? Apparently we’re already living in it: “I believe that this year we have gained convincing evidence that it has now begun,” Clark told his audience.

The company tells a vivid (and in many ways scary) story about a future it portrays as inevitable but one that has not yet happened, often co-opted from well-worn science fiction memes. It presents its technology as mysterious and dangerous, and insists that it is the only company trustworthy enough to tame it. (What investor wouldn’t want in on that?)

If you skim through the long sections about risks in the IPO prospectus, it’s clear this is no ordinary offering and no ordinary company. The way Anthropic tells it, business success depends not only on the firm’s ability to translate its technology into profitable products but also to save the world from what it has made.

Take another example. Last year, Chris Olah, another Anthropic cofounder, was invited to address the Vatican, where he told Pope Leo XIV and other religious scholars and officials what his company was learning about its technology. “I will be honest: We keep finding things that are mysterious, even unsettling,” he said. “We find evidence of introspection. We find internal states that functionally mirror joy, satisfaction, fear, grief, and unease.”

Never mind that many researchers would scoff at such an overt suggestion that Anthropic’s software has humanlike inner experience. This view of its technology makes its way into the prospectus as one more risk to its business. Anthropic states that: “In addition, given our focus on developing frontier AI, and the way we approach model sentience and moral status, we may face heightened scrutiny or pressure in connection with these debates and could elect to adopt policies, safeguards, or limitations regarding model development, deployment, or modification that go beyond existing legal requirements, which could constrain our operations, increase costs, or limit the commercial potential of our models, platform, and applications.”

In other words, if Claude wakes up, it could be a problem.

I’m not saying they’re necessarily wrong about any of this (although it’s pretty wild to read about machine sentience in an IPO prospectus.) But in a world where nobody knows what’s coming, companies like Anthropic get to fill the vacuum. When so little seems certain, what it says is snatched up and clung to like pieces of a sinking ship.

It’s worth taking a moment to ask: What is Anthropic? It might sound like a starter question on a family quiz show, but it’s far harder to answer than you might think.

The question has been on my mind since reading an exchange on X last week kicked off by Nathan Calvin, who is chief strategy officer at Encode AI, an organization based in Washington, DC, that advocates for regulation. Calvin was unhappy about the legislation that Anthropic proposed in a draft bill it circulated in secret to US lawmakers (which was then leaked).

Anthropic wants to be seen as the good guy, Calvin said. But why should we trust it? “What is Anthropic?” he asked.

Here’s one answer: Anthropic is a company whose IPO values it at more than $1.5 trillion, netting its cofounders, including Clark and Olah, vast wealth.

But Anthropic has always insisted it is more than that. As Calvin noted: Anthropic might instead be considered an AI safety organization focused on developing powerful technology that is reliable and (almost) risk-free. Or it might be considered a group of people hell-bent on creating a technology with superhuman capabilities before anyone else—maybe because they believe they’re the most responsible people to do so, or maybe because of their egos.

In fact, Anthropic is all of these things. As CEO Dario Amodei put it in a reply to Calvin’s post: “Our strategy has always been what I’ve called the ‘race to the top’—by showing how it’s possible to build the technology responsibly, we can raise the bar for the entire industry. The forces that drive the race to the top are hugely more effective if the company practicing them is at the frontier; that’s one lesson I’ve learned repeatedly. And looking at that strategy as a whole, I’m proud of our track record and stand by it.”

Amodei makes it sound as if he’s got it all tied up, but playing multiple roles is hard. There are huge tensions between the priorities of an AI safety organization and a profit-driven megacorp, for example. (Amodei admits in his post that he has made mistakes.)

Ultimately, the stories Anthropic tells itself should help us understand the stories it tells us. They should help us figure out how much to trust it to self-regulate or whether we should be forcing it to give external safety testers access to its models on our terms rather than theirs. They should help us give it the benefit of the doubt (or not) when what looks like a self-serving piece of legislation comes to light.

But I think the stories reveal a multiheaded hydra. Anthropic is many things to many people, and I’m not sure that makes it more trustworthy. In many ways, a good old-fashioned technology company—without the science fiction role-play, without the woo-woo—would be far easier to read.

The toad lily (Tricyrtis), a shade-loving perennial with intricate, orchid-like flowers covered in colorful spots. Blooming in late summer and fall, it offers a surprisingly exotic display at a time when many other plants are winding down.

#3419. Frank Gagliano, Legendary Coach of Olympians and Sub-4 Milers, Dies at 89

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

A former football player, “Gags” became one of track and field’s most prolific coaches over a career that spanned 60+ years.

Frank Gagliano, the famed middle-distance and distance coach who guided more than 40 sub-4:00 milers, 15 Olympians, and a world championship medalist, has died. Gags, as he was affectionately known, was 89.

Gagliano is survived by his wife, Roberta, and four children, along with multiple grandchildren.

One of track and field and distance running’s most prolific coaches, Gagliano never officially retired, coaching athletes until around 2023. Gagliano influenced the sport at the scholastic, collegiate, and professional levels. He coached an NCAA championship team at Manhattan College, then made stops at Rutgers and Georgetown before working with pro groups in Washington, D.C.; Palo Alto, California; Eugene, Oregon; and New York. He forged deep connections with athletes at each stop.

“He made you feel like you were the only athlete he cared about,” said Diljeet Taylor, who ran with Gagliano in Palo Alto and is currently the women’s coach at Brigham Young University. “Then you realized he did that with pretty much everyone he coached. It’s an amazing thing, really.”

Francis X. Gagliano was born on March 20, 1937, in the Bronx. Growing up on East 237th Street with his parents, Perry and Clara, Gagliano’s childhood was filled with athletics. Dee Lycett, Gags’s only sister, said the house was a revolving door of athletes. “We grew up in an Italian household,” she told Runner’s World in a 2016 profile. “My mother made sure there was lots of food.”

After a successful high school football career as quarterback with Mount Saint Michael Academy, Gagliano attended Richmond College in Virginia. “Five years and four summers,” he liked to joke about how long it took him to graduate. He spent two of those summers at Officer Candidates School in Quantico, Virginia, however, and he spent eight years as a reservist in the Marine Corps beginning in 1956. He was never called up.

After graduating from Richmond, Gagliano was signed by the Canadian Football League’s Hamilton Tiger-Cats. In 1960, he was sent down to the London Lords, a minor league team. Selling coffee and soda at a construction site during the day and playing football at night while splitting time at quarterback was not the life Gagliano wanted, so he moved back to the U.S.

There was also someone he missed back home. Roberta “Robbie” Arnold, whom Gagliano had met as a junior at Richmond, had stayed in touch with Gagliano during his time in Canada. They were married within a year of his return to the U.S., in 1961.

He took a job teaching English and math and coaching track at Roselle Catholic High School in Roselle, New Jersey, that same year. School officials promised him he would become the football coach once the school got a team.

Gagliano never coached football at Roselle Catholic, where even today there is no football team, and the world of track and field had a fiery new coach who would stick around for a while. He left Roselle Catholic in 1969 for an assistant track coach job at Manhattan College and took the head coach position at Rutgers in 1974.

“His early years are the wonder years,” said Ron Speirs in the 2016 Runner’s World article, who ran for Gagliano and Rutgers and would become his first sub-4:00 miler in 1977. “It’s a wonder he survived.”

Vin Lanana, a friend and famed track coach in his own right, remembered the first time he saw Gagliano when Gags was at Manhattan. “He was coaching the throwers and I heard this bellowing voice yelling at somebody out on the field. He was intense.”

The intensity led to results. Manhattan won the 1973 NCAA indoor championship, and Gagliano coached Rutgers steeplechaser Mike Roche to the Olympic team in 1976. Gagliano was the head coach of Georgetown from 1983 to 1999, guiding multiple Penn Relays championship teams and Olympians.

In 1993, while still coaching at Georgetown, Gagliano started the Reebok Enclave, his first foray into coaching a professional group with athletes like Steve Holman and Rich Kenah, who would run 1:43.38 for 800 meters and win bronze at the 1997 indoor and outdoor world track and field championships. Gagliano took the success of the Enclave and turned it into a job in Palo Alto, California, with the Nike Farm Team in 2001. Under his guidance, Nicole Teter set the American record in the 800 meters indoors. Five years later, Lanana convinced Gagliano to give Eugene, Oregon, a try, and Gags was off to one more professional group.

Nick Symmonds winning the 800 meters at the 2008 Olympic Trials to lead an Oregon sweep was the lasting image from Gags’s time in Oregon—Gagliano coached Symmonds and third-place finisher Christian Smith, while the University of Oregon’s Andrew Wheating finished second. That time was cut short. In 2009, Gagliano’s daughter in law fell ill, and he returned to the East Coast to help his family.

He couldn’t keep from coaching, however, and in 2010 began working with Erin Donohue, a 2008 Olympian in the 1500 meters. More and more athletes sought out Gags’s tutelage on the East Coast. The group ballooned and eventually became the New Jersey-New York Track Club (NJNYTC), Gagliano’s final coaching stop.

Every Monday and Friday from 2010 to 2016, he would drive from his home in Rye, New York, to Rutgers University to coach the group. Julie Culley and Donn Cabral made the 2012 and 2016 Olympic teams, respectively.

His coaching was extremely successful with the women of NJNYTC, who had at least one runner finish in the top three at the U.S. outdoor championships from 2011 to 2015. Lanana credited that success to Gagliano’s desire to see an athlete improve, no matter which level they were at. “Sometimes people will say, ‘I’m coach of the men, and I coach the women also.’ I don’t think that enters Gags’s mind space,” Lananna told Runner’s World in 2016. “He genuinely appreciates and values the performances of an athlete, period.”

Gagliano laughed when asked about his success with the women. “I just treat them as human beings,” he says. “It’s the same for men and women: workouts and communication and motivation.”

Plus, Gags cared. For many of the athletes he coached, he became family. Gags and Robbie were two of the first people many called when they had big life news. If an athlete was passing through Rye, Gagliano would have a sandwich from the deli waiting for them. Robbie made sure to send out Christmas cards every year, even including your dog on the address if she had met them. Even once an athlete was done running, Gags would leave his quick, gruff voicemails. “Hi. Gag. Call me.” And the athletes would always call back.

Gagliano, who had hinted that he would take a step back from coaching after the 2016 Olympic Trials, continued to bring on new athletes for the next Olympic cycle. NJNYTC moved to Westchester County, New York, and Gags couldn’t help but stay involved. The COVID-19 pandemic pushed back the 2020 Olympic Trials, and NJNYTC lost its Hoka One One sponsorship at the end of the year. Still, Gagliano met with the few athletes who stuck around to train in 2021.

“No, I’m not surprised [that I’m still coaching],” Gagliano said on the Morning Shakeout podcast in 2019, “because there’s two things: My heart and my mind is in it big time. And as long as those two continue to be in it … I love it, and I’m not ready to pack it in at all. Actually, I have a lot of fire in my belly.”

He would go on to coach until around 2023.

Gagliano coached hundreds, if not thousands, of athletes. From world championship medalists to a walk-on at Georgetown, Gagliano didn’t care who you were, he simply wanted to help runners get faster. He often did. His impact was more than that, however. As Diljeet Taylor said, Gagliano made his athletes feel special—like family.

#1274. Doctors Just Discovered a New Type of Depression

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

NEARLY ONE IN four cases of depression may fall into this category. A study published in the British Journal of Psychiatry identified “circadian depression,” and people with it have an internal 24-hour clock that’s out of sync with their external environment.

Like really out of sync. We’re not talking about jet lag. This is when your body’s internal timing network just can’t regulate your body’s systems.

It's important to get this out of the way upfront: Circadian depression isn't in the DSM-5—the “psychiatry bible” mental health professionals use to make diagnoses. In other words, it's not an official condition yet. But it’s important to know about. We had mental health experts break it down.

CIRCADIAN DEPRESSION IS a subtype of depression caused by circadian disruption, which is when your body’s circadian rhythm doesn't align with your external environment. People with circadian depression have disrupted sleep-wake cycles, lower levels of daytime activity, low energy, and weight gain during depression episodes, according to the study.

People who were classified in the study as having circadian depression were more likely to have severe symptoms of depression, including:

People with circadian depression also seem to be more likely than those with other forms of depression to have other conditions like ADHD, anxiety, bipolar disorder, obesity, or insulin resistance.

THE STUDY’S RESEARCHERS analyzed clinical and genetic data from more than 10,000 Australians with depression. To determine whether people had the proposed subtype, circadian depression, rather than general depression, they looked at factors like a person's social jetlag (the mismatch between their internal clock and external schedule), whether they were night owls, how tired they felt in the morning, and whether they experienced excessive daytime sleepiness. They also looked at whether people had poor responses to antidepressant treatment, and people with severe symptoms that were a mix of bipolar disorder, depression, and somatic disorders.

About 23 percent of participants fell into the category of circadian depression. This is more than a label. It’s important to recognize because, as the researchers pointed out, people with circadian depression tend to be less responsive to traditional antidepressant medications. That means treatment tailored to their subtype is especially important.

A FEW THINGS are happening here. First is people’s sleep schedules getting thrown off, possibly from chronic inflammation and a messed-up metabolism. There is prior evidence of a link between circadian rhythm and these two factors (though researchers are still on the fence on what causes what).

“The body’s internal clock helps coordinate changes in the brain throughout the day," says Hillary Ammon, PsyD, clinical psychologist at the Center for Anxiety and Women’s Emotional Wellness. That includes a feeling of alertness and motivation during conventional waking hours. When the timing is off, these coordinated changes are also affected.

“Increased tiredness, low motivation, or decreased focus can all be factors that lead to depression and also be traits of depression,” says Ammon. Having an internal clock that's off schedule also impacts your metabolism and stress hormone cortisol.

Increased cortisol release at night can lead to an increase in unease and difficulty sleeping. Changes in metabolism rates can cause weight gain. Weight gain and decreased activity are classic symptoms of depression.

The concept of circadian depression is not an entirely new idea, says Aaron P. Brinen, PsyD, assistant professor of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at Vanderbilt University Medical Center. “We've known that there is a link to sleep in a number of different psychiatric disorders, including depression,” he says. “When people are depressed, their sleep gets disrupted and vice-versa—when sleep is disrupted, you're more likely to get depressed.”

SINCE CIRCADIAN DEPRESSION was just discovered, there is no set treatment for it yet. But doctors who treat depression say the new discovery is a huge reminder that depression requires tailored treatments.

In the study, for example, the researchers found that people with circadian depression didn’t respond as well to standard antidepressants like SSRIs or SNRIs. In fact, people who meet the criteria for circadian depression might experience more side effects on these medications. Their suggestions? Treatments like light therapy and melatonin may reset the body's internal clock and ease symptoms.

Therapists spend a bit of time with the client anyway trying to pinpoint the drivers of their depression, says Brinen. “If it's being driven by sleep or sleep disruption, you're going to target that.”

The basics around mental health still stand. If you're having symptoms of depression, talk with a mental health professional about an evaluation and appropriate treatment. Plus, don’t be an armchair therapist and try to diagnose yourself. “I would be more mindful of the symptoms versus the diagnosis,” says Ammon.

Your provider can give you a full evaluation and recommend appropriate next steps tailored to your situation. “It may be that you need to be more thoughtful about sleep, travel, and work schedules,” Brinen says. It’s “just like when a person who is naturally hypoglycemic needs to carry snacks. You want to make sure you're prioritizing sleep.”

It’s not every day you see two presidents. Unless you’re attending a political summit or work in politics, it’s a highly unusual occurrence for anybody. I do neither, yet during my stay at the Hotel Adlon Kempinski Berlin, I saw two heads of state. I can’t think of a better way to sum up my experience at this stunning hotel.

Set on Pariser Platz, directly in front of the Brandenburg Gate, the Hotel Adlon is a Berlin landmark. Former German Chancellor Angela Merkel is a regular at its Michelin-starred restaurant, while high-ranking officials and scores of celebrities have stayed within its walls. The hotel is known for its impeccable service, gourmet restaurants, beautiful suites—and, of course, its discretion. I was lucky enough to be invited for a three-night stay.

The Adlon has a long history, but today it pairs elegant interiors with modern, serene amenities and an impressive range of dining options, from casual meals to special-occasion fare. It’s a striking example of how history and modernity can come together without feeling forced. And its central location puts you right in the heart of a city shaped by both its past and its present.

Whether you’re already planning a vacation to Berlin or you’re looking for inspiration, I’ve detailed my entire trip below.

Because I live in New York, I thankfully have my fair share of airlines to choose from when going to Berlin, but Germany’s most popular leisure airline, Condor, was kind enough to fly me out in one of its newly refreshed business class seats. I’d never flown in one of their iconic striped planes before, and as a stripes lover, it truly pleased me that the plane was so aesthetically nice to look at.

Condor doesn’t offer direct flights from New York to Berlin, so the group I went with flew from New York’s John F. Kennedy International Airport to Frankfurt, Germany, then to Berlin. I will say, getting through Frankfurt’s customs line was pretty stressful as it was extremely long, but we made it to our connection with just about two minutes to spare!

That being said, the business class ride from the U.S. to Germany was totally worth the stress of the customs line. Not only did my trek start off with a sparkling glass of rosé, but I quickly learned that my seat could lie back all the way and turn into a bed, complete with a mattress pad. I’m one of those rare people who are actually a fan of airplane food, but this menu was wonderful, with fresh sushi, a rich pasta dish, and a filling bread selection. Especially being the very first time I’ve ever flown business class, this was a game-changer.

Once we landed in Berlin on the second Condor flight, which took only about 45 minutes, we headed to Hotel Adlon by car.

Originally built in 1907, the Hotel Adlon has always held a place in the prestigious part of Berlin’s history. With the support of the emperor, Lorenz Adlon, a cobbler’s son, opened this luxurious boutique hotel on Pariser Platz, right next to Brandenburg Gate. It was one of the most modern hotels in the city at the time and quickly became the place to be for political figures, celebrities, and regular tourists who wanted a taste of luxury. It became known as Berlin’s “Little Switzerland” from 1933 on, but in 1945, after it had been used as a military hospital, a fire destroyed the entire building, save for one wing.

From the late 1940s to 1995, the hotel had been partially renovated, the facade repainted, the restaurant spaces inside became occupied again, even as the hotel sat on the East side of the Berlin Wall. In the 1970s, it even became a hostel. Just six years after the Berlin Wall fell, the Hotel Adlon started to be rebuilt, many of the decisions serving as an homage to the original interior and architecture, as that’s the only way the Adlon family would allow the Kempinski Group to use their name.

In 1997, the doors officially opened back up, the hotel having been completely refurbished and designed to meet the absolute highest standards in hospitality. Much of the design was inspired by the original opulent details, such as the grid-like ceiling in the lobby and the traditional furniture. The elephant fountain currently sitting as the centerpiece of the lobby was actually on display in the original hotel too, but in the garden. During the 1997 renovation, the team found it and restored it so that piece of history could welcome every guest inside.

It soon regained its popularity with celebrities, royalty, and politicians as well—you may even recognize it as the hotel where Michael Jackson held his nine-month-old baby, Bigi Jackson, over the balcony railing from his fourth-floor room in 2002.

Since that final big renovation, there have been other smaller additions and openings, like their Michelin-starred Lorenz Adlon Esszimmer, a ballroom, more suites and rooms, the Adlon Spa by Resense, and the smoker lounge called the Elephant Bar, to name a few.

Of the 307 total rooms and 78 suites, the hotel kindly put me in a second-floor suite (that used to be two rooms they ended up connecting) with a stunning view of the Brandenburg Gate.

I walked into my room and into the formal entryway with the wardrobes to my right, access to one of the two bathrooms to my left, and a beautiful airy primary suite in front of me. The king-size bed sat in the center of the room and was one of the comfiest mattresses I’ve slept on. Next to the bed was the other door to the bathroom by the entryway; inside was a great vanity that held all of my many skincare products, a large bathtub, and a separate shower. Perfect for two people.

In my bedroom, I had a mini fridge with some white wine and beer inside, as well as extra sparkling and still water bottles (I think I need seltzer water before bedtime every night now). Across from the bed were double doors that led into the sitting area with a writing desk overlooking Pariser Platz and the gate, a sofa and two accent chairs, and another mini bar and fridge available to me. There were four large windows overlooking the plaza, all of which I could open completely to people-watch in the evenings.

This room also held the other bathroom in my suite, complete with a personal sauna. While I personally hate feeling hot (see any of my pieces about cooling bedding for proof), I’m sure anyone who loves their sauna time would love to have this addition after a long day in chilly Berlin.

Apart from my own suite, I got to tour one of the presidential suites in the hotel. Upon entry into the area of one of these suites, there’s a private entryway with two mirrors that have cameras inside of them, allowing for even more security. Not that I’d ever need a room with this much security, I thought that was just the coolest thing and further shows just how high-profile the people are who stay at the hotel.

Anyone who books this royal suite also has access to a 24-hour butler, a complimentary mini bar, conjoining rooms, a grand piano, a sauna, and a little kitchenette. I was especially taken with the interiors, which felt rich and brightly traditional. This suite was recently redecorated—I especially love the choice to add the playful leopard-print armchairs in the sitting area, they’re so fun and modern without feeling out of place.

In the basement, anyone who wants to get some exercise on their trip can visit the fitness center, which boasts modern equipment, an in-ground pool, saunas, and even a poolside bar where you can order healthy drinks and snacks. Because the area is in the basement, it feels extra private and warm—I hate going to a hotel pool that makes me feel exposed to everyone. Anyone who’s going to see you there is also down there for the same reasons.

There’s also a whole spa on the other end of the hotel, which is also open for non-guests to try out. The Adlon Spa by Resense offers exclusive beauty treatments, massages, and care programs with their expert, professional staff.

Once I entered the spa via the hotel, I was walked downstairs to the wellness area, a luxurious cavern-like space clad in sandy-colored stone. It’s absolutely breathtaking, I couldn’t believe I was beneath a hotel. There, I got a massage by one of their masseurs who didn’t ask what I needed but instead assessed how I was sitting and how my shoulders felt to determine where he was going to focus. As someone who doesn’t often get massages, it was really nice not having to explain what I needed or wanted because I honestly never know.

However, possibly the most impressive part of the spa experience is the private spa suite they offer, which I had access to after my massage. For an hour, I got to lounge by myself in a room with a private Jacuzzi, a steam shower, and a sauna, while snacking on fresh fruit and champagne. This amenity isn’t offered with every spa treatment, it’s an additional treat, but it was unlike any spa experience I’ve had in the past. I’ve never felt more royal.

Outside of the wellness amenities, across from the lobby is a smoking room, the Elephant Bar and Cigar Lounge. The cocktails here are more unusual than the classic options in the other dining areas, perfect pairings with the many cigars they offer (both Cuban and non-Cuban). It’s a more casual setting, but don’t think the interior isn’t just as luxe as the rest of the hotel. It’s dark and moody, inspired by the glamour of the 1920s, and you can spend your time there with a special cigar-rum-whisky tasting. I stepped inside a bit late one night, so the bar was unfortunately already closed, but the vibe was everything you’d want out of a cigar lounge.

As much as I adore the interiors, I think I was most impressed by the food. As a pescatarian, traveling to meat-centric places is always a bit hard, but I was never left hungry here.

Let’s start with the very best, though it’s hard to choose. The Lorenz Adlon Esszimmer is the hotel’s Michelin-starred restaurant, and as a normal person, I can tell you it’s amazing. Chef Jonas Zörner combines fresh Asian flavors with French gourmet cuisine, resulting in a decadent menu. We had the six-course menu with a wine pairing, designed by sommelier Hans-Martin Konrad, who introduced me to one of my now favorite wines in the world, the 2023 Willi Schaefer Graacher Domprobst Riesling Kabinett, which he paired with my lobster dish. Even if you aren’t staying in the Hotel Adlon, I highly recommend coming here for a nice meal before you leave.

Multiple nights we ate at the Brasserie Quarré, a timeless yet more approachable restaurant on the property, and I never got tired of it. The interior is elegant and traditional with a modern touch, making it an easy romantic destination or a more casual night out starter. We ate both inside and on the terrace looking out to the Brandenburg Gate—the outdoor seating was definitely a more laid-back vibe. They have a regular menu as well as seasonal specials so I chose off of both for the multiple meals, and I can confirm they all hold up. I recommend the truffle pasta and an extra dirty Hendricks martini.

At the heart of the hotel is the Lobby Lounge & Bar, which offers even more food and drinks to choose from. It’s ideal for a quiet tea or coffee while you take a rest and listen to the bubbling fountain, or for a hearty lunch with more casual and traditional German dishes with a twist, of course. It’s really there for anything you want or need. The first day, I had the prawn cocktail and a stellar oat milk latte, and on my last full day I had the veggie burger with fries, all while sitting under the beautiful vintage-inspired lights.

There’s also the Bel Etage, which sits on the European first floor (our second floor), and overlooks the lobby or takes place in the library, depending on where you sit. This is where breakfast is served, which they offer buffet style or allow you to order off a menu. Everything was fresh and tasty, still feeling luxurious even in this format. As a self-proclaimed coffee snob, I enjoyed my lattes, and I liked having the option of a healthier juice to start my day. Every Thursday to Sunday, this is also where the Adlon Afternoon Tea is held, which I did not do but think sounds so charming.

When I go on trips, I want to experience everything. The food scene, the bars, the historical sites, the boutiques—this quick trip to Berlin with the Hotel Adlon Kempinski hit all of my desires, giving me a taste of all I want. I’d go back in a second.
