It is a mildly sad sight at the end of the summer when many plants lose their luster, with flowers fading and leaves looking a bit burned, as if they forgot their sunscreen. But late summer and early autumn is when perennial asters can save the day, and the garden.

Coming up: the most colorful season at Old Court Nurseries in England, a small family-run operation specializing since 1906 in propagating its own Michaelmas daisies (and many other varieties of asters). Photographer Britt Willoughby Dyer visited the nurseries’ Picton Garden at the height of the season:

Asters have a deep history in Greek mythology. According to legend, the goddess Astrea created asters with her tears. The story goes that she was so distraught at how few stars appeared in the dark sky that she started crying, and as her tears fell they turned into star-shaped asters on the ground. Aptly named for the ancient Greek and Latin word for star (because of its long, radiating petals), asters are a star of the late-summer garden.

As other perennials are starting to slow down, asters are winding up to add hints of necessary color. Asters come in a range of colors, including pink, purple, red, and white.

With more than 600 species in the Aster genus, it also shares the same family as the daisy. Found wild in North America and southern Europe, asters are mainly perennials but a few are annuals and biennials. The two main groups of asters are the New England aster (Aster novae-angliae) and the New York asters (Aster novi-belgii).

While there is a galaxy of asters to choose among, a favorite is Aster frikatii ‘Monch’ for its long bloom time; it grows to a dramatic height of up to three feet and bears large lavender-blue flowers.

The downside to asters is they can be tricky when it comes to water. They tend to drop their lower leaves if not kept well-watered in the hotter months. Also, aster plants have a tendency to eventually die out in the center, making them a great candidate to rejuvenate by dividing and replanting in new clumps every two to three years.

Another issue is powdery mildew. If your garden is plagued with mildew in the fall and your asters are afflicted with this punishment, consider planting mildew-free varieties, such as the low-growing variety Aster dumosus. Another solution is to make sure asters have good air circulation around them and plenty of sun.

Editor’s note: This is the latest in a series of posts by Sally Kohn— journalist and CNN political commentator, TED talk giver, and design aficionado—chronicling her adventures in remodeling.

Let’s level set. I live in a 1780s barn that was turned into a house in the 1950s, and as far as I can tell the windows and the siding haven’t been changed since. The fact is, many parts of our money pit farmhouse need updating. Some, like the 1950s asphalt roof built directly on top of a much older shake shingle roof, we replaced last year. Other things we’re still waiting on, like the vintage 1950s bathroom tile with vintage crumbling grout. But since we can’t close many of our windows without pushing on them from the outside, and we have an inexplicable leak behind our very ancient siding, we put off the inevitable long enough. It is time for some grand-scale renovations.

Stay tuned for some of the other things we’ll be changing, including a kitchen refresh. But first up, if we’re going to go to all the trouble—and, ahem, expense—of replacing all of our siding and windows, can we make our old home more environmentally responsible in terms of both materials and energy efficiency? Let’s find out.

My guide on this journey is Shannon Pendleton, principal of Sanderson Sustainable Design and co-host of Passive House Accelerator. In addition to being a friend and neighbor, Shannon is a leading expert on how to make homes more sustainable and energy efficient. And while those concepts might immediately make you think of solar panels and the like, Shannon explains that in energy efficiency, an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure. In other words, using green energy to power your home is great. But designing your home to need less energy in the first place is even better.

But with apologies to Shannon, when she explained the science of the “building envelope”, all I could think about is that envelope feels like the wrong word. Maybe it should be “building envelop”—as in, how enveloped is your building. Because your “building envelop” is what stops the outside environment from getting in and the inside environment from getting out. That means everything from bugs and dust to wind to heat and cold.

So I’m making two big changes to my “building envelop”, both of which create an opportunity for more energy efficiency. Let’s start with the siding. We have very old, very stained, very falling-apart cedar. I could just replace it with cedar, maybe even sustainably harvested cedar. But the fact is, our old cedar is cupping and splitting. Yes, more maintenance would help, but that’s hard to do with second-story wood. Also I’m lazy. Alternatively, I could have gone with some plastic product or chemically treated wood but, like I said, we care about the environment and also want to preserve the look and feel of our historic home. So we’re going with Thermory siding.

Thermory takes sustainably forested wood—in our case, we’re using pine—and puts it through an intense heat-treating process that makes the wood more stable and rot-resistant. It’s an updated twist on the Japanese shou sugi ban process, where wood is charred to make it water and rot resistant. Thermory is that, without the blackening (though they actually do have a product that mirrors the shou sugi ban look, but with the added benefits of Thermory’s advanced thermal modification). In other words, Thermory gives us everything we like about natural wood plus all these added benefits.

Using a more stable rot-resistant siding definitely envelops our house better (take that, flying squirrels), but the energy efficiency upgrades come from what will be behind the siding. Our contractor—the wonderful, lovable, fantastic-to-work-with John Gemmi and his company, Gemmi Construction—were already planning to weather-seal behind the siding, and of course put new insulation behind that where we find any lacking. But Shannon suggested installing “a rain screen, a continuous air barrier, and continuous exterior insulation” to give us the most enveloped envelope possible. Ultimately, we settled on Zip R sheathing, which does all three of those things in one system. So that’s our plan.

As for the windows, well….The most energy-efficient option is triple-pane glass, but in our case, since we’re replacing a gajillion windows, we just couldn’t make it work in our budget. We have two walls of windows in our great room, most of which are currently fixed panes that we’re excited to replace with operable awning windows. We looked into doing triple-pane for just those windows, but that would have made the windows too heavy to open. So yes, we’re choosing a little less efficiency there but counting on the fact that having new windows that don’t require a screwdriver and grunts to close will nevertheless up our efficiency. Plus, thanks to Ambiance Design, we’re adding retractable Duette Honeycomb shades over those large window walls, which will give us some extra energy efficiency on super hot days.

Niece Lumber helped us pick out Lincoln Windows, a family run U.S.-manufactured brand with two advantages. First, we could get retractable screens which we’re very excited about. And second, they offer custom colors on their exteriors. You’ll have to stay tuned to figure out what color choice we’ve made. It’s gonna be…dramatic.

Work on the siding and window part of the renovation is starting soon. I’ll be posting updates on my Instagram (@sallykohn), plus more updates along the way here as we get into our kitchen renovations, including the most sustainable and cool countertop and our very locally made and custom color stove. Watch this space!

#3834. Carminati lift-and-slide windows frame Dubai skyline at Bvlgari Hotel and Resort

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Minimalist timber windows from luxury brand Carminati’s Skyline Minimal Frames collection were chosen to frame views from the Bvlgari Hotel and Resort in Dubai.

Designed to make the “least possible visual impact”, Carminati‘s Skyline Minimal Frames range includes both windows and doors defined by minimal profiles, natural materials and modern, patented technology.

The frame’s slimness is the result of Carminati’s patented Skyline Minimal Frames construction, where the timber sash is structurally bonded to the glass pane, which reinforces the whole frame.

“With a front-facing profile of just 37 millimetres for lift-and-slide systems and 50 millimetres for all other opening types, Skyline Minimal Frames combines outstanding thermal and acoustic performance with a minimal visual impact, making it the world’s slimmest wooden profile,” said Carminati.

The collection was selected by Milan-based studio ACPV Architects to be integrated into their design for the Bvlgari Hotel and Resort, which reached completion on Dubai’s Jumeirah Bay Island in 2017.

“Our Skyline Minimal Frames range, personally selected by the architects, possesses all the characteristics required for the project,” Carminati said. “The frames provide the perfect setting for enjoying a breathtaking panorama while feeling at the centre of Dubai’s vibrant social life and exclusive luxury.”

“The new resort is the result of refined Bvlgari style, professionalism and taste of its design team,” the brand added.

Carminati’s Skyline Minimal Frames collection offers a range of windows and door products, including Skyline Sliding, Skyline Bifold, Skyline Pivot, Skyline Drive, Skyline NoForce and the Skyline Handle.

Each of the collection’s products is available in a range of styles, from Skyline Modern to Skyline Classic, which can be manufactured entirely from wood or paired with an external aluminium cladding.

For the 158,000-square-metre Bvlgari hotel, ACPV Architects opted for Carminati’s Skyline Sliding lift-and-slide system – combining its sleek 37-millimetre profile with pale oak on the inside and a bronze-coloured aluminium cladding on the outside, chosen for its resistance to coastal environments.

This offered expansive views throughout the hotel, intended to create “continuity between the indoors and outdoors”, along with access to adjacent terraces.

“Large lift-and-slide doors connect the living rooms with expansive shaded terraces, while full-height glazing gives guests the feeling of being outdoors, conveying a sense of luxury associated with greenery, privacy and the tranquillity of the interior,” Carminati said.

The Skyline Sliding system operates through a lift-and-slide mechanism and is available in configurations with two sliding leaves – one fixed and one sliding – among other combinations.

Integrated into the product is the brand’s patented bottom track, designed exclusively for the Skyline Minimal Frames collection.

Its carriages are set entirely into the floor and covered by a flexible belt, which protects the mechanism from dust and allows for “maximum sliding smoothness”.

According to Carminati, the large-scale commission for the Bvlgari hotel involved the development of a bespoke product to meet the project’s specific technical requirements and local building regulations.

With glazed panels reaching up to four metres in height and subject to significant wind loads, the project required a bespoke system: a concealed steel structure between the lift-and-slide doors and the fixed transoms above.

Built around a composite beam, with three welded steel tubes set within the cavity between the frames, it allowed the profiles to stay slim while meeting the structural requirements.

“The structure takes on the challenges of the environment and combines its contrasts in an original and sophisticated solution,” Carminati said.

Carminati was founded in 1894 near Bergamo, Italy, and is still run by the founding family.

The company specialises in architectural timber windows and doors, holds patents on its Skyline Minimal Frames system, and develops bespoke solutions for each project through its in-house technical office.

#2225. Mali: Army, Russia’s ‘Africa Corps’ Commit New Abuses

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Aftermath of the apparent Malian military drone strike on September 16, 2026, in Guélédié village, Mopti region, Mali. Screenshots from video analyzed and geolocated by Human Rights Watch. © Private

(Nairobi) – Mali’s armed forces and the allied Russia-controlled Africa Corps have committed new atrocities against civilians in central Mali during renewed fighting, Human Rights Watch said today. 

On September 16, 2026, an apparent military drone strike in Guélédié village, Mopti region, killed eight civilians and injured at least six others. Between May and August, Malian and Africa Corps forces held numerous detainees inhumanely in a metal container at an army base in Sévaré town, Mopti region, where many were tortured, including with severe beatings, electric shock, and waterboarding. 

“Civilians are bearing the brunt of the Mali junta’s failure to rein in its abusive forces and allies,” said Ilaria Allegrozzi, senior Sahel researcher at Human Rights Watch. “Recent atrocities implicate the Malian army as well as Russia-controlled Africa Corps, for which Russian officials should also be held accountable.”

The Malian army’s offensive followed coordinated attacks across Mali by the Al Qaeda-linked Group for the Support of Islam and Muslims (Jama’at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin, JNIM) and the Azawad Liberation Front (Front de Libération de l’Azawad, FLA) in late April. Malian forces and the allied Africa Corps killed civilians, including many children, and destroyed civilian property and health facilities in airstrikes and other attacks.

Human Rights Watch remotely interviewed fifteen people between August and September, including three witnesses in Guélédié, four former detainees at the Sévaré military base, and eight members of nongovernmental organizations. Human Rights Watch sent letters to the Malian and Russian defense ministers in early October but received no responses.

Guélédié

On September 16 at about 10 a.m., an apparent military drone delivered a munition on civilians on the outskirts of Guélédié village, killing eight men, ages 25 to 40, and injuring six others. The villagers had gathered around a tree, where a man was providing paid internet access via Starlink. Residents said JNIM fighters often used this service to support operations. 

Witnesses said a drone flew over the village for about one hour before delivering its munition, suggesting that the area was being surveilled. “The drone passed over the village at about 9 a.m. and came back at least four times,” said a 40-year-old man. “It was white and had long, thin, straight wings. It would spin around, disappear, and come back again.” This description is broadly consistent with larger drones the Malian government is known to use. 

Witnesses said that JNIM has a base in Guélédié and over 100 JNIM fighters were present in the village at the time of the strike, although no JNIM fighters were among the casualties. They also said JNIM fighters alerted the local population via WhatsApp about the possibility of a drone strike at the Starlink location, before leaving the area along with many civilians. Witnesses said that only 14 civilians had remained at the site, continuing to use the connection, when the drone delivered its munition.

“The explosion was deafening, really strong,” said a 30-year-old villager. “I saw very thick black smoke rising from the Starlink connection area.”

A 52-year-old health worker said he went to the scene about an hour later and found six men dead and seven injured “with their bodies burned,” one of whom died there. He said he called the Ténenkou military base commander and that he would take the injured to the Ténenkou Referral Health Center there. “But he did not only categorically refuse our evacuating them,” the health worker said. “But he also warned me not to provide them with any support. For him, they were JNIM fighters and he told me that the drone could not have targeted civilians.”

The injured were eventually evacuated. In a September 24 statement, Doctors Without Borders (Médecins Sans Frontières, MSF) said its staff at the health center treated four “seriously injured” people with “burns over their entire bodies,” and one “died shortly after arrival.”

Human Rights Watch verified a video shared by witnesses of the aftermath of the strike and geolocated it to an area about 600 meters northeast of the village center. It shows the bodies of three men, two under a burned and broken tree and one in a nearby pond. Two burned motorcycles and a destroyed solar panel are also visible.

In a September 17 statement, the armed forces chief of staff said that on September 16, military units “spotted terrorist elements concealed under vegetation northeast of Guélédié” with “war equipment and satellite communication devices,” after which they “neutralized” them and destroyed their equipment.

Mali’s junta has increasingly used airstrikes against armed groups. Human Rights Watch, other human rights groups, and severalmedia and investigative organizations have documented airstrikes that killed civilians. 

Sévaré

The Malian military and the Africa Corps have taken hundreds of civilians into custody during counterinsurgency operations across Mali and unlawfully detained many in military bases. Human Rights Watch, other human rights groups, and the media have documented torture at several bases.

On May 7, soldiers and Africa Corps fighters apprehended a 30-year-old civilian at his home in Guirowel, a village in a JNIM-controlled area in Mopti region. On July 4, they arrested three Quranic students, ages 32, 37, and 40, in Thy village, Mopti region, following a JNIM attack on a military position there.

The four men later said the forces took them to the Hamadoun Bocary Barry army base in Sévaré, holding them incommunicado in a shipping container about 20 meters long with no windows, toilet, or washing facilities, in extreme heat without ventilation, alongside dozens of other detainees. The men said they received a small amount of poor-quality food once a day, were denied necessary medical care, and were verbally abused.

“We got a small piece of bread and sometimes beans and half a bottle of water once a day,” said the 32-year-old man. “The heat was unbearable. On the first day, we were 27, but over the days we reached more than 70 people. We relieved ourselves in a bucket, it was always full and spilling onto the floor. They didn’t empty it.”

The men said they identified Malian soldiers by their uniforms and languages, including French, Bambara, and Fulfulde. They said Africa Corps fighters had “light skin” and spoke a foreign language they did not understand. 

Three of the men said Malian soldiers and Africa Corps fighters repeatedly tortured them in a room in a building near the base entrance, alongside Sévaré’s main road, so they could hear traffic sounds. The room had a metal bed, a barrel filled with water, a table, and electric wires on the floor, where the men said they were tortured and interrogated to confess to their alleged association with JNIM. 

The man from Guirowel said Malian and Africa Corps personnel tortured him twice for about two hours each. “I almost lost my life when two Russian soldiers drowned me in a barrel of water,” he said. “Every time I lost consciousness, I was resuscitated with an electric shock and drowned again. “They burned my buttocks with hot plastic, and they lit a cloth on my back. They forced me to give information about JNIM, and to admit I was a fighter.”

The 37-year-old man said a Malian soldier tied him to a bed and demanded: “Tell us everything or we’ll kill you.” Then “the blows started raining down on my buttocks and back. ‘Talk!’ they said to me. [A] soldier choked my neck and banged my head against the bed. I don't remember anything after that.”

The 40-year-old man said soldiers tied his feet to a bed and threatened to kill him if he withheld information about JNIM fighters. “I was hit on my buttocks, fire [put] on my back,” he said. “A soldier held my penis and put electricity on it,” while another “poured water on my back and hit me.”

The men said several detainees died in the container following torture. “When a prisoner was about to die, we would bang on the door to alert the guards,” said the 40-year-old man. “They told us to inform them only when a person died. They had no mercy. Eight people died during my detention.”

The military released the three men from Thy on July 20 and the man from Guirowel on August 6. Relatives reportedly paid the military 1.8 million CFA francs (about US$3,000) for the three Thy men and 1 million CFA (about $1,800) for the Guirowel man to secure their release.

All parties to Mali’s armed conflict are bound by international humanitarian law, notably Common Article 3 and Protocol II to the Geneva Conventions of 1949 as well as customary laws of war. Parties must take all feasible precautions before an attack, including verifying that the target is a military objective. Attacks with no military objective or that do not distinguish between civilians and military personnel are unlawfully indiscriminate. 

Anyone deprived of their liberty must be treated humanely in all circumstances and protected against murder, torture, and cruel, humiliating, and degrading treatment. Denying medical care to the wounded or detainees can be a war crime. Individuals who commit serious violations of the laws of war with criminal intent or as a matter of command responsibility may be prosecuted for war crimes.

In March, the United Nations Human Rights Council renewed the mandate of the independent expert on Mali to assess the human rights situation and assist national authorities in promoting human rights and the rule of law.

“These recent abuses should serve as a stark reminder that concerned governments and international institutions should press the Malian authorities to conduct credible investigations into military abuses and bring those responsible to justice,” Allegrozzi said. “The UN Human Rights Council should increase its scrutiny of the situation in Mali and take steps to prevent further abuses."

#4020. Packaging Design: Tenderleaf Botanical Care by Svetlana Kalinova

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Explore the Tenderleaf botanical skincare packaging design and brand identity by Svetlana Kalinova, pairing serif type with frosted glass.

Skincare shelves are usually loud. Walk down any beauty aisle and every clinical serum bottle is shouting percentages at you in stark black sans-serif type. For Tenderleaf, designer Svetlana Kalinova went the complete opposite route. She built a botanical skincare and bodycare identity rooted in quiet restraint, treating daily routines like a slow, meditative pause rather than a rushed chore.

Look closely at the wordmark first. Most minimalist beauty brands default to either a rigid, high-fashion didone or a safe geometric grotesk. Kalinova instead sets "Tenderleaf" in a crisp, narrow-proportioned serif and breaks the center wide open by drawing the "le" in "leaf" as a sweeping, ribbon-like calligraphic script. That single gestural stroke changes the whole mood. It keeps the typographic authority of an apothecary label while injecting a soft, organic pulse right in the middle of the name, underscored by widely tracked "ORGANIC â BODYCARE" micro-type.

Where this packaging design really wins me over is the material honesty across the containers. Kalinova anchors the entire palette in two deep, earthy tones: Dusty Sage (#626C49) and a dark, shadowy Sleeping Moss (#1F2608). Instead of glossy plastic tubes, the line lives inside heavy, acid-etched frosted glass bottles and squat jars topped with raw, open-grain dark timber caps and droppers. You can almost feel the cool, matte resistance of the glass against the warm wood grain before you even twist the cap off.

The secondary packaging carries that same restraint through contrast and texture. Rigid outer cartons use uncoated, fibrous moss paperboard stamped with oversized, tone-on-tone script loops that catch the light at an angle without needing flashy metallic foil. Even the 350ml hand cream pouches and single-dose 5ml hair conditioner sachets stick to strict editorial grids, pinning functional notes to the outer corners of textured cream labels so the center stays calm and uncluttered.

#2052. Trump administration asks justices to allow border wall construction to continue

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

The Trump administration returned to the Supreme Court on Wednesday afternoon, asking the justices to allow it to resume construction of its border wall in the Big Bend region of southwestern Texas. U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer called the order by a federal trial judge in El Paso temporarily halting the construction “baseless” and “unprecedented.”

During President Donald Trump’s first campaign and his first term in office, his efforts to build a wall along the U.S. border with Mexico were a central part of his immigration agenda. President Joe Biden largely stopped construction when he took office in 2021.

Under the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, the Secretary of Homeland Security has the power to “waive all legal requirements” necessary “to ensure expeditious construction” of border walls and roads. Moreover, federal courts can only hear challenges to such waiver decisions if they allege a violation of the Constitution.

Trump sought to resume construction efforts when he returned to office in 2025. In 2026, Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem and her successor, Markwayne Mullin, issued three waivers of various environmental and conservation laws to allow construction of the border wall in the Big Bend region, including areas in Big Bend National Park. This region comprises almost one-fourth of the U.S.-Mexico border. Noem and Mullin determined that additional border barriers are required in the Big Bend area because it “is an area of high illegal entry”: Sauer states that, in the four-year span between 2021 and 2025, U.S. Customs and Border Protection apprehended more than 89,000 immigrants there who were attempting to illegally enter the United States.

An environmental group, a local landowner, and a group formed to preserve a church went to federal court, challenging the legality of the waivers. On Oct. 2, U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone temporarily barred the Department of Homeland Security from continuing construction on the border wall in the Big Bend region “until and unless” the government complies with environmental and other applicable laws.

Cardone issued a written opinion detailing her reasoning on Oct. 14. #2540. "The Three Words That Explain American Government: 'Right to Sue,'" by Stanford Law Prof. Diego Zambrano

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

I read and much enjoyed—and highly recommend—my Stanford colleague Diego Zambrano's book, How Americans Enforce the Law. He was kind enough to write up for us this post that captures the core of his argument, and I'm delighted to pass it along (though I encourage people to read the whole book):

The headlines are full of stories about a federal government retreating from enforcement. Agencies are dropping investigations, abandoning regulations, shrinking staffs and changing enforcement priorities. One week, the EEOC drops discrimination cases. The next week, the SEC and CFPB wind down investigations into securities fraud and wrongdoing affecting consumers. Critics fear that the Department of Justice's antitrust division will overlook mergers when it is convenient for the administration. And the DOJ has abruptly reduced enforcement of the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act. Facing these changes, Americans might worry that a wide range of laws will go unenforced.

But that concern misses something unique about the American system of government. We have never relied only on bureaucrats to enforce our laws.

All the way back to the colonial period, American legislators have repeatedly done something unusual: they have given ordinary people the right to sue alongside government officials. In almost every regulatory area that most Americans care about—be it regulation of wages and hours, environmental pollution, consumer protection, or securities fraud—statutes allow both government officials and private parties to file suit to enforce the law. All of this happens through little-known provisions called "private rights of action," which is just a technical name for a right to sue. Thousands of these provisions build a second enforcement system alongside the bureaucracy, turning employees, consumers, competitors and their lawyers into co-enforcers of public law.

This system of private enforcement of the law is highly unusual. In much of the rest of the world, whenever a government faces a regulatory problem, it typically empowers government officials and bureaucrats to police legal violations. In France, Germany, Japan, and even the UK, legislators are far less likely to let anyone sue over, say, environmental pollution or employment discrimination.

Consider the case of oil spills. In 1989, the United States faced the Exxon Valdez oil spill off the pristine waters of Alaska.  Millions of gallons of oil laid waste to coasts, waters, and fisheries for hundreds of miles. What most Americans don't know is that a decade later, France also faced a catastrophic oil spill, caused by a ship called the Erika. So how did governments react? In the United States, private plaintiffs sued Exxon directly under existing tort and maritime law, eventually winning substantial damages. Congress, meanwhile, enacted a statute called the Oil Pollution Act with a new right-to-sue those responsible for oil spills. By contrast, in France, the oil spill led primarily to a major criminal prosecution and, eventually, to new national and European regulatory measures (including a new bureaucracy called the European Maritime Safety Agency). When France finally adopted a right to sue in 2016, it paled in comparison to the American version.

Why is the United States so different? Part of the answer, as I argue in a new book, is that Americans have always done this. Back in the colonial period, state legislatures adopted what they called "penal statutes," that empowered any person to enforce regulatory statutes and collect a bounty. So when Massachusetts wanted to regulate the storage of gunpowder, it allowed private informers to sue neighbors who stored it improperly. And this model applied across many areas of colonial life: alcohol consumption, trade, taxation, public morals, church attendance, and more. The Founders used the same tool across the board: Thomas Jefferson and James Madison co-wrote Virginia statutes full of penal statutes, and John Adams handled these suits routinely as a colonial lawyer.

The key feature of this system is that even though private parties file suit for their own aims—the employee wants their full wages paid or the consumer wants a refund for a shoddy product—they inevitably enforce the law. And those lawsuits benefit everyone else because employers and manufacturers may change their behavior with respect to other employees and consumers. In that sense, every single plaintiff is a little bureaucrat or, as some courts call them, a "private attorney general."

This system has the added benefit of reducing political disruptions in enforcement. Even though any single administration can change the enforcement priorities of the EEOC, SEC, CFPB, or Department of Justice, the president cannot control thousands of lawyers and their clients. Private enforcement, then, makes enforcement less dependent on what the bureaucracy and president want.

Right. So you might think that when the bureaucracy won't pursue wrongdoing, the obvious solution should always be new rights to sue. Indeed, many news stories decrying a lack of government enforcement immediately call for more private lawsuits.

But there's a problem with that solution. Private enforcement is not tailor-made for every regulatory area. It makes the most sense to empower private parties when the bureaucracy is truly faulty, under-resourced, politically vulnerable, or likely to be captured by regulated entities. When this isn't the case, private enforcement can cause more problems than it solves.

Consider, then, the enormous problems that rights to sue pose in the environmental context and in the construction of housing and infrastructure. There, broad rights to challenge government approvals can let private parties oppose the construction of housing, mass transit, green energy, and other projects. Examples of ruinous lawsuits abound. Neighbors in a Maryland suburb—backed for years by a nearby country club—sued to delay the Purple Line light-rail project over concerns for a microscopic organism, while the Sierra Club sued to stop a major Mojave solar energy project over the potential impact to a desert turtle. So even if a city government has approved a project that might benefit thousands of people, a single crank can use a right-to-sue to stop the project. In these settings, rights to sue have contributed to delays and increased costs of housing and infrastructure.

We need a careful approach to rights to sue. Yes, we need more of them in some areas. Especially if the government won't enforce anti-corruption or consumer protection statutes. And yes, in employment law, securities, and antitrust, private enforcement often makes a lot of sense. But this is not necessarily the case in environmental law and construction. There, we need better bureaucracies, not thousands of people with the ability to oppose construction projects for any number of reasons.

When the bureaucracy retreats, Americans should rest assured that private parties will still enforce the law. But that power to enforce the law should be given carefully.

(describing the class as "Americans who either had questions about the [COVID-19] vaccine or did not want to be forced to take a shot"). The district court correctly held that Berenson's proposed class does not constitute a class for Section 1985(3) purposes. Instead, this is precisely the kind of class that the Supreme Court cautioned against in Bray—a class composed of individuals who share a desire to engage in conduct, i.e., to forgo COVID-19 vaccination, that the defendants allegedly disfavor. To the extent Berenson suggests that the class falls within the ambit of Section 1985 because it "includes a disproportionate number of African-Americans, political conservatives, and evangelical Christians," we are unpersuaded. Even if the composition of the alleged class is accepted as true, we have held that "plaintiffs cannot proceed under a disparate impact theory of liability in … claims brought pursuant to [Section 1985]."

The district court also correctly determined in the alternative that, assuming arguendo that persons not vaccinated against COVID-19 constituted a protected category, Berenson did not sufficiently allege "that the defendants acted with class-based, invidiously discriminatory animus." … In Gleason v. McBride (2d Cir. 1989), we affirmed the dismissal of a Section 1985 claim because the plaintiff "[did] not claim discrimination based on his political party affiliation," but rather "alleged only that he was discriminated against because he was a political opponent of the defendants and was extremely vocal in his opposition to [them]."

Here, Berenson alleges that he was "a highly visible obstacle to [the Biden Administration's] efforts," and that "the White House had targeted [him] specifically" as "ground zero for COVID-19 misinformation." Indeed, the FAC is replete with allegations that Berenson, and not a general class of unvaccinated Americans, was specifically targeted. The FAC also indicates that Berenson was a vocal critic of the Biden Administration. Thus, as in Gleason, Berenson's Section 1985 claim must be dismissed because he failed to plausibly allege "that the defendants acted with class-based, invidiously discriminatory animus."

And the court also rejected Berenson's tortious interference with contract claim:

Under New York law, a claim for tortious interference requires "(1) the existence of a valid contract between the plaintiff and a third party; (2) the defendant's knowledge of that contract; (3) the defendant's intentional procurement of the third-party's breach of the contract without justification; (4) actual breach of the contract; and (5) damages resulting therefrom." A claim for tortious interference will not lie if the plaintiff fails to plead an actual breach of the contract.

Here, there was a valid contract between Berenson and Twitter, i.e., Twitter's Terms of Service, which provides that Twitter "may suspend or terminate [a user's] account or cease providing [the user] with all or part of the [contractually defined] Services at any time for any or no reason." Berenson argues that the Terms of Service were modified by Twitter's five-strike policy and by Borrman's "assurances to [Berenson] regarding his posts pursuant to [the five-strike] policy." … [But] neither the five-strike policy nor Borrman's statements to Berenson modified the Terms of Service….

The relevant provision of the Terms of Service is located in the section entitled "Ending These Terms," and provides the means by which a user may terminate the agreement, i.e., "by deactivating [a user's] accounts and discontinuing [a user's] use of [Twitter's] Services," and the circumstances under which Twitter

may suspend or terminate [a user's] account … at any time for any or no reason, including, but not limited to, if [Twitter] reasonably believe[s]: (i) you have violated these Terms or the Twitter Rules and Policies or Periscope Community Guidelines, (ii) you create risk or possible legal exposure for us; (iii) your account should be removed due to unlawful conduct[;] (iv) your account should be removed due to prolonged inactivity; or (v) our provision of the Services to you is no longer commercially viable.

The five-strike policy is clearly among the "Rules and Policies" that the Terms of Service contemplates. However, the provision makes clear that suspension or termination of an account is not limited to violations of that policy; rather, as noted above, such suspension or termination could be "at any time for any or no reason." …

We reach the same conclusion with respect to Borrman's alleged remarks, which are too vague to modify the Terms of Service. The alleged remarks are: (1) "I think you have some nuanced points that could be helpful as [sic] try to move ahead" and "[w]e're trying to make sure that factual debate finds a way through the emotion, but we're obviously not successful all the time"; (2) "The points you're raising should not be an issue at all…. The policy is designed to allow debate and discussion, but to discourage conspiracy theories, etc. Please let me know if you run into any issues"; and (3) "I will say that your name has never come up in the discussions around these policies…. If it does I will try to ensure you're given a heads up before an action is taken, but I am not always made aware of them before they're executed. If something happens, please let me know," Collectively, even drawing all inferences in Berenson's favor, these remarks suggest at most that Borrman would attempt to notify Berenson of action taken against him—not that no action would be taken against Berenson or that Berenson would necessarily receive advance notice of any action.

At bottom, Twitter's Terms of Service provided that Twitter could suspend Berenson for "any or no reason," and it did so. There was no modification of that contract and Berenson has failed to allege an actual breach of contract. Therefore, the district court correctly determined that Berenson failed to state a tortious interference claim.

{Before the district court and now on appeal, Berenson relies heavily on Berenson v. Twitter, Inc (N.D. Cal. 2022), a decision in a case Berenson initiated against Twitter in December 2021. There, the district court granted in part and denied in part Twitter's motion to dismiss, holding that the five-strike policy and Borrman's "specific and direct assurances to [Berenson] regarding his posts pursuant to that policy" "amount[ed] to a modification of the [Terms of Service]" and that Berenson had plausibly alleged that "Twitter's conduct … modified its contract with [him] and then breached that contract by failing to abide by its own five-strike policy and its specific commitments set forth through [Borrman]." Id. at (emphasis in original). We find that analysis unpersuasive for the reasons stated above.}

India swung for the moon with Chandrayaan-1, its first deep space mission.

On Oct. 21, 2008, the Indian Space Research Organization launched its first mission to the moon. The mission was named Chandrayaan-1, and it consisted of both an orbiter and an impactor.

Chandrayaan-1 launched from the Satish Dhawan Space Center on an Indian rocket called the Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle, or PSLV-XL. (Launch occurred at 8:52 p.m. EDT, though it was about 6:22 a.m. local time at the launch site on Oct. 22.)

It arrived in lunar orbit about three weeks later and dropped off the Moon Impact Probe, which crashed into the moon on Nov. 14. The rest of the Chandrayaan-1 spacecraft stayed in orbit, where it collected and transmitted data from the moon to Earth for about a year. The mission ended abruptly 14 months ahead of its planned end date when scientists lost contact with the probe.

The Indian Space Research Organisation, or ISRO, had a lot riding on the success of Chandrayaan-1 moon mission, which marked the first-ever deep space mission by the Indian space agency. It was also the first mission to the moon for India, a country that hopes to launch its own crewed spacecraft and build a space station.

Chandrayaan-1 did more than just demonstrate that India's space program was capable of launching missions to the moon; it also returned some amazing science results, like evidence of water ice on the moon.

"In September 2009, scientists published results of data collected by the American M3 instrument which had detected absorption features on the polar regions of the surface of the Moon usually linked to hydroxyl- and/ or water-bearing molecules," NASA said in a mission overview.

The spacecraft carried 11 instruments to study the moon, five of them built by ISRO. The rest were experiments for the European Space Agnecy and NASA.

"In addition to the five Indian instruments, the spacecraft carried scientific equipment from the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Sweden, and Bulgaria," NASA said.

It took until Nov. 8, 2008 for Chandrayaan-1 to enter lunar orbit, where it stayed until contact was lost in August 2009, most likely due to a power supply failure caused by overheating.

"This was short of the spacecraft’s planned two-year lifetime, although ISRO noted that at least 95% of its mission objectives had been accomplished by then," NASA wrote.

India did not stop at the moon with Chandrayaan-1. In 2019, the country launched the Chandrayaan-2 orbiter, which carried the Vikram lander and Pragyan rover. Chandrayaan-2 reached lunar orbit successfully, but the Vikram landing failed.

The Chandrayaan-3 misson followed in 2023, which successfully landed a new Vikram lander and Pragyan rover near the moon's south pole. India plans to launch the Chandryaan-4 mission to return moon samples to Earth in 2028.

#1430. Report: Cyber Attacks Increasingly Exploit Trusted Workplace Tools

In its latest threat intelligence report, Ontinue found that attackers are increasingly using trusted workplace tools to trick users into giving them access to enterprise networks. Approximately 30% of organizations monitored by the cybersecurity company experienced at least one attempt to impersonate IT support through Microsoft Teams during the first half of 2026. About one in every 990 external Teams contacts showed signs of someone pretending to be an internal IT worker.

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. The person warns of a security issue, a software problem or an update that can't wait. Then they walk the employee through opening Windows Quick Assist, installing remote-access software, or running a PowerShell command. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

In these attacks, criminals trick employees into completing what appears to be a normal Microsoft authentication process. But doing so can give the attacker access to the employee's account and system without actually breaking their password.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

The security firm says that IT needs to keep a closer eye on unexpected Teams contacts, remote-support requests, suspicious PowerShell activity, and changes to authentication settings. Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.

New observations from the James Webb Space Telescope reveal a concentration of heavy metals near the Milky Way's central black hole. The discovery deepens a mystery about why our galaxy's black hole behaves the way it does.

The Milky Way's supermassive black hole is surrounded by a surprising trove of heavy metals, according to new observations by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). The metals may offer new clues about how Earth got its share of heavy elements — but it could also make one of the greatest mysteries about our local black hole even harder to solve.

In astronomy, metals refer to any element heavier than hydrogen or helium, with common examples including oxygen, carbon, iron or gold. These elements are forged through nuclear fusion in the hearts of stars that are reaching the ends of their lives, before being sprayed into space and incorporated into new generations of stars.

Scientists say the material spotted by JWST likely came from supernovas ‪—‬ stellar explosions that blasted the metal into the region around Sagittarius A (Sgr A*), the monster black hole in the middle of our galaxy.

The metals, detected as gas, appear to have the same composition as several nearby stars, which suggests both the stars and the gas originated at the same time. Researchers shared the observations in a paper published Oct. 19 in The Astronomical Journal, following up on work published in The Astrophysical Journal in May.

"We know there's a black hole at the centre of our galaxy pulling material into itself, but we see that it's eating a lot less material than other supermassive black holes, and more slowly," Nicole Ford, first author of the new paper and a doctoral student in astrophysics at McGill University in Montreal, said in a statement. "Part of understanding why this is happening means looking at the properties of the materials available for it to consume, including gas, dust and the stars orbiting around it."

JWST orbits at Lagrange point 2, which is about 1 million miles (1.5 million kilometers) from Earth in the opposite direction of the sun. The telescope identified the heat signatures of specific gas clouds billowing near the black hole, as well as the metal content within those clouds.

Three views of the galactic center in infrared light, with the location of the black hole Sagittarius A* marked by a white star. From left to right, the images zoom in and show the region at different wavelengths.

"These findings could advance understanding of how metals are distributed around supermassive black holes in more distant galaxies, as well as the processes that supplied Earth with many of these elements," McGill researchers said in the statement. The metals' presence "influences how quickly gases cool, which in turn influences how readily the gas can be drawn into a black hole," they added.
