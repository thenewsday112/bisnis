#564. Lubetkin warns Flávio Bolsonaro that “it is not easy to leave Mercosur”

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Montevideo, October 30th 2026 - 00:16 UTC

Uruguay’s Foreign Minister Mario Lubetkin has warned Brazilian presidential candidate Flávio Bolsonaro that “it is not easy to leave Mercosur”, in response to his criticism of the bloc during the campaign for the October 25 runoff. In the same interview, with the newspaper Folha de S.Paulo, he said Uruguay would not restore diplomatic relations with Venezuela while Delcy Rodríguez governs.

“It is not easy to leave Mercosur. There are too many interrelated agreements in the business sphere, and that would cause major losses for the Brazilian economy. And there are too many mechanisms that prevent such a quick exit,” Lubetkin said. “I hope that, if that gentleman ends up presiding over Brazil, when he starts governing he will realise that many statements made during the election campaign are impracticable once in government.”

The government programme Bolsonaro registered with the Superior Electoral Court does not mention Mercosur. On the campaign trail, the senator has spoken of “freeing Brazil from the shackles of Mercosur”, referring to the constraints the common external tariff places on negotiating its own trade agreements, a phrase some media have interpreted as raising the possibility of leaving the bloc. Uruguay has held the Mercosur pro tempore presidency since July 1.

Lubetkin said Uruguay would work with whoever is elected: “First, let the elections be clean, transparent, democratic and calm. Then, naturally, the Brazilian people will choose whoever they consider best, and we will work with whoever wins, as state policy.”

On Venezuela, the minister ruled out resuming relations: “In no way, and we are not going to resume them.” He argued that from elections Uruguay had questioned “came a president who is now, on top of everything, imprisoned in the United States, and from that president comes a president as illegitimate as he was”. Uruguay will restore ties, he said, when “an independent court confirms” the winner of a democratic election. Caracas withdrew its diplomatic staff from Uruguay and six other Latin American countries in July 2024 after they questioned Nicolás Maduro’s re-election; Maduro has been detained in the United States since January. Rodríguez announced at the UN in September that elections would be held, without setting a date.

#573. IMF trims Latin America’s outlook to 2.3% and sharply cuts Argentina’s

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Montevideo, October 30th 2026 - 00:16 UTC

The International Monetary Fund has lowered its 2026 growth forecast for Latin America and the Caribbean to 2.3%, a tenth of a point less than in July, and sharply cut Argentina’s, from 3.5% to 2.1%. Chile and Uruguay are also among the region’s largest downward revisions, according to the World Economic Outlook presented on Monday in Bangkok, during the annual meetings of the Fund and the World Bank.

For Argentina, Fund economist Petya Koeva Brooks attributed the 1.4-point cut to “lower activity observed through the third quarter of this year” and to “weaker domestic demand in a context of still tight domestic financial conditions and the continued structural transformation of the economy”. The Fund also raised its forecast for average inflation this year to 32.5%, from 30.4% in April, and for 2027 to 21.2%, from 15.7%. It projects unemployment of 7.6% in 2026 and 7.1% in 2027. Argentina’s growth forecast for next year was revised from 4% to 3.5%. Economy Minister Luis Caputo is attending the meetings.

Uruguay’s forecast was cut by half a point to 1.3%, in line with the Fund mission that visited the country in September, which attributed the slowdown to the impact of drought on farming. Chile saw the largest adjustment, of 1.8 points. Brazil would grow 2.2% this year and 1.9% in 2027, two and three tenths less than in July, because of still-tight monetary conditions and the withdrawal of fiscal support. For the region, the Fund expects 2.5% growth in 2027 and inflation of 6.8% this year, excluding Venezuela. According to the report, higher energy prices benefit commodity exporters and hurt importers.

Globally, the Fund forecasts growth of 3.1% in 2026 and 3.4% in 2027, while global inflation would rise to 4.7% this year, driven by energy and food. It assumes an average oil price of US$95.74 a barrel in 2026, against US$67.74 in 2025, and that the Strait of Hormuz will reopen in March 2027.

In the United States, headline inflation would rise to 3.6% this year and fall to 2.8% in 2027, while the economy would grow 2.3% in both years, supported by consumption, fiscal policy and investment in artificial intelligence. The Fund identifies a prolonged war in the Middle East and an abrupt correction in expectations about artificial intelligence as the main risks.

#583. A magnitude 7.7 earthquake shakes Panama

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Montevideo, October 30th 2026 - 00:16 UTC

A magnitude 7.7 earthquake shook Panama on Friday at 12:56 local time, centred on the Azuero peninsula in Herrera province, according to the US Geological Survey. Panamanian authorities have not so far confirmed any casualties, though inspections are continuing and they have not ruled out injuries or people trapped. Damage to buildings was reported in several provinces.

The epicentre was about 14 kilometres southwest of Pitaloza Arriba, a sparsely populated area near the Pacific coast some 240 kilometres from the capital, at shallow depth. Panama’s Institute of Geosciences estimated the magnitude at 7.2. Its director, Néstor Luque, attributed the quake to the Azuero-Sona fault system, which had been accumulating stress for some time. More than 30 aftershocks were recorded.

The National Civil Protection System reported damage in Penonomé, in Coclé province, including to the cathedral of San Juan Bautista, the governor’s office and a hotel, as well as in Chitré and Venao. Its director, Omar Smith, warned that aftershocks would continue, though with decreasing intensity.

The Education Ministry cancelled afternoon classes nationwide, public offices were closed, water supply was cut in some areas because of power failures and Tocumen international airport temporarily suspended operations. The Panama Canal Authority said the waterway was operating normally.

The Pacific Tsunami Warning Center issued an alert for the coasts of Colombia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, Peru, Nicaragua, Guatemala, El Salvador and Mexico, which was lifted hours later. Smith had considered a tsunami unlikely because the epicentre was on land. In Chile, the navy declared a precautionary alert for Easter Island and lifted it after the magnitude, initially estimated at 8.0, was revised. The quake was felt in the Colombian cities of Medellín and Cartagena.

President José Raúl Mulino called for calm and asked emergency agencies for a report on the effects. “What has happened must not be used to generate alarm and disorder,” he wrote.

The earthquake comes two months after a magnitude 7.4 quake that killed 335 people in Colombia’s Chocó department on August 10.

Montevideo, October 30th 2026 - 00:16 UTC

US President Donald Trump has not endorsed either of the two leading candidates in Brazil's presidential election on Sunday, unlike in recent elections in Honduras, Colombia, Chile, Argentina and Costa Rica.

Asked twice this week by reporters before boarding the presidential helicopter, Trump gave vague answers. “I'm following it very closely. It's a very important country. The people of Brazil are fantastic. It's a very important election,” he said on Thursday. “We're looking at the whole thing and following it very closely,” he repeated on Friday.

Over the past year, Trump publicly backed candidates including Nasry Asfura in Honduras, Abelardo de la Espriella in Colombia ahead of the runoff, Javier Milei in Argentina, José Antonio Kast in Chile and Laura Fernández in Costa Rica. Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, met him in the Oval Office in May, leaving with a joint photograph but no statement of support.

Trump's caution contrasts with his administration's tensions with Brasília. In July, Washington imposed new tariffs on Brazilian goods and sanctioned two Brazilian citizens and three companies over alleged links to the First Capital Command, which it designated a terrorist organisation along with Comando Vermelho. In 2025 it had sanctioned ministers and judges involved in the trial of Jair Bolsonaro, sentenced to 27 years for attempting a coup, a process Trump called a “witch hunt”.

The Brazilian Intelligence Agency raised the risk of US interference to “critical level” in a classified report sent in late August to the presidency, the Superior Electoral Court and the Justice Ministry. The assessment is preventive and does not claim that actual interference has occurred. According to Reuters, agency officials told a French and German delegation in September that there was interference from both the United States and Russia. In Washington, some thirty Democratic lawmakers, including Senator Bernie Sanders, accused Secretary of State Marco Rubio in a letter of acting “as an agent of interference and destabilisation” in Brazil.

Polls show Lula and Bolsonaro tied in a possible runoff, which would take place on October 25 if no candidate wins more than 50% of valid votes today. Voting is electronic and results are usually known within hours of polls closing.

#71. Putin's War of Aggression: A Journey Along the Dnipro in Wartime Ukraine

📅 Sep 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

What the Rhine River is to Germans, the Dnipro is to Ukrainians. It is not just the largest river in the country, it is also the most sung about. In songs and poems it embodies Ukraine. It is an old Cossack who dreams of past greatness and sharpens his saber at night for new deeds. "Grandfather Dnipro will rejoice," the original text of the national anthem says, when fortune once again smiles on Ukraine.

At the same time, the Dnipro is the greatest natural obstacle to Russia's invasion of Ukraine. From north to south, from the forests on the Belarusian border to the steppe on the Black Sea, it flows right through the middle of the country. Only briefly did the troops of Russian leader Vladimir Putin manage to cross the river, until they were forced to abandon the west bank again in the first year of the war. Since then, the Dnipro's lower reaches have marked the front line between Russian-occupied and free Ukraine. The river that forms Ukraine's backbone threatens to become a permanent border.

A trip along the Dnipro is thus a journey through both the history and present of Ukraine. Together with photographer Emile Ducke and longtime DER SPIEGEL producer Fedir Petrov, I set out to search the riverbanks for Ukraine's identity – and for the scars of the war.

It is a journey that leads from light into darkness. It begins in Demydiv in the north, where Ukraine repelled Russia's advance on the capital in 2022. It leads through villages and towns where people wrest a peaceful everyday life from the war, with growing difficulty the closer the front gets.
