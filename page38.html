#1128. The 30 Best Halloween TV Episodes of All Time

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

There is no shortage of sitcoms, superheroes, or Snoopy to binge before trick-or-treating.

Fall TV died a slow and painful death many years ago. But that doesn’t mean we’ve all lost the plot on Halloween. Even in the streaming era, television shows still know when to drop the occasional Halloween episode, wherein familiar fictional characters dress up as other familiar fictional characters. So now is a great time to throw on a few favorites while you put together your last-minute costume. (You do have a costume, right?)

If horror movies, even the not so-scary ones, aren’t the vibe you’re looking for, let good ol’ TV help set the tone for All Hallows' Eve. From classic shows like The Brady Bunch to more modern favorites like Abbott Elementary, there is no shortage of great Halloween-themed episodes to binge before November 1. In the spirit of Halloween, Esquire’s entertainment editors spent some time digging through our favorites to settle on the best 30 Halloween episodes ever.

Grab some candy corn, put on your coziest jack-o'-lantern socks, and settle on the couch. These are the best Halloween-themed episodes of TV ever made.

Ted and John’s annual tradition of throwing eggs at bad Halloween costumes is thwarted when Blaire ropes Ted into being her designated driver for a party. Between Blaire’s impressive Catwoman costume, a killer gag referencing the Milgram Experiment, and a surprisingly moving story about seizing the day, Ted somehow has one of the best Halloween episodes in the entire streaming era.

One of the most popular episodes of Bob’s Burgers is this Halloween episode from season 6. When Louise claims she’s never been frightened by anything, the family tries to scare her good with their own haunted house. Just as the kids are ready to write off their parents’ lame efforts, the house seems to come alive. Stay for the credits to hear the hot new single from Boyz 4 Now, “I Love You So Much (It’s Scary).”

“Home” is one of the darkest episodes in all of The X-Files, which is really saying something. Originally aired in October 1996, the episode sees Scully and Mulder investigate a secluded farm home in Pennsylvania. While the episode is basically a tribute to American horror classics like The Texas Chain Saw Massacre, “Home” delivers its own hair-raising scares in the Peacocks, a family of violent inbreds. Though The X-Files was limited in what it could show onscreen due to broadcast restrictions, its suggestive nature lends the episode its pitch-black power.

For quintessential ‘90s sitcom antics, look no further than Home Improvement. The first Halloween episode of the series mostly centers on Brad being in his first-ever fight with his girlfriend, which only Tim can help him sort out. But the Halloween stuff happens in the subplot, where Tim and Al build a haunted house to scare the daylights out of one smug kid in particular.

Zoinks! This immaculate crossover from Supernatural’s thirteenth season feels ready-made for an All Hallows’ Eve marathon. (Never mind that it aired in March 2018.) In “Scoobynatural,” Sam, Dean, and Castiel get sucked into a television set and crash the Scooby-Doo, Where Are You? episode “A Night of Fright Is No Delight.” The Scooby Gang has met a lot of pop culture legends—Dick Van Dyke, Batman, John Cena—but their outing with the Supernatural guys marks the first time they ever tangled with genuine mystical evil.

Decades of Halloween parties are our way into Mr. Peanutbutter’s psyche and patterns of problematic behavior. In this season 5 episode of BoJack Horseman, the annoyingly upbeat Mr. Peanutbutter throws his annual Halloween party at BoJack’s place, which happens to be ground zero for his biggest relationships coming apart. Set against four different Halloween parties—in 1993, 2004, 2009, and 2018—audiences find new angles to a staple character we thought we had figured out.

South Park has been on the air long enough to have several Halloween episodes, but this season 1 hit is still the best. After Kenny is killed (again), he’s brought back to life as a zombie, whose condition is mistaken for a severe case of pinkeye. As “pinkeye” spreads through South Park, the boys try to celebrate Halloween with some very questionable costumes.

In Abbott Elementary, Halloween festivities descend into madness when a stolen bag of candy turns all the kids into hyperactive, sugar-infused monsters straight out of 28 Days Later. As the teachers try to corral the student body, Janine stresses over a Halloween party invitation. The episode caps off with a perfect Avengers: Infinity War reference.

This season 5 episode of Boy Meets World is technically not a Halloween special at all. It premiered in February 1998. But when teen slashers like Scream and I Know What You Did Last Summer were ruling the box office, Boy Meets World got in on the craze with a surprisingly emotional episode that interrogates Shawn’s guilt over the breakup of Cory and Topanga. During detention, the characters get locked inside the school with a masked killer on the loose.

After the Brady boys scare the girls with a fake ghost, the girls strike back with a taste of their own medicine. “Fright Night” is classic Brady Bunch antics, as the whole household gets in on the fun. Never mind that at this point in season 4, Greg and Marcia look old enough to be at parties instead of coming up with pranks.

It's Halloween at Cloud 9, which is an oddly fitting time for them to find a dead body in the store. A creepy employee, likely peeping in on the women's restroom, apparently got stuck and starved to death in the walls. With hours to spend before the coroner can get the body, everyone at Cloud 9, employees and shoppers alike, must go about their day as a rotten corpse watches over them all.

The Paddy's gang experiences a real scream when Dee gets pregnant, and everyone retraces their steps to figure out what the hell happened at their Halloween party. Leave it to It's Always Sunny in Philadelphia to turn their Halloween episode into a Rashomon scenario.

Before Deadpool, Ryan Reynolds was a sitcom star in the forgotten but outrageously funny Two Guys, a Girl, and a Pizza Place. In the show's first Halloween episode from season 2, it breaks all its rules by unleashing an evil doppelgänger of Reynolds' Berg who hacks his way through the group. Unfortunately, you cannot stream Two Guys, a Girl, and a Pizza Place on any platform in the U.S. But the heroes over at Shout! Studios produced a DVD box set that deserves a spot on your shelf.

When Ben starts living with Andy and April, he has to put up with their (unannounced) Halloween party. But while most of the episode follows conventional All Hallows hijinks, the real attraction is the cold open where Andy and April talk about “blood orphans” that sealed it as a classic. Would it surprise you to know that Widow’s Bay creator Katie Dippold wrote this one?

You can make the reasonable argument that It's the Great Pumpkin, Charlie Brown is still the greatest Halloween special ever made. It gave us Snoopy’s World War I battle with the Red Baron, Lucy and the football, “I got a rock,” and so much more. Honestly, without Great Pumpkin, we wouldn't be here making this list at all.

Just days before The Walking Dead premiered and became a cable TV monster, Community spoofed zombie movies with the hilarious and memorable “Epidemiology.” At the shockingly well-decorated Greendale Halloween party, some bad mystery meat turns the partygoers into grotesque husks with a taste for flesh. The episode peaks in the climax, when Troy reclaims his impressive Aliens loader cosplay to save his friends. He fails, but it’s still a cool moment.

All of Buffy the Vampire Slayer is tailor-made for Halloween. But in season 2, the cult series delivered a banger with the simply-titled episode, “Halloween.” When Buffy and the gang buy cursed costumes, they spend Halloween night believing they are their costumed alter egos—including Buffy, whose badass slayer skills are neutered when she believes she’s a dainty noblewoman from the 18th century.

If you think this Power Rangers Zeo episode has the mighty morphin’ heroes clashing with a pumpkin monster or something, think again. In this Halloween special from the 1996 iteration of the show, the Green Ranger, Adam (Johnny Yong Bosch), falls asleep while watching a horror movie marathon. He then wakes up in a black-and-white world of vampires, werewolves, and mummies. Here, the Power Rangers pay tribute to the Universal Monsters, plus a few karate kicks.

One year after Stranger Things became a Netflix juggernaut, it returned for its blockbuster second season. The premiere takes place during the greatest day of all: Halloween. While it is just the first chapter of another long and terrifying ordeal with the Upside Down, “Trick or Treat, Freak” is full of hijinks and house parties, and some of the sickest Ghostbusters costumes I’ve ever seen.

You know what's scarier than a ghost? Firing someone. In the first Halloween-themed episode of The Office, Michael Scott is forced by corporate to fire one of his employees before EOD. While later episodes of The Office would crank up the volume on the outrageousness—including a wildly controversial cold open from season 6 that was pulled from DVD and streaming—“Halloween” represents exactly the sort of banal black comedy that made the series so great in the first place.

From its perfect cold open to Jake proposing to Amy, “HalloVeen” takes the belt (sorry, cumberbund) as not just one of the best episodes of Brooklyn Nine-Nine but among the best Halloween episodes in any TV show. It’s the fifth annual Halloween Heist at the Ninety-Ninth Precinct, but this time, the stakes are higher than ever. Normally, the Halloween episodes of Brooklyn Nine-Nine are skippable filler, but given how this one cements Jake and Amy’s relationship in stone, fans can’t miss this one.

Friends might have some great Christmas and Thanksgiving episodes, but season 8's “The One with the Halloween Party” tops them all. As the pals throw a costume party in their improbably large New York City apartment, Phoebe runs into her twin sister, Ursula, and her fiancé (played by guest star Sean Penn). Peep some corporate synergy, as this Warner Bros.-produced sitcom dressed up Monica and Phoebe as Catwoman and Supergirl.

This show aired for just one season, so it has only one Halloween episode. But it’s a great one. Lindsay thinks she’s too cool to get into Halloween, but her mom wants her to stay home and pass out candy to trick-or-treaters. Meanwhile, we get some pretty solid costumes (“I’m not a girl, I’m a... Bionic Woman!”) from Sam, Bill, and Neal, who take to the streets for one last childhood All Hallows’ Eve.

Only Larry David would accuse trick-or-treaters of rigging the system. In this episode from season 2, Larry gets irritated after some older kids end up at his doorstep. But he gets what's coming to him when he wakes the next morning to find his backyard vandalized. “I didn't know there was going to be felony or treat!”

What starts as a pretty normal Halloween episode becomes a love letter to filmmaker Alfred Hitchcock. In this season 3 episode of That ‘70s Show, the guys feel stuck between childhood and adulthood while watching a Hitchcock movie marathon on Halloween. But when they suspect that Bob has murdered Midge, they spring into action, Rear Window style.

You can spend all of Halloween watching every Treehouse of Horror special from The Simpsons. There are dozens of them. Literally. But “Halloween of Horror” from season 27—the first time that The Simpsons ever did a “normal” Halloween episode—is a strong episode that uses the season to explore real emotions like fear and bravery. After a traumatic night at Krustyland, Lisa has a hard time getting over her new fear of everything.

It’s Halloween in New York City, and what’s Ted Mosby doing? Waiting for the Slutty Pumpkin. This season 1 episode of How I Met Your Mother playfully rips off Charlie Brown as Ted, dressed in a comically dated costume, waits for the mysterious girl who got away all those years ago at a Halloween party. Years later in season 7, Ted finally closed the book on the Slutty Pumpkin when she surfaced at last, and was played by guest star Katie Holmes.

In the first Halloween special for Modern Family, Claire tries to claim Halloween as her holiday (after Gloria had seized on Christmas and Thanksgiving) by creating the greatest haunted house in the neighborhood. While the episode is primarily concerned with Claire opening up about feeling sidelined in holiday traditions, this season 2 episode soars when Mitchell, dressed as Spider-Man, tries to avoid embarrassment at work... by climbing out of the building.

When the Marvel Cinematic Universe returned for Phase Four, it did so with WandaVision, the franchise's first Disney+ series. A tribute to TV sitcoms, “All-New Halloween Spooktacular!” grounded the show in the late ‘90s and early ‘00s with a Malcolm in the Middle-style episode where Uncle Pietro (guest star Evan Peters, whose appearance bridges the X-Men franchise to the MCU) comes to hang out on Halloween. Peep Elizabeth Olsen and Paul Bettany donning their characters' original costumes from the comics.

New Girl knew how to have fun on Halloween, and in season 2, it used the spooky season to interrogate the scariest thing in dating: situationships. When Jess takes a job working as a zombie at a haunted house, her not-that-serious boyfriend Sam comes to see her—but Nick finds out just how detached he is from her. Meanwhile, Schmidt and Cece wind up with an “accidental” couples costume.

#951. The Free K-Beauty Way to Stop Hair Loss

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

It’s the great generational debate: Side part or middle part? Where we part our hair dictates the facial features we want to play up and sets the tone for our overall vibe.

But what if this defining hair feature that we spend a lot of time contemplating comes with unexpected consequences? According to Korean hair clinic Lee Moon Won, sticking to one hair part forever might be the cause of one of our most pressing hair concerns: unwanted shedding.

There are a few reasons why this might be. Wonuk Hwang, MD, clinic director at the Cheongdam Lebelle Clinic in Seoul, explains that the exposed skin where we part our hair has less protection from environmental stressors such as the sun’s harmful UV rays, and that should always be looked at seriously. Hwang points to studies that show UV radiation can cause cellular damage to the hair follicles and scalp tissue, which could increase hair shedding after severe scalp sunburn. Even though those studies don’t mention hair parts specifically, he says that exposed scalp will always need sun protection.

“It is definitely important to be mindful of cumulative sun exposure on the scalp where we part our hair,” says Helen He, MD, director of lasers and cosmetic surgery and codirector of Mount Sinai-Clinique Healthy Skin Dermatology Center. “Since that area of skin is more exposed to the sun without innate protection from hair, it may be more susceptible to developing skin cancers and precancerous lesions. While there is no current evidence that chronic UV exposure directly leads to increased hair shedding, there is a clear association with skin cancer risk, oxidative stress, and inflammation.”

Treating inflammation is a key part of treating hair loss in K-beauty. As He explains, inflammation around your hair follicles can disrupt the normal hair cycle and lead to increased hair loss and shedding. Several hair conditions, she says, such as seborrheic dermatitis and scalp psoriasis, showcase this; when they increase inflammation on the scalp, it will eventually exacerbate thinning hair. Autoimmune diseases such as alopecia areata, and cicatricial alopecia are considered chronic inflammation disorders that also cause hair loss.

“The thinking is that an inflamed scalp makes follicles more vulnerable,” adds Michelle Lee, chief marketing officer of K-Beauty World and former editor in chief of Allure. “It’s the same logic K-beauty applies to the skin, where you calm the barrier first and everything else works better.”

There’s also some mechanical stress with your regular hair part. He says that when hair is repeatedly parted, brushed, and styled in one direction, it may inadvertently put tension on your strands. “Over time, this repeated friction can accumulate and lead to hair breakage,” she explains. “While excessive pulling or tension can damage hair follicles and potentially lead to traction alopecia.”

He wouldn’t call this method essential, but she says it may be helpful. “It will prevent any one area of the scalp from excessive UV exposure,” she explains. “It would also prevent excessive tension to the same areas of hair.”

“It’s something so easy and immediately actionable, but it’s more about the mindset rather than thinking that changing your part will completely solve your hair-loss issues,” adds Lee. “It’s more about being thoughtful about how we’re treating the scalp in general and making sure that we care for it and keep it protected and nourished.”

Should you want to give this method a try, she says the Lee Moon Won Clinic, which she visited last year, suggests changing your part every six months and moving it at least four centimeters away from old parts so that the newly covered area isn’t the same spot that has been exposed and it ensures that the same strip of the scalp won’t be subjected to constant sun and dust, both of which can lead to inflammation.

Hwang says that there’s no single K-beauty approach to hair loss. Instead, it’s a holistic approach that assesses the scalp and identifies the causes of hair loss. “It is focused on optimizing scalp health to create an environment that supports healthy hair growth,” He agrees. Other than reducing inflammation, she says K-beauty prioritizes fortifying the scalp barrier and microbiome, gentle exfoliation to decrease buildup, and improving microcirculation.

For everyday care, she recommends regular washing to prevent buildup and excess oil. You can turn to known K-beauty soothing ingredients such as cica, centella asiatica, and tea-tree and green-tea extracts, which she says are staples in K-beauty skin care and scalp products for optimal scalp health. Hwang recommends avoiding styles that repeatedly pull on your hair and adding scalp sun protection into your routine with a hat or a water-resistant sunscreen with SPF 30 (or higher) broad-spectrum protection.

Lee says that the hair clinic recommended that she tweak certain lifestyle habits, such as changing her pillowcase every two to three days and taking a closer look at her diet. The clinic also gave her tips on how to better dry her hair and sleep to keep hair healthy. “What struck me is that hair loss was treated as a scalp and lifestyle problem,” she says. “At the end of my analysis and treatment, they weren’t trying to treat my issues with medications. Rather, they have their own concoction of natural ingredients as well as a whole list of lifestyle changes.”

And when in doubt, see a doctor for any persistent or unexplained shedding. Addressing any underlying causes, whether it be inflammation, hormones, stress, or an autoimmune condition, will always be the most important step when it comes to hair loss. Whether you have a side part or not.

#425. How Apple Built Watch Series 12 to Track Heart Rate 24/7—Without Nuking Battery Life

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The newest models take measurements every five seconds, thanks to brighter LEDs and redesigned sensors.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. Why Trust Us?

When Apple announced its new lineup of smartwatches—Apple Watch 12 and Apple Watch Ultra 4—in September, one of the biggest highlights was that the devices are now capable of keeping tabs on your pulse roughly every five seconds around the clock, with even greater accuracy than before. This is a 60 times the improvement over the previous models. To do so, Apple had to create larger, brighter green LEDs on the back side of the watch. Those same LEDs also allow the device to check in on your heart rate variability (HRV), an indicator of stress and recovery, 24 times as often, throughout the day.

The biggest question we had following the announcement: How does this not nuke the battery on a device that’s already criticized for needing daily fill-ups?

We spoke with the team at Apple that was tasked with not only hardware modifications but also algorithmic advancements that allow the Watch to take your HR more often while being more power efficient.

Apple also claims that the new Health Sensing System results in the most accurate heart rate tracking of any wearable—and went so far as to distribute the findings of its own study on the matter.

So, does it work? And how well? Let’s dive in.

In a nutshell, all wrist-based optical heart rate monitors work in roughly the same fashion: LEDs shine light into your skin, your blood absorbs some of that light, and photodiodes read the amount of light that gets reflected back. From there, the watch can determine your heart rate and HRV.

There are some complications to overcome, however. The physical real estate on the backside of a watch is limited. And there’s a ton of movement that happens at your wrist, which makes accurate readings challenging—anything that requires a strong grip, like strength training, creates significant interference.

For the new watches, Apple looked to maximize the use of that sensor space. If you look at the backside of the older models of Apple Watch, you’ll see eight small dots in a circle at the center of the unit. Four of them shine a combination of green, red, and infrared light. The other four are sensors to capture the reflected light. Those are tiny in comparison to what’s been rolled out on AW12 and AWU4.

On the Series 12, Apple separated the LEDs—four exclusively emit green light, because that works best in challenging daytime conditions and workout scenarios. The other four combine red and infrared, and activate when you’re largely motionless or sleeping—times when those are adequate. They’ll be less bothersome while you sleep, and the red, in particular, is needed to measure blood oxygen levels.

Those larger and brighter LEDs, though, take up the space where the old photodiode receptors were set, so Apple replaced them with a ring of eight trapezoid-shaped photodiodes arranged in a ring surrounding the LEDs.

The larger sensors improve the readings, but only if the bright light shining out of the LEDs can be properly isolated. To prevent any light leakage, Apple had to re-engineer the crystal covering the sensors. That casing is not there just to keep out dirt and water, but it’s set up so light can be directed at your skin, yet shielded from the sensors.

“What's really different here, we took red and white zirconia and did a couple processes with a lot of heat and pressure,” said Eugene Kim, vice president of hardware engineering at Apple. “It's a very unique singular piece of zirconia that has specific opaque and clear areas—black and clear—which really helps us isolate the light source from the receiving source, because you only want to see the light off your blood, not just random stray light that might be emitting from the LEDs.”

Apple used a new process to create those black and clear sections of glass. Previously, the housing was made up of multiple pieces of crystal that were essentially glued together. Now, it’s created using a “double-shot” process where the red zirconia is molded first for the areas that will end up being dark. Then a second injection mold of the white ultimately becomes the clear sections. After it goes through the heat and pressure processes, it’s polished to give the single, shiny glass cover you see on the back of the Watch.

One knock on Apple Watch versus sport-specific GPS devices is its relatively short battery life. Compared to many other smartwatches, Apple Watches have historically needed to be charged every day. Devices from Garmin and Coros, on the other hand, can last days or weeks—you charge them so infrequently you might forget where you placed the charging cable. The battery life of an Apple Watch Ultra, say, has been extended, but you’re still putting it on the magnetic puck every other day at least. So, Apple knew it couldn’t reduce those run times, even though it wanted to improve the HR sensing capabilities.

“The name of the game there is just efficiency,” said Kim. “We really wanted to improve our performance by improving the efficiency of the system. And I mean that in very broad terms. It's physical packaging, it's energy usage, it's computation.”

Apple didn’t plan to just pack in a bigger battery, but to ensure that everything required less power to operate.

“We actually took our work on the AirPods heart rate,” Kim continued, “where that is also a very power-constrained product, and we took the [machine learning] approaches there and actually brought that to the Watch to increase efficiency of the algorithms, to not only improve performance but also use less power.”

Steve Waydo, Director of Health Sensing, explained that the biggest impact is the improved efficiency. “For workouts, for example, we expect similar heart rate performance for that five-second stream, but we're achieving that performance with a lot less power. So that can translate into longer workout durations or just a smaller overall impact on your battery life.”

The efficiency improvements also mean bigger changes to the rest of the day. Previously, HR features were based on infrared light largely because they required less power. But, with the more efficient LEDs and sensor, and the ability to provide the data with a lower power cost, Apple is able to fire up the green lights all day without overly taxing the battery.

I have been fairly impressed with the battery on the AWU4. One of my first runs with it was a 10-miler that included five fast intervals each one mile long. After 75 minutes of running, I only drained 6 percent of the battery. In the first mile, I realized that I messed up the transfer of my cellular data plan from my previous Apple Watch, so I couldn’t fire up music to power me through that workout—which would certainly have delivered a bigger drain on the battery. (I got it all sorted later that day.)

As a competitive distance runner who follows a structured training plan, I use my watch to keep tabs on my sleep, too. I want to know not only how well I slept but also how well I’ve recovered from the workouts of the previous day or two. Tracking overnight HRV stats can give me insight into that, but can also be factored into a Readiness score (1 to 10) that Apple surfaces in the Health app. The score is an easy way for me to know whether I’m recovered enough to tackle another speed session on the track, or if I should pull back and go for an easy jog—or take a rest day completely. (To be fair, most other watchmakers offer a similar metric.)

But even non-marathoners and athletes can benefit from the overnight data as well as the new all-day HRV readings. Dr. Asha Chesnutt, a member of Apple’s clinical team, explained why that matters.

“As a clinician, obviously, continuous heart rate monitoring is part of the hospital, the ICU where I come from,” she said. “For some users, they really just like to be able to connect the dots—like not drinking enough water corresponds to increasing your heart rate, or drinking that extra cup of coffee and making you jittery might increase your heart rate. For me as a clinician, I think that that's pretty obvious, but to a lot of people it's not. Even before you physically have a fever and get sick, you might see some subtle changes in your heart rate and HRV. I think it helps people become more in tune with their bodies.”

As I noted above, Apple shared its own findings that suggest the new Watch Series 12 and Watch Ultra 4, using the new Health Sensing System and the S11 chip, have the most accurate heart rate measurements of any wearable. It compared the new watches to the Garmin Forerunner 970, Google Pixel Watch 4, Huawei Watch 5, Samsung Galaxy Watch 8, Whoop 5, and Oura Ring 5.

I didn’t simply want to take the brand’s word for it, though. I’ve used the Watch Ultra 4 to track more than 200 miles of runs over the past month, and have been looking at how the data recorded compares to a Garmin Fenix 9 Pro Solar and Forerunner 970, as well as heart rate captured with a Coros armband monitor and Garmin HRM600 chest strap.

If I want the most accurate heart rate data, say for a session of fast intervals where my HR rises and falls quickly, I still reach for the chest strap. Nothing I’ve seen from any optical sensor comes close to its accuracy. AFF of athletes get chafing or irritation from a chest strap—and you obviously won’t wear one casually all day.

By taking a closer look at graphs of my heart rate from the various devices during workouts, I’ve seen the Apple Watch Ultra 4 mirrors the strap remarkably closely—with one quirk.

While Apple says that the Watch measures heart rate every five seconds, it’s not truly every five seconds exactly. In my data files, I see gaps—sometimes a minute or longer—where there is no recorded value. I asked Apple’s team why that happens, when other devices display measurements up to every second.“We took a strong stance, really from the early days of the first Watch, that if we're going to claim to be doing a measurement—if we're claiming to measure heart rate—we need to have the data coming from the sensor to back that up,” said Waydo. “Any optical measurement at the wrist—a hardware device interacting with physiology—the signals are not perfect all the time. If we don't have real data to support the measurement that we're going to present, we'll choose to withhold that measurement rather than present something that we aren't really sure is the right answer.”

When I asked what goes into that “confidence check” to determine whether or not to present a reading, he explained the following:

“The algorithms are a large machine learning–driven neural network that takes in the optical signals from the sensor as well as the motion data from the accelerometer. And inside the system, it outputs two things: It outputs, ‘here's what I think the heart rate is,’ and ‘here's how sure I am about that.’ And we train the, ‘here's how sure I am’ component based on data from thousands of people, where we're looking at the ability of the system to accurately estimate the heart rate on their signals.”

When that’s combined, the algorithm might have low confidence even if the reading is correct—so it leaves a blank gap. What that means is the data that is actually reported has a stronger likelihood of being the true measurement, compared to devices that just give you a number regardless.

The times I have found the watch to struggle the most were at the beginning of runs, or at the start and end of speed intervals. In the case of the beginning of runs, I’d see a flat line on my heart rate graph, but then the Watch’s data would snap up to match what my chest strap was reading nearly dead-on.

In the case of intervals, I think what happens is that I mark the beginning and end of my efforts by pushing the Action button on the side of the Watch to record a segment. In doing so, I may be jostling the Watch just enough to create a gap between the sensor and my skin, destroying the algorithm’s confidence in what the sensor is seeing.

In the end, the changes that Apple rolled out with these devices do move the needle to make wrist-based heart rate monitoring more useful and reliable. The accuracy, even if not as spot-on as a chest strap, proves to be good enough that most of my running is done without a chest strap—and I’m confident that I’m pushing at the right effort level.

And the efficiency improvements give you better access to your heart rate all day. The usefulness of that insight right now might not be apparent, but that data will continue to be rolled into features and apps for even more meaningful actions. It’s currently being used within the Heart Rate app, where you can see a graph of your last two minutes as well as your resting HR and even your walking HR average.

Best of all, Apple managed to make these improvements without any hit to the Watches’ battery life.

#1726. Why the talent crisis in advertising is real and what comes next

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Bradley Keefer, Chief Revenue Officer, Keen Decision Systems

The advertising industry has a talent problem it desperately needs to confront: AI is eliminating the work that used to develop future leaders, and most agencies aren’t building a replacement pipeline.

No one wants to discuss entry-level jobs, but those jobs are how people obtain three years of experience and how they move into the middle management positions that keep companies functioning. Companies can keep using AI, but if employees don’t know how business systems function because they were never trained in the trenches, those companies are fragile.

So far, the industry’s response to AI’s impact on work has been counterproductive. Staff-level jobs at ad agencies in the United States are down 10% since 2022 as junior roles are treated like short-term costs to manage rather than long-term investments in capability.

The apprenticeship model, where junior employees learn by watching decisions as they’re made, building judgment over time, is also eroding. That’s a mistake.

Here’s what can be done to reverse these trends and how the industry can move forward to create a sustainable future.

To start, agencies must redefine their hiring needs to ensure that future entry-level hires are not button-pushers executing routine tasks.

A recent whitepaper published by DBC in collaboration with the 4As argues that agencies need people who can think strategically, connect dots across disciplines, understand business context, communicate clearly, demonstrate curiosity and adaptability, and use AI as a tool rather than fear it. That’s a complete departure from how agencies have traditionally hired juniors, and it’s the correct approach.

Structure matters too. Organizations should double down on early-career hiring and invest in continuous development conversations and mentorship. They should hire juniors because they believe people are the ones who drive strategy, not automation.

Some progress is being made in this area. The 4As’ “I’m New Here” and MAIP programs create a strong strategic talent infrastructure for agencies that want to survive the next decade. However, more needs to be done industry-wide to create long-term sustainability for the agency model.

To succeed, agencies need to invest in structured onboarding and mentorship, build development systems intentionally, hire for adaptability and critical thinking, articulate career paths clearly and shift from annual reviews to continuous learning conversations.

Those steps will help future-proof the industry.

If entry-level work is genuinely shifting from task execution to strategic thinking, then the industry must get more specific about what people are trained to think about.

What frameworks do juniors need? What language do they need to speak?

Agencies must teach outcomes thinking — what changed and why — at the entry level, and they must do it intentionally. That includes: thinking in terms of incremental lift, not volume metrics; reading a P&L and understanding how marketing contribution connects to profit and forecast variance; knowing the difference between correlation and causation; building hypothesis tests instead of execution plans; and thinking in terms of probability to goal and risk-adjusted decisions.

Currently, juniors learn the language of channels, campaigns, impressions, CPMs and whether metrics were hit. Outcomes thinking is the opposite of that. It teaches juniors to understand the impacts of a decision and how it connects to a business. That’s judgment, and it’s trainable at entry level.

When agencies teach juniors to think this way, their positions become fundamentally harder to automate. Juniors begin building plans not just executing them, interpreting reports rather than simply running them, and deciding which channels deserve investment based on evidence not only optimizing them.

More importantly, juniors become better partners to the clients they’ll eventually lead. Clients who move the needle on incremental revenue and margin contribution work with teams that think this way.

When agencies finally understand that this is what they should be hiring and training for, the talent problem mostly solves itself. People who think like operators and strategists build careers at agencies; they don’t leave them.

AI is automating execution, which is what it is supposed to do. But it’s also creating a moment where agencies have to choose whether to use the freed-up time to ask juniors to do more execution work, or to teach them how to think.

Making that choice means being specific about what thinking skills matter. For marketing services, it starts with understanding outcomes and decision quality — not only as a strategic overlay, but as the core curriculum.

This is where structured mentorship programs and intentional development systems become powerful.

The industry has started to confront the problem of how to train junior employees in response to AI’s impact on work. Next, agencies must be specific about the solution.

Beyond providing more mentorship or changing hiring methods, that includes teaching juniors about business impact, changing how they think about decisions and connecting those learnings back to their career paths.

Agencies that build deliberate training systems around outcomes thinking will attract better talent, retain employees longer and produce better client work. Agencies that continue hiring for task execution while hoping mentorship solves their problems will continue to lose people.

#3935. The 3-Minute Rule for Having a Hard Talk

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

While we might absolutely love our partner, sometimes knowing that we have to have a hard conversation makes us feel really negative. Maybe you lie awake working out exactly what you will say to your partner, and about a minute into the actual conversation, it has gone sideways. Maybe it was about the kids, or a furtive glance at a stranger. Maybe it was about money. Maybe it was about whose turn it is to empty the dishwasher, again. Experience has shown me that putting the talk off does not help much either.

The heart of these hard discussions is surprisingly direct. From an evolutionary perspective, conflict between partners is not a sign that love itself has failed. Buss (1989) proposed that conflict arises when one partner’s strategy interferes with the other’s goals, and that anger (among other feelings) is an emotional alarm signalling that interference. While the results of Buss’ study are mixed, it suggests a very simple insight, which is that the issues we find hardest to raise are often the ones where our interests and our partner’s do not really overlap.

In other words, the emotions you feel before a hard conversation provide valuable information and should not be ignored. They tell you that something matters to you. Is it your partner’s opinion? The outcome of the chat?

The challenge is delivering the information without setting off your partner’s alarms in return. The first three minutes are really going to matter. Carrère and Gottman (1999) filmed 124 newlywed couples arguing about something. Six years later, 17 of those couples had divorced, and they were couples who had started their arguments with more negativity and less warmth than those who stuck together did. Those critical three minutes at the start were enough to predict how the marriages turned out. What this means, at least to me, is that the opening for the hard discussion sets the tone. A conversation that begins with criticism or contempt leaves little room for anything positive to happen, whereas one that begins with some warmth, interest, or humor gives both people a chance to be positive. There is room for something other than negativity.

When having a hard discussion, most of us know that we should try to be present and open for our partner. Most of us also know that we have to be wary of the classic demand-withdraw pattern, where one partner pushes for change, often with criticism or blame, while the other avoids the topic, becomes defensive, and pulls away. A meta-analysis of 74 studies with more than 14,000 participants linked the pattern to lower relationship satisfaction, less intimacy, and poorer communication (Schrodt et al., 2014). What might be missed, though, is what this pattern is saying, which is that the partner who wants more change on the topic under discussion is more likely to demand, while the partner who wants less change may be more likely to withdraw (Holley et al., 2010).

We also know that emotions can be modified by food, and prepping for a hard conversation is particularly susceptible to hunger. Bushman and colleagues (2014) tracked blood sugar in married couples over three weeks. In one of my all-time favourite study designs, each evening, participants stuck pins into a voodoo doll representing their spouse, depending on how angry they felt. (Does science get any better?) More pins were correlated with lower evening glucose levels. Lange and Kurzban (2014) have questioned whether this is really about self-control, but in any case, the practical lesson seems low risk, which is do not open a difficult topic when either of you is hungry, or worse, “hangry.”

One final consideration that might be worth a thought is to imagine how an objective observer would view your discussion. Over two years, Finkel and colleagues (2013) followed 120 married couples. In year two, half of the couples spent just seven minutes, three times over the year, writing about their most recent disagreement from the perspective of a neutral third party who wants the best for everyone involved. While average marital quality declined for both groups in the first year, the decline stopped for those who did the writing, but not for the other couples.

Now, for some tips. Let’s start with remembering that, above all else, the first three minutes matter when starting a hard conversation.

While I would personally love to think otherwise, difficult conversations will never be easy, and perhaps they should not be, given that they tend to arise where two people’s interests are not aligned. When they do happen, a warm and positive first few minutes, a full stomach, and a little distance from the fight may help the person you love actually hear you.

For many years, a great debate raged: boxers vs. briefs. Men were divided. Allegiances forged. Some preferred the boxer for its roomy comfort; others, the brief for its sleek profile and ability to gather and contain the important parts. Then came the dark times. What had once been a good-faith debate devolved into name-calling, mockery, and shoddy science. Both sides entrenched themselves, blinded by their own sense of righteousness.

Then, a savior arrived: the boxer brief. Offering the best of both worlds, it combined the coverage and comfort of a boxer with the containment and discretion of a brief, without any of the tightie-whitie stigma. At last, there was peace.

Nowadays, though, decades into the boxer-brief strife, men have begun to rediscover the pleasures of the old order. As ever, there’s a strong case for boxers, briefs, and boxer briefs, but in this age of unprecedented menswear latitude, they might not be what you think. Here’s why we think you should keep all three main underwear variants in your drawer, and when to reach for each.

According to data from a 2016 fertility study of American men, 39% wore boxer briefs during the day, 35% wore boxers, and 26% wore briefs. Boxers gained ground at night, however, with 43% of men wearing them to sleep, compared with 24% in boxer briefs and 13% in briefs. Another 20% wore nothing at all. The study’s real purpose, though, was to better understand whether underwear choice affected male fertility, and when researchers looked at semen quality and how long couples took to conceive, they found no real link between underwear type and fertility.

Dr. Michael Eisenberg, a urology professor at Stanford University, co-authored the 2016 study. He says the question of boxers versus briefs comes up when he counsels men about fertility, largely because the testicles need to remain cooler than the rest of the body to function properly. “When we’ve actually studied it, the particular type of underwear tends not to be predictive of semen quality,” he confirms. “It’s unlikely any single layer is going to make that big of a difference.”

In other words, from a health perspective, there’s really no reason to choose one over the other. “Just wear what’s comfortable,” says Eisenberg.

Like Calvin pulling on his lucky rocketship-print briefs before a big day of sledding with Hobbes, it’s less about what is right or wrong and more about what fits the occasion and outfit. That’s why the person with access to all three types of underpants in their top drawer will likely be the most comfortable and outfit-appropriate. “It really depends on the pant,” says Daniel Salmon, a Brooklyn-based men’s personal stylist and design studio instructor at Parsons School of Design. “If you’re wearing a slim or fitted pant, you don’t want a pair of baggy boxer shorts stuffing extra fabric in there,” he says. “It’s definitely going to show up.”

For slimmer trousers, particularly those made from lightweight fabrics like tropical wool, Salmon recommends briefs or boxer briefs. The latter help maintain a clean silhouette, since traditional briefs can show through fitted layers. But with trousers now wider and waistbands hanging lower, could old-school boxers be poised for a comeback? Salmon points to recent looks from Haider Ackermann at Tom Ford, where boxers peeked above the waistbands of relaxed trousers. “It just adds that little bit of a styling element,” he says. “Sometimes it can allude to what’s underneath, if you want to be a little provocative.”

Of course there are practical considerations, too. “If you’re wearing really short shorts, you need to be contained,” he says. “I would say no to a boxer short.” For bigger guys, he suggests avoiding the extra bulk of loose boxers while still paying attention to fit, regardless of style. Too tight and you’re spilling over the waistband. Too loose and you’re dealing with bunching and sagging.

Ultimately though, Salmon echoes Eisenberg’s advice about personal comfort. What fits depends on your body, your clothes, and even your mood. “It varies on how you’re feeling, what you’re wearing,” he says. In other words, why limit yourself to one when you can say yes to all three? What a time to be alive.

#4004. Finding the Brain’s Mood Switch in Bipolar Disorder

A multi-institutional consortium led by researchers at the Keck School of Medicine of USC’s Stevens Neuroimaging and Informatics Institute, Stanford University, and Johns Hopkins University has received a $4.5 million grant from BD² to investigate the biological drivers of bipolar mood switching. Focusing on a specialized population of neurons termed “retrosplenial switch cells,” the team will combine postmortem single-cell histology, polygenic risk scores from a 4,000-patient clinical cohort, and large-scale ENIGMA connectome neuroimaging to decipher how the retrosplenial cortex alternates between depression and mania.

Bipolar disorder affects nearly 3% of the global population, characterized by unpredictable, cyclic oscillations between debilitating depressive lows and elevated, high-energy manic or hypomanic states. Despite its prevalence and severity, the fundamental neurobiological mechanisms that govern mood switching, the physical biological process that shifts the brain from one extreme affective state to another, remain one of the most stubborn mysteries in clinical psychiatry.

As a result, current treatment regimens often rely on broad mood stabilizers and trial-and-error pharmacology, leaving many patients struggling with chronic instability, medication side effects, or misdiagnoses.

To resolve this biological blind spot, a cross-disciplinary team led by Christopher R.K. Ching, PhD, assistant professor of research neurology at the Mark and Mary Stevens Neuroimaging and Informatics Institute (Stevens INI) at the Keck School of Medicine of USC, has launched a multi-scale investigation. Co-led by Laramie Duncan, PhD, of Stanford University, and Fernando Goes, MD, of Johns Hopkins University, the project is backed by a $4.5 million, three-year Discovery Research grant from Breakthrough Discoveries for thriving with Bipolar Disorder (BD²).

Located in the posterior medial cortex, the retrosplenial cortex (RSC) has historically been studied for its structural role in episodic memory retrieval, spatial orientation, and navigation. When compromised by focal lesions or pathological insults, damage to the region frequently produces severe dissociative phenomena—states where individuals feel profoundly detached from their personal identity, emotional reality, or external surroundings.

In a landmark 2025 study published in Nature Neuroscience, Duncan and colleagues revealed that specific neuronal subtypes within the retrosplenial cortex show pronounced transcriptional and genetic vulnerability in bipolar disorder.

The researchers dubbed this distinct population “retrosplenial switch cells,” hypothesizing that these cells serve as critical regulatory nodes in the brain’s mood architecture. Under normal conditions, they maintain affective stability; when genetically or functionally disrupted, their breakdown may trigger the switch into mania or depression.

The new research initiative bridges three complementary biological dimensions:

“We will apply some of the cutting-edge structural and functional brain network modeling approaches we have developed in our large, international ENIGMA Bipolar group studies,” said Melody Kang, a USC Neuroscience PhD student working on the project. Ching directs the ENIGMA Bipolar Disorder Working Group, a worldwide collaborative network pooling neuroimaging and clinical datasets across more than 250 institutions worldwide.

By evaluating the retrosplenial cortex across genomic, cellular, and whole-brain network scales, the team aims to establish an integrated model of bipolar mood instability.

“This is the type of ambitious, multidisciplinary project that would have been difficult to fund through traditional scientific grants,” Ching noted. “This BD2 project gives us a rare opportunity to link the changes we observe in the retrosplenial cortex at the human neuroimaging level to the underlying cellular and genetic factors that may lead to more clinically actionable markers aimed at improving diagnosis and treatment of bipolar disorder.”

While exploratory discovery research that does not yet test direct pharmacological compounds, the findings aim to identify specific molecular pathways within retrosplenial switch cells, laying the groundwork for targeted, next-generation therapeutics engineered to arrest the mood-switching cycle.

But if you’re more sensible and take it easy on descents, then the 6 mm drop shouldn’t be a problem at all.

Back on the roads toward the end of my long run, I felt the same road-shoe ride I get on my neighborhood runs. Though my legs were more tired after battling the dusty, hilly terrain, the flexibility of the upper and the outsole encouraged me to keep my pace until the end of the run.

Durability seems mixed so far: After testing the shoe for a month of road and trail runs, the outsole has progressively felt more flexible under my feet and can take on rocks and tough objects on the trails. The edges of the lugs have retained their sharp definition from when it was new, while the upper and overall cushioning still feel just as comfortable. The midsole bounce, however, is much gentler than when it was new (for better or worse), and the bright Orange Crush splash has since faded.

Quality moderate-stack trail shoes are becoming harder to find. If your favorite model lost its way in the “space-age arms race of trail super shoes,” as our editor Cade Michael aptly put it, and you don’t like feeling every rock beneath your feet through a thin minimalist sole, this one’s for you.

Runners with narrow or medium-narrow feet who prefer a tight fit should appreciate this shoe. The heel and midfoot’s gentle padding hug my narrow feet quite tightly, and the toe box has some room for toe splay, though it’s not wide — it’s right in the middle. This close-fitting shape is useful for tight cornering and platform stability, but if you have wide feet and need breathing space for your toes, the M2C H1, an Altra, or a Topo trail shoe might be a better fit.

So, is this the lower-stacked hybrid shoe that we’ve been waiting for? With its dual personality, the Golden Gate LT certainly handles roads and trails like it’s built for both. You can also totally wear this exclusively as a trail shoe for runs of any length. Though difficult trails with demanding terrain may not be its greatest strength, this shoe provides a comfortable, energetic ride on all other terrain, achieving exactly what it’s designed for.
