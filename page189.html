#2382. What Is “House Blindness”? I Think I Have It

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

House blindness makes clutter, half-finished projects and little repairs fade into the background. Here’s how to spot it in your own home.

Hear no mess. See no mess. Speak no mess.

If only that actually made the mess go away. Sometimes in the life of a DIYer, you get a little too comfortable with living in a half-finished house.

For me, I often see straight through the small mountain of tools and materials stacked in the corner of the room where we’re currently (…have been for a while…) renovating. And the spackle on the wall that needs to be sanded down and painted? Invisible to my eyes.

The missing trim in the kitchen, sticky door to the basement, loose handle on the trash can are all obvious to anyone stepping foot in my home, but not so much to me and my family.

House blindness is simply when you get so used to the mess, clutter or unfinished jobs around your home that you stop noticing them. Think of it like going nose blind, but with imperfections in your home. It can also be referred to as “clutter blindness,” “mess blind,” “clutter creep”, or something similar.

A post shared by Emma Shoemaker | DIY & Home (@shoemakesnew)

Once I heard the term, it immediately made sense. I’m not purposely ignoring the pile of tools in the corner or that missing piece of trim. I’ve just walked past them so many times that they’ve basically become part of the room.

The concept of house blindness isn’t overly complex. However, it might be confronting or hard to realize that it’s something you’re doing too.

For me, the biggest giveaway is when I suddenly notice something that’s been sitting there for weeks or months. If guests are coming over, I take a photo of the room or I simply look at it from a different angle. All of a sudden, that pile I’ve walked past 100 times is impossible to miss. If that sounds familiar, you might be doing it too.

Now, for solutions. Here are some things I’ve discovered that help.

Because our house is usually a revolving door of major renovations, there’s almost always at least one major project going on. To help with construction mess spilling into other rooms, I keep a designated tool and materials box that everything goes into once the work is done for the day.

I’ve also prioritized 95% days, which are days I set aside specifically to finish all those little jobs I’ve been putting off instead of starting something new. Think missing trim, nail holes, outlet covers and all the other annoying final steps that keep a project at 95% instead of actually done.

Another one that might seem silly but does work is to look at your home from angles you don’t normally see from. If you’re tall, try kneeling or sitting on the ground and looking around. Or if you’re short, get a step ladder for a new perspective.

One trick I recently learned but haven’t tried is to take photos around your home and look at them on your phone later. Viewing them through this new lens helps strip away the usual filters your brain uses to block that clutter noise and can help you see more clearly.

Try one, or multiple, of these approaches and see what works best. Thankfully, clutter is usually a pretty easy issue to fix and if it’s something you’ve gone house blind to, it doesn’t take much to start noticing it again.

#3300. Russian Team Moving Up on Everest

The only team currently on Everest has set off across an untouched Khumbu Icefall. There are no ladders or ropes at this time of year, and everything ahead of them is uncertain.

Earlier this week, the Russian team of Andrei Vasiliev, Sergey Kondrashkin, Vitaly Shipilov, and Natalya Belyankina acclimatized toward the Nuptse shoulder, to the right of the Khumbu Icefall. Heavy snow slowed them, eventually forcing them to stop for the night at 5,650m.

Nuptse in the sun, with the Khumbu Glacier at the base and Everest’s West Shoulder on the left. Photo: Russian Everest Expedition 2026

Returning to their quarters in Lobuche, the team focused on rest and pondered their options. “We are considering two alternatives: either going up across the Khumbu Icefall or trying the West Ridge via the Lho La, the mountain pass to the left of the Khumbu Glacier that leads to Everest’s west ridge,” they said. Jost Kobusch has attempted the West Ride/Lho La route several times in winter.

The Russians have now chosen the first option. “We’re heading up, attempting to get through the Khumbu Icefall,” Vasilief told Mountainclub. “We’ll see how things go from there.”

The team plans to spend six days on the mountain and has not confirmed whether they will attempt the summit or complete another partial trip.

#3559. Can Your Road Bike Handle a Cyclocross Race?

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A 33mm tire may fit, but mud clearance, gearing, and frame durability still matter.

A decade ago, it was still common to see Tour pros racing on 23mm tires, with 25mm considered progressive. Views on tire width have shifted. At this year’s Tour de France, most riders were on tires labeled 28mm to 30mm; mounted and inflated, many measured 30mm to 32.5mm. The widest setups measured in the paddock were more than 33mm—above the UCI cyclocross limit.

Road bikes with room for 32mm tires are increasingly common. Some clear rubber as wide as 40mm. So you might be wondering: Can I install a pair of 33mm cyclocross tires on my road bike and race the local ’cross?

As these photos show, I mounted 33mm cyclocross tires on Trek’s Madone 8 road bike. And there was even a bit of clearance, even though Trek states the Madone’s maximum tire clearance is 32mm.

But just because you can doesn’t mean you should. There’s more to racing cyclocross on a road bike than stuffing knobbies into any frame that clears them.

CX race bikes are designed to take a 33mm knobby tire with generous mud clearance. A road bike’s stated maximum tire width leaves room for some debris around a slick tread, but not for peanut-butter mud.

Then there is all the other stuff you will find on a cyclocross course: sand, ruts, off-camber turns, barriers, and sharp corners.

You’ll dismount, shoulder the bike, pound into bumps and holes, and crash.

Your road frame wasn’t built with all of cyclocross’s many charms in mind. The engineers I’ve asked about this point to the seatstays as particularly vulnerable to damage in cyclocross.

There is precedent, though. Toon Aerts won the 2025 European Cyclocross Championships on an Orbea Orca OMX road frame fitted with a fork from the Orbea Terra Race gravel bike. That’s a pro-level workaround for a sponsored athlete, not a green light for every road bike.

Your road bike’s gearing is probably too high for a ‘cross course, which is likely to feature steep, slippery climbs and slow technical sections. Road gearing can mean more dismounts; an easier gear can help keep you riding instead of getting off to push.

If you want to race ‘cross without buying a cyclocross bike, a gravel bike is a better place to start. It usually has lower gearing and is built for bigger impacts and rougher use. A gravel bike definitely has room for a 33mm tire. If it’s a modern model built for 50mm-plus tires, it’ll have heaps of mud clearance—so much that a 33mm CX tire might look a little lost in there.

And if you use your gravel bike, you can also use wider gravel tires. The UCI caps CX tire width at 33mm, but that only applies to races they sanction.

Your local CX series is probably not UCI-sanctioned.

Local rules vary, so check before you line up. If wider tires are allowed, a wider gravel tire may offer more comfort and grip—and could even be faster, depending on the course.

There’s a handling trade-off. CX bikes are made to whip through tight, slower-speed corners; gravel bikes usually favor stability. So a gravel bike might feel too long, too stable, and the steering too slow, on a tight ‘cross course. But you might actually prefer a more placid-handling gravel bike in the anaerobic blur of a cyclocross race.

So, can you ‘cross your road bike? Technically, yes—if a 33mm tire clears without rubbing, the course is dry, and your gearing suits the terrain. But just because you can doesn’t mean you should. If you already have a gravel bike, start there. Check the rules, fit the tires, and go have a race.

#3309. Trekking and Packrafting 700Km Across Northern Siberia

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Two Russian explorers have completed a journey of about 700km across the remote Putorana Plateau in northern Siberia. During their expedition, they encountered drought conditions, wildfires, and a brazen bear that raided their food supplies.

Danil Barashkov and Sergey Malykh set off in early July. They flew to the small town of Svetlogorsk in northern Siberia, before taking a motorboat up the Kureika River system toward Lake Dupkun. On July 7, they started on foot across the Putorana Plateau from a nearby scientific research base.

A mountainous region between 67°N and 70°N in the Arctic Circle, the plateau is the size of Great Britain. Featuring some 25,000 lakes, it is the second-largest store of fresh water in Russia after Lake Baikal. The men set out with 40kg packs and entered the plateau’s remote eastern section, expecting to use packrafts for most of their journey. Instead, they found drought conditions, with water levels too low to paddle, and even drinking water in short supply.

“There was almost no rain all of June. So we ended up like this: ‘I’m standing on the pavement, wearing skis.’ We arrived with our packrafts, and there was no water,” Malykh told Russian news site NGS24.RU. Though water was scarce, the mosquitoes remained. “I’ve never seen anything like this before: you lift the lid of the pot, and there’s a layer of mosquitoes floating around. Yeah, we definitely had no problem with protein.”

The pair found very dry conditions early in their trip. Photo: Danil Barashkov

Their original plan was to cross the plateau and continue to the settlement of Khatanga in northern Siberia. However, the exceptionally dry conditions forced a change of plan. Instead, they crossed toward the upper basin of the Kotuy River, 300km of hiking that sometimes required shuttles to move their gear. They covered only 15km per day and realized that their trip would run longer than their initial six-week plan. But this slog paid off; from the Kotuy, they could finally use their packrafts.

Barashkov and Malykh had a few scary bear encounters. In one instance, they almost walked into a sleeping bear, and in another, a curious bear only retreated from their campsite after coming as close as 15 meters — and after ignoring their shouts and a shot from a flare gun.

A bear that didn’t come too close. Photo: Danil Barashkov

However, the worst instance came after they cached some food. A bear had found the supplies and was still eating them when they returned. With hundreds of kilometers still to travel, Barashkov and Malykh couldn’t afford to lose everything. They collected their bear deterrents and managed to briefly scare the bear away before hurriedly packing what remained of their food into their backpacks and escaping downwind.

Over the next three weeks, they used packrafts to travel 400km and their luck improved. They found plentiful berries and caught fish in the river to supplement their remaining food.

Their arrival at Chirinda in the second half of August marked the end of their expedition after around 60 days. Yet forest fires forced the pair to remain in the village for another 10 days. Wildfires swept through the surrounding tundra forests and smothered the area in smoke, grounding flights.

#1671. Navigating your job search with AI: 5 key insights

Program Manager of Corporate Marketing | Oct 9, 2026

Artificial intelligence (AI) is everywhere at work, and the honeymoon is over. Glassdoor Economic Research found 81% of Glassdoor reviews mentioning AI listed it only as a pro in 2019, versus 43% in 2026.¹ Use AI for job search, but write for humans.

"The key is to focus less on the AI algorithm and more on the human one - i.e., the one running between every recruiter's two ears!" said Jeremy Schifeling, founder of The Job Insiders.

Jeremy Schifeling, expert AI career coach of The Job Insiders, has seen firsthand how AI is reshaping the job search landscape. The author of the AI careers book Career Coach GPT brought his expertise to a Glassdoor Community Hot Seat session, sharing hard-won wisdom from his own career pivots — from teacher to recruiting expert — and the thousands of job seekers he's helped navigate this new terrain.

From AI's biggest pitfalls to the soft skills that will define the next decade of work, here are five of Schifeling's most useful insights to help workers navigate their job search.

Schifeling sees this error everywhere from applicants in today's job market.

"By far the #1 mistake is letting AI turn your unique application into a generic one. When everyone uses AI to write their cover letters and every cover letter begins 'I am writing to express my interest in X role at Y company,' everything that's unique and different about you has just been lost," he said. "That makes it all too easy for the recruiter to reject your application immediately in favor of the handful truly differentiated ones..."

A platform technical lead agreed on Glassdoor Community: "Many recruiters can tell if a cover letter feels generic or doesn't reflect your real experience." Draft with AI, then rewrite using our guides to writing a cover letter and being yourself in a job interview.

When most job seekers are taking the easy route, Schifeling advocates for effort over automation. "Standing out is everything! That means NOT doing what everyone else is doing but instead doing what NO ONE else is willing to do," said Schifeling.

One member on Glassdoor Community has hired hundreds. Their take: "I always gave better credibility that candidate was solid if I knew and respected the referrer."

What does Schifeling think about the future of AI? It flips conventional career wisdom on its head.

"My crystal ball prediction is that, because AI is so good at the hard skills we've all focused on for the last few decades (coding, data, etc.), its emergence will actually make soft skills (empathy, storytelling, relationship-building) the scarce and desirable skillset of the next decade," Schifeling said.

#4330. What is somatic therapy? Plus 5 ways it can support healing

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

The busyness of life can leave you feeling tense from head to toe — jaw clenched, chest tight, stomach in knots, shoulders up to your ears. For many of us, stress creeps up slowly, settling into your body even after your mind has moved on, until tension starts to feel normal.

Somatic therapy works with the mind-body connection directly. It's a body-based approach, most often used to work through chronic stress, anxiety, and trauma. The idea is that tuning into physical sensations can help you process what your mind and body have been holding onto.

Ahead, we’ll break down what somatic therapy involves, what the evidence supports, and a few gentle practices to start noticing what your body’s been carrying.

In somatic therapy, a therapist helps you become aware of physical sensations like tension, breathing, posture, and restlessness. This is often called a "bottom-up" approach. It starts with the body and works toward the mind. Traditional talk therapy tends to move “top-down,” from thoughts toward feelings.

Somatic Experiencing, developed by Peter Levine, is the most studied approach. Others include sensorimotor psychotherapy, which blends talk therapy with movement, and body-focused techniques that are integrated into broader trauma treatment.

Note: Some of the theories behind somatic therapy remain scientifically debated. Polyvagal theory, often used to explain why the nervous system gets "stuck" in a stress state, has been challenged by researchers who argue the evidence doesn’t support its core physiological claims.

In a session, a therapist might open the conversation by asking what you notice physically as feelings and memories come up. For example, if a hard memory makes your chest tighten, they may guide you to gently stay with that sensation, rather than pushing through or ignoring it.

They may draw on a range of tools: breathing exercises, grounding techniques, gentle movement, and sensation tracking. The aim is to help your body and mind feel safe enough to process difficult experiences at a manageable pace.

Related read: What is holistic therapy (and how can it benefit you)?

Somatic therapy is most established as a support for trauma, though it may also be worth exploring if:

you feel stuck in traditional talk therapy

stress tends to show up physically for you

you prefer an approach that involves movement and physical awareness

If you're working through trauma, it’s especially worth finding a licensed professional trained in a somatic approach. This process can bring up intense feelings, and they can help you move at a comfortable pace.

Somatic therapy isn't a fit for everyone, and it’s not a substitute for other evidence-based treatments when those are recommended.

The research here is early but promising. Most studies are small, and somatic therapy hasn't been tested as thoroughly as treatments such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT). Here's what the current evidence points to.

1. It may reduce PTSD symptoms: The first randomized controlled trial of Somatic Experiencing found meaningful drops in post-traumatic symptoms and depression in people with PTSD. While the results were positive, researchers noted more study is needed.

2. It may help when talk therapy isn’t working: Some people find it hard to put their trauma into words or haven’t felt any relief from talking about it. A body-based approach offers another path to processing trauma.

3. It may support people with both pain and trauma: One trial looked at a brief Somatic Experiencing intervention for chronic low back pain and PTSD. It found reduced trauma symptoms and less fear of movement, though the overall effect was modest.

4. It can ease the physical side of stress: Stress and anxiety often show up as muscle tension, shallow breathing, or a racing heart. Somatic work targets these physical patterns.

5. It may build body awareness over time: Learning to pick up on early signs of stress, like restlessness or a churning stomach, can help you catch stress before it builds up.

The research above looked at somatic therapy involving trained professionals. These practices below can be done on your own as a complement to everyday self-care — not a trauma treatment. If any of this brings up feelings of distress, it may be useful to reach out to a professional.

A body scan is a mindfulness practice and a foundational way to build physical awareness. You move your attention slowly through your body, noticing where you're holding tension without trying to fix it.

Over time, this makes it easier to recognize tension early, so a tight jaw or clenched shoulders doesn’t turn into a headache or a bad night's sleep.

Try: Sitting or lying down and shifting your focus from your toes to the top of your head, pausing at each area for a breath or two. If you notice tightness, acknowledge it and keep going.

Tune into your senses to pull your attention back to the present. When your mind races ahead to what might go wrong, or loops back to replay something you said or did, observing what's around you gives it something tangible to hold onto. This works well before a stressful conversation or any time your thoughts start swirling.

Try: Naming five things you can see, four you can touch, three you can hear, two you can smell, and one you can taste. Take your time to really notice each one. If a sense isn’t accessible to you, swap in something else to focus on, like your breath.

Slowing your breath tells your nervous system it’s safe to relax. It’s one of the quickest ways to ease a stressful moment, and you can do it anywhere.

Try: Breathing in for a count of four, then out for six. Start with a few rounds, and repeat until you feel settled.

When you’re restless, movement can help you release that energy — in whatever way is comfortable and available to you.

Try: Rolling your shoulders, shaking out your hands, stretching your neck, or gently tensing and releasing different muscle groups for a minute.

When you name a physical sensation, it turns a vague sense of unease into something concrete: tightness in your chest, buzzing in your hands, a knot in your stomach. That specificity alone can make it feel less overwhelming.

Somatic therapy works by helping you become aware of and gently process physical sensations linked to trauma.

Instead of only talking through what happened, a trained therapist guides you to track what's happening in your body, pausing whenever a feeling becomes too intense. Research on this approach for PTSD is still early, but the results so far are encouraging.

A body scan is a common example of a somatic practice. You slowly move your attention through your body, checking in on where you’re holding tightness.

Other examples include grounding yourself using your five senses, slowing your breath, and gently moving by either stretching or shaking out your limbs.

When you're stressed, your nervous system reacts as if you're facing a threat, releasing hormones like cortisol and adrenaline and priming your body for action.

Normally, your body relaxes once the moment passes. But ongoing stress can keep those patterns switched on, which is why tension, headaches, or sleep issues can stick around.

Somatic practices, including body scans, grounding, and slow breathing, are safe to try on your own — especially if you don't have a significant trauma history.

If you do have a trauma history, it may be worth exploring deeper body-based work with a therapist instead, since it can bring up strong feelings you may want support to work through.

Somatic therapy provides another way to work through stress or difficult experiences by connecting how you feel physically to how you feel emotionally.

This can be especially useful if talking through things hasn't brought relief, or if certain emotions are hard to put into words. Over time, tuning into your body can also help you catch stress before it accumulates.

EMDR (eye movement desensitization and reprocessing) and somatic therapy are related, but different. EMDR uses guided eye movements or other rhythmic stimulation while you recall a difficult memory, so the memory feels less distressing to recall. Somatic therapy focuses on observing and working with physical sensations as they come up.

EMDR has a larger research base and appears in more clinical guidelines, while the evidence for somatic therapy is still developing. Some therapists draw on both.

Some of its underlying theories, such as the polyvagal theory or the idea that trauma is stored in the body, are also scientifically contested. Critics argue that body-based approaches are sometimes marketed as more proven than the evidence supports.

The timeline for somatic therapy depends on what you’re working through. Research trials have generally used treatment courses of several months.

If you're working on everyday stress, you may notice a shift in how you handle pressure fairly quickly, while deeper trauma work usually takes longer. A good therapist will check in on your progress along the way.

Warm, wet air from the ocean can result in massive precipitation inland, and for Aconcagua — South America’s highest peak — that means lots of snow.

A graphic illustrating increasing temperatures in the Pacific Ocean.

Argentina’s meteorological service forecasts a colder, more humid spring than usual, both in coastal areas and in Patagonia, the location of Aconcagua. The effects of the superniño may unfold over several months. It will surely change conditions in mountain areas by the time the Aconcagua season starts in November for trekking groups, and on December 1 for climbing expeditions. Local guiding companies fear that snow-covered trails and flooded rivers may isolate lower camps. Further up the mountain, climbers might have to progress on icy ramps instead of scree slopes, and deal with significant avalanche risk.

Given the circumstances, park authorities will wait before announcing when the season will begin. Local media suggest either December 5 or December 15 as tentative opening dates.

#734. 12 Dishes and Drinks Everyone Should Try in Mexico City

I’ve spent over a decade writing about Mexico’s food scene, along with popping up on TV shows to talk about it occasionally. My favorite way to navigate it all is still right here on the ground with my food tours. Sidestepping the obvious spots and encouraging travelers to go deeper into the food culture here constantly reminds me of the fascinating nature of how we chilangos eat.

To me, that means having the flexibility to move between completely different culinary worlds. We can spend lunchtime standing on a crowded sidewalk, leaning over a taco drenched in salsa and lime juice, then dress up that same evening for a multicourse dinner at a dimly lit restaurant. We’re fiercely loyal to the neighborhood places that have fed us for years and know our names, but we remain insatiably curious about the next opening or dish worth crossing town for.

That same flexibility shapes the city’s food. CDMX knows exactly how to pull tradition into modernity without losing its spirit. These 12 spots offer a taste of the city’s versatility: dishes and bites deeply rooted in history but shaped by the wonderfully chaotic, ever-evolving way we eat today.

I was fully awake by the time a plate of refried beans with scrambled eggs and a stack of warm tortillas made it to the table. At Panadería Gala, a speakeasy-style bakery, the extra-bouncy conchas get an extra layer of indulgence when topped with a white or dark chocolate ganache quenelle.

The Margarita may be known worldwide as the quintessential Mexican cocktail. Legend has it that the iconic drink was first conceived in Ensenada, Baja California, and its ties to coast and borderlands are still strong. But chilangos tend to save it for specific occasions and places such as a beach vacation, a favorite cantina, or a particular restaurant where we know they’ll make a killer one. At Despacho Margarita, the classic shaken version gets a new life, alongside spicy and frozen variations. In the slushy machine there are usually two flavors: a classic tequila and lime version and a seasonal mezcal-based one that changes monthly, with unique flavors such as nopal and shiso or raspberry and cayenne pepper.

Cantinas have long been places to gather over good food and several rounds of drinks. In these laid-back establishments, both are given equal importance. At Covadonga, a cantina without background music, generations mix at the tables, from the young and cool to sixty-somethings who come to play dominoes (available on request).

Banderas have been a popular order here from the beginning: three shot glasses lined up, one with tequila, another with lime juice, and a third with sangrita, a red-hued blend of tomato and fruit juices. Together they mimic the three colors of the Mexican flag (hence the name). They’re not meant to be knocked back but rather sipped slowly, alternating between glasses.

There’s a certain type of stand you’ll see repeatedly throughout the city centered around a large, round comal, usually fired with charcoal, with a bucket of corn masa and, more often than not, two women working side by side. Gorditas, quesadillas, and sopes are all on the menu, but the tlacoyo may be the most emblematic of central Mexico and Mexico City.

Football-shaped and stuffed with your choice of fava bean purée, requesón (a soft cheese similar to ricotta), or refried beans, tlacoyos are typically topped with cactus salad for a tangy contrast, grated queso fresco, and red or green salsa, depending on your preference. They're the kind of morning or afternoon snack you might grab during errands or before wandering into the adjacent market.

Calle Versalles 113, Casa Versalles, Juárez+52 55 8568 7397

You can’t talk about Mexico City without talking about its obsession with seafood. Mexico City runs on fondas and street food. These casual eateries serve homestyle food that’s quick, affordable, and feels like a warm hug. Next comes the main course, which at some places, like Nuevo Tehuacán, is what dictates the price of the meal; meatballs in chipotle, pork with tomatillo and purslane, and chicken in peanut sauce are classics. Look around, and you’ll see schoolchildren sitting elbow to elbow with office workers all savoring the comfort of one of the city’s most democratic meals.

Mostly homemade, flan is a staple of casual Mexican cooking, but it’s one of those desserts that look deceptively simple. Siembra may be a temple dedicated to heirloom corn masa, tacos, and antojitos, but its flan has all the right qualifications to receive just as much attention.

Ice cream parlors used to be an almost daily afternoon ritual for families and couples alike. And thanks to mamey’s lush, natural creaminess, it’s easy to forget there’s no dairy in the cup.

Banderas have been a popular order here from the beginning: three shot glasses lined up, one with tequila, another with lime juice, and a third with sangrita, a red-hued blend of tomato and fruit juices. Together they mimic the three colors of the Mexican flag (hence the name). They’re not meant to be knocked back but rather sipped slowly, alternating between glasses.

There’s a certain type of stand you’ll see repeatedly throughout the city centered around a large, round comal, usually fired with charcoal, with a bucket of corn masa and, more often than not, two women working side by side. Gorditas, quesadillas, and sopes are all on the menu, but the tlacoyo may be the most emblematic of central Mexico and Mexico City.

Football-shaped and stuffed with your choice of fava bean purée, requesón (a soft cheese similar to ricotta), or refried beans, tlacoyos are typically topped with cactus salad for a tangy contrast, grated queso fresco, and red or green salsa, depending on your preference. They're the kind of morning or afternoon snack you might grab during errands or before wandering into the adjacent market.

At El Vilsito, a garage by day that moonlights as one of the city’s best taquerías, they take the pastor one step further, turning it into the most Chilango version possible by making it into a torta. You can’t talk about Mexico City without talking about its obsession with seafood. It may seem strange for a city far from the coast, but seafood from both of Mexico’s coastlines makes its way here through the second-largest seafood market in the world (surpassed only by Tokyo’s Toyosu) before being distributed across the rest of the country.

At Taller de Ostiones, clams, barnacles, and tuna arrive three times a week by air from Baja California Sur, alongside sustainably farmed oysters. Those bivalves pair perfectly with a house-made sauce that’s rich, briny, deeply umami, and slightly spicy.

Mexico City runs on fondas and street food. These casual eateries serve homestyle food that’s quick, affordable, and feels like a warm hug. Stepping inside a fonda means being a part of a daily ritual known as comida corrida, where a meal typically unfolds in four acts: a steaming bowl of soup or broth to start, followed by your choice of rice or pasta. Next comes the main course, which at some places, like Nuevo Tehuacán, is what dictates the price of the meal; meatballs in chipotle, pork with tomatillo and purslane, and chicken in peanut sauce are classics. Everything is washed down with a pitcher of freshly made agua fresca and finished with a sweet treat like a cup of gelatin. Look around, and you’ll see schoolchildren sitting elbow to elbow with office workers all savoring the comfort of one of the city’s most democratic meals.

Mostly homemade, flan is a staple of casual Mexican cooking, but it’s one of those desserts that look deceptively simple. Getting the texture just right (silky, firm, not overly sweet) is way harder than it seems, which may be why the best versions are often found in homes. Siembra may be a temple dedicated to heirloom corn masa, tacos, and antojitos, but its flan has all the right qualifications to receive just as much attention.

Ice cream parlors used to be an almost daily afternoon ritual for families and couples alike. Nevería Roxy was one of the early ones, and its 1950s-diner palate of teal and red has barely changed, even in the newer locations. And thanks to mamey’s lush, natural creaminess, it’s easy to forget there’s no dairy in the cup.

#3332. Six Long Walks Across Australia in 2026

A number of long-distance solo walks have been taking place across Australia this year. Some started in 2026, others ended this year. The walkers have all taken different routes. Some are pulling trailers, others wheelbarrows, and one man is doing it dressed in a leopard-print onesie.

Musician Britt Franzmann, known as Birdgirl, spent much of 2026 walking 3,125km from Perth to Melbourne. She started in May, camped along the route, and carried her equipment in a cart. Speaking to SuzieScribbles, she said she has been obsessed with the idea of crossing the country on foot since reading Robyn Davidson’s book, Tracks. She wanted to see what it was like to be in the remote areas of Australia and do something far outside her comfort zone. From the start, she has been very clear about the rules for her expedition: no support vehicles and no shortcuts.

The logistics became one of the defining features of the walk, mainly because Franzmann was pushing her belongings rather than carrying a backpack. Even though this meant she could take more through remote backcountry areas, including (most importantly) her guitar, it also meant that terrain, heat, and the condition of the cart became constant considerations. More than once, the cart fell apart and needed repairs so that she could continue.

The isolation was one of the hardest parts. For huge sections, she had almost no human contact. Franzmann describes herself as an introvert, but she relishes meeting people on the road and having a chat. A huge boost came in mid-June when her dad joined her for a few days. At the same time, it made the loneliness feel more severe when he left.

After walking for just over four months, Franzmann made it to Melbourne and finished by performing a homecoming concert at the Northcote Social Club.

He started hiking from Steep Point in Western Australia to Cape Byron in New South Wales in July 2025 and reached his endpoint in June 2026. Of the 331 days, 223 were spent walking. His route went via Lambert’s Centre, Australia’s geographical midpoint, and he covered around 35km a day, walking up to eight or nine hours daily. With temperatures reaching 50°C at times, he followed the weather rather than a particular schedule.

Unlike several other journeys in this roundup, Gresser’s expedition was genuinely solo and self-supported. He had no vehicle support, and his route took him through some of the remotest parts of the country, with limited water sources. His solution to the huge distances between settlements was to pull a 1.9m wheeled cart that carried up to 200kg of food, water, clothing, and equipment. Before setting out, he dehydrated hundreds of meals and posted them to resupply points along the route.

He had to modify the route and take a more southerly path when his cart could not make it over the sand dunes of the Simpson Desert. Yet he could not avoid sand entirely. In the Strzelecki Desert, it took him 18 days to cover 463 km, including 120km over dunes. Many were too steep to pull the cart over, forcing him to repeatedly unpack it and shuttle his load in stages.

In civilization, he often stayed with locals, making the most of any hospitality offered. One reason his walk took years to complete was that he stopped periodically to rest and work.

The wheelbarrow may seem like an unusual choice, but it was not a gimmick. He was just used to using one. “I felt a sense of destiny with the wheelbarrow. I worked in construction for six years, and the wheelbarrow was a familiar tool,” he commented.

He finally reached the Sydney Opera House on March 14, 2026, but decided to extend the journey. Now he is cycling along Australia’s east coast, pulling the wheelbarrow behind him, and still in his cat-man outfit.

Simon Harvey, also known as Captain Australia, completed his Big Lap of Australia in April of this year. He began from Brisbane in July 2023 and eventually returned to King George Square in his home city after walking over 15,000km around mainland Australia.

He originally estimated that he would walk 30–35km per day, at least six days a week. That meant around 18 months of walking. In reality, the journey stretched across almost three years due to the number of rest periods he needed and the practicalities of life on the road.

Harvey was never going to do this as an unsupported walk, and relied heavily on the network of people he met along the way. Nor was he always solo. On many occasions, he was joined by his wife, friends, and locals he met along the way.  After finishing the walk, he told The Kids’ Cancer Project that he had been given “fresh pairs of boots, hot meals, and countless conversations by people encountered on the road.”

One Step Max, who has no other public name, is taking a very different approach from everyone else walking across Australia. His journey has no fixed route, no declared finish line, and by his own account, deliberately no plan.

“When I first started this journey, the first rule I made was to make no plans,” he told the Junee Independent. “That way, I can never be disappointed because I never know what’s going to turn up. So, if I need to spend time with somebody, I’ve got time up my sleeve.”

Steve Roberts began his 4,200km walk across Australia on September 14. Unlike other expeditions, this is not an unsupported hike. Apart from many pre-positioned caches of food and water, his family is accompanying him in a support van.

At the end of November, he will take a 10-day break with his family, then resume from where he left off. Regardless of the style of his journey, he has been very clear about one thing — he will walk every one of the 4,200km on foot.

2. Incorporate all locally made or vintage furniture and materials. When local wasn’t available, the goal was handmade or made using artisanal methods. We largely achieved this, with the exception of our upholstery, which was made in California and North Carolina. The plaster came from Montana, but there aren’t any New England plaster companies to choose from. The antiques and vintage furniture were all sourced in New England (shoutout to Brimfield) or NYC, with the exception of the dining chairs which I imported from someone in Denmark. There’s no such thing as perfection, but I think this project is a good example of my ethos of trying to avoid major big box stores and work with those I can develop a relationship with.

3. Update the bathrooms and tired second floor without compromising the historic beauty of a coastal saltwater farmhouse.

HW: So many! I am a Remodelista/Pinterest/magazine/vintage book addict, so images abound in my brain. Rose Uniacke’s plaster and warm wood kitchen with the Axel Einer Hjorth Uno chairs was a touchpoint for the finishes of the upstairs. Ulla Johnson’s Brooklyn brownstone by Elizabeth Roberts. Every project that Reath Design and Nickey Kehoe touch felt important for textures and fabrics downstairs. And my grandma’s uncanny ability to create a home by mixing antiques and incredible art and new furniture.

HW: Downstairs, we painted throughout, refinished the maple floors, re-did the laundry room (with Tess Tile checkered yellow and white floors, Bench Dogs blue millwork with walnut countertops, Boråstapeter wallpaper, and Urban Electric Co lighting), and swapped out a bunch of light fixtures.

Upstairs was the bigger chunk of the project. We removed all of the lead paint-laden pine floors (which we saved to potentially strip and use in another project) and restored the plaster walls and ceilings, which were sagging and not doing great. We created a new massive wall of millwork in our bedroom where there was a tiny nonfunctional closet. We redid both upstairs bathrooms. There also were only pull-chain lights in the entire upstairs, and not a single plug. So the electrical work was a HUGE part of the renovation to get everything operational for a more modern life. Ditto the radiators: The heat was via very unsightly radiators from the 1980s ,and we replaced them with Runtal units, which I love.

HW: I wish I could take credit for the blue floors but they’re original! It’s actually linoleum.

HW: Living with plaster walls is the greatest gift one can give themselves! We only did plaster upstairs because downstairs was a mix of drywall and original plaster walls, but everything had been painted, so plastering on top of that would have largely defeated the purpose of using plaster (avoiding paint fumes, allowing the wall to remain breathable, controlling moisture in the air—none of that can happen if there’s a layer of paint behind it).

But happily, upstairs was the original plaster and lath, so we could use it and get to experience how lovely it is to have a space with no drywall. It smells like warm wood and minerals upstairs, which is the most delightful smell. We had a lovely friend of Michael’s named Errol Towers apply the plaster, and it was amazing to watch him work. He was able to completely restore the original sagging laths and problem cracking areas in the ceiling, and he did it perfectly. I did take a virtual plaster application class with Jersey Ice Cream Co. to see if I might be able to give it a shot but I was too afraid in the end. We used untinted plaster from New Age Artisans and painted the trim and doors in Sherwin Williams’ Snowbound.

HW: Yes! I did so much research on this. I wanted them to be completely matte but have a super soft feel underfoot. We went to a bunch of different mills in the midcoast to look at samples of local wood floorboard and settled on red birch from AE Sampson. We then had our flooring installer test a ton of different stains and finishes on top and landed on Bona Traffic HD Raw. The installer, who is definitely accustomed to shinier and waxier finishes, was like “this is wrong,” but it was EXACTLY what we wanted. It has the same color as the unfinished wood, which was what I wanted because it has a pink tone that reflects onto the plaster and makes everything feel warm and cozy.

HW: I was pregnant during the renovation, which was insane—we moved in the week after he was born (would not recommend!). Bert’s bathroom was definitely designed to be playful for a kid, but I have things I’d do again to protect the house and the kid from each other. Some of the fabrics aren’t performance, and I’d also have designed the stair railing to be more kid-safe. It’s not to code at all, but it was historic and so beautiful that we couldn’t bring ourselves to remove it. It gives us a heart attack every time he walks down the hallway, though.

HW: Biggest difference is probably the Runtal radiators. I love the quality of heat that radiators give off. It’s just so pleasing and cozy, and the faint radiator sound feels nostalgic but these are very efficient and also made in the US.

HW: To be honest now that I’ve lived with plaster, I don’t think I’ll ever paint anything ever again. Paint peels over time and smells bad after you do it (even when you use nontoxic paint), and repainting is such a slog with all of the sanding that’s required. Plaster requires almost no prep and it has zero smell. The texture on the walls makes everything glow. It feels simultaneously grounded yet ethereal. It’s a perfect material. Also, surprisingly, plastering was less expensive than painting!

HW: The kitchen! It’s from the people who did the 2001 renovation and they really botched it, which is why it’s not pictured. The floors are blue linoleum, which we’ve actually become fond of and are quite functional, but the cabinets have a peeling plywood veneer on them, the organizational layout is terrible, the sink is tiny, and the oven barely works (to cook at 350 we have to heat it to 450)! So that, plus a major overhaul of the lighting in there, is the game plan.
