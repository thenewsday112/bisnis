And it might be one of the easiest cuts for curly hair.

Earlier this year, my colleague wrote that Timothée Chalamet’s haircut—a grade 3 crop at the time—was the reason his Oscar campaign failed. Well, after a year of experimentation following his foray into buzzcut territory as Paul Atreides in Dune, the actor has returned to a medium-length cut that we’re likening to his Call Me by Your Name ’do.

It was at the Victoria’s Secret fashion show, where his girlfriend Kylie Jenner walked in the Fantasy bra, that he debuted the longer hair. The bountiful curls are well-nourished (his girlfriend, I repeat, is Kylie Jenner) and really hark back to his hair-god heyday. But naturally, there’s been some growth: it’s a more grown-up take on his former cut.

“Chalamet’s latest look feels like one of his more refined and mature styles to date. The center part and longer, loose curls give the hair a relaxed, natural feel while still looking considered,” explains Maxwell Oakley, founder of the London-based barbershop brand Supply 91. “It’s a great example of how growing your hair out can completely change the feel of a look without needing to do anything too drastic.”

So, curly-haired friends, how do you get it? “The key to achieving this hairstyle is getting the layering right. You want to keep the external length relatively long while introducing mid-length layers throughout the hair. These shouldn’t be too long or too short; you’re looking for that middle ground where the layers create movement and texture without taking away from the overall length.”

Chalamet’s fringe is cut slightly shorter than the rest of the hair, which helps give it a little more lift and life. Oakley adds: “It also allows the hair to fall naturally away from the face when center-parted, keeping it out of the eyes without losing that relaxed, slightly undone feel.”

But what’s so good about this hair is its functionality and sense of ease. It requires little maintenance away from the barber’s chair. “This style works particularly well on Timothée because he has a natural wave in his hair. For anyone with curly hair, growing it out can be a great way to embrace your natural texture. As the hair gets longer, the added weight can help loosen tighter curls into a softer wave, creating a more relaxed shape that’s often easier to manage. Rather than fighting against your natural texture, the idea is to work with it and let the hair fall more naturally.”

And if you don’t have Chalamet’s curls? Well, Oakley thinks you can still get the look. “If your hair is naturally straighter, the first step is to grow it out to a similar length. From there, a loose perm can help introduce that natural-looking wave and movement. The aim isn’t to create tight curls, but to give the hair enough texture to fall into place naturally.”

Chalamet knows this type of cut is reigning supreme in 2026. His is just a better version. “This is also a great look for the year because we’re seeing a continued move towards longer, looser hairstyles,” Oakley says. “We’ve seen the mod cut evolve from the mullet, with the length gradually increasing into what’s become known as the flow cut. Timothée’s look feels like a natural progression of that movement: a little more grown out and refined, with a softer, wavier finish. It has a distinguished quality without feeling overly styled, making it a great option for anyone wanting to grow their hair out while keeping it contemporary.”

#3403. 2 Mental Tricks to Help You Finish Any Race—No Matter How Badly You Want to DNF

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Even the best runners think about stopping. They don’t—and you don’t have to, either. Here’s how.

I’m going to say something obvious: Racing can be very hard and uncomfortable. An obvious result of this is people are often tempted to stop running.

So if you’ve ever been in the midst of a competition—on a hot day, on a tough hill, at the course’s halfway point—and thought of dropping out, it’s not just you. As solitary as those moments can feel, they are happening to athletes all around you, too.

Elite runners aren’t immune, either. Conner Mantz said that he entertains the thought in every race, and he’s an American record holder! Author and coach Steve Magness shared in a Twitter thread that the feeling is very common among pro runners. Morgan Beadlescomb confirmed he has—and has overcome—these thoughts all of the time.

So these two things can be true—many runners consider stopping and many of them complete races every year. It’s possible to successfully navigate these thoughts.

I’ll give you an example of how I navigated a moment like this that turned into one of the best races of my life.

It was pretty early in the 2022 New Bedford Half Marathon, and I had dug myself into a hole. The race started at 11 a.m. on one of the first warm spring days of the year. I went out way too fast, with a 5:06 first mile, followed by a series of tough uphill climbs. By the third mile, as I was climbing a big hill in the sun, my brain was deep into doubtful thoughts. There’s no way I can keep doing this. I messed up. Maybe today isn’t my day.

Instead of stopping, I started a negotiation—with myself. I wasn’t near the start line, and I knew it was going to be a long walk home if I stopped where I was. I made an offer, to myself, that I would drop out when I got closer to the starting area, around mile 4.

Once I reached mile 4, I was a bit more relaxed and still in one piece, so the pitch became How about we drop out at mile 7 and we jog back toward the start? Once I got to the 7-mile mark, the effort was hard, but not overwhelming. So when the course reached a 3-mile loop, I bargained again, with the option to drop out at mile 10. When I hit the 10-mile mark, it occurred to me that I had actually PR’ed in the 10-mile distance and that I would likely run a decent time even if I slowed down to a jog, so the bargain was that I could back off on effort instead of stopping.

I reached a big hill at mile 12, and the next deal was that I could stop after I got to the top of the hill. But when I got to the top, there was maybe a minute or so of running remaining, and I went in for a massive kick. I finished in 1:08:59, a PB by 32 seconds, in a race where I once had been convinced that I was going to drop out at mile 4.

Looking back, I used two mental tools. Life coaches call them “bargaining” and “chunking.”

The series of negotiations put me in a position to think about the race as a collection of smaller distances within it, or “chunks.” In my head, I was no longer running a 13.1-mile race. Instead, I was racing for a few miles at a time. This allowed me to panic less and focus a little more, moment to moment. It helped me to calm down and eventually find my stride. The physical sensations were the same, but committing to race just a few more miles was easier to wrap my head around. I could focus a little more.

Ultimately, the thoughts that I had in the beginning were not ironclad truths, but interpretations of how I was feeling. Reframing them made the same sensations tolerable. You may or may not find chunking or bargaining helpful as a mental tactic. You might prefer another mental framework that works better for you. But the point is that these kinds of thoughts are very normal and there are tools that you can use to navigate them.

So, if your brain wants to stop when you hit the second mile of a 5K, for example, don’t assume that the desire to stop is a final offer. Perhaps it might help to think of it as the beginning of a negotiation.

On Oct. 26, 2004, NASA's Cassini spacecraft took the first close-up images of Saturn's largest moon Titan.

The Cassini spacecraft would later drop off a probe on Titan named Huygens, which was a European spacecraft that hitched a ride to the Saturn system with the Cassini mission. But before Cassini dropped off its robotic passenger, it flew by Titan a few times and took some amazing photos.

Cassini completed its first flyby on July 3, 2004, but when it swung by a second time on Oct. 26, it got about 300 times closer than it did the first time. At the closest point of this flyby, Cassini was 745 miles (1,200 kilometers) away from the surface of Titan. The spacecraft took hundreds of photos during this flyby, and these were the highest resolution views of Titan anyone had ever seen.

Astronomers knew Saturn's moon Titan had an atmosphere before Cassini arrived at the smog-shrouded moon, but the spacecraft's first photos (and innumerable more that followed) revealed a strange world that looked eerily similar to what scientists suspect a young Earth might have been like.

The first close-up views of Titan by Cassini showed a large continent-sized region that scienitsts called Xanadu. It included bright features that hinted at a mountain range, as well as a large basin and smooth plains regions.

In all, Cassini would make 127 close flybys of Titan during its 13 years at Saturn before the mission ended in 2017. The probe even dropped the European Huygens lander on Titan, which landed in January 2005, to see the moon's surface first hand.

This flyby image of Titan (and other instrument observations taken at the same time) allowed scientists to measure Titan's atmosphere density and composition to better plan the path and descent for the Hygens lander.

#3774. Nathan Fielder Is a Performance Artist in Disguise

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

“You Can See Everything” is toted as a documentary, but is Fielder a performance artist playing a documentarian?

Editor’s Note: This review contains spoilers.

As an art historian, it's hard not to relate what you see in everyday life to history — not in the sense that every lily pad recalls Monet or snowballs bring David Hammons to mind, but in the sense that sometimes art really does imitate life. This happened to me recently, maybe 10 minutes into Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim’s You Can See Everything. I watched Fielder hold painful, prolonged eye contact with Elizabeth Holmes, the convicted fraudster whose eerily large blue eyes seem to enact their own kind of performance. Naturally, I thought of Marina Abramović’s “The Artist is Present” (2010). Perhaps it’s an obvious, pretentious comparison. But hear me out: Is Fielder a documentarian, or is he a performance artist playing one?

Artist Martha Wilson recently defined performance art as an intervention that unfolds in real time, often placing the artist’s body at the center of the action. The genre has long tested the limits of what an artist’s body can do, and what an audience will tolerate: Chris Burden had himself shot with a .22-caliber gun in “Shoot” (1971); Yoko Ono invited audience members to cut away her clothing in “Cut Piece” (1964); and Carolee Schneemann pulled a scroll from her vagina in “Interior Scroll” (1975). These works differ considerably, but each makes the artist’s actions and the responses they provoke central to the work.

Fielder, too, puts himself in the middle of things. In You Can See Everything, he spends 34 days filming and questioning Holmes, her partner Billy Evans, and their two children. Eventually, he moves into the guesthouse of their California beachfront rental, just before Holmes begins her 11-year prison sentence for defrauding investors and patients of Theranos, whose blood-testing technology falsely claimed it could run a wide range of tests on a few drops of blood. The couple frames their participation as an act of “radical transparency,” hoping to persuade the public that Holmes is innocent. They chose Fielder as their documentarian, they explain, because he “thinks outside the box” just like they do. Game recognizes game.

Fielder’s move turns observation into participation. His proximity to the family becomes part of the experiment, shaping the behavior he records. In one scene, he films himself lurking at a distance as Billy and Holmes embrace. In another, he sits close to Holmes on a couch; a hand strokes her shoulder, and for a moment it seems to belong to Fielder, until the camera reveals Billy on the other side. But the film invites us to imagine a silent intimacy growing between Holmes and Fielder that it never confirms. Elsewhere, Holmes texts flirtatiously, “fyi you can totally see everything.” The title begins to feel less like a promise of transparency and more like provocation: Who is watching who? What does each person want seen?

#640. “Strangest of Its Kind”: Scientists Uncover a Bizarre Bacterial Self-Destruct Switch

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

An unusual bacterial defense uses DNA to switch on toxin production, suggesting a new way to build molecular tools that control genes. nb An infected bacterium can protect its neighbors by destroying itself before the virus inside it has a chance to multiply. Bacterial immune systems called retrons trigger this sacrifice when they detect particular strains of phages, the viruses that infect bacteria.

Decades of research have established a familiar explanation for how this works. A toxin stays ready inside the cell, restrained by small, single-stranded DNA molecules. An invading phage disturbs that DNA, releasing the toxin to kill the cell and stop the infection from spreading through the colony.

Researchers at Gladstone Institutes have now discovered a class of retrons that reverses the relationship between DNA and the toxin. In type VI retrons, DNA switches toxin production on rather than holding an existing toxin in check. The study, published in Molecular Cell, comes from the team of Gladstone Investigator Seth Shipman, PhD, its senior author.

“The way we typically detect retrons inside uninfected cells is by looking for this single-stranded DNA,” says Karen Zhang, a PhD student in Shipman’s lab and lead author of the study. “But type VI retrons caught our attention because they didn’t appear to have this signature. It was very odd, and we wondered if they might be broken.”

Tests in bacteria carrying type VI retrons showed that the defenses still worked against phages, leaving the researchers to explain how a functioning system could lack its expected DNA signal.

“That kicked off a long molecular odyssey,” Shipman says. “Whenever we tried the experiments we normally use to study retrons, they would fail, so we kept having to think of new ways to figure out how these retrons work.”

One early breakthrough came from Spacer-seq, a tool previously developed in Shipman’s lab. It uses molecular components of CRISPR-Cas, another bacterial immune system, to create a record of gene activity inside a cell. After standard methods repeatedly failed to detect DNA made by type VI retrons in uninfected cells, the team turned to this alternative approach.

“We had just about given up hope that any DNA was being made when this approach finally revealed a very, very low level of production in uninfected cells,” Zhang says.

That trace gave the researchers a reason to keep investigating. Further experiments showed that type VI retrons produced no noticeable levels of toxin in uninfected cells, either. Phage entry triggered single-stranded DNA production to rise to the levels needed for defense. As the DNA accumulated, it interacted with an RNA molecule that had been blocking toxin production. This interaction removed the block, allowing toxin production to ramp up.

The toxin appears to be a tiny protein that embeds itself in the membrane surrounding the bacterial cell. By destabilizing that membrane, it kills the infected cell.

“It’s almost the mirror-image version of how we traditionally view retrons, because the DNA switches on toxin production instead of inhibiting an already-produced toxin,” Zhang says. “It would be fascinating to explore how bacterial evolution could have resulted in such a complex molecular system—whether type VI retrons are older or newer than other retrons, or whether they’re a stepping stone between different types.”

Retrons can also efficiently modify and deliver single-stranded DNA, capabilities that have drawn the attention of researchers developing DNA-editing tools. Shipman and his team are working to harness those abilities to rewrite disease-causing mutations inside human cells with unprecedented precision. Understanding the machinery of different retrons provides molecular designs they could adapt for that work.

“This is, by far, the strangest of the retron systems that we or anyone else has come across,” Shipman says. “These retrons operate with Rube Goldberg-level complexity, using molecular mechanisms that we would never have thought to invent, but that we’ll use in future technologies.”

The type VI machinery is already inspiring work in Shipman’s lab on synthetic sensors and switches that could control gene activity.

“This system represents a unique way to potentially build a molecular sensor and switch encoded in DNA and RNA, that no one has thought of before,” Zhang says. “Synthetic sensors and switches have many possible applications, both in treating disease and in designing experiments to better understand how certain diseases operate.”

The tiny, highly potent toxins could have medical uses as well. The scientists are working on ways to incorporate them into new antimicrobial therapies that could target and kill bacteria responsible for human disease.

“This study highlights why it’s so important for a molecular technology development lab like ours to keep looking to nature for inspiration,” Shipman says. “We can use what evolution has given us as starting points to build complex machines that would have been tremendously hard to even conceptualize.”

Apple Responds to the iPhone Duo’s ‘Crackgate’ Controversy

iPhone Duo screen cracked within 6 hours of ownership… by r/iPhoneDuo

As is always the case with online complaints, it is important to consider the inherent bias in a situation like this. Those who don’t have any issues are far less likely to pop into the iPhone Duo subreddit to post, and many more are likely entirely unaware there’s even a fresh “-gate” controversy.

As The Verge reports, iFixit has already torn the iPhone Duo. iFixit does teardowns like this to see how a company engineered its latest product and how easy it is to repair. But what makes the iPhone Duo teardown especially notable is that several users’ devices broke in the same way. Is there a manufacturing issue or design problem at play here?

The exact nature of the problem, though, remains unknown.

It’s important to note here that “physical” doesn’t necessarily mean that the screen is physically damaged. Faulty wiring can cause a similar effect: it can look like pixels are damaged when, in reality, they just aren’t firing correctly. What some people describe as a “crack” is not necessarily a crack at all.

It’s important to temper any speculation of a cause, though. Online commenters, some of whom have broken $2,000 phones and have every right to be upset, have floated ideas about manufacturing defects, design flaws, and other notions that could spell disaster for Apple’s new flagship device.

What’s much more likely for a carefully engineered device from one of the world’s biggest companies is that the problems are relatively limited in scope and affect only a specific subset of devices. It is important to note what Apple says about contacting AppleCare for support and a replacement device. A common thread among those who have taken to Reddit and social media with cracked screens is that they have received prompt support and, in many cases, already gotten replacement devices.

Hopefully the company can quickly service any affected customers and nip whatever exactly is going on in the bud. It seems like Apple believes the problem is relatively limited.

#2345. Growing Bromeliads in the Pacific Northwest

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

With their unique appearance, intriguing textures, colorful blooms, and diverse array of forms, the bromeliad family is an exciting world to explore if you’re thinking of transforming your indoor space (or outdoor space, if you’re in a frost-free area) into a tropical paradise.

If you’re unfamiliar with this remarkable group of plants, let’s have a quick foray into the bromeliad family before narrowing down to a few of the most popular varieties available commercially. Then I’ll share some growing and care tips for those of us gardening in more temperate climates.

The family Bromeliaceae is a diverse group that includes over 3,000 species, mostly native to the tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas. Known for their rosette-shaped foliage, vibrant flowers, and adaptations to their environment, bromeliads come in a range of shapes, sizes, colors, textures, and forms. They occupy a variety of habitats, and many grow high in the canopies of trees. In fact, many bromeliad species are epiphytes—meaning they grow on other plants (like trees) rather than being rooted in soil!

Did you know that pineapple (Ananas comosus, Zones 10–11) is a member of the bromeliad family? “Air plants” (Tillandsia cvs., Zones 8–11) are as well; they get their common name from their ability to live in a soil-less environment, obtaining nutrients and moisture from the air!

Best known among houseplant enthusiasts (and our frost-free gardening friends) are gorgeous guzmanias (Guzmania cvs., Zones 10–12), blushing bromeliads (Neoregelia cvs., Zones 10–11), vrieseas (Vriesea cvs., Zones 10–12), and urn plants (Aechmea cvs., Zones 9–12)—beloved for their structure, colorful foliage, astonishing long-lived floral displays, and unfussy nature. These beauties have similar growth habits and care requirements and are the plants I’ll be focusing on today. Pineapples and air plants deserve their very own article!

Watch: Check Out Our Super Cool Plants Podcast on YouTube

While bromeliads in the wild thrive in warm, humid environments, growing them in our region is easy if you follow a few basic rules and provide them with the correct exposure, moisture, soil, and humidity, and keep them within their comfort zone as far as temperatures are concerned.

While most of us here are familiar with bromeliads decorating indoor spaces, many varieties (including air plants) can also be grown outdoors in a bright, sheltered location such as an atrium or covered patio. Protection from frost and cold winter winds is crucial, as is protection from hot, dry drafts and intense summer sun. Read on for hints and tips on how to ensure the best success for your bromeliad.

Bright, indirect light is best. In the temperate climate of the Pacific Northwest, cloudy and overcast days are relatively common throughout the year, so placing your bromeliad close to a bright window is ideal, while avoiding harsh afternoon rays.

The unique ability of bromeliads to store water means that their watering requirements differ from many other houseplants. Unlike many air plants, tank-forming bromeliads such as guzmanias and neoregelias collect water in the central cups formed by their leaves. Keep these cups supplied with fresh water, flushing them periodically to prevent stagnation and mineral buildup. Use room-temperature rainwater or suitable tap water to fill the tank, and let the potting mix dry out between waterings to prevent root rot.

Your bromeliad will thrive in a coarse, airy potting mix made up of 60 percent bark, 20 percent perlite, and 20 percent organic potting soil, if you’d like to make your own bromeliad mix. Alternatively, a commercial well-draining orchid mix works well too. Excellent drainage is key—their roots dislike sitting in water.

Bromeliads appreciate higher humidity levels (they’re from the tropics, after all!). While some of us experience relatively high humidity in our climate outdoors, your indoor bromeliads benefit from additional humidity during drier periods. Running AC or forced-air heating can dry out the air, so extra attention to your bromeliad at this time is important. A pebble humidity tray is another option.

Bromeliads typically flourish in temperatures ranging from 60°F to 80°F. Indoor plants will be fine, but if you’re growing yours outdoors, you’ll want to bring them inside before temperatures become too cold for the variety you’re growing. Protect all bromeliads from cold drafts and from blasts of warm air from forced-air heaters.

Bromeliads are not heavy feeders. During the spring and summer growing season, a diluted (half strength), water-soluble, low-nitrogen fertilizer, such as one formulated for orchids (or special bromeliad food), will provide all the necessary nutrients. Don’t fertilize directly into the water tank, as it can cause a buildup of fertilizer salts.

While bromeliads are generally pest resistant, watch for mealybugs or aphids and treat as needed!

I have become completely obsessed with these relatively easy-care, incredibly rewarding tropical beauties. Now that you know more about the world of bromeliads and how to grow one of your very own, maybe you’re ready to join me in my obsession? Happy gardening!

#650. Scientists Discover a Strange Insect in the Himalayas Where No One Expected It

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A newly identified Himalayan wormlion challenges the idea that its genus lives only in the tropics, raising the possibility of undiscovered relatives on nearby mountain slopes.

In rain-sheltered patches of sand and soil on the southern slopes of the Himalayas, wormlion larvae build funnel-shaped pits to catch prey. Larvae belonging to this group were first collected in southern Xizang in 1978, but without adult flies to examine, scientists could not confirm their identity.

Adult specimens collected during a 2025 expedition to Yadong County have now helped resolve that 48-year mystery. A team led by researchers at Dali University in China has described the new species, Vermitigris tsangyanggyatso, in a recent paper published in the open-access journal ZooKeys.

Wormlions belong to Vermileonidae, a family of brachyceran, or short-horned, flies. They are the only known family of flies whose larvae capture prey by constructing these pits. Their hunting strategy evolved independently of the similar traps built by antlions, an example of evolutionary convergence.

The researchers’ search for adult wormlions had already taken years of patient work with another species. Asked what first drew them to the family, the team recalled:

“Well, it was probably an accidental discovery that first motivated us. In 2015, while searching for pit-building antlions on Cangshan Mountain in Dali, China, we discovered an unfamiliar larva that we found particularly interesting. However, because the adults have an extremely short lifespan, we failed for many years to determine its identity.

It was not until 2024 that we finally obtained adults through artificial rearing in the laboratory. It turned out to be a new species, which we named Vermiophis cangshanensis. This first discovery therefore took nine years. Since then, we have paid close attention to wormlions wherever we encounter them.”

The adults collected in Yadong revealed a species that is unusually large for its group. Their bodies measure up to 19.5 millimeters, about three-quarters of an inch, with wings nearly 16 millimeters, or 0.63 inches, long. A uniformly yellowish-brown to reddish-brown abdomen distinguishes Vermitigris tsangyanggyatso from its closest relatives, which have light and dark rings or longitudinal stripes.

Its mouthparts also offer a clue to how it lives as an adult. Some related lineages diversified during the Late Mesozoic while developing elongated mouthparts for feeding on nectar. The new species instead has a markedly shortened rostrum, or snout, suggesting that it may not feed at all during its final life stage.

Fully grown larvae are larger than the adults, reaching 20 to 28 millimeters, or about 0.8 to 1.1 inches, in length. They have a single semicircular proleg, a fleshy, leglike appendage, bearing a distinct row of five to seven spines. Yet their anatomy has not fully explained how they construct traps in the different materials available to them.

The team found larvae in three types of rain-protected shelter: fine-grained soil beneath giant rocks, wood debris under a pavilion, and fine-grained river sand accumulated beside a river. Field observations indicated a strong preference for the river sand. These sheltered sites provide the stable conditions the larvae need to maintain their pitfall traps, although the physical features that allow them to work across such different textures remain uncertain.

“We honestly do not know yet. Perhaps future behavioral experiments will help answer this question,” the team said when asked which features make that possible.

Wet forests have traditionally been shown to act as barriers to wormlion dispersal. Finding this species in protected pockets within such an environment shows how members of Vermitigris can live there despite the surrounding wet conditions.

The adult observations offered another piece of the dispersal picture. On sunny days, most flies rested quietly on leaves near the places where their larvae lived. One specimen, however, was found farther away around agricultural fields, which the researchers interpreted as evidence of strong flight and dispersal abilities.

The Himalayan location also broadens the climates in which researchers might expect to find the genus. As the team explained:

“The previously known species were first discovered in tropical Southeast Asia, and these insects were therefore thought to be restricted to tropical regions. The discovery of this species suggests that they may also be capable of adapting to subtropical climates. We hope that more unknown species will eventually be discovered in mainland Southeast Asia, such as Thailand, as well as in southwestern China, including Yunnan, Guizhou, and Sichuan Provinces.”

The species name honors Tsangyang Gyatso, the sixth Dalai Lama, a celebrated 17th-century Tibetan poet-monk from Xizang known for his romantic verse and unconventional life. Its description raises the number of Vermitigris species recorded in China from one to two and the genus’s worldwide total from four to five. There are now 67 known living species of wormlion flies.

Many neighboring areas along the southern Himalayan slopes remain unsampled, leaving room for further fieldwork to uncover additional members of this poorly documented group. For co-author Ji-Shen Wang, the work also reflects a curiosity that extends beyond his original research specialty:

“From the perspective of science communication, I would say that although my primary research interest is the insect order Mecoptera (scorpionflies), I have gradually expanded my interests into Diptera (flies) as well.

As a naturalist, I find the diversity of life on Earth endlessly fascinating, and this curiosity about nature continually motivates me to explore and wonder. I would like to quote Herman Melville from Moby-Dick: ‘As for me, I am tormented with an everlasting itch for things remote. I love to sail forbidden seas, and land on barbarous coasts.”

Reference: “After forty-eight years: An enigmatic new wormlion fly from Xizang, China (Diptera, Vermileonidae)” by Li-Xia Shan and Ji-Shen Wang, 7 April 2026, ZooKeys. DOI: 10.3897/zookeys.1276.184675

#1222. The 8 Best Fig Colognes to Try Right Now

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Every product was carefully curated by an Esquire editor. We may earn a commission from these links. Here’s how we test products and why you should trust us.

The sweet, jammy fruit has claimed the spotlight this season.

A lot of fig has been coming across our desk at the Esquire office. Not on charcuterie boards or in jams, but in perfume bottles. Fig is having a little moment as one of fragrance’s more interesting and versatile notes due to the variety of scents different parts of the fruit provide.

Green fig leaf smells fresh, herbal, crisp, and dewy; its milky sap can feel creamy and velvety, almost like coconut milk; and the ripe fruit brings a warm, jammy sweetness. Even fig wood and bark get in on the action, lending scents an earthy, dry, grounding quality.

That range makes fig a great scent for any season. Blended with citrus and aromatic herbs, it’s bright and refreshing enough for spring and summer. Paired with sandalwood, amber, vanilla, and other warm gourmand or woody notes, it becomes cozy enough for fall and winter. In other words, there’s a fig cologne for nearly every taste, season, and occasion. To help you find the right one, we rounded up eight of our favorites.

Philosykos is one of our all-time favorite Diptyque fragrances, and it’s also our favorite fig fragrance in existence. It doesn’t just use one part of the fig; it emphasizes the fruit’s leaves, sap, and tree wood, creating an alluring blend of green, herbal, sweet, and woody notes. No other fragrance truly captures the essence of the fig tree like this one.

There’s a Tom Ford fragrance for pretty much any mood, occasion, or scent note you can think of, so it only makes sense that the designer has a great fig cologne. Figue Érotique captures the green freshness of fig leaves accented by citrusy bergamot and mandarin before leaning into the juicy, fruity side of fig and its nectar. At its base, it’s a true gourmand with sweet notes of brown sugar and licorice, but it never feels artificially sweet or syrupy.

If you prefer something a little lighter, fresher, and a bit floral, Maison Louie Marie makes a beautiful fragrance that focuses on lily-of-the-valley and bright lemon before moving into a heart of creamy Tuscan fig, which brings a bit of richness to an otherwise fresh fragrance. Though fig isn’t the main character here, it definitely plays an important part and blends beautifully with a sweet base of tonka bean, agave, and amber.

Debaser is a more complex cologne in which fig plays a big part at the heart of the fragrance. It’s one of our favorite D.S. & Durga scents, thanks to its intriguing blend of woody, earthy, fruity, and floral notes, making it pretty much impossible to classify into just one category. But the main players here are fig, coconut, and tonka; milky, fresh notes of fig mingle with sweet and creamy coconut, which give way to a blend of smooth tonka bean, blond woods, and earthy moss.

Instead of being overly bold and spicy, London 1983 has the perfect level of soft spice balanced with musks, florals, and woods—and, of course, fig. It starts out with a gorgeous blend of fig and black pepper, then moves into light florals and blond woods. It’s a nice, understated cologne that sits close to the skin.

Inspired by the ancient Hanging Gardens, Jo Malone’s Fig & Lotus Flower cologne is one of our favorite nature-inspired fragrances. Like the name says, it features fresh fig leaves and milky fig fruit alongside lush lotus flowers, and vetiver provides a grassy, woody base to round out the dewy top and heart.

We’ve written a lot about Arquiste’s aromatic, fougère, Mediterranean-inspired fragrance called “A Grove by the Sea,” and that’s a testament to how versatile and complex it is. Every time I smell this scent, I notice something new. Not only does it feature a gorgeous array of Adriatic fruits, herbs, and woods, but it uses both fig leaves at the top and ripe fig fruit at the heart to capture two sides of the fig.

One of my favorite new fragrances that launched this season is Granado’s Figo Confit. It blends woody, fruity, and gourmand notes to create something rich, smooth, and delicious. Jammy fig meets juicy plum, sweet dates, light florals, and resinous labdanum for the perfect cold-weather scent.
