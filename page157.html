Hostas (Hosta spp.) are beloved backyard staples. Celebrated for their lush foliage, incredible leaf variety and excellent shade tolerance, these reliable perennials make a big impact from spring through late summer into fall. However, as October rolls in, with shorter daylight hours and potentially the season's first frosts, these blue-green leaves beauties begin to yellow, collapse, and prepare for dormancy.

While it might be tempting to view fall as a time to step back, this is a pivotal month for hosta care. Proper fall maintenance goes far beyond basic backyard tidiness and cleaning up your beds. The right interventions now can protect plant crowns, prevent overwintering pests, and reduce fungal spores. The right October care for hostas can be a vital investment in next spring’s finest plants.

Because hosta varieties vary widely, from massive Empress Wu to miniature Blue Mouse Ears, your plants may transition into dormancy at slightly different rates. But if you adjust the timings according to your hardiness zone and weather, these essential tasks will help you set the stage for an explosion of lush foliage next spring.

October marks an important transition for your hostas. Above ground, the lush leaves stop photosynthesizing and begin transferring their stored starches down into the roots and crowns. This means lots of yellowing leaves, eventual foliage loss, and plant dormancy. Below ground, energy reserves fuel the development of dormant eyes (buds) that will push up as fresh leaves next April.

Understanding your local weather patterns and USDA hardiness zone is essential for timing the following tasks correctly. In colder northern climates (zones 3-5), heavy frosts often arrive by mid-month, signaling an earlier start to cutting back foliage and insulating crowns. In milder southern regions (zones 6-8), hostas may hold green leaves for a few more weeks, requiring a more gradual approach to fall cleanup.

Both in-ground hosta plantings and container-grown specimens require help before winter sets in. Taking time this month to check in with plant health, inspect leaf debris for pests, and prepare potted plants for freezing temperatures ensures every hosta in your yard enjoys a peaceful, protected dormancy. If you’re unsure about soil moisture, use the Raintrip 4-in-1 Soil Meter, available from Amazon, to get a heads-up about ground conditions before you start these tasks.

Your hostas are naturally preparing for a period of rest this month, so you don’t want to encourage growth right now. So stop applying nitrogen-rich fertilizers in early fall and help them rest. Feeding hostas in October encourages a sudden flush of soft leaf growth that cannot harden off before winter, wasting vital energy that the plant needs for dormancy and leaving the crown vulnerable to severe freeze damage. It’s not a bad idea to add a tiny sprinkle of a diluted seaweed tonic, such as GS Plant Foods Organic Liquid Kelp from Walmart, to help winterize roots, but that’s enough.

You also need to adjust and reduce hosta watering this month. While hostas needed consistent moisture in summer to keep their large leaves plump and turgid, autumn rainfall and cooler temperatures reduce soil evaporation. When you do want to water, check the top couple of inches (5cm) of soil first. If it feels damp, hold off. Only water during dry spells to keep roots from desiccating before the ground freezes. Once the topsoil freezes in late fall, stop watering completely until spring thaw.

There is some debate among gardeners as to whether or not you need to trim or prune hostas in fall, but if you do, timing matters. As long as you can see a leaf has some green or yellowish-green color, it is still working at converting light into energy and pumping those energy reserves down into the roots for next year’s growth. If you cut this process short by trimming back foliage prematurely, you risk starving the plant, resulting in smaller crowns and weaker leaf development next spring.

Even if they look raggedy, you need to wait until the leaves are clearly dead. For many, this happens after the first frosts of fall. At this point, you can use clean, sharp bypass pruners or grass shears to cut the leaf stems back to roughly 1-2 inches (2.5-5cm) above the ground. Make sure to wipe your pruners before and after use to reduce the risk of cross-contamination with a little isopropyl alcohol or some wipes, such as Clorox Disinfecting Wipes from Lowe’s.

Take care not to slice into the main crown or damage dormant eye buds sitting at the soil surface. Varieties with thick, corrugated leaves, like Frances Williams or Sieboldiana, may take longer to collapse than thin-leaved varieties, so be patient and adjust your cutting time accordingly.

A good fall cleanup is the best way to protect hostas from slugs and fungal disease. Fungal spores love to overwinter in decaying hosta foliage. When hosta leaves die back and collapse, this soggy blanket also creates the ultimate winter resort for slugs and snails. Slugs lay clusters of tiny eggs beneath the collapsed foliage, and these can hatch right when tender hosta shoots are emerging come spring.

Once the leaves have died, trim them back and pick up all the debris around the plants. Rake up all dead leaf material, spent flower stalks, and surrounding leaf litter. If the removed foliage was healthy, it can be composted. However, if you see leaf spots, crown decay, or pest damage, dispose in the trash to reduce the risk of spreading pathogens. For a gentle soil amendment, add a sprinkle of Down to Earth Crab Meal, from Amazon, which can help soil microbes break down pathogens.

October can be a good time for dividing overgrown hosta clumps, provided you live in a moderate or warm climate (zones 6-8). Dividing in fall means the separated sections have time to settle before winter hits (ideally, they need 4-6 weeks before the first hard freeze). However, if you are in northern regions (zones 3-5) where the ground freezes solid by November, it’s best to postpone division until spring.

To divide a mature clump, wait until foliage begins to yellow. Carefully lift using a garden fork. Using a sharp serrated knife, slice the crown into sections, ensuring each division has a cluster of roots and 2-3 dormant eyes. If you need a good knife, try the versatile and highly rated Fiskars Hori Hori Knife, available from Walmart. Replant the divisions immediately at the same depth as the original plant, firming the soil and watering thoroughly. Hostas are long-lived perennials that only require division every 3-5 years when their centers become crowded or hollow.

Applying a winter mulch over tidy beds protects delicate crowns from the dangers of frost heaving. Frost heaving occurs when freeze-thaw cycles cause the ground to lift shallow roots out of the ground, exposing vulnerable crowns to freezing air and drying winds. So mulch is a great protection. However, timing is everything: applying too early traps residual heat and moisture against the crown, encouraging fungal rot.

Wait until nighttime temperatures drop and the topsoil begins to freeze before applying a 2-3-inch (5-8cm) layer of loose organic mulch. Go for shredded bark, pine needles, or well-rotted leaf mold. Team favorite Back to the Roots Organic Premium Wood Mulch from Amazon is a dye-free bark mulch that insulates against freeze-thaw risks while preventing spring weeds. Just avoid piling mulch directly over the center of the crown. Keep it loosely brushed around the perimeter.

Trimming back foliage will mean that crowns become invisible beneath mulch. So drive some durable, weather-resistant garden markers into the soil directly beside each crown. Labeling prevents accidental spade damage when carrying out early spring digging and helps you track special cultivars like Patriot or June. It’s a good idea to take a picture of the ground with the markers, too, just in case.

Growing hostas in pots and urns is a fantastic way to brighten up shaded patios and porches. However, container-grown hostas face higher winter risks. Because potted soil lacks the insulating mass of earth, roots can get colder much faster and plants are more vulnerable during dormancy. Extreme temperature swings can crack pots and kill dormant crowns. So it’s a good idea to protect and overwinter potted hostas.

If you are in zones 3-6, extra winter protection is essential. Once foliage dies back and soil cools, move dormant pots into an unheated garage, garden shed, or cold frame where temperatures stay between 25°F and 40°F (-4°C to 4°C). You can use rolling plant caddies to move heavier pots, such as the Bloem 12-Inch Terracotta Caddy, available from Walmart, which has 75lb capacity.

Where indoor storage space isn't available, group pots tightly together against a warm house foundation. Wrap the exterior with bubble wrap or burlap, and top-dress with straw. Check potted soil once a month during winter, and apply a small splash of water if the soil becomes bone-dry, in order to prevent root desiccation.

These fall tasks are essential for keeping your hostas healthy, protecting crowns from freezing winter weather, and eliminating pest and disease harbors. Just one crisp October afternoon tidying up your shade beds will do wonders for protecting your plants over winter and ensuring they break dormancy with lush leaves next spring.

#3932. Comment | How do we protect artists from gallery insolvency?

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The panel “Supporting artists as sustainable businesses” at the Art Business Conference 2026 in London Photo: David Owens

A monthly look at recent developments in art law, written by Alexander Herman, the director of the Institute of Art and Law

At the Art Business Conference in London in September, I was fortunate enough to moderate a panel discussion about artists as sustainable businesses. How are artists best supported in the current art market ecosystem, and what specific roles do galleries and studios play? It was not long before one panellist made a bold suggestion for legislative change in the UK to protect artists consigning works to galleries.

We have witnessed several gallery insolvencies of late. What then happens to an artist’s consigned stock when a gallery goes bust and the liquidators move in? The situation can be especially perilous for the artist if the gallery has been keeping their work with an unpaid third-party storage company. There is a fear that the artist may never see the art again, since recovery is usually premised on paying unpaid storage costs. It may sound like the art is being held hostage, although I am told that reasonable deals can sometimes be worked out.

In the US, unlike the UK, many states have statutes that seek to protect artists from unscrupulous financial machinations by dealers. In 2012, New York went one step further by forcefully requiring dealers to hold works on trust for artists and to keep money accrued on artists’ behalf in trust accounts rather than mixing it with general operating funds. That way, in theory, artists can be more predictably paid after each sale and, in catastrophic times, rescue their funds once the ship goes down.

The panellist was the art lawyer Jon Sharples, who repeated his call for the UK to pass similarly protective legislation in an article for The Art Newspaper in 2023. There are compelling reasons for doing so: it offers a safety net to artists and it would not harm the London market vis-à-vis New York, since galleries in the latter would already comply. In fact, it is possible that major galleries with footholds in both jurisdictions already comply with the more stringent requirements across all their operations (though it is hard to know this for sure).

Contemporary art galleries are unlikely to applaud the possible imposition of new legal obligations. Galleries already take significant financial risk, investing in artists to support the development of their careers. Onerous regulatory demands already exist in the UK: anti-money-laundering rules, data protection and artist resale royalties, none of which are shared by New York galleries. For small galleries living hand to mouth, could this be the straw that breaks the camel’s back?

Sharples nimbly suggested another, separate layer of protection for artists, one already present in the law that is not well known—and one that does not require new legislation: the law of agency. At common law, agents act as fiduciaries on behalf of their principals. Mapped onto the art world, galleries are fiduciaries for their artists and so owe them fiduciary obligations. This would include duties to account, a prohibition on secret profits and a general requirement to treat the artist’s interest as they would their own—the Golden Rule, as adapted to the art market.

So, is new legislation required for commercial galleries in the UK, or is it simply that galleries need to better understand the duties they already owe their artists? Sharples, I suspect, would like to see both, especially since only legislative change would help in cases of insolvency. Short of that, perhaps the answer is also more education.

Should the legislative route be contemplated in the UK, the art sector would need to be united in its advocacy. At present, there are different sector bodies, but no umbrella organisation to represent them all. There are calls, however, for a UK visual arts trade body, much as there is for music. As it happens, that was the topic of the next panel at the conference, but I’ve run out of room!

In this podcast, based on The Art Newspaper's regular interview series, our host Ben Luke talks to artists in-depth. He asks the questions you've always wanted to: who are the artists, historical and contemporary, they most admire? Which are the museums they return to? What are the books, music and other media that most inspire them? And what is art for, anyway?

George Shaw, who was born in 1966 in Coventry, UK, makes paintings and drawings that are a response to a particular place but also a deeply personal engagement with memory.

Although his paintings depict identifiable locations within Tile Hill, the postwar housing estate on which he grew up in the suburbs of Coventry in England’s Midlands region—from the houses and garages on the estate, to playing fields, community centres, pubs and shops and the woods beyond—they are steeped in recollection, potent with signs of human presence, including Shaw’s, but barely ever occupied by people, and even then only by the artist. As a result, these paintings have a curious relationship with time, a factor only enhanced by his use of his own photographs as the source image for his compositions.

Tile Hill is the setting in which his identity was formed as a young person, in which he became himself, and the hundreds of paintings he has made of it since follow its changing face, moments of aging, destruction, dereliction and renewal. Over time, this lifelong magnum opus has come to tell a multi-volume story of Shaw’s life—a multifaceted self-portrait.

He reflects on this idea of reflecting a person or body through place, with reference to one of his more enduring heroes, James Joyce. He also discusses the symbiosis of his subject matter and his use of enamel paint and the relationship between his photographs of Tile Hill and the paintings he makes from them.

He reflects on the importance of music and the social and political culture around it in defining his identity and its legacy in his life and work today. He discusses the role of imagination in the extraordinary output of William Blake, his early response to fantasy artists like Chris Fosse, and a huge wealth of historic painters that have been important to him since, from John Everett Millais to John Constable, Mike Kelley, Titian and Rembrandt. He also reflects on the role of his father’s “curriculum” of films that he watched from his youth onwards, and its significance in relation to his paintings. #3342. At 83, Triathlete Bob Plant Isn’t Retiring—He’s Just Reloading

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Bob Plant had never worried about an Ironman cutoff.

He had been to Kona before. Plenty of times, actually. This year marked his 10th start at the 2026 Ironman World Championship, and more than four decades of endurance competition had given him a good understanding of what his 83-year-old body could handle.

Kona reminded him that experience doesn’t grant immunity to a bad day.

Plant began having severe cramps in his quads and hamstrings during the bike portion of the race. He believes insufficient sodium was the primary reason, and mechanical issues compounded the problem when his chain repeatedly came loose. Each stop cost his precious time, but he didn’t realize how much until he finally reached the second transition and received news he’s never heard during an Ironman.

He had missed the bike cutoff by three minutes. His day was over.

For a triathlon Hall of Famer with seven Kona-age-group podium finishes, and 20 USA Triathlon national titles, a DNF could provide reason for second-guessing himself. But that isn’t how he’s wired.

“I just turn the page,” Plant told Muscle & Fitness. “I’ve learned something, and I’m ready to get going again.”

There wasn’t any self-pity attached to his responses around the end result. Plant believes he prepared the same way he had for his prior trips to Hawaii, only this time the clock won.

“Stuff happens like that,” he said. “You have to accept it and learn from the experience.”

That mindset helps explain as much about Plant’s athletic longevity as any training program could. He still wants to improve and remains competitive enough to care about his performance, but the decades of knowledge have taught him that surviving in the game requires knowing when to push, when to recover, and when yesterday needs to simply remain yesterday.

Plant considers himself a “minimalist trainer,” which sounds contradictory for someone who decides to line up for an Ironman.

Plant says even his friends are surprised by how much he can accomplish without the training volume you come to expect from any endurance competition. But the objective stopped being proving how much work he could put into training a long time ago.

A typical week now includes two workouts for each triathlon discipline. That’s swimming, cycling, and running. Perhaps the most important day in this routine is the one where he does absolutely nothing.

“I take one day completely off, whether I need it or not,” Plant said. “Because if I don’t take that day off, two days later, I wish I did.

For Plant, aging doesn’t necessarily mean that he’s forgotten how to work hard. It’s about knowing what the bill is that comes afterward.

“The biggest difference is you need more recovery time,” he said. “You might be able to do similar physical things that you did 10 years ago, but you’re going to need more than one day to recover. You’re probably going to need two or three days to recover.”

It’s that reality that has shaped how he thinks about training. Plant no longer does speed work because the potential injury risk and added stress aren’t worth any potential gains. Most of his work now places a priority on endurance while staying within limits he has learned over a lifetime of experience.

The discipline is knowing those limits without using age as an excuse to avoid doing anything difficult. Plant’s philosophy is listening to his body closely, so it never has to scream for his attention. Recovery isn’t lost training time. It’s when the previous session becomes useful.

“That’s when you get strong—is when you rest,” he said. “You have to repair your body and give it a chance to benefit from your hard workouts.”

Kona has only reinforced that there are other variables to manage. Plant had never missed a cutoff before. He had completed enough races on the island to believe he understood what the conditions required.

He doesn’t view that as evidence that it’s time to stop. It’s another piece of information for an athlete who still views himself as a work in progress.

For everything Plant has learned about endurance, there is one part of his current fitness that does bother him some.

Before the pandemic, strength training was a consistent fixture in Plant’s routine. He would get into the gym up to three times a week, sometimes getting a lift in right after a run. When that routine disappeared as gyms closed during covid, it never fully returned.

Plant said he could see and feel the difference.

“I think I’ve lost about–maybe just age as well–but a good seven pounds of muscle mass because I’ve not been in the gym much,” he said.

The number matters less to him than what also left with that muscle loss. Plant notices less power in his legs than he had five years ago. He still goes through his core work, stretches, and trains at home. It just can’t consistently replicate what resistance training once provided. He did acknowledge that he intends on lifting again during the winter.

Returning to strength work isn’t about hitting his max anymore or even seeing how much weight he can move. It’s about preserving what allows him to continue doing the activities he enjoys.

Plant doesn’t spend his days recovering at home in between training sessions. He still works full-time as a dentist, a career he has been in for 55 years.

Four days a week can mean arriving at the office around 7 in the morning and remaining there until after 5 p.m. On many of those evenings, he heads home to train. As he sees it, the relationship between work and fitness is mutually beneficial.

A demanding career does limit the amount of time he can dedicate to training, but Plant believes this helps protect him from overtraining. In return, his fitness gives him the physical and mental stamina to handle days in which his patients expect his complete attention regardless of how he feels.

“You just can’t say, ‘Oh, I’m going to do an average job today,’” Plant joked.

With a full day of work, there is always a reason to skip working out afterward. Like most people, Plant knows what happens once someone gets home, sits in their favorite chair, and starts negotiating with themselves about pushing their workout to another day.

He also knows what happened to him when one form of training disappeared from his own routine. The loss of strength after getting away from the gym has served as a personal reminder that physical capability isn’t something the body promises to preserve indefinitely.

“You stop doing something that you’ve been doing for a while, and you can tell the difference,” Plant said. “No doubt about it.”

Plant doesn’t expect everyone who hears his story to sign up for an Ironman. In fact, he encourages people to walk, run a 5k, or begin lifting weights. Simply find something you enjoy enough to continue doing it.

“Whether you’re walking or you’re doing some activity, just get out there and get your body moving,” he said.

He has seen people around him take versions of that message and make it their own. Someone will start exercising or complete a race and will tell Plant what they accomplished. It’s those moments that seem to matter more to him than his own results because they confirm that his example can open a door without requiring anyone to follow him through 140.6 miles.

“If I can do it at my age, surely you can do something,” Plant said. “I just encourage them to somehow look at the possibilities.”

The physical benefits are obvious, but Plant believes movement gives him something just as valuable psychologically. Training has taught him that he can tolerate discomfort, overcome difficult situations, and keep going when something doesn’t go according to plan.

“It gives you confidence,” Plant said. “You can handle an Ironman or whatever, you can handle other things in your life that may have seemed big before, but now they’re not.”

Plant would have loved to finish in Kona. He believed he could have continued if he had been allowed through transition, but the cutoff didn’t care about what potential outcomes might have been. Three minutes ended his day.

Plant accepted it. He learned what he could from his cramps, the mechanical issues, and a cutoff he had never previously needed to think about.

Then he did what more than four decades in endurance sports have taught him to do.

“I’ll continue as long as I can,” he said. “As long as it’s fun.”

Goal: Build lower-body strength, core stability, and aerobic capacity without turning every session into a maximal effort.

Time: 35–40 minutes Equipment: Dumbbells or kettlebells, bench or box, cardio machine or open space

Plant repeatedly emphasized listening to the body and giving it enough time to handle the work. The warmup should make you feel better than when you started, not tired.

Plant believes getting away from regular weight training contributed to a noticeable loss of muscle and leg power, which is why he plans to make resistance training a priority again. “If you don’t have strong legs, that’s the key to life, isn’t it?” he said.

Choose a bike, rower, treadmill, elliptical, or brisk outdoor walk.

#3850. Dezeen launches "richer and more versatile" new website

Dezeen has updated its website for the first time in 10 years. Dezeen’s co-CEOs Benedict Hobson and Wai Shin Li explain the changes.

You might have noticed that things look a little different around here. Yes, after many years, we have redesigned our website!

It has been 10 years, in fact – the last major revamp of Dezeen was in 2016. And as with our previous redesign, evolution, not revolution, has been the aim.

Dezeen was launched by Marcus Fairs from his spare room as a basic WordPress blog in 2006. When it started to take off, he brought designer and typographer Micha Weidmann on board to design a logo and layout for it, and this has been the foundation upon which Dezeen has been built ever since.

We really like it, and millions of readers around the world seem to like it too.

So our ambition for this redesign was to bring the site up to date while staying true to the core identity that has helped Dezeen become what it is today.

Underneath the bonnet, there’s been a complete overhaul

We have worked with creative technology studio Made by On to refresh the look and feel of the site and improve the user interface to make it more useful and enjoyable to use.

Underneath the bonnet, there’s been a complete overhaul.

Our previous site was built on ageing technology that had grown clunky over time. Things broke too often and, as regular readers will know, fixing them was a slow and often painful process.

So we tasked Made by On with rebuilding both the front- and back-ends of the site from the ground up to make Dezeen much more stable and responsive.

Reducing the carbon impact of the website was also a key priority, so Made by On focused on performance and efficiency throughout the build, doing a lot of work on image optimisation in particular.

We also used the opportunity of the rebuild to move our hosting to The Positive Internet Company, whose server infrastructure runs on 100-per-cent-renewable energy, and moved our search and recommendation engines, which were previously run by external services, onto our own infrastructure.

Dezeen has expanded far beyond a daily news publishing platform

The new website is faster, richer and, we hope, more enjoyable to use. It also better reflects what Dezeen is today.

Astronomers have discovered the lightest known pair of neutron stars — two ultradense stellar remnants that circle each other every 2 hours, 22 minutes, veering toward an inevitable collision about 82 million years from now. By timing radio pulses from one of the stars, the researchers detected three subtle effects predicted by Albert Einstein's general theory of relativity and found that the theory describes the pair's motion to within about 1% — proving the visionary physicist right yet again.

The researchers published their findings Sept. 15 in the journal Physical Review Letters.

Neutron stars are the collapsed cores of massive stars that exploded as supernovas. Each packs more mass than the sun into a ball roughly 12 miles (20 kilometers) in diameter. Some, called pulsars, spin rapidly and sweep beams of radio waves across the sky like cosmic lighthouses. Their rotation is so steady that their pulses work as extraordinarily precise clocks.

Very few pulsars orbit another neutron star. "There are only 30 such double neutron star systems, and the relativistic effects are very strong if they are in compact orbits," study co-author JinLin Han, an astronomer at the National Astronomical Observatories of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, told Live Science via email.

The first such pair, discovered in 1974 and known as the Hulse-Taylor binary, provided the first evidence for gravitational waves — ripples in space-time predicted by Einstein. Such waves carry energy away from an orbiting pair, so its orbit should gradually shrink, and the Hulse-Taylor orbit shrank at just the rate Einstein's theory predicted.

Later, the "double pulsar," which completes an orbit every 2.45 hours, matched the predicted rate to within about 0.01%. Eventually, this shrinking forces such pairs to merge, unleashing gravitational waves like those the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory and Virgo interferometer picked up in 2017.

The new system, designated PSR J1856-0039, lies roughly 4,200 to 6,500 light-years from Earth. In 2020, it turned up in a survey of the Milky Way's disk by China's Five-hundred-meter Aperture Spherical Radio Telescope (FAST), the world's largest single-dish radio telescope.

"We have discovered about 900 pulsars," Han said of his team's work. "After a few follow-up observations, this one is very extraordinary."

The pulsar spins about 43 times per second, and the two stars are separated by roughly twice the distance between Earth and the moon. But the "most attractive feature" is the short orbital period of about 2.36 hours, Han said. "This is the second-shortest one, and it must show stronger relativistic effects than the famous double pulsar and the Hulse-Taylor system."

The team observed the pair with FAST 17 times between 2020 and 2025, sometimes long enough to cover a whole orbit. The researchers measured the arrival times of the system's pulses and then fit that data to orbital models that accounted for relativistic effects, like subtle changes to the pulse arrival times at different parts of the stars' orbits.

"With so many measurements by FAST, which would be hard to match in accuracy with other telescopes, we got not only the basic parameters of the pulsar and its orbit but also three relativistic effects," Han said.

First, the orbit is shrinking as the stars radiate gravitational waves, drawing them about 9 feet (2.7 meters) closer each year. Second, the oval orbit turns slowly, by 17.6 degrees per year — some 150,000 times faster than the relativistic drift of Mercury's orbit. Third, because both gravity and speed slow time, the pulsar's clock runs at a varying pace along the oval orbit, shifting its pulses by up to about half a millisecond.

An illustration of two neutron stars generating gravitational waves (purple) as they spiral toward each other in a tightening binary orbit. The rate of this orbital shrinkage offers a natural test of Einstein's theory or relativity.

"From these measurements, we determined the pulsar mass and the companion mass," Han explained. "The total mass is the lowest among all known double neutron star systems." The pulsar weighs about 1.3 times as much as the sun, and its companion is just 1.19 solar masses — one of the lowest-mass neutron stars known. Simulations suggest that supernovas can't forge neutron stars much lighter than that.

The stars will merge in about 82 million years. That merger will probably leave behind a heavier neutron star of about 2.26 solar masses, though the remnant could instead collapse into a black hole, the researchers estimate.

Any two of the three effects are enough to calculate both stars' masses. The third then serves as an independent check, because if general relativity is correct, all three must point to the same masses — and they do.

"We found that general relativity is needed to explain the data, and the consistency is about 1%," Han said. The double pulsar test is more precise, but it drew on 16 years of data, compared with about five for the new pair. "Unfortunately, we could not beat this description of gravity," Han added, referring to physicists' hope of finding cracks in Einstein's theory that might reveal new physics.

Next, the team hopes to detect "frame dragging," an effect in which a spinning mass twists space-time around it, making nearby orbits wobble. "In the future, we will monitor this pulsar, probably over decades, to detect the frame dragging induced by the neutron star's spin," Han said.

Measuring the effect would reveal the pulsar's moment of inertia, which depends on how its mass is spread out inside. That would provide "a novel constraint" on how matter behaves at the crushing densities in neutron star cores, Han said — one of the biggest open questions in physics.

Architecture studio Dosorozco has used a palette of blackened timber, stonework and pale plaster at Casa Sima, a Mexican home designed to echo the textures and tones of its forest surroundings.

Named Casa Sima, the 150-square-metre dwelling sits on a sloping forest site in Sierra de Tapalpa in Jalisco, with panoramic views towards mountains in the distance.

Dosorozco’s decision to avoid excavation or tree removal informed the home’s elongated plan and the placement of its blocky volumes, which instead align with the contours of an existing clearing.

“Casa Sima started with something very simple, the place already had its own order before we arrived,” the studio’s creative director Guillermo R Orozco told Dezeen.

“One of the first decisions we made was that no existing trees would be removed. That sounds simple, but it affected almost every decision that came after,” he explained.

“Their position affects the geometry of the house, the views, the shadows, the terraces and even the points where the building can touch the ground.”

Casa Sima is accessed via a stepped walkway elevated above the forest floor, which leads from the base of its sloping site to the home’s rectilinear volumes perched at the top.

Marking the transition from the forest into the home is what Orozco calls an “elevated courtyard”, comprising a timber wall supported by a steel frame, positioned above this walkway.

This timber was finished with a local technique that uses oil and heat to weatherproof its surface, giving it a rough, blackened finish intended to blend with the surrounding trees.

“There is a brief moment there where you are not really outside anymore, but you are not inside either,” Orozco explained.

“You become aware of the ground below you, the structure around and above you, and the trees beyond it,” he continued.

The living spaces of Casa Sima are housed within two intersecting, rectilinear volumes, with the ground floor given over to a large living, dining and kitchen space flanked by two stone walls.

“The house is compact, but it doesn’t really feel compact when you are inside it,” said Orozco.

“The rotation between floors, the terraces, the diagonal views and the constant relationship with the exterior make the spaces feel much more open,” he added.

#3000. Jack O’Connor Dodged Hungry Lions to Take His First Leopard

THE LITTLE four-place plane shook itself free from the runway in Nairobi and we were airborne. My third safari in Africa for OUTDOOR LIFE was underway. As we gained altitude we could see the sprawling city of Nairobi, the metropolis of East Africa—wide streets, tall new buildings, country homes, native huts.

All around us lay the mile-high Kenya highlands. We could see the snowcapped peak of Mt. Kenya with shoulders clothed in purple forest. Lower down were wooded hills, rocky outcrops, dry riverbeds white and parched. It was in October, toward the end of the long dry season, and the rolling plains were dusty and brown.

Then as we swept along 1,000 feet above the ground I saw our first game—a zebra herd in the thin shade of a group of the flat-topped acacias called thorn trees. From above they looked very small and very white.

I turned to my wife, who was sitting beside me dressed in her brand-new safari outfit of cotton khaki. “See the zebras?” I asked.

“Where?” And then she saw them. You could have knocked off her eyes with a stick. In a moment we were over a bunch of slate-gray dots that I knew were wildebeests, and a little later three giraffes, frightened by the shadow of the plane, went rocking off with their towering necks swaying.

“Golly,” said my wife. “Just like the pictures.” She tapped Syd Downey, who was sitting in front with the pilot. “It’s wonderful, Syd. We’re already seeing game and we’ve hardly started.”

“You should see a bit more,” he grinned. “We’re going to a rather nice place.”

Syd Downey, member of the firm of Ker & Downey, and dean of the East African white hunters, had guided me on my first African shoot back in 1953. Even then we talked around the campfire of the possibility of my coming back. No visitor can forget the sights and sounds of Africa—the roaring of the lions as the prides came out to hunt in the coolness of dusk, the sawing grunt of a hunting leopard in a nearby donga, the leaping impala, the great slate-gray buffalo, the zebra herds. A man’s memory holds the sight of bright close stars in the African night and the sweet, meadowy smell of the dry grass.

Since that first East African safari, I had hunted in India and Iran, in the Yukon and Alaska, and far to the west in the African continent on the fringes of the French Sahara. But always I remembered East Africa with acute nostalgia. With the increase of native population and the herds of cattle, it isn’t what it used to be before the war or even what it was when I was there in 1953. But there’s still no place like it. I know of no other hunting area in the world where one will see more game, get more interesting trophies, take better pictures, or have a more pleasant hunting trip than in Kenya and Tanganyika.

Syd and I corresponded about this and that through the years, and finally my wife and I decided to take the trip in October, 1959.

Our objectives on the trip were limited. The No. 1 trophy I was after was a leopard, something I hadn’t shot on two previous safaris. On my first trip, the game warden told me I’d save money by not getting a license for leopard. Chances were about 10 to 1 against my seeing one, he said. Those were poor odds for my $75, so I bought no leopard license. Thus I saw at least 10 leopards while I was hunting lions. About half of them offered good shots, but there was nothing I could do about it.

When I went to French Equatorial Africa in 1958, I found that my general license included a leopard. My companion, Elgin Gates, knocked over an enormous leopard. I saw not a one.

So on this third safari my principal objective was a good leopard, and Syd was taking us into a pet spot of his—a piece of unspoiled Africa where the combination of plentiful game, rocky hills for shelter, good water, and little hunting has resulted in a leopard paradise.

Also on our trophy list was a lion, a trophy that these days is one of the toughest to get in all Africa. Lions have gone down in numbers in recent years and more and more country has been closed to lion hunting. I also wanted to take another sable and to have my wife get one. And high on my list was the spiral-horned, elk-size antelope called the greater kudu.

Because the leopard range in northern Tanganyika was closed to lion hunting, we planned to fly down to southern Tanganyika for the last part of our trip, and there we’d try to knock off a lion, hunt sable, have another go at kudu, and see virgin country that’s well off the safari path.

On my previous trip I’d shot a fairly good rhino and a very good buffalo. I wanted no more buffaloes nor rhinos, and I’ve never had much yen to shoot an elephant. As a consequence, my wife and I took a rather modest battery. We shipped by air freight the old .375 Magnum Model 70 Winchester that had been remodeled and restocked by Griffin & Howe. It was equipped with a Lyman 48 receiver sight and gold bead front sight and a Redfield Bear Cub 2¾X scope on a Griffin & Howe side mount. My other rifle was a .30/06 with a Mauser action and a 22-inch barrel. The scope on it was a 2½X Lyman Alaskan. My wife’s rifle was a little 7 x 57 Mauser with a Leupold 3X scope on a Buehler mount. In addition I brought with me a 12 gauge Winchester Model 21 shotgun.

Our shotshells we purchased in Nairobi, but we shipped 40 cartridges for the .375, and 80 each for the .30/06 and the 7mm. On my first trip to Africa I took enough ammunition to last me through a small war. This time I was more modest, and at that we sent home or gave away most of the ammunition.

In our luggage we brought two Bausch & Lomb binoculars, a 9 x 35 and an 8 x 30. Our cameras were a pair of Contax IIs, a Rolleiflex, a Robot, and a Cine Kodak 16 mm. movie camera. We had a 135 mm. telephoto lens for the Contaxes and a three-inch telephoto for the movie camera.

I also shipped four pairs of khaki work pants, four cotton khaki shirts, and the top part of a suit of down underwear that I put on for warmth in the chill and dewy early mornings. My safari jacket was one I’d purchased from Norm Thompson of Portland, Oregon. In Nairobi I bought a cheap felt hat of what the British call the Terai pattern because it was designed for wear on the hot plains of northern India. I also got my boots in Nairobi—a pair of light eight-inch jobs with soft rubber soles. They were light, full-leather lined, quiet. They cost me about $20 at the African Boot Company. My wife had a safari jacket and three pairs of khaki slacks tailored at Ahmed Brothers, and a pair of boots made where I got mine. We had all the clothes we needed and no more.

Incidentally, for those planning a trip to Africa, it’s folly to ship a lot of clothes over, as they can be purchased about as cheaply in Nairobi as in the United States. A Model 70 Winchester can be bought there for the American price plus duty, and all Weaver scopes are available. American as well as British ammunition is in good supply. Film for still and movie cameras is available at the same prices prevailing in the United States. The .375 is an international cartridge, of course, and so is the 7 x 57. The .30/06 is as popular in Kenya and Tanganyika as it is here. If I ever go to East Africa again, I’ll buy all my ammunition and all my film there. The battery we took was all we needed, but if we had planned to hunt elephant, rhino, and buffalo I would have taken along a .416 Rigby or a .458 Winchester.

Our hunting camp was a little more than an hour’s flight from Nairobi. As we neared the area, the country became greener. We saw more game from the air.

Then I heard Syd say, “There’s the lorry.”

I looked down and saw a truck parked on a low ridge and white smoke trailing from a fire to show us the direction of the wind. Our little plane made a slow turn, lost altitude, and then bumped along the ground to a stop.

We were met by R. E. (Steve) Stephens, business manager of Ker & Downey, and by Dave Williams, who had driven in with the hunting car and the big lorry (truck) and set up camp. The dining tent, tents for the crew, and sleeping tents for my wife and me and for Syd had been pitched at the foot of a little rocky hill, called a kopje and pronounced “copy” in East Africa.

My wife has made many a hunting trip with me, has cooked many a meal in frying pans and Dutch ovens, and she was astounded at the plush accommodations. In our tent the beds were made with clean white sheets. Our tin trunks had been unpacked, our rifles laid out. Soap, hot water, and towels were handy.

In the dining tent, a kerosene refrigerator produced cold drinks, the table was set with silver and linen, and two messboys stood by in long white gowns and green jackets to serve lunch. In little more than an hour the plane was enroute back to Nairobi with Steve and Dave.

“Let’s have a nap now,” Syd suggested. “We’ll start the serious business of leopard hunting a bit later.”

My wife Eleanor was so excited she couldn’t sleep, though an afternoon nap is one of her favorite indoor sports. Instead she sat in a chair at the door of the tent with binoculars to look over the green meadows below. “Look,” she’d call out every few minutes. “I see a herd of zebras. Jack, there are some giraffes. Oh, come here and tell me what all these funny antelope are.”

Once a herd of zebras came wandering by not over 100 yards away. They saw the tents and went thundering off in a cloud of dust. Always we had only to look out over the green plain to see game.

Leopard hunting is at once an art and a science, and Syd Downey is one of Africa’s great leopard hunters. I’m sure he can even think like a leopard. In Asia, leopards are generally baited with tethered live goats or sometimes with dogs. A young goat is tied up in an area known to be inhabited by a leopard. The hunter waits in a machan (a platform in a tree) or in a brush blind. When the leopard shows up, he lets him have it.

In East Africa a dead bait in a tree is almost always used. Because there are so many hyenas in Africa, a leopard always hangs his kill in a tree to keep it away from those strong-jawed scavengers. Actually a leopard seems to like this meat ripe (just as many people like cheese that’s pretty far gone) and the decaying carcass of an antelope must smell to a leopard like a charcoal-broiled steak to an American.

The usual practice is to shoot a bait, hang it in a tree, then come back morning and evening to see if a leopard is working on it. It sounds simple, but it isn’t. About three fourths of getting a leopard consists of picking out the right tree in the right spot. The tree should be near likely leopard country, usually a rocky hill where a leopard would like to loaf during the day. The ideal tree should have a branch suitable for the leopard to lie on and feed. It should have enough foliage to make a leopard feel secure but not enough to deflect the bullet. In addition, the tree should be near some place of concealment such as brush or rocks so the hunter can lie in wait. To complicate matters further, the shooting spot should be so located that the prevailing wind won’t carry the hunter’s scent to the leopard along the route by which it’s likely to approach.

Finding a good leopard tree is no easy matter. If the tree is right, there’s no place to shoot from. If there’s a place to shoot from, the wind may be wrong. A good tree near an excellent shooting blind may be an unlikely spot because no leopard lair is near.

All this Syd told Eleanor and me as we cruised around looking for the right spot.

Finally Syd stopped the Land Rover. “Here,” he said, “is a good spot and we’ll put up a bait.”

It was a big tree close to a series of low, rocky hills. There was water in the donga (stream bed) that ran along the foot of the rocks, and thick brush nearby. When we got out to scout around, we found leopard and lion tracks in the sand.

“And now,” Syd asked, “who will shoot the bait?”

“Mamma, grab your rifle and hitch up your girdle. You’re the meat hunter today,” I said.

“Better take the .30/06,” Syd said. “Zebras are rather tough creatures and sometimes hard to kill.”

We cruised around in the hunting car and finally Syd spotted a zebra herd. Some trees and brush downwind would make stalking easy. I sat in the car with Bitu, Syd’s gunbearer of many years whom I’d known on my previous trip, while Syd and Eleanor sneaked off. Presently a shot crashed there in the scrub and I heard the sound of a bullet striking.

I found Syd and Eleanor by a zebra stallion. Syd was smiling and Eleanor wore a slightly mixed expression. “Good shooting,” Syd said.

“I feel silly shooting one of these circus animals,” Eleanor told me. “I can’t help thinking a ringmaster is going to show up and be awfully mad about it.”

We cut the zebra in two and hoisted one half into a big tree with a good horizontal branch from which a leopard would enjoy feeding. Then we took the other half and hung it in another tree about a mile up the donga. The prevailing wind would blow our scent past any leopard that would approach from a rock hill about 100 yards away, and about 60 yards on the other side of the bait was a rock we could hide behind. We got results in short order, and if I were writing fiction instead of fact I couldn’t invent a crazier experience than the one I’m about to relate. The next morning we drove across the beautiful green plain in the opposite direction and shot a kongoni for bait. We then hung it in a perfect location on the edge of a wide donga, a spot where the wind was right and where there was a thick patch of brush to hide in. Then that afternoon we went over to see if a leopard had been on the first bait. As we stopped the hunting car on the far side of a rise, we got out with our binoculars.

“There he is, right in the tree,” Syd whispered.

The leopard was about 10 or 12 feet above the bait, lying on a limb and fairly well shielded by the leaves. It wouldn’t have been much of a task to sneak up to within 100 yards or so and pot him.

“It’s a male,” Syd said judiciously, “and a rather good one.”

“But look at the lion right at the foot of the tree,” Eleanor said.

And there was a lion there, squatting right at the foot of the tree staring hungrily up at the bait. No wonder the leopard hadn’t come down. “What a shame,” Syd said. “It wouldn’t be sporting to shoot a leopard under these circumstances.”

“To say nothing about having the lion get excited and chew your leg off,” Eleanor put in.

“We’ll have to get rid of that lion,” Syd said bitterly.

So we got into the hunting car, drove up to the tree. The leopard stared at us nervously. The lion watched us with uncomprehending eyes. He hadn’t the faintest idea what the hunting car was. This area has been closed to lion hunting in recent years, and this young lion saw no particular menace in our outfit.

Syd put his head out of the window. “Shoo!” he said. “Go away. Get out.” The lion, a handsome young male with a yellow mane, backed off about 10 feet and then stood his ground. “Go on!” said Syd again.

But the lion refused to budge. Syd jumped out of the car, rushed at the lion, waving his arms.

The startled lion ran off into the rocks. The leopard now saw its chance. It jumped from the tree and fled. “Well, Syd,” said Eleanor, “you have chased off that silly lion.”

But he hadn’t. A moment later we saw the lion’s shaggy head peering at us over a rock. He plainly had thought the matter over. For the life of him, he couldn’t figure out just what authority Syd had to run him away from the delicious smell of decomposing zebra.

Again Syd ran at him and again he fled. But there was no getting rid of that hungry lion. In a couple of minutes his head popped up at another spot.

“Oh, dear,” said Syd. “We’ll have to pull him off with a bait.”

We drove about a mile and spotted a herd of Grant’s gazelles. I shot one and we took it back to our bait tree. Once more the lion was sitting under it.

We drove past dragging the carcass of the Grant. For a moment the lion stared at it as if he couldn’t believe his eyes. Then he realized that it was a gift from us to him. What looked like a smile of joy spread over his face. He began walking hungrily toward the dragging Grant. Syd speeded up the car and the lion began to trot. Syd drove faster and the lion began to gallop. Growling ferociously he made a rush and grabbed the bait. The gunbearer in the back of the car let go of the rope.

“Now,” said Syd. “We’ve got rid of that lion. He was hungry, but when he’s fed he’ll move off.”

But we underestimated our lion. Apparently he spread the word that there were some suckers about who enjoyed feeding lions. When we went back toward dusk the next afternoon, we saw no leopard in the tree, but we did see our little pal and two more lions—a larger male and a sleek, full-grown female. Fifty yards away in the rocks was another big lion and a lioness.

“Oh, no!” said Syd. “We must go into this feeding routine again.”

“I think they’re cute,” Eleanor said. This time we shot a kongoni to lure the big cats away. The young lion trailed our drag joyfully. The others, who may have been shot at in times past, followed slowly and cautiously. But before long we saw them all congregate on the kongoni, growling and snarling as they fed. The next day they were all back again, and of course the leopard had not come near the tree. We decided we’d lost.

What with one thing and another, we were having a tough time. The lions loused up one hot prospect and the Masai natives another. A big leopard was coming to the bait in the donga across the plain, but the Masai moved in some cattle and drove them right up the donga past the tree in early morning and late afternoon, exactly the time when one would expect a leopard to feed. Although we waited several times until dark, the leopard never showed up.

We had tough luck on another bait, an impala this time. A leopard came to it, but he was an unorthodox fellow who lived in a spot where by all leopard rules he shouldn’t live, and he could get our wind as he came to feed. Once we caught just a brief glimpse of him.

The country was lousy with leopards. One night a young one that lived in the rocks of the kopje right by our camp stole a Thomson’s gazelle hanging in a tree a few feet from where the cook was sleeping. On several occasions we saw leopards galloping home from their night’s hunting. Another time, when we drove 15 or 20 miles from camp to look over some new country, we saw a herd of topi intently looking toward the top of a hill. We got our binoculars and saw a big male leopard beautiful in black and gold sitting on a ledge of red stone, looking down on the plain. We ran across several cheetahs—those round-headed, long-legged cats that are supposed to be the fastest runners on earth. They can catch the swiftest antelope in a short race.

We saw more lions. Once we put a bait in a tree only to find when we came back the next morning that a lioness had managed to climb up, break the rope that held it, and devour it. On another bait we had about the same luck as on the first one. A couple of lions that lived in a nearby donga hung around it and kept the leopards away. We finally gave up, cut the bait down, and let the lions feed.

Then we got what should have been a break but wasn’t.

One morning we found that during the night a large leopard had come to our second bait. About 4 o’clock that afternoon Syd and I got behind the rock we’d picked out and waited.

Safari life is tough on rifles. The constant jiggling as they sit in the rack loosens scope and guard screws, and scopes are apt to get bumped. Knowing this, I had shot my .30/06 at a mark every day. At noon, I’d have one of the gunbearers make a mark on a tree with a panga and then at 100 yards I’d take a rest over the hood of the Land Rover and fire a shot. Always, the bullet had struck just right. But that day, lulled by the fact that the rifle had not changed point of impact, I neglected to do this.

Syd and I must have waited about an hour when the leopard came stealthily down out of the rocks, all black and gold and gliding smooth as a shadow. He stopped and looked around suspiciously. For about 15 minutes he sat on a rock, watching. Then he moved down toward the bait tree and lay down on an ant hill.

He was about 60 yards away, broadside but looking away from me back toward the rocks. It was a set-shot. I eased myself up, placed my left hand on the rock to steady the fore-end of the rifle. The ant hill was slightly downhill from me. Since the rifle was sighted in to strike three inches high with the 180-grain bullet at 100 yards, it should have been shooting a bit over one inch high at 60 yards. I held the intersection of the crosswires behind the leopard’s shoulder and very slightly low. The rifle was rock steady as I squeezed the trigger.

As the rifle cracked, the leopard shot about four feet into the air, roaring and growling, and when he lit he took off for the rocks just like a racehorse.

I couldn’t believe my eyes and neither could Syd.

“What have you done?” asked Syd. I had visions of a wounded leopard hiding in the rocks and our having a sticky experience in following him up. A wounded leopard is one of the world’s most dangerous animals. But investigation showed that my bullet had only grazed the leopard. We found where it had struck the ground and we found a bit of hair that had been cut off. There was not a single drop of blood. I thanked my lucky stars that I’d held a shade low. Otherwise I’d have wounded the leopard slightly and we would have been in a peck of trouble.

“Your rifle must be off,” Syd said gloomily as we got in the hunting car. “Let’s try it,” I said.

On the way back to camp we paced off exactly 60 yards, made a mark on a tree. Syd and I both shot it, and our bullets were right together but about four inches low. On the eyepiece of the scope there was a bright abraded spot as if the rifle had been dropped and it had struck with the weight on the scope. I hadn’t dropped it. The gunbearers said they hadn’t when they were cleaning it. It must have been mice, or something. So we got Old Betsy shooting three inches high at 100 yards again and sorrowfully went back to camp to tell Eleanor that I’d missed a leopard at 60 yards.

“Don’t write this up,” she advised. “All the people who read OUTDOOR LIFE will think you’re an awful boob.”

“I call them where they fall, honey,” I said, “and I have the rare distinction of being one of the few gun writers who ever misses anything with a typewriter.”

We took another round of the leopard baits the next morning. Leopards had been feeding on three of them. We saw one, but found it was a female—and Syd Downey’s dudes don’t shoot female leopards. Another was the big cat that fed only at night and the third, judging by tracks and claw marks, was small.

“Not much chance at the tree where you missed the leopard last night,” Syd said gloomily as we headed back to camp. “That’s one leopard that will never be shot over a bait. He’s learned his lesson. But anyway we’re close by and it won’t hurt to stop and look.” When we were about 200 yards from the bait, Syd stopped the car and lifted his binoculars.

“Well, what do you think?” he said after a moment. “A leopard has fed since last night. Let’s come back this afternoon and wait.”

About 4 o’clock Syd and I were behind our rock and Eleanor was waiting with two gunbearers in the hunting car about half a mile away. A patrol of flies were buzzing over the bait, which by this time was very high, and a couple of vultures were sitting in the top of the tree.

The wind blew gently down the donga, and over in a tree about 200 yards away we could see some monkeys playing.

Gradually the sun slid toward the west. Then it dropped below the horizon and the light began to go.

I had about given up hope, when Syd punched me.

“The leopard’s here,” he whispered. “He’s on the bait and he’s a very large male. Don’t look now. He’s facing this way.”

For five minutes we waited and then Syd took another cautious peek around the rock.

“Now,” he whispered. “You have plenty of time.”

When I raised up from behind the rock the leopard was feeding on the bait, standing on the limb above it and tearing off chunks of meat. However, his rear end was toward me, so I waited for a better shot.

I had my .375 that day, a lucky rifle with which I had shot a tiger, a couple of lions, and a pair of Alaska brown bears, as well as other game. I watched the leopard, ready to duck down if he started to turn my way.

Then he shifted position so that his body was quartering. I saw that if I held back of the ribs on the left side the bullet would go up through his lungs and should break his right shoulder.

I hoisted the old .375 up and rested the fore-end over my left hand.

The crosswires were steady right where I wanted them. Slowly I started the squeeze. When the rifle bellowed, the leopard pitched out of the tree and fell to the ground like a bag of potatoes. Syd and I approached cautiously but he never even twitched. “Well done,” Syd said. “Whoopee!” I shouted.

We heard the Land Rover start up and presently Eleanor drove up with the two gunbearers.

We all admired the leopard. It was a big male, chunky and powerful. The 300-grain Silvertip had hit exactly right, had gone through the lungs and shattered the right shoulder. Luckily it had shed the core and had not gone through to tear up the hide.

As we put the leopard in the car, Syd gave me a sidewise look.

“I don’t think I need to tell you this,” he said, “but you’re a very lucky man.”
