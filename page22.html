New York City: It’s not quite the seismic “October surprise”, but the final week of the US midterm election has been partially consumed by a debate about a restaurant chain – The Cheesecake Factory.

It all began when Barack Obama, stumping for the Democrats at a rally in Cleveland, Ohio, mocked President Donald Trump’s extensive renovation and redecoration of the White House.

“There’s gold everywhere,” Obama observed. “What’s up with the gold? He’s got the White House looking like Saddam Hussein opened a Cheesecake Factory.”

Trump was triggered. “The White House … is a symbol of America,” he told reporters during a lengthy rant. “It has to be clean. The columns can’t be broken. The bricks can’t be falling off from under the plaster. And for you [Obama] to say it’s a Cheesecake Factory, that just shows why you were a lousy president.”

Senior fellow at the US Studies Centre and former political staffer

Moreover, though, Obama’s jibe led Republicans to proclaim that he and the Democrats were elitist and out of touch. Here he was, they argued, mocking a restaurant chain that is enormously popular with middle America – and at the pointy end of an election campaign.

The Cheesecake Factory is a market-leading operation, with some 370 restaurants across the US (not all under the same brand). It’s renowned for its vast menu: you can order pretty much anything – pastas, steaks, burgers, salads and breakfast items – and, of course, multiple varieties of cheesecake.

It’s also a family favourite – not a cheap night out for mum, dad and the kids, but affordable for a regular treat or special occasion. Most mains are under $US30 ($42.50) – a sandwich with fries and a side salad is $US21 or so.

The Trump administration has responded to Obama’s joke with full force. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had lunch there on Tuesday, with Fox News’ cameras in tow. He ordered the French dip cheeseburger – his usual, he claimed.

“I had a great meal, always have a great meal, and I would tell the Martha’s Vineyard set to give it a try,” Bessent added, referring to the island synonymous with celebrity retreats.

I felt compelled to see what all the fuss was about, so I headed to the only Cheesecake Factory in New York City, in Queens about 25 minutes by subway from Manhattan.

The restaurant is on the ground floor of a shopping mall, and at lunchtime on a Wednesday, the cavernous room is scattered with friends catching up over burgers and shakes, two solo diners at the bar, and four men near me speaking German. Black, white, Latino – it’s a kaleidoscope of Queens.

The menu – sometimes called “the Bible” – runs to 22 pages, including drinks, plus a separate “skinnylicious” menu with lower-calorie choices, from which I select the grilled steak medallions ($US28.95, 440 calories) and a little house salad ($US9.50, 260 calories).

My server says the restaurant has been busy, but not because of the ruckus between Obama and Trump. Indeed, he hasn’t heard of the kerfuffle, and neither have any patrons. Rather, people are here to try the Cajun salmon, which recently had a viral moment on social media.

“What Obama said was funny and accurate,” says patron Dakota Rylander. She doesn’t buy Trump’s indignation. “That’s just what he does – he tries to distract from what he’s doing by pointing the finger at other people.”

Her friend Faith is more interested in how the restaurant apparently short-changed her on a slice of cheesecake that was meant to be half price for rewards members.

I receive my free bread, with a slab of butter and an unknown number of calories, and the salad, which is fresh (a rarity in the US) and plentiful. The beef medallions come with lots of asparagus, mushrooms, mash and a delicious Madeira wine sauce. Honestly, I can’t fault the meal.

The service is also friendly without being over-the-top in that American way that can sometimes get annoying.

As for the decor, I don’t find it tacky. The lighting, wood panelling and furniture blend orange, red and sandy yellow hues, rather than the overt gold of Trump’s Oval Office trinkets.

I don’t think Obama was really dissing The Cheesecake Factory, and I think Trump quite famously can’t take a joke at his own expense. It’s likely neither of them has been to a Cheesecake Factory in some time. I couldn’t recommend it to them highly enough.

#202. Spain begins mourning period as search continues for train crash victims

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Emergency services, drones and dog unit continue to comb carriages as Civil Guard sets up clinics for relatives to provide DNA samples

How did the Spain train crash happen and what will investigators be asking?

Spain began three days of mourning on Thursday for at least 69 people who died in a high-speed rail collision, as hospitals continued to treat dozens of injured passengers and train drivers went on strike to urge more investment in railway safety.

The trains – one from Spain’s state-owned Renfe and the other from the private rail company Iryo – were travelling from Madrid towards the southern region of Andalucía on Wednesday when they collided just before 9am. After the Iryo train came to a halt, the other slammed into its rear, knocking several carriages off the track near Urda in the central region of Castilla-La Mancha.

Spain’s foreign ministry said 42 foreign citizens from seven countries were involved in the crash, adding that one had been killed and at least seven injured.

The crash came 10 months after 46 people were killed and dozens more injured when two trains – one from Renfe and one from Iryo – collided on the same line near the town of Adamuz in Andalucía.

The disaster has raised fresh questions about safety on Spain’s large, modern high-speed rail network. The transport minister, Óscar Puente, said rear-end collisions such as Wednesday’s were “highly unlikely”, and suggested the accident could have been caused by human error under “inexplicable” circumstances.

Emergency services combed the wreckage through the night for more victims, using cranes to hoist pieces of mangled metal. At daybreak, police once again launched drones to survey the crash site.

Emiliano García-Page, the leader of the Castilla-La Mancha regional government, said on Thursday morning that rescue teams did not think they would find any more bodies in one damaged carriage that was ‌still overturned, but added that body parts had been found that would need to be identified.

“Yesterday we experienced a catastrophe that without a doubt has left all of Spain in shock,” said García-Page. He confirmed that 50 of the people who had been taken to hospitals in the region were still being treated.

The prime minister, Pedro Sánchez, speaking during a visit to the emergency command centre in the city of Toledo, said the authorities were doing all they could to establish the cause of the crash and prevent another rail tragedy.

“I’d like to let all Spaniards know that every institution that is part of the state – from the smallest municipality to the government of Spain – is working day and night, not only to provide a response to the families of the victims, but also, of course, to uncover every last detail of this tragedy and to strengthen safety measures so that something like this never happens again.”

King Felipe, who visited a hospital in Toledo where some of the injured were being treated, said Spain had “once again suffered an extremely harsh blow” and called for lessons to be learned.

“There is obviously an ongoing investigation, which should establish how and why this tragedy happened,” he said on Thursday. “Measures will need to be looked at and adopted so that this doesn’t happen again.”

Meanwhile, Spanish train drivers went ahead with a previously planned strike to press for more investment in railway safety. The crash was Europe’s deadliest train accident since a derailment in Santiago de Compostela in north-west Spain that killed 80 people in 2013.

“We will continue demanding the necessary measures to make this sector safe again ... It must have all the safety systems required to maintain the quality standards we have always had,” said Diego Martín of the train drivers’ union Semaf. “We cannot tolerate, under any circumstances, this type of incident happening again.”

The Guardia Civil police force has set up four offices in the cities of Madrid, Toledo, Córdoba and Seville to enable relatives to provide DNA samples and help identify victims. Blood donation points have also been set up in Castilla-La Mancha and Madrid. The force said 64 people had been reported missing by relatives by Thursday morning.

A school in Urda, which lies about 130km (80 miles) south of Madrid, has been turned into an information centre where relatives can inquire about loved ones. Teams of Red Cross workers offered support to groups of people who had arrived, some of them in tears.

Carmen García, a Red Cross psychologist, said “long and hard hours” lay ahead and that “every person will manage it in their own way”. She added: “We have people who are in shock, who haven’t taken on board what has happened, angry people, sad, worried people. The grieving will be complicated.”

José Miguel Fernández, a parish priest in Urda, joined the emergency services in offering support to the victims’ families.

“It is important for relatives to find their loved ones, even if they have died, because the grieving process is quicker when you have been able to say goodbye at that final moment,” he told Onda Cero radio.

Survivors have spoken of being thrown from their seats by the impact of the crash, and of trying to help the injured in the immediate aftermath.

Among the first victims to be named was José Manuel Chacón, a 31-year-old Guardia Civil officer. The national law enforcement agency expressed its “deepest condolences” to his family, friends and colleagues.

Also among those killed was Fátima Báñez, a former labour minister for the conservative People’s party (PP), who had gone on to chair the social and corporate responsibility arm of Spain’s ⁠employers’ association.

The PP leader, Alberto Núñez Feijóo, called for an immediate investigation into what had happened, as well as a “complete and independent review” of Spain’s entire rail network. “Spain has the right to know what is going on with its infrastructure,” he told reporters.

Pope Leo’s visit to Argentina comes amid allegations of sexual abuse and other crimes involving Catholic clergy and lay members, with survivors demanding stronger action from the Vatican against those accused or convicted.

Victims have expressed frustration over the Vatican’s silence on church proceedings involving priests convicted in criminal courts. They are demanding that the clergy be defrocked, the Catholic Church’s most severe disciplinary punishment.

The papal visit, initially scheduled for November 8-11, has stirred mixed feelings. Some survivors hope to meet Pope to share their experiences and demand action, while others, including members of a network of clergy abuse survivors, oppose his visit, saying the church has failed to deliver on its promises of “zero tolerance” for abuse. Here are some of the major scandals involving the Catholic Church in Argentina:

Pope is scheduled to visit Córdoba province, where priest Alejandro Nicola faces allegations of sexually abusing children at a Catholic school’s kindergarten, mostly in 2025. More than a dozen families have filed complaints. Prosecutors are investigating, while the local church has opened a preliminary inquiry.

Priest Nicola was seen celebrating Mass at Córdoba’s cathedral in July despite the allegations, angering families of the alleged victims. The Pope is expected to visit the cathedral during his trip. Florencia Navarro and Fernanda Agüero, who accuse Priest Nicola of abusing their daughters, criticised church and school authorities for their handling of the allegations.

“I would like him to provide explanations, since they talk so much about protecting the vulnerable,” Mr. Navarro said of the pope. Ms. Agüero said she hoped the Pope’s message would be backed by “concrete actions” rather than words alone.

Cordoba Archbishop Ángel Rossi invoked the priest’s presumption of innocence when questioned about the case. Priest Nicola has not addressed the allegations, telling journalists only that “the lawyers will take care of that.” His defence team has not commented publicly on the case.

A group of 43 women who allege they were subjected to human trafficking and forced labor at Opus Dei centres in Argentina hope to meet the pope in Buenos Aires.

The allegations implicate senior members of the conservative Catholic organisation, including its global leader, Fernando Ocáriz. Three former Opus Dei vicars in South America and a former regional secretary of its women’s section have been formally accused in the investigation, along with Mariano Fazio, the organisation’s second-highest-ranking official.

Prosecutors have requested that the defendants be summoned for questioning, but the judge has not ordered it. They are also investigating claims that the women were recruited as teenagers under false promises and forced to work 12 to 16 hours a day as domestic servants without pay, under conditions of isolation and strict control, between the 1970s and 2015.

Their lawyer, Sebastián Sal, is seeking a meeting with the Pope and has requested that Mr. Ocáriz also face charges. The women want Pope to intervene in Opus Dei and demand a public apology from its leaders. They say the Vatican has not responded to a formal complaint filed in 2021.

“We were just little girls when we entered. They will never give us back the dignity they stole from us,” said Andrea Martínez, 52, who joined an Opus Dei centre in Buenos Aires as a teenager. Another complainant, Rosana Vaccon, said she was isolated from her family and never received letters from her mother informing her of her father’s serious illness.

Opus Dei authorities in Argentina have categorically denied the allegations, which carry potential prison sentences. One of Argentina’s most notorious clergy abuse scandals involved dozens of deaf children at the Antonio Próvolo Institute in Mendoza between 2005 and 2016.

In September, a court sentenced former nun Kumiko Kosaka to 20 years in prison for sexually abusing five students. Two priests received lengthy prison sentences in 2019: Horacio Corbacho was sentenced to 45 years and Italian priest Nicola Corradi to 42 years. Corradi later died while under house arrest.

Survivors have criticised the Vatican for failing to publicly announce the outcome of canonical proceedings against Corbacho, which began in 2017. A Vatican-appointed judicial vicar had recommended that both priests be dismissed from the clergy.

Survivors’ groups are also demanding the removal of clerical status from several high-profile priests convicted of serious crimes, including Christian Von Wernich, Julio Grassi and former Bishop Gustavo Zanchetta.

Von Wernich, a former police chaplain, was sentenced to life in prison in 2007 for his role in seven murders and dozens of kidnappings and torture cases during Argentina’s 1976-1983 military dictatorship. Argentina’s bishops announced a church investigation that year, but no canonical sanction has been made public.

Grassi, once a prominent figure who ran homes for orphaned children, is serving a 15-year sentence for sexually abusing a minor. Survivors say the Vatican has not announced a decision on his clerical status despite receiving a formal report from the Diocese of Morón. Francis, who at the time was still Cardinal Jorge Mario Bergoglio and the archbishop of Buenos Aires, in 2010 commissioned a four-volume, 2,000-plus-page forensic study of Grassi’s case that concluded he was innocent, that his victims were lying and that the case never should have gone to trial.

Zanchetta, a former bishop close to Pope Francis, was sentenced in 2022 to 4 1/2 years in prison for sexually abusing two seminarians. He was granted parole in 2025, prompting renewed demands that he be defrocked. Pope Francis had initially rejected the accusations against Zanchetta and created a job for him at the Vatican that got him out of Argentina. The late Pope eventually authorised a canonical criminal proceeding against Zanchetta in 2019, but the Vatican has not publicly announced a ruling.

The Vatican did not respond to an Associated Press request for comment on the canonical status of the convicted clergy. The Argentine dioceses responsible for overseeing their cases also did not respond.

The Vatican tends to not initiate canonical trials until all criminal appeals are exhausted, to not prejudge the outcomes, though it does recommend that bishops take precautionary measures during investigations such as removing priests from active Ministry.

#673. Egyptian man who fire-bombed pro-Israel rally in Colorado pleads guilty to federal hate crimes

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A backpack features patches combining Israeli and United States flags during the 30th annual Boulder Jewish Festival, a week after an attack that injured multiple people in Boulder, Colorado, U.S. June 8, 2025. REUTERS/Mark Makela

DENVER, Oct 29 - The man who lobbed gasoline bombs at a pro-Israel rally in Colorado last year, injuring several people including a woman who later died, pleaded guilty on Thursday to federal hate-crime charges, the US Department of Justice said.

Mohamed Sabry Soliman, 47, an Egyptian national, is already serving a life sentence for his guilty plea in May to more than 100 state charges, including first-degree murder.

Soliman pleaded guilty to nine counts of hate-crime offenses and three explosives charges in federal court in Denver, according to the DOJ. The charges are punishable by life in prison after federal prosecutors declined to seek the death penalty.

The plea deal asks that Soliman be sentenced to life in prison plus 50 years. Soliman will be sentenced on January 7.

"The defendant admitted that he committed the attack because of the actual or perceived Jewish religion and Israeli national origin of the group he targeted," the DOJ said in a statement.

David Kraut, a federal public defender listed as Soliman's lawyer, did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Soliman has admitted tossing two Molotov cocktails at people taking part in a rally in downtown Boulder on June 1, 2025, held in solidarity with hostages seized by Hamas militants who stormed Israeli villages from the Palestinian enclave of Gaza on October 7, 2023.

Prosecutors said Soliman yelled "Free Palestine" as the gasoline bombs he lobbed at the crowd burst into flames outside a municipal courthouse in the heart of Boulder's popular Pearl Street shopping district.

Admitting his guilt in state court nearly six months ago, Soliman denounced his own crimes as contrary to "the teachings of Islam" and insisted he bore no ill will toward Jewish people. He went on to lament the "thousands of families who have lost their families in Palestine and Gaza," and said the "enemy is Zionism."

Authorities in Colorado identified 29 victims from the attack, including 14 who were burned or injured while fleeing and 15 who were close enough to be considered targets of attempted murder. One victim, 82-year-old Karen Diamond, died of her injuries later that month. Her husband suffered severe burns and survived.

The firebombing came amid heightened tensions over the 2023 Hamas raid and Israel's military assault in Gaza, which sparked a surge in anti-Jewish and anti-Muslim hate crimes across the US and a wave of pro-Palestinian protests that many Israel supporters branded as antisemitic. Pro-Palestinian protesters reject accusations their support of Gazans amounts to antisemitism.

According to affidavits filed in court by prosecutors, Soliman told investigators after his arrest that he had planned his attack for a year, but delayed going through with it until after his daughter had graduated from high school.

Soliman, who had lived with his wife and five children in Colorado Springs, also has asserted that his family members were unaware of his intentions beforehand.

The family was taken into immigration custody in June 2025 after his arrest and transported to a detention facility in Texas. They were held until a court-ordered release on April 23, 2026, more than 10 months later.

The children and their mother, who subsequently divorced Soliman, were rearrested on April 25 of this year just after returning to Colorado. They were freed again when attorneys intervened a day later, according to court documents.

Court records entered at about the time that Soliman reached a plea deal with federal prosecutors earlier this month showed that he had withdrawn motions seeking to preclude any further attempts at removing his family. REUTERS

#60. Brazil’s victory for the planet and democracy: seven lessons from Lula’s win

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The trouncing of the far right provides a playbook for social and environmental movements in other countries facing elections, including the US, Spain and France

Thank you, Brazil, for demonstrating how the far right can be trounced while protection for the Amazon is strengthened. In a world where nature and democracy appeared to be bleeding out in tandem, voters and forest defenders in this global south powerhouse have shown it is possible to staunch the flow. This should provide much needed inspiration and, more practically, a playbook for social and environmental movements in other countries facing elections, including the US, Spain and France.

The astonishing comeback victory of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in Sunday’s presidential runoff against his extreme right challenger Flavío Bolsonaro has brought oceans of rapturous supporters on to the streets, while also thrilling conservationists, climate activists and social justice campaigners across the world.

Coming just weeks after the Lula administration announced a record fall in Amazon and Cerrado destruction, they hope Brazil can now go on to achieve its target of zero deforestation by 2030 and continue to support a global transition away from fossil fuels. That would provide a huge boost to flagging global efforts to stabilise the climate and protect degraded ecosystems. It would also set a remarkable example of leadership by the global south.

Big battles lie ahead. In the Amazon region itself, far-right members of Bolsonaro’s party have won the governorships of seven of the nine states and a large number of municipal mayorships. Brazil’s congress is now dominated by agribusiness and mining interests that have set the political agenda in recent years, and will push hard to allow more extractivist industries in indigenous lands, expand road-building, privatise rivers, weaken environmental protections and possibly attempt to impeach the president.

In the regional sphere, Lula, alone among the leaders of main South American nations in not aligning with Donald Trump’s so-called Shield of the Americas, will face pressure from Washington to channel critical minerals and other natural resources to the US, rather than selling to China or Europe, or leaving them in the ground. And, on the planetary level, Brazil faces the threat of accelerating global heating, which is pushing the Amazon ever closer to the point of no return.

So although Sunday’s result will hopefully provide a little breathing space for the Amazon and other crucially important biomes, the joy and hope that Brazil has gifted the world will quickly fade unless it inspires political change in other countries. In that sense, the biggest environmental impact of Lula’s victory may be the lessons it provides for campaigners elsewhere. Here are some initial takeaways:

1 Politics matter. There is no higher environmental priority than defeating the far right, which is a Trojan horse for extractivist – and often criminal – groups to capture states and erode protections for people, democracy and the environment. Just look at the 59.5% surge of Amazon deforestation under the 2019-2022 term of Jair Bolsonaro – the highest level of any four-year presidency in Brazil. Compare that with the 71.2% fall during the Lula administration. Over the past year, forest loss was 3,335 sq km, the lowest level since the start of satellite measurements in 1988. And Lula is not, by any means, an environmentalist. He has approved several destructive projects himself, but he is a people person who listens to the voice of social movements.

2 Don’t give up. Lula was largely written off by the markets and some polls after his first-round defeat. No first-round loser had ever previously come back to win the runoff. Lula was able to do so by securing an extra 10m votes over three weeks. The energy for this came from the streets and the forests. Social movements organised giant rallies in cities. In the Amazon, indigenous, riverine and quilombola groups travelled by hours – sometimes days – to reach polling stations by boats or buses.

3 Mobilise a campaign army. The first round of Lula’s campaign had been stodgy, old-fashioned and defensive – three traits that are also often applicable to environmental campaigns. This was transformed during the runoff, when federal deputy André Janones launched a “Lula’s Army” WhatsApp group that used humorous memes, live streams and algorithmic outreach to seize digital territory that had long been dominated by the right. By voting day, this army had 10m “soldiers”, about 8% of the electorate. Volunteers devoted hours posting stories and spreading messages across social media, speeding up the Lula campaign’s response time and emotional impact. Everyone was instructed not to share or respond to posts by the opposition camp.

4 Go on the offensive against US tech giants. Lula’s campaign accused Meta, which runs the Facebook, WhatsApp and Instagram social media platforms, of political favouritism towards Bolsonaro in its algorithms and AI tools. Meta denied bias but acknowledged technical failures. Brazil’s electoral court ordered corrective action. Among the most popular chants of Lula supporters was “Hey Meta, vai tomar no cu!” (Hey Meta, take it up the arse!). Many on the left have blamed US social media platforms for spreading rightwing disinformation and fearmongering, causing recent electoral defeats in Colombia, Chile and Peru. Lula made clear that would not be allowed in Brazil: “We had to overcome foreign interference through digital platforms, which we will have to regulate. We cannot have a monstrously large corporation earning millions and billions paying no taxes to this country and trying to steer our election results.”

5 Reclaim the flag. Brazil’s green, yellow and blue national colours had been associated with Bolsonaro’s movement in previous elections. This time, Lula seized it back by posing in a national football team strip while his “army” launched a campaign called “The green and yellow is ours”. Before voting day, millions of people across Brazil and the world – from Indigenous leaders in the Amazon to Hollywood celebrities and European football stars – flooded social networks with images of themselves in Brazil colours making an L (for Lula) sign with their thumb and little finger extended.

6 Increase social spending. Amazon protection may have galvanised conservationists, but the far greater motivations for many Lula voters were record low unemployment, robust wage growth, a 15% increase in “bolsa familia” poverty relief payments and a promise to raise the minimum wage. The importance of combining social justice with climate and nature action is widely recognised by Brazilian environmental groups. Without it, elections are lost and protests can erupt in the streets – as the world learned from France during the violent gilets jaunes (yellow vests) demonstrations of 2018.

7 Defend sovereignty, democracy and sustainability. Lula and his army called out the interlinked threats posed by the Bolsonaro family’s fealty to Donald Trump (now doubly confirmed by Flávio’s post-election flight to the US). They highlighted the interlinked threats posed by the obsequience to Trump, Bolsonaro’s desire to change the constitution and his promise to open up exploration of the Amazon and other biomes. This reflects a global phenomenon. Now that seven of the planetary boundaries essential for the flourishing of life have been breached, more and more people are affected by business activities that make the situation worse by destabilising the climate, damaging ecosystems and polluting the air, soil and rivers. This makes it harder for extractive companies to secure a social mandate so they become more inclined to either break the law or support extreme politics that override democracy.

The former environment minister Marina Silva pulled these threads together during the victory celebrations, saying Brazil had come together against these threats: “Long live the people who emerged from every corner of Brazil to sow hope for a future that is socially just, sovereign, democratic and sustainable.”

Brazil has given the world a remarkable example of hope through action. Now it needs others to follow.
