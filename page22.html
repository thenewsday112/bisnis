#4253. AT&Ts $177 million class-action settlement payout is coming, but probably wont change your life

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AT&T settled a $177 million class-action lawsuit last year, agreeing to pay customers whose personal information was stolen in two data breaches. A Texas district court has since approved the settlement agreement on Oct. 2. However, even if you were impacted, this doesn't mean a windfall is coming your way.

In March 2024, AT&T announced that data belonging to approximately 73 million current and former customers had been found on the dark web. The data appeared to be from 2019 or earlier, and included personal information such as social security numbers.

Four months later in July, AT&T discovered a second major data breach. A bad actor had accessed AT&T's cloud server and downloaded records of customers' calls and texts, as well as their phone numbers. This breach impacted nearly every AT&T customer who used the telco's network between May 1 and Oct. 31, 2022, as well as some on Jan. 2, 2023.

Customers filed multiple class-action lawsuits against AT&T which were later combined. The company ultimately agreed to pay a cash settlement fund of $177 million: $149 million for those impacted by the first breach, and $28 million for the second. Though that is no small chunk of change, the amount each individual claimant receives will be significantly smaller. After more than $18 million is subtracted for lawyers' fees, the remaining settlement must be divided among several million people.

Claimants in the AT&T class-action settlement are split into three tiers, each of which will receive a different payout. Tier 1 class members had their social security number exposed by the breach discovered in March 2024. Over 1.65 million people made claims as members of this class, and will each receive around $39-$40.

Tier 2 claimants were impacted by the same breach, but did not have their social security number exposed. More than 687,000 people submitted claims as part of this tier, and will each get approximately $7.50-$8.10.

Finally, Tier 3 claimants were impacted by the July 2024 breach. Over 1.5 million of these class members will take home around $6.50-$7.10 each.

People in Tier 1 could submit a claim for up to $5,000 if they had documentation proving they suffered actual losses due to the AT&T breaches. Similarly, those in Tier 2 could claim up to $2,500 if able to show such losses as well. These aren't mutually exclusive either, so claimants could receive up to $7,500 if they are part of both tiers. Even so, the majority of class members are unlikely to have documented losses.

Unfortunately, if you haven't already submitted a claim, it's too late to do so now. The deadline to opt in for a payout from AT&T's settlement was Dec. 18 last year. If you didn't submit a claim before then, you will not receive any money. Those applying for a higher payout due to documented losses also had to submit their evidence by Sept. 8 this year.

If you didn't opt in but also didn't explicitly exclude yourself from the class by Nov. 17, you are still bound by the settlement's terms. This means that you can't bring your own lawsuit against AT&T for the same breaches, so there's no chance of you getting anything at all.

It isn't clear exactly when class members will receive their money.  The official settlement website states that even after the court approves the settlement, appeals may delay any payouts. Class members will only begin receiving settlement money after the time window for appeals has expired, and all claims have been reviewed. However, if no appeals are filed, payments are expected to begin around December this year.

#509. An ancient ice shelf’s collapse may hint at what’s coming for Antarctica’s glaciers

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

During the last ice age, a floating ice shelf filled Baffin Bay and helped hold three ice sheets in place. Its collapse may hold a lesson for Antarctica.

An ice shelf floats on the ocean, so it’s easy to treat it as the least important part of a frozen coastline. In practice, it works like a brake on the ice sheets behind it.

During the last ice age, one ice shelf covered all of Baffin Bay, between Greenland and Canada, and helped hold three of them in place.

When that shelf broke up, the ice behind it probably began retreating fast. The researchers see the same setup in Antarctica, where floating shelves still hold back glaciers.

Shannon Klotsko of the University of North Carolina Wilmington’s Department of Earth and Ocean Sciences led the study. The ice melted thousands of years ago, so the team estimated its size from marks it left on the seafloor.

Robert Hatfield, an associate professor of geology at the University of Florida, co-authored the study. He compared the ice to a church.

“These ice sheets are like a gothic cathedral,” Hatfield said. “You have this big, tall cathedral of ice and these flying buttresses of floating ice shelves that support them.”

The team wasn’t looking for an ice shelf. Its members spent five weeks in Baffin Bay aboard the research ship Neil Armstrong, and they wanted sediment cores.

These long tubes of mud pulled from the seafloor help to better understand what made the Greenland Ice Sheet unstable as the last ice age ended.

Sonar mapped the seafloor along the way. The team added older maps from other ships and studied the steep slope that drops into the deep bay.

Around most of the bay, they traced a boundary on that slope. It marks the deepest point where ice touched the seafloor.

On one stretch off Greenland, bare rock lies above the boundary and mud covers the slope below it. Off western Greenland, winding ridges and small pits appear above the line. Scientists read similar features off Norway as marks of a retreating ice edge and of icebergs breaking away.

“The thing that really surprised us was this evidence was over a mile deep into the ocean,” Hatfield said. “That’s at least half a mile thicker than any previously known ice shelf in the Northern Hemisphere.”

The bay isn’t as deep now as it was 19,000 years ago. The ice sheets were heavy enough to press the land down, and sea level was different.

Jacqueline Austermann and Lauren Lewright of Columbia University’s Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory ran a computer model to show how the ground and ocean responded to that weight. With their correction, the team estimates the shelf was about 4,600 to 6,900 feet (1,400 to 2,100 meters) thick.

The team puts its area at about 58,000 square miles (150,000 square kilometers). That’s roughly the size of Illinois.

Earlier researchers had found signs of something smaller. A 2022 study by a separate team found evidence of an ice shelf about 1,600 feet (500 meters) thick in the bay’s northern part.

The shelf touched three ice sheets. The Greenland Ice Sheet lay to the east, the Innuitian Ice Sheet to the north, and the Laurentide Ice Sheet to the west.

The team concluded that the shelf did more than slow them down. It connected the three ice sheets and helped them grow thicker.

Sediment cores from across the bay suggest the shelf disappeared over about 500 to 1,000 years. The researchers infer that its collapse triggered a rapid retreat of the surrounding ice sheets.

“That’s one of the scary takeaways we didn’t expect, that an ice shelf the scale of the ones in Antarctica could completely disappear in a matter of centuries,” Hatfield said.

Antarctica has already shown a smaller version of this. After its Larsen B ice shelf broke apart, a 2004 study found that the glaciers flowing into it sped up and thinned.

Most of the ice that Greenland and Antarctica have lost since 1979 left through faster-flowing glaciers, not surface melt. Scientists are watching the shelf in front of Antarctica’s Thwaites Glacier for that reason.

In Greenland, a channel of seawater carved into the Petermann Ice Shelf may have weakened it before a large piece broke away.

“One of the biggest concerns in Antarctica right now is how fast will that ice that’s on land flow into the ocean when we lose that buttressing effect,” said Hatfield.

Scientists who build computer models of ice sheets have a true historical case to test against, Hatfield said. That could lead to better predictions of how quickly Antarctic ice loss will raise sea levels.

The thickness range is wide. That range depends on how soft the rock deep inside Earth is and how the ice grew, so the team ran several versions of the model. Some seafloor maps weren’t sharp enough to show whether the boundary existed at all.

Timing comes next. Researchers still need to work out how quickly each of the three ice sheets pulled back once the shelf was gone.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Geoscience.

#355. 4K Screens, Free WiFI, & Self-Service Snacks: How The World's Best Economy Seat Continues To Stand Out

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For airlines, economy class represents a delicate balancing act between making the most of available cabin space and delivering an experience passengers will remember for the right reasons. While premium cabins attract attention with their lie-flat seats, exclusive dining, and personalized service, economy class remains the most important part of the journey for the majority of travelers.

Korean Air recognizes that improving this experience requires more than offering a competitive fare, investing in everything from high-resolution entertainment screens to better catering and onboard connectivity. Kenneth Chang, Executive Vice President and Chief Marketing Officer of Korean Air, explained to Simple Flying's Sumit Singh that the airline's approach centers on understanding what passengers genuinely value rather than making compromises to reduce costs.

From maintaining generous seating arrangements to introducing Starlink connectivity and self-service snack bars, the SkyTeam member is working to make long-haul travel more comfortable. This demonstrates how practical improvements can make a substantial difference to the passenger experience.

Comfort remains the foundation of any successful economy class product, particularly when passengers spend 10 hours or more in the same seat. Although airlines frequently focus on cabin density to maximize revenue, Korean Air believes the physical experience should not be compromised simply to accommodate additional passengers.

Chang explained that "the seat has to be comfortable. If you're comfortable, then your mind starts going in different angles, saying 'what else do I want?'" The philosophy reflects the belief that comfortable passengers are more likely to appreciate other elements of the onboard experience.

Korean Air also takes a firm position on seat pitch and cabin density, resisting configurations that could negatively affect passenger comfort. Chang noted that some airlines consider a 3-4-3 arrangement on aircraft like the Boeing 777 or Airbus A350 acceptable, but Korean Air believes such layouts can compromise the experience it wants to deliver. As he explained:

"We don't sacrifice pitches nor density. I know that a lot of airlines say three-four-three on triple sevens or A350s is good enough, but the way we see it is that's too much."

By prioritizing personal space, the airline aims to distinguish its economy class product in a competitive market where even small differences in seat dimensions can affect comfort over several hours. That commitment extends to evaluating seat designs, including recline and how passengers of different heights experience the available space. Chang described how Korean Air received complaints about the seats introduced on its Boeing 787-10 aircraft despite maintaining the intended seat pitch.

The airline discovered that a five-degree tilt could provide sufficient comfort, while six degrees made the experience more difficult for shorter passengers, particularly those below five feet five inches tall. This willingness to reassess a seemingly minor design decision demonstrates how passenger comfort depends on practical testing rather than specifications alone.

In-flight entertainment has become an important differentiator in economy class, as passengers increasingly expect the sharp displays and intuitive viewing experiences they enjoy at home. Korean Air has established a standard of 13-inch or larger entertainment monitors for its economy class cabins, with resolutions of 4K or better on newer installations.

Chang explained that incoming aircraft would feature these upgraded displays, including the Boeing 787-10 fleet, giving passengers a more immersive way to watch films, television programs, and other entertainment during their journeys. Screen quality forms part of a broader commitment to providing substantial entertainment choices rather than simply installing impressive hardware.

Korean Air invests considerable time and money in assessing the content passengers want, recognizing that a large, high-resolution screen becomes less valuable if the available programming fails to match their interests. Chang explained that "we have really strict policies on what we want to do for customer experience. We give them the full content of what they want to enjoy."

Combining better displays with carefully selected programming helps deliver a more complete experience. These improvements are particularly relevant on longer flights, when entertainment becomes one of the most important ways passengers manage their time onboard. A sharper display makes films more enjoyable, while a larger screen can make extended viewing more comfortable.

Although premium cabins often receive the most attention for entertainment technology, Korean Air's approach shows that economy class passengers can also benefit from meaningful hardware upgrades, making entertainment an essential component of its offering rather than an optional extra.

Premium economy is gaining popularity all around the world, as passengers are spending more on travel.

Food is another area in which Korean Air seeks to deliver a considered economy class experience, particularly because meals can shape passengers' perceptions of an airline during long journeys. Rather than treating catering as an opportunity for straightforward cost reductions, the carrier aims to ensure economy class passengers receive satisfying meals.

Chang described how he works closely with David Pacey, who oversees catering, through weekly meetings that also involve customer relations representatives, allowing the airline to examine feedback about what passengers enjoyed and which elements failed to meet expectations.

This process gives Korean Air a structured way to assess catering decisions, bringing operational experience together with the opinions of passengers. Feedback can identify whether dishes are appealing, whether the overall service feels adequate, and where adjustments might improve the experience. Chang emphasized that "if you go on economy class and get a meal from us, you'll feel like it's fully served."

His comments underline the airline's intention to maintain satisfying standards of presentation and quality even in the cabin where fares are generally lowest. Maintaining that standard matters because passengers often judge a journey through a series of relatively small interactions, from the meal they receive to the attention offered by cabin crew.

A well-presented meal will not transform an uncomfortable flight by itself, but it can strengthen the overall impression when combined with comfortable seating, good entertainment, and attentive service. Korean Air's approach suggests that catering quality is an important part of the broader passenger experience, reinforcing its commitment to customers across cabin classes.

Changes to flight routes have influenced Korean Air's approach to onboard refreshments, particularly as the war between Russia and Ukraine has forced some services to take longer routes. Chang explained that certain flights have become approximately two hours longer, creating additional challenges for passengers who must remain onboard for extended periods.

These include routes between Incheon International Airport (ICN) and the likes of London Heathrow Airport (LHR) and Paris Charles De Gaulle Airport (CDG). In response, the airline introduced more substantial snacks to help travelers manage the extra time between scheduled meal services.

In doing so, it recognized that longer journeys require more than simply maintaining the original catering schedule. Korean Air has also introduced self-service snack bars on some flights, allowing passengers to access refreshments without waiting for a crew member to distribute them, as Chang explained:

"We've even introduced a self-service snack bar, so you don't have to rely on someone coming in every two or three hours to serve your snacks. We try to introduce whatever we can to make customers comfortable."

This arrangement offers greater control over when passengers eat, whether they want something between meals or need a refreshment after waking during an overnight flight. It also provides a practical way to make extended journeys more accommodating. Convenience becomes increasingly valuable when passengers have limited space and few opportunities to move around freely.

A self-service arrangement gives travelers greater flexibility while allowing the cabin crew to concentrate on other responsibilities. By responding to longer flight times with more substantial snacks and easier access to food, Korean Air demonstrates how practical changes can address operational challenges without requiring a major cabin redesign.

The largest-ever order placed in Korean Air's history will see greatly increased capacity across both widebody and narrowbody fleets.

Reliable internet access has become an important consideration for passengers who want to communicate with family, work during a journey, or stream content beyond the airline's entertainment library. Korean Air chose Starlink after evaluating the technology and working with the provider for six months.

hang explained that the airline believed it offered the right solution for improving the onboard experience, with installations introduced across Airbus A350 aircraft, Boeing 777 aircraft, and some single-aisle aircraft as of September 2026. Passenger adoption figures indicate that demand for connectivity increases with flight duration.

Chang reported uptake of approximately 50% on short-haul flights, rising to around 65% on medium-haul services lasting five to seven hours, and reaching approximately 90% on long-haul journeys. These figures suggest that internet access becomes especially valuable when passengers have more time to fill, whether through messaging, online entertainment, or work.

Korean Air continues investing in its entertainment content, while Starlink provides another option for passengers who prefer familiar online services. The airline hopes to complete installation across its entire fleet by the second half of 2027, although certification requirements mean that fitting the same equipment to every aircraft is not straightforward.

Chang explained that each fleet must be recertified because differences in aircraft interiors can affect the approval process, even when another airline has certified the same aircraft type. Once approval is secured, Korean Air can complete an installation in approximately one day.

This process should help expand connectivity while maintaining the required standards across different aircraft configurations. The latest data from ch-aviation shows that Korean Air currently has a total of 150 aircraft in its fleet, and a further 231 on order, including 62 Boeing 737 MAX 10 and 40 Boeing 777-9 aircraft.

A comfortable economy class cabin depends on more than seats, TV screens, and refreshments, since cleanliness and passenger behavior strongly influence how people feel throughout a flight. Korean Air considers comfort and attentive care fundamental to the experience of purchasing a ticket.

Cabin crew members are trained to walk through the cabin regularly, while bathrooms are cleaned every 15 minutes under the airline's procedures. These routines help keep the cabin welcoming, particularly on longer services where repeated use can quickly affect cleanliness. Passenger behavior is another consideration, especially when travelers are trying to sleep in a confined environment. Chang explained that loud conversations can disturb nearby passengers and that Korean Air expects customers to respect one another.

Where passengers fail to comply with instructions, the airline can subject them to a review process that may result in a warning or temporary placement on a no-fly list. Such measures reflect the challenge of balancing individual preferences with the needs of everyone sharing the cabin. Korean Air's attention to feedback ties these elements together, allowing the airline to identify problems and test improvements rather than assuming its original decisions are always correct.

Chang confirmed that customer comments are reviewed during weekly meetings and that the airline may test an issue directly so decision-makers can understand the experience for themselves. The seat-recline adjustment on the Boeing 787-10 illustrates this approach, showing how passenger feedback can lead to practical changes.

Korean Air continues refining its economy class cabin. As it does so, its emphasis on comfort, connectivity, food, cleanliness, and responsive service reflects an ongoing effort to improve the experience without sacrificing the standards that distinguish its product on a global scale.
