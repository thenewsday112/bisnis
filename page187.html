#3009. The Silent Stress Of Taking A Family Vacation On A Razor’s Edge

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A couple days before I published Broke Again At 49, my wife said going to Las Vegas over the long weekend would be a lot of fun.

I had floated the idea a couple weeks earlier. Unbeknownst to me, she then spent several hours over several days researching hotels, flights, shows, and costs to make it happen. The holiday was only nine days away, and she wanted to lock in good seats and better rates before they disappeared.

Normally, I’d be thrilled by this level of initiative. Vacation planning for four is not easy. The logistics alone are complicated, especially with food allergies and disruptive sleep patterns.

But I wasn’t thrilled. Between the time I brought up Vegas and the night she did all the planning, I got hit with another $25,000 capital call from a different venture fund.

So after she walked me through everything, at a total cost of between $6,000 – $7,000 I told her I needed to think about it. Understandably, she wasn’t pleased. I was the one who suggested the trip in the first place. She did the work. And instead of an enthusiastic thank you, she got a “let me get back to you.”

She also didn’t know I was about to publish a post announcing I was strapped for cash again. Ever since the advent of AI, she no longer edits my posts.

Every father wants to give his family the best life possible. I’m no different. It stings when I can’t deliver.

To her credit, my wife said she’d be fine with a staycation. That mattered a lot. It showed she understood our situation even though I hadn’t fully explained it. She also was aware that I've been longing to do something different for quite some time, and the long weekend gave us that chance.

A week went by, and I had gone through a money manifesting exercise and raised enough liquidity to meet future capital calls. I didn’t raise the money to spend it. But at least I knew my investment obligations for the next three-to-six months were covered, and that took a huge weight off.

Further, we compromised on three nights instead of four, saving about $1,000 – $1,500.

Ultimately, I said yes for one simple reason. I was the one who estimated a 30% capital call within 12 months. Instead, 46% got called in just six. That was my mistake, not theirs. I wasn’t going to make my family pay for my bad forecasting. Let’s go on a memorable adventure.

When you’re the provider and you mess up, you own it. Even if your intention was to invest for your children’s future, the people who had nothing to do with your decision shouldn’t be the ones absorbing the cost.

Before agreeing to the trip, I made myself a promise: no grief about the hotel, the shows, or anything else she picked due to money. Going on vacation and stressing about money every day is the worst of both worlds. You spend the money and still don’t get the joy.

We booked two adjoining rooms at the Palazzo so we could spread out instead of cramming four people into one room. Thanks to my wife, we got great seats to Blue Man Group, Cirque du Soleil, and Popovich Comedy Pet Theater, the show starring rescue animals. Closer seats were important to help our son see more clearly.

Tickets averaged between $450 – $500 per show for the four of us.

We also went to the Illusions Museum, the Flyover, and took a tour out to Red Rocks, which was spectacular.

For dinner, we went to Smith & Wollensky, a nostalgic spot where I used to dine in New York City while going through seven rounds of interviews at GS. The firm gave me a $100 daily meal budget during that time. We also went to Lavo for Italian.

The kids had a blast. Mission accomplished.

The night before Red Rocks, I played poker from about 9:40pm until 1:30am. I was doing fine until I lost $350 in a bomb pot. My full house got beat by a bigger full house after my opponent flopped top set. That’s poker. No crying at the casino.

I went to bed at 2am and promised my wife I’d be up by 6am to bring back breakfast before our 7:30am departure. My wife and daughter aren’t morning people. I am. I spent 13 years waking up before 5am in finance, so four hours of sleep felt manageable.

At one of the Red Rocks vista points, I asked my wife if she could take a few portrait shots of me for my newsletter or future media requests. I didn't have an updated profile picture since 2025. She said sure.

After snapping some shots, then I asked if she could raise the camera to my eye level, since she’s nine inches shorter, and switch to vertical. That’s when I noticed she was getting irritated. When she finished, she handed back the phone without looking at me.

I felt hurt. I thought it was a simple request.

It also stirred up an old feeling of being a burden. I almost never post pictures of myself because it feels superfluous and awkward. I don’t celebrate my birthday either, since it falls in June when school is out and everyone’s busy.

I’m the one who takes the pictures, which I enjoy. The footage is often a surprise when you look back, and it captures moments that disappear too fast.

So asking someone to take photos of me already felt uncomfortable, even if that person is my wife. Asking for retakes with directions felt worse.

With some distance, I've come to realize I'm the problem.

She had already taken a bunch of shots. Then I started giving camera direction like a fussy fashion photographer. Raise it. Turn it. Eye level. Vertical. On four hours of sleep and $350 lighter, my tone was probably more critical than I realized.

I should have just trusted her. She’s taken thousands of great photos of our kids. She didn’t need coaching.

And I bet she had her own stress. She planned the entire trip, got up early, wrangled two kids through a casino city, and was dealing with a husband who’d been quietly anxious about money for weeks. Being on the receiving end of that energy isn’t fun.

When you feel money stress, small interactions get magnified. A neutral look feels like rejection. A sigh feels like a verdict. A nothing-burger becomes a full meal of self-doubt. And you might not even realize what the stress is doing to you until a blowup happens.

We must control how we react to suboptimal situations.

Men are often taught to bury our feelings. If we admit we’re stressed, scared, or worried, we assume we’ll be seen as less capable. So we soldier on through bear markets, layoffs, and capital calls, and keep grinding. Most of us make it through. Some of us sadly don't. While others simply opt out of family formation altogether.

The same goes for any primary provider, regardless of gender. When the household depends on your income or your investment decisions, every bad outcome feels like your personal failure. And because you don’t want to worry anyone, you often carry it alone.

The problem is, stress doesn’t stay buried. It leaks out as irritability, sensitivity, and an inability to enjoy the moment you’re paying for.

If you’re the provider: say something. You don’t need to dump every number on your partner. But “I’m feeling stretched right now and might be more irritable than normal” goes a long way to set expectations. Silence forces your partner to guess, and they’ll often guess wrong.

If you’re the partner of a provider: know that the person who seems tense about a $1,200 hotel upgrade or a simple photo request may be carrying something heavier than they’re letting on. A little patience, a thank you, or a “how are you doing with everything?” can mean more than you’d think.

Since 2009, I’ve always told readers to save and invest until it hurts each month. Otherwise, you’re not saving enough. After 10 years of deliberate saving and investing, you’ll likely have far more than you imagined. Compounding sneaks up on you.

But I’ve always taken an extreme approach, and there’s a reason.

The summer after high school, a prank went wrong and I got in trouble with the law. I thought my life was over. I assumed no company would ever take a chance on me.

When one finally did, I swore I’d save and invest as much of every paycheck as possible so I’d never have to depend on someone accepting me again. I didn’t think many would.

Delaying gratification was painful yet comforting. Every dollar saved was a step toward never needing permission again. Luckily, the worst case scenario did not happen.

But now I have people depending on me who had nothing to do with my past mistakes. They shouldn’t have to live under the strain I put on myself. For the sake of harmony and happiness, I need to ease up.

From now on, I’m keeping at least six months of living expenses in cash or cash equivalents in my taxable brokerage account. No exceptions.

For years, holding cash felt wasteful because it earned next to nothing. But with money market funds and short-term Treasuries paying a respectable yield, the opportunity cost of sleeping well at night is much lower.

Cash isn’t just a return. It’s emotional capital. It’s what lets you say yes to a family trip without feeling like you’re stealing from your future. If you’re fully invested and a capital call, layoff, or market crash hits, you’re forced to make decisions from fear.

I’m a recovering optimizer. Saving $1,000 by cutting a night is fine. Spending the entire trip mentally tallying costs is not.

At some point, the marginal benefit of squeezing out another dollar is smaller than the cost to your relationships and your mood. Your kids won’t remember whether you got a great rate on the second room. They’ll remember whether Dad was present or distracted.

The portfolio: a happy family on vacation, kids laughing at rescue animals doing tricks, a jaw-dropping view at Red Rocks.

The positions: a photo angle, a lost poker hand, a capital call that will be met come hell or high water.

Any investor knows not to let one red position ruin a green portfolio. Yet I let one tiny position ruin an afternoon worth more than any return. Don’t be like me.

Once you hit middle age, it’s hard to change. It’s why so many couples split over irreconcilable differences. Both sides know what the other wants, both want to stay together, yet sustaining change is impossible.

But just like a near-death experience can make you stop taking life for granted, a stressful moment blown out of proportion can be a great catalyst for change. It shows you what’s actually going on beneath the surface.

I’m tired of optimizing every last dollar. Spending freely in Vegas for four days, minus that bomb pot, has loosened me up. And this change should make me a better husband, father, and personal finance writer.

Oh, and here’s one of the pictures my wife took of me during that moment. Turns out she knew exactly what she was doing all along.

Readers, have you ever felt like a burden to your loved ones, no matter how hard you try to provide? Has financial strain ever made you more sensitive to perceived slights? And for the partners out there, how do you support the provider in your household when you can tell they’re stressed?

In the Northeast, October is a special month. As red maples begin to blaze, maitake, a prized edible mushroom, appears at the base of some old hardwood trees (which it is slowly killing). Favoring oak, the dense, heavy mushrooms may be nestled against the tree’s trunk, or a few feet away, fruiting from—and feeding on—old roots. The mushrooms reappear on the same trees year after year. And they are exceptionally good to eat. It is my twelfth season of hunting maitake in the wilds of New York City, and the sense of fun has not diminished.

Here’s where to find maitake, how to cook them, and some precautions to bear in mind.

The name maitake means “dancing mushroom” in Japanese. It is very hard to control the excited wiggle in your walk as you approach the first mushroom of the season, and your next dinner. Maitake grow in temperate forests in the Northern Hemisphere, favoring cold winters and old hardwoods, and they do not differentiate between rural and urban forests. In cities you might even spot them on street trees.

Maitake is also known as hen of the woods: picture a fluffed-out gray hen sitting on her brood.

Oak trees are maitake’s usual host, and I have also seen them at the base of beeches, cherry, and even a London plane. The mushroom is parasitic, feeding on the trees’ roots, and causing a white rot, which will weaken its benefactor and contribute eventually to so-called tip-over (when a tree topples from the base).

While immature mushrooms can fit in the palm of your hand, grown-up maitake can weigh upwards of 10 pounds and sometimes double that. It is hard not to feel pleased carrying home such a prize on your way past a gourmet store charging upwards of $20/lb for foraged maitake. They are cultivated, too, a process pioneered by Japanese growers. These tame mushrooms look very different from their feral cousins: small, delicate, and frilly, versus sturdy and heavy. Their flavor is milder, although quite good.

Maitake appearing in carpets of fallen leaves are cleaner than those surrounded by bare soil, which splashes up if there is rain and is incorporated in the mushroom as it grows, making it gritty to chew. Use a sharp knife to slice the maitake at the base, trimming off any debris stuck to the bottom. Avoid mushrooms growing at the base of trees used by raccoons as a latrine (there will be tell-tale signs of scat). And always cook your wild-collected mushrooms.

What part of wild maitake is best to eat? In a young, butter-tender mushroom, the whole mushroom is delicious, cooked. On larger maitake, the gray caps, or “feathers” remain tender.

At home, slice smaller maitake in half to see how clean they are. Trim away any debris, and wash. For  tender mushrooms, you can also tear the caps, keeping a strand of the soft white core attached to each. Then wash.

For large mushrooms, cut off the caps for dinner and save the woody core to roast for broth.

In terms of flavor and fragrance maitake is exceptional. While the mushroom cooks, especially if it is roasted, the scent is—at least to my nose—reminiscent of chestnut honey. When I first made maitake chips that first bite was a revelation: It tasted as though the mushroom had been marinated in soy sauce. Maitake contains l-glutamate, a natural flavor-enhancer, and the embodiment of umami.

Two layers of maitake complexity make this woodsy pilaf sing autumn. The woodier heart makes a flavorful broth, while the roasted ears  intensify that unique flavor. (I soak the farro for 72 hours; the resulting texture of the grain, which will have begun to sprout, is wonderful.) The recipe doubles easily.

Broth: In a saucepan over medium-high heat warm the oil and add the maitake heart. Cook for 10 minutes, stirring occasionally. Add the onion, garlic, and carrots, stir well and cook for another 5 minutes. Add the wine, water, salt, sugar, and pepper. Bring to a boil, then lower the heat to cook at a simmer for 40 minutes. Strain the broth through a fine mesh sieve. Measure out 1 cup for the pilaf (use the extra for soup, sauce, or just do broth shots!).

Roast mushrooms: Preheat the oven to 375 degrees F. In a bowl toss the maitake caps with the oil and salt. Transfer to a large skillet and into the oven. Roast for 40 to 45 minutes; they should be turning crisp and smelling good. Remove from the oven, take out the mushrooms and reserve, leaving the mushroom-infused in the skillet.

To finish: Place the skillet over medium heat and add the farro with the chives, stirring for a minute. The grains will make appealing, popping noises. Push the farro to one side and add the nuts. Leave them to toast for a minute then stir everything together (you will notice that the germ of the farro is showing up whitely at the tip of each grain, now). Toast together for another minute and then pour in all the broth. Bring to a gentle boil and cook, simmering, until it has all been absorbed, about 20 minutes. Taste. Add salt. Now stir in the roasted mushrooms, and give them a minute to heat through. Serve at once.

#4066. Just 10 minutes of daily exercise could unlock deeper sleep

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

What if getting better sleep depends not just on how much you exercise, but how often you move? Research from The University of Texas at Austin suggests that being physically active every day, even for short periods, may help people spend more time in the deep, restorative stages of sleep that support recovery, mood, and mental health.

The findings suggest that consistency could be an important but often overlooked part of the relationship between exercise and sleep. Rather than saving all their workouts for the weekend, people may benefit from making movement a regular part of their daily routines.

The study, published in the Journal of Physical Activity & Health, examined activity and sleep data collected from University of Texas students who wore Fitbit devices over several months. While previous studies have largely examined how much time people spend exercising, the researchers focused on a different question: Does the number of days someone exercises influence how well they sleep?

"We wanted to know whether it matters if someone spreads out their exercise over the week versus doing it all at once, like a 'weekend warrior,'" said Benjamin Baird, a research assistant professor of psychology in UT Austin's College of Liberal Arts. "And for sleep health, frequency does seem to matter."

Just 10 Minutes of Daily Exercise May Improve Deep Sleep

The researchers found that participants who exercised more frequently tended to experience more restorative sleep. The findings build on previous research connecting physical activity with non-REM sleep, a stage that includes the deep sleep essential for physical recovery and healthy brain function.

Chris Corral, who recently earned a master's degree in health behavior and health education from UT Austin and co-led the study, explained that deep sleep is especially important during the early hours of the night. During this period, the body carries out many of the processes that help it recover from the day's physical and mental demands.

Participants who were active more often spent more time in these valuable stages of sleep.

"That's exactly what you want," Baird said.

"Better sleep means better brain health and potentially lower risks of chronic disease and depression."

One particularly encouraging finding was how little exercise appeared to be necessary. Among the young adults studied, as little as 10 minutes of moderate to vigorous physical activity per day was associated with better sleep.

That level of effort does not require an exhausting workout. According to the researchers, moderate to vigorous activity involves breathing harder than usual while still being able to carry on a conversation. On a scale of 1 to 10, the effort would feel like approximately a 6.

Even gentler movement appeared to offer benefits. Regular walking and taking breaks from sitting to stand were also associated with healthier sleep patterns and a more positive mood the following day.

Better Sleep Linked to Improved Mood and Energy

The potential benefits extended beyond what happened during the night. Participants who exercised more regularly also tended to feel more energetic and experience less stress the next day.

"We saw that more non-REM sleep was tied to better next-day mood and energy," Baird noted.

This connection is important because sleep affects far more than whether someone feels rested in the morning. Deep sleep helps the brain and body recover, supporting processes involved in memory, emotional regulation, and overall health.

Previous research has repeatedly linked physical activity with improved sleep, while better sleep itself is associated with greater well-being. However, many earlier investigations took place in controlled laboratories and examined relatively brief periods, sometimes measuring the effects of exercise over a single night.

The UT Austin team wanted to understand how these relationships played out under ordinary living conditions, where exercise habits, sleep schedules, and daily activities can vary considerably.

To accomplish this, the researchers used wearable fitness trackers to collect information over several months. The Fitbit data allowed them to compare participants' movement habits with their sleep patterns over time, offering a more detailed picture of how everyday activity relates to sleep quality and next-day mood.

Although the findings reveal meaningful associations, the observational approach does not establish that exercising more frequently directly causes improvements in sleep.

Why Daily Exercise May Be Better Than Weekend Workouts

The results also raise questions about how people should organize their exercise routines.

Current recommendations from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the World Health Organization call for approximately 150 minutes of moderate physical activity each week. However, those recommendations do not require people to distribute their activity evenly across the seven days.

For some, meeting a weekly exercise target means taking long walks or completing challenging workouts on Saturday and Sunday. Others prefer shorter sessions spread throughout the week.

The UT Austin findings suggest that the second approach may offer particular advantages for sleep, although more research is needed to determine whether daily activity is better than completing an equivalent amount of exercise on fewer days.

"Our results suggest that daily movement may be better for sleep than doing it all on the weekend," Corral said.

If further studies confirm the importance of exercise frequency, the findings could eventually influence how public health organizations advise people to stay active.

"Current guidelines don't reflect the importance of frequency for sleep health," Baird said. "Having this kind of data allows us to start thinking about whether they should."

What Better Sleep Could Mean for Brain Health

The possible implications go well beyond feeling more alert after waking up.

During sleep, the brain performs essential maintenance functions, including strengthening memories and removing waste products that accumulate during waking hours. Healthy sleep is also closely connected with emotional well-being and long-term physical health.

"Sleep is when your brain stores memories, clears waste and resets," Corral explained. "Better sleep means better brain health and potentially lower risks of chronic disease and depression."

The research is part of UT Austin's Whole Communities -- Whole Health initiative, an interdisciplinary program that works with communities to investigate factors influencing health and well-being.

The team is now preparing to examine whether the same patterns appear in a larger and more diverse population through the Whole Communities -- Whole Health five-year cohort study. This next phase will help researchers determine whether the findings extend beyond college students to people of different ages and physical activity levels.

For now, the results point toward an accessible way to potentially improve sleep: Make physical activity a regular part of everyday life.

Rather than focusing exclusively on long workouts or ambitious fitness goals, people may benefit from finding small opportunities to move throughout the day. A brief exercise session, a walk, or simply getting up regularly instead of remaining seated may all contribute to healthier routines.

"You don't need to run marathons," Corral said. "Just moving a little each day helps. Light activity counts too. Doing something is better than doing nothing."

#4092. Loneliness may steal nearly 6 years of good health

📅 Oct 10, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Loneliness and social isolation are widespread in the United States. According to a 2023 report, about half of U.S. adults experience loneliness, while nearly as many are socially isolated. Despite health consequences that can rival other major risk factors, both problems remain relatively overlooked as urgent public health concerns.

Until recently, however, researchers had little idea how many years of good health might actually be lost to loneliness and social isolation.

Researchers at Tulane University's Celia Scott Weatherhead School of Public and Tropical Medicine have now attempted to put a number on that toll. Their first-of-its-kind observational study, published in Nature Communications, found that loneliness and social isolation were associated with the loss of nearly six years of life free from mental disorders such as depression and anxiety. The effects on physical health were smaller, but still significant.

"Overall, the study found that social isolation and loneliness can have a significant impact on disease-free life expectancy," said lead author Lu Qi, HCA Regents Distinguished Chair and professor in Celia Scott Weatherhead School of Public and Tropical Medicine at Tulane. "This includes both physical and mental disease."

Although loneliness and social isolation are often treated as the same thing in everyday conversation, researchers distinguish between them. Social isolation refers to an objective lack of social connections. Loneliness, by contrast, describes a person's subjective dissatisfaction with the quality of their relationships, regardless of how many social interactions they actually have.

"The findings weren't surprising to me," Qi said. "Previous studies found that social isolation and loneliness are related to mortality, especially premature mortality, and this study provides additional evidence."

The researchers analyzed data from 277,489 adults between the ages of 40 and 69. At the beginning of the study, none had seven major chronic conditions: diabetes, heart disease, lung disease, dementia, cancer, depression and anxiety.

Using age 50 as a reference point, the team calculated how loneliness and social isolation were associated with the number of years participants could expect to live without these diseases.

Compared with people who experienced neither loneliness nor social isolation, women experiencing both had 2.4 fewer years free from physical disease. Men experiencing both had 1.7 fewer years.

The difference was even larger for mental health. Women experiencing both loneliness and social isolation lived about 3.7 fewer years without mental disorders, while men lived approximately 5.8 fewer years free from those conditions.

"This comes down to the gender differences between men and women," Qi said. "Our research found men are more susceptible to being affected by loneliness. We also know that women tend to live longer than men. There are gender advantages with certain diseases."

Healthy Habits Help With Isolation, but Not Loneliness

The researchers also examined whether lifestyle could alter these associations. Healthier habits, including better diet, regular exercise, sufficient sleep, and not smoking, measurably reduced the loss of disease-free years associated with social isolation.

Loneliness produced a different pattern. A healthier lifestyle did not significantly reduce the additional health risks linked to feeling lonely. That distinction suggests loneliness and social isolation may require different approaches when it comes to protecting health.

The research team plans to study additional population groups in the future and use clinical trials to investigate whether loneliness and social isolation directly cause the health effects observed in the study.
