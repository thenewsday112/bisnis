#2182. On mall culture, stranger danger, and the neon 90s

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

As a Gen Xer growing up in the boring suburban Midwest, boomers repeated certain adages meant to guide me into adulthood:

“Finish your plate. There are kids starving in other countries.”

“Guys don’t make passes on girls who wear glasses.”

“If someone tries to kidnap you, just tell them to kill you right there.”

I remember wearing a Rainbow Brite nightgown and watching Saturday morning cartoons when my mother imparted that latter piece of wisdom. She swooped in without warning, and she seemed to be focused on malls in particular, the lawless parking lots of malls.

“If he shoots you on the spot,” Mom explained patiently, “you still have a chance to be saved. Someone’ll find you quickly. If he takes you with him, he can do whatever he wants. And your body will never be found.”

Hey, at least she thought about what would happen if she lost sight of me. I won’t go into the boomer parenting philosophy of sending us out on our bikes as the cure-all remedy to keeping our brains off drugs and our eyes off MTV (I was corrupted by the “Faith” video, by the way; this is a real thing), but every so often, horrifying front page news would make them tighten their grip on their latchkey kids.

Adam Walsh is the first case that comes to mind—the little boy kidnapped from Sears. He wanted to look at video games, and his mother made the decision so many parents would make. Despite Mrs. Walsh returning quickly, he was gone. I can’t imagine how confused authorities must have been as they searched every store. A mall is a crowded, safe place. Crowds are safe. There are people to hear you scream. Witnesses to see you carried off. But as John Douglas writes in Mindhunter when trying to describe the mindset of a murderer:

“…I’ve got to know how I am going to get this child out of the mall quietly and without creating any fuss or suspicion when his or her parents are probably two stores down…”

My mother was thinking of a different scenario when she interrupted cartoons that one morning, when she readied me for future combat. Most people have long forgotten Jerilyn Stanfield. She majored in fashion at the University of Cincinnati. She and her new husband lived in a cottage in Anderson Township. She worked at the nearby McAlpins and then Shillitos. And at 2:30pm on January 14th, 1982, she vanished from the Eastgate Mall parking lot.

A mall where my own family shopped with my grandparents.

There were no cell phones back then, so Jerilyn’s husband didn’t realize something was wrong until 10:30 that night. By then, she’d been murdered by a serial killer who saw her, wanted her, took her. Left her tied up in a barn.

I really didn’t need another reason to be afraid of the world around me. Spooky books were my drug of choice, and my drug dealer was the elementary school librarian in Paris, Kentucky. She’d let me check out The Creature Catalog, Edgar Allan Poe graphic novels, Scary Stories to Tell in the Dark. Vampires could be avoided, but apparently the boogeyman stealing me from my mother could not. While shopping, I warily stuck to her like those puffy Poochie stickers that would never come off the back of my bedroom door.

The 1990s hit like a freight train at the same time I went through puberty and suddenly, nothing could ever happen to me.

A 90s mall was a candy-colored refuge, an escape from a stifling home with a raging stepfather who had buyer’s remorse on the whole white-picket fence gig. At that very Eastgate Mall, I puked Zima in the parking lot. My best friend did doughnuts at midnight, techno blaring from his car speakers. The malls around me were always packed; kids waited for hours to see Santa; our mothers stood in line to buy Beanie Babies they swore would make them millionaires but could never really explain how. Malls filled my room with Gen X tchotchkes; Teen Beat posters, Apple Paperbacks, Ace of Base cassette singles. I’m going to go out on a limb and say suburban malls were the best representation of the American Dream during a time when it was affordable and attainable. There were different stores for different budgets—Kmart blue light specials around the corner from junior sections selling Esprit, Bugle Boy, Umbro shorts—and if you didn’t have money, you could aspire to one day shop in Express or Structure or Petite Sophisticate. My new book, Night of the Mallrats, revels in this neon hedonism; a world with hope and a middle-class and the assumption you’ll get what you want, especially that one movie at Blockbuster.

In the selfish haze of my own youth, ignorant of the troubles of others, not once did I think of Jerilyn Stanfield or heed my mom’s warning as I went off into dark corners to kiss boys I barely knew. It took one night trapped inside a mall to make me wish I’d paid more attention.

Though the following story isn’t the snowy fate of a character in Night of the Mallrats, it definitely inspired the mood. Over the years, I worked in many bookstores in many malls, and this event took place an hour away from home. Forced by my district manager to drive through a level two snowstorm, I cussed all the way there and parked sideways on a foot of snow as more fell from the sky like a white waterfall. As the dozen or so store employees wandered around as if lost, it was clear there would be no customers. We were snowbound. After dark, law and order thinned quickly as everyone fought over sample beds in a department store. That’s when what little survival instincts I had kicked in. Not wanting to partake in Lord of the Flies: Mall Edition, I crept back to my B. Dalton, pulled and locked the gate, and settled on the hard floor of the backroom, pretending my coat provided some cushion.

The public doesn’t know that in older malls there are secret passages thanks to the backends of long gutted-out stores, and internal alleyways for shipments. Those alleys are always covered in concrete and have flickering lights near exposed ceiling pipes that sometimes don’t work. And you never see anyone back there, you just hear footsteps.

That’s where someone hid as they tapped my store’s receiving door.

“Sleeping” only feet away, I knew I didn’t imagine the sound. Then it came again, a tap, tap, tap. I turned the key and yanked the heavy door back. I am the girl in the horror movie who runs upstairs instead of out the front door. I am the big-bosomed side character who laughs at danger before getting the knife.

As I looked down both sides of the shadowy corridor, I waited for anything: Sneakers, the childish giggles of Spencers employees. I closed the door and within fifteen minutes it happened again. This time I didn’t open it.

If he takes you with him, he can do whatever he wants. And your body will never be found.

All my mother’s catastrophizing wasn’t really about the mall. It was about a stranger picking the most distracted person out of a crowd and then…just waiting.

#4427. The focus on AI safety disasters ignores everyday harms

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Lawmakers are passing industry-backed bills to guard against the worst outcomes, rather than addressing more common problems.

Consider what passes for success in technology policy. An AI company promises that its chatbot will not encourage suicide. A social media platform makes it more difficult for children to buy fentanyl. These are important protections, and they should have existed all along. But companies can clear these low bars while continuing to make products that leave young people dependent on simulated friendships or trapped in feeds built to keep them scrolling.

We have historically allowed the debate over technology to be defined by the worst thing a product might do, with remarkably little discussion of the insidious damage it does every day. As we transition into an era defined by powerful AI products, we should demand that the tech industry do more than merely address catastrophic harms. We should take measures to ensure that its products don’t erode how we think, learn, and interact with one another.

A teenager who develops an unhealthy attachment to an AI companion may never appear in a lawsuit or a newspaper, except in the most egregious cases. Neither will the student who begins relying on a chatbot to write every paper, or the young person who measures their life against a social media feed engineered to provoke envy and keep them watching. A product can meet every narrow safety requirement and still leave our entire culture worse off in ways that don’t always make headlines.

A narrow definition of success defined around safety is politically convenient for the technology industry, and companies actively promote such definitions. Taking steps to prevent spectacular disasters is good for business: It can mean fewer lawsuits and less pressure for serious regulation. Confronting the everyday consequences of dependence would require something considerably more threatening to their bottom line. It might mean asking companies to add more friction to their products or actively limit engagement or features that lead to negative outcomes, such as addictive attention-hacking algorithms.

Technology giants have instead worked to shape legislation around the harms they are already under direct pressure to address. This year, for example, Google backed bills to regulate AI chatbots in several states. The legislation mandated basic protections against sexual content and promotion of self-harm that tech companies should have established regardless, but with broad carve-outs that could exempt some of the most popular services. Passing these bills allowed legislators to claim a safety win while efforts to promote stronger legislation—laws designed to prevent children from becoming emotionally dependent on AI—failed. This playbook worked in states like Nebraska, producing legislation heavily influenced by industry at the expense of bills that would have done more to hold companies accountable.

We have been through a version of this with social media. Globally, people who use the internet now spend an average of more than two hours a day on social media platforms—time that has been quietly taken from ordinary life and handed over to companies whose profits depend on keeping us there. No one, outside perhaps a few tech CEOs, wanted these algorithms to occupy such a large portion of our waking hours, and their cultural engineering can actually contribute to violence on a devastating scale. Meta’s platforms have been cited for contributing to the Rohingya genocide in Myanmar, anti-Muslim riots in Sri Lanka, and alleged calls for violence during Ethiopia’s Tigray war. The same incentives operate in the feeds that shape young Americans’ understanding of politics, their peers, and the wider world.

AI is extending this logic into parts of life that social media could influence only from a distance. It offers to write our thoughts for us and stand in for the people we might otherwise turn to. A third of teens now choose to discuss serious matters with AI companions instead of other people. MIT researchers found that young people using ChatGPT to write essays showed weaker brain connectivity during the task, and their evidence indicated that “cognitive debt” persisted even after the task was complete. There is plenty we still need to learn about the long-term effects. We already have reason to ask whether technology marketed as making us more capable is eroding the habits and relationships that help us develop capabilities in the first place.

Follow this way of thinking to its logical endpoint and you get a world of perfectly sanitized entertainment, with chatbots ready to spare us the difficulty of thinking or of dealing with another person’s feelings (or even our own). But anyone who has struggled to write something worth reading, or repair a damaged friendship, knows how much gets lost in that bargain. We build judgment through effort and friction, and we learn to live with other people by confronting the fact that they have needs and opinions of their own.

We should demand safety as a minimum, yes—and then demand considerably more. Technology companies and school districts should have to show beyond a reasonable doubt that AI improves learning and critical thinking before making it a fixture in classrooms. Companies should be prohibited from designing companions that pretend to be conscious or profess love to users, and they should face independent scrutiny of how their systems affect relationships and people’s ability to think for themselves. The companies that have inserted their products into nearly every part of modern life have earned obligations far greater than avoiding the next terrible headline.

Legal experts and news industry leaders remain skeptical of Meta’s statements.

“Publishers should always be concerned about tech companies attempting to take content for free, and should assume that Meta is taking this additional time to either find a new way to try to mislead publishers into granting Meta free access to valuable copyrighted content or finally give up in the face of pressure,” said Matt Topic, a media and intellectual property attorney at Loevy & Loevy. “Publishers don’t need more PR statements or explanations. They need Meta to keep its hands off their copyrighted material and respect publishers’ terms of service.”

“I don’t think that repeating the same non-explanations for another three weeks will make much difference,” James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell Law School, added. “But maybe they’ll use the time to come up with better explanations.”

Thomas Höppner, a professor and attorney specializing in competition law in Germany, told Nieman Lab that “Meta’s conduct constitutes a prima facie infringement of European Union competition law, and a textbook case for imposing interim measures.”

The client commissioned me to design an original book cover for a new fantasy novel, A Bleeding of Heavens, whose central protagonist is a powerful dragon. They hoped the overall atmosphere would echo the iconic scene of Daenerys travelling with her fleet across the ocean at sunset in Game of Thrones.

Once I got the design requirements, I started planning the overall layout right away. I depicted the main dragon in a vivid dynamic stance, dashing forward from a distance in the frame, straight towards audiences with its mouth wide open in a fierce roar.

Floating clouds and sailing fleets spread across the sea in the lower part of the picture. The huge dragon and the tiny distant ships form a sharp contrast, building remarkable spatial depth. The whole scene is set against a glowing backlit sunset sky, and striking light rays enrich the visual layers.

For the back cover, I drew the other two dragons from the novel locked in an intense battle. I arranged the three dragons with clear visual hierarchy, and concentrated the finest details on the main dragon’s head on the front cover to highlight the core character.

I quickly sketched out the overall composition and mood. Using simple photobashing, I overlaid some scene photos on the bottom layer, then blended elements into the scene with broad textured brushstrokes. For the dragon, I referred to 3D models posed in Blender.

After the client approved the sketch, I moved on to lighting rendering. Following backlighting rules, I focused on reflections across the sea, rim-lit clouds and subsurface scattering on the dragon’s wings. Once roughly 60–70% complete, I sent the draft for detail checks.

The client quickly approved most elements and requested that the lower fleet should be depicted in battle to match the chaotic tone of Jasmine Young’s fantasy novel (the fifth in the Dragon Breaker series). I revised accordingly and refined the artwork to final completion.

I use cloud brushes to quickly block out shapes. I intentionally vary the size of near and distant clouds to create depth. Additionally, it’s important to differentiate warm and cool tones between sunlit areas and shadowed back sides of the clouds. As you can see in the illustration, I tint the rear sides of the clouds with cool blue hues.

Given the top-down perspective requiring abundant details, I added figures on ships and multicoloured foliage atop island hills to amplify the sense of scale.

The vivid red on the dragon’s wings and bright pink within clouds stem from subsurface light reflection, enhancing the overall luminous effect.

My tip for detailing: paint sea foam only on lit water and leave shadowed areas under ships sparse. This draws eyes to highlights, saves work and boosts visual impact.

For the ships, I arranged multiple vessel orientations with 3D models in Blender to create a chaotic layout and avoid tedious perspective issues.

Here’s my tip for cloud painting: keep shadows unified and focus on lit areas to build volume, while keeping the scene’s perspective in mind.

People who rely on artificial intelligence (AI) to solve simple problems do worse at, and are more likely to give up on, similar tasks when that assistance is removed, compared with people who do not use AI for quick answers, new research finds.

The study, which was presented at the 2026 Conference on Language Modeling in San Francisco earlier this month, investigated the impacts of shifting mental work onto an AI chatbot across arithmetic and reading comprehension tasks. A growing body of research suggests this process of "cognitive offloading" harms long-term learning and critical thinking abilities.

"The concern is about what cognitive scientists call 'desirable difficulties' — the productive struggle that builds skill over time," study first author Grace Liu, a doctoral student in machine learning at Carnegie Mellon University, told Live Science in an email. "If AI routinely removes that struggle, people may get the right answer in the moment but develop less robust independent capability."

Liu and her colleagues recruited 1,222 participants for the study, which was released as a preprint earlier this year and has now undergone peer review. The researchers designed three randomized controlled experiments, including two sets of mathematical fraction problems and a series of reading comprehension questions. The participants were split into three groups, so there was no overlap between the experiments. Each experiment took 13 to 15 minutes to complete.

In the first experiment, the team randomly assigned 354 participants to two groups: one that had access to an AI chatbot and one that didn't. The researchers gave both groups the same 12 fraction problems, which the AI group could get the answer to immediately or obtain clues about by prompting the chatbot. Then, the researchers removed the AI assistant without warning for a final set of three fractions, which all of the participants were given, regardless of their group.

Both groups had the option to skip problems. The researchers explicitly told the participants that there was no penalty for wrong answers, so skipping a problem was a deliberate decision to not engage with it, they wrote in the study. The participants in both groups were also given the correct answer to each problem if they got it wrong, so they could learn from their mistakes.

This first experiment showed that the participants in the AI group were more likely to solve fractions and less likely to give up when they had access to the chatbot compared with the other group. But when the AI was removed, their performance dropped and they skipped or failed more problems, suggesting that using AI impaired unassisted task performance, persistence and motivation.

The second experiment was a copy of the first experiment, but it had 667 participants and small tweaks in the trial design, including the addition of a pretest phase that helped the researchers eliminate participants who were unable to solve the most basic problems. The findings were similar to those of the first experiment, hinting that the use of AI for simple tasks harms perseverance and drive to complete those same tasks without AI, especially when AI is used to get direct solutions.

Liu acknowledged that calculators also allow people to offload computations, which could cause similar declines in independent performance. But "the reason we study AI specifically is that it allows people to offload reasoning across tasks across many domains," she said.

Calculators allow people to offload math problems, but their effects on reasoning aren't as far-reaching as the effects of AI.

In a third experiment, the team explored the impacts of AI assistance on reading comprehension, which draws on meaning-making skills and mental-model construction — the cognitive process of building internal representations of external reality. The trial included 201 participants, who were divided into a group with access to AI and a control group. The researchers gave both groups five comprehension questions before disabling the AI chatbot and handing each participant another three questions.

The results mirrored those of the first two experiments, suggesting that reduced persistence, in particular, was not an artifact of the math problems but a general outcome of cognitive offloading onto AI.

"The most significant finding is that people who offload more have higher declines in persistence (increased skip rate), which is an important skill that cuts across domains," Liu said.

One possible reason people stop trying once they have experienced help from AI is that they get used to the immediacy of answers and perceive any method that takes longer as inefficient, the researchers wrote in the study. But studies show that persistence through difficulty is essential for skill acquisition and learning, raising concerns about the long-term impacts of using AI for simple tasks.

Scientists don't yet have a full picture of how small instances of cognitive offloading affect lasting competence and independent ability. To find out, researchers would need to track the same people for a long time and regularly measure their learning outcomes across a wide range of tasks, Liu said. But research so far suggests that an overreliance on AI erodes our cognitive fitness and mastery of subjects, according to the study.

To encourage long-term learning, capability and autonomy in people, AI models and chatbots should be designed to give directions and instructions toward a solution, rather than the solution itself — more like a tutor, the researchers argued.

"We need to work toward a future in which human abilities are, as much as possible, augmented rather than supplanted," study co-author Brian Christian, a computer scientist and researcher in the Center for Human-Compatible AI at the University of California, Berkeley, said in a statement.

Screw extractors come in different types to suit the different sizes of damaged fasteners you can’t remove with a screwdriver. Read on to learn more about each type and crucial factors, including gripping power and the material one will want to consider when choosing the best screw extractor.

Screw heads come in a wide variety of sizes, so it’s important to pay attention to what sizes the screw extractor can accommodate. Most sets include sizes ranging from 0.125 inch up to 0.625 inch. Larger sets have extractors as large as 0.875 inch and are capable of removing large bolts on vehicles and industrial machines. Other sets can remove tiny machine screws found in electronics, such as laptop computers, with extractors as small as 0.0781 inch. While a small kit with four or five extractor sizes is adequate for most, it makes sense for pros or DIYers to invest in a set that can cover a broad range of sizes.

Different types of screw extractors use different strategies for gripping a stripped or rusted screw or bolt head. Some drill into the head and use reverse threads to bite into and grip the head, while others grab the head by fitting over it. Although the latter generally creates more gripping power, this style of extractor is limited to instances where the bolt or screw head is jutting out of the material enough to allow the tool to grip it.

The material type also affects gripping power. Drill bits and extractors made of high-quality steel alloys do a better job of boring into and gripping fasteners and last longer than those that use cheaper materials.

Since screw extractors must burrow into metal screws in order to work, they need to consist of hard metal. Most screw extractors are made of high-speed steel, the same steel alloy used in many drill bits and saw blades. This type of steel is harder than other alloys and is capable of enduring high temperatures without softening, a key characteristic for drilling into the heads of metal fasteners.

If it’s in the budget, extractors made of cobalt steel are even better, but they’re also pricier. Cobalt steel is harder than high-speed steel and holds up better to the high temperatures created by metal cutting into metal. Cobalt steel also has a higher tensile strength, which makes it a better choice for extractors with a narrower flute of 0.125 inch or less. Stay away from extractors that consist of softer alloys, as they will not be hard enough to do the job.

Screw extractors have features that make them easier to use and ensure that the right drill bit is used with the right extractor. Those that work in conjunction with a drill bit must match the bit size to be effective. This type of extractor should have the compatible drill bit size etched onto its side to avoid confusion.

Double-sided extractors are some of the most convenient models as they feature the drill bit and extractor on a single reversible shank, eliminating the need to hunt for a compatible drill bit. Simply drill the hole with one side, then flip the shank to apply the extractor.

Some kits use magnetic extension bit holders that can hold either the drill or extractor bit. This allows the user to quickly swap between them, speeding the screw-removal process along.

Since screw and bolt extractor kits can include 30 pieces or more, most come with handy carrying cases that keep all the pieces organized so they can be found when needed.

The drill bit is a crucial part of the extractor that is used to bore a hole in the head of the damaged bolt or screw, creating a place for the extractor to penetrate and grip the screw head so you can remove it.

There really is no standard length for a screw extractor. In fact, short and long extractors work equally well at removing damaged screws and bolts. It’s more important to choose the correct- diameter drill bit and extractor to suit the damaged fastener.

To determine the proper extractor size, begin by choosing the proper drill bit size. The drill bit should be about half the diameter of the screw head. For example, to remove a 0.375-inch screw, begin by drilling a hole in the head with a 3/16-inch drill bit. Once the hole is drilled, use an extractor that’s slightly smaller than the drill bit—most kits conveniently match the bits on the kit to the extractor. The slightly smaller extractor should fit inside the hole but still be tight enough that its threads can lock into the hole’s contours.

Screw extractors are reverse threaded. This means that you’ll need to set your drill/driver or impact driver in reverse to remove the fastener. Keep in mind that the drill bits in screw extractor sets are also usually (though not always) reverse threaded, meaning you’ll need to set your drill in reverse when drilling the pilot hole.

A straight flute extractor is a smooth tapered bar. After drilling a pilot hole in the screw head, you have to hammer the straight extractor into the screw head, then use a pair of pliers to twist the extractor counterclockwise to remove the screw. A spiral screw extractor doesn’t require any hammering. It has threads that bite into the pilot hole, allowing you to remove the damaged screw with a wrench or drill.

Learning how avian influenza viruses might pose a pandemic risk “is absolutely a normal part of virology research,” says influenza researcher Thomas Friedrich of the University of Wisconsin–Madison School of Veterinary Medicine. Such research can help scientists assess whether emerging bird flu viruses pose a greater risk to humans.

But experiments that deliberately aim to boost pathogen traits such as transmissibility or disease severity draw scrutiny, Friedrich says, because of concerns about accidents or misuse.

Though the report comes amid broader debates about the security of AI models, arguments over this “gain-of-function” research predate generative AI. In 2012, studies involving H5N1 bird flu and airborne transmission in ferrets prompted an international debate over whether the scientific benefits outweighed the risks. The company did not respond to questions from Science News about why it considered the case significant, how it assessed Claude’s role in the research or whether any proposed experiments had actually been performed.

Anthropic released too little information to determine the purpose of the work, says biodefense researcher Gregory Koblentz. “There is no evidence that any of this research was connected to the development of biological weapons,” says Koblentz of George Mason University in Fairfax, Va.

The same kind of biological research can serve very different purposes, from civilian science to military defense. Even work done at a military-affiliated institution does not, on its own, show that researchers are developing a biological weapon, Koblentz says.

Anthropic also did not provide enough detail to show exactly how much Claude helped, Friedrich says. The company’s own account suggests the AI model “did not meaningfully add scientific capability,” he says. In other words, it may simply have sped up the researcher’s work, rather than enabling something they could not have done otherwise.

Even if Claude’s role was only to speed up the work, that can make a big difference in a scientist’s workflow, says virologist David O’Connor of the University of Wisconsin–Madison. Systems such as Claude can help researchers find papers, synthesize results and parse data more quickly, he says. “Work or analyses that would have taken me months can now be done in an afternoon.”

As a result, existing scientific information, including about avian flu viruses, may become easier to find and use, O’Connor says. O’Connor says frontier AI models could let scientists tackle questions they might not have attempted before. “The frontier models will change the ambition of research questions that are asked,” he says.

The bird flu research was one of five instances in Anthropic’s report involving uses of Claude that the company says could support biological weapons development. None of the five look technically sophisticated or particularly concerning to biologist Kevin Esvelt of the MIT Media Lab. Esvelt is more suspicious of the researchers’ motives; he says the five cases showed signs of ill intent. But none “represented a competent attempt at weaponization.”

The poor quality of these particular attempts does not show what a more capable user might be able to do with AI-assisted biology, Esvelt says. AI can help with research and analysis, but turning an idea into a biological threat takes additional work, Koblentz says. “Even if AI is used to design a potentially dangerous pathogen, it is not a threat until the organism can be modified in a lab or synthesized.”

That requires facilities, measures for keeping viruses contained, funding, trained personnel and hands-on experience. Those barriers vary depending on who is doing the work, O’Connor says. They would be a major obstacle for an inexperienced person working alone, but less so for scientists with specialized training and access to a well-equipped laboratory.

Regardless, the remaining laboratory steps are substantial, Friedrich writes in an email. “There is a big gap between identifying mutations that could potentially adapt an avian influenza virus to humans and a ‘harmful biological outcome’.”

According to Ellucian's 2024 Student Voice survey, 59% of surveyed students have considered dropping out due to financial stress. Nineteen percent reported they had dropped out, with financial uncertainty cited as the leading cause.

Higher education leaders invest enormous time and energy refining aid strategies, analyzing enrollment outcomes, and communicating college costs to students and families.

Far less attention is given to what happens after a student accepts. Yet the financial pressures students face don't disappear after the enrollment deposit is paid. For many students, that's when those pressures become magnified.

While the financial aid package may help a student enroll, the billing process often determines how realistic it feels to stay enrolled.

Financial aid plays a critical role in expanding access to higher education. But even the most thoughtfully designed aid package doesn't eliminate the financial decisions students and families face throughout their academic career.

That's where billing either reinforces the promise of higher education or undermines it.

A billing process that creates confusion, surprise, or unnecessary friction can undermine the very affordability goals an institution worked so hard to achieve.

The University of Notre Dame offers one example of how institutions are thinking more holistically about access.

Most institutions can’t afford to eliminate loans from financial aid packages. However, every institution can ask whether its payment practices reflect the same commitment to affordability that appears in its mission statement and strategic plan.

Financial aid should be measured not only by what institutions award, but by how easily students can navigate what comes next. The challenge is carrying the promise made in the award letter through every financial decision a student faces.

For the students and families weighing the costs of attending your institution, the award letter isn’t the end of the affordability conversation. It's the beginning.

Two reports examine what’s working — and what’s not — as schools try to combat chronic absenteeism.

These are the conclusions of two reports released today, one of which was based on research into evidence-based practices to tackle attendance problems.

In 2019 — the year before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic — the chronic absenteeism rate was 15.4%. That number rose to 28.3% in 2022 and then fell to 22.6% in 2025, according to a 45-state analysis by the American Enterprise Institute. Student chronic absenteeism is broadly defined as missing 10% of the school year, or about 18 days for any reason, including excused and unexcused absences.

Both reports find that it is important for schools to understand why their students are not attending school. The joint release of the two reports is meant to help education leaders and their partners commit to proven strategies to increase attendance, which education experts say benefits students' engagement and learning.

School leaders pointed to the use of multiple evidence-based strategies to boost attendance as an effective approach to tackling absenteeism, according to the analysis of principal surveys. That analysis was conducted by the Everyone Graduates Center, Partnership for Student Success and the GRAD Partnership. The groups analyzed two surveys of principals during the 2024-25 school year about why some schools continue to struggle with chronic absenteeism while others have made attendance improvements.

The survey findings show that substantial reductions in chronic absenteeism are more common in schools that have multiple effective attendance strategies that are aligned with the root causes of why their students are absent. These schools also have the capacity to implement these strategies at scale.

Principals who said their school had four or more effective attendance strategies were more likely to report substantial reductions to chronic absenteeism (77%) compared to school leaders reporting just one effective strategy (41%).

One approach, however, was at the top of the principals' list of effective attendance strategies: focusing on school connectedness. That includes improving student-teacher and student-peer relationships in addition to creating supportive school environments.

In the second report, published by FutureEd and Attendance Works, researchers provide a collection of more than 30 evidence-based strategies to address students’ various barriers to attendance, including those that focus on early childhood education and social-emotional supports.

Some of these attendance barriers are unique to a post-COVID school environment, such as the shift in parental attitudes to keep their children home from school due to potential illnesses or the assumption that schoolwork can be done at home digitally.

Meanwhile, some traditional attendance barriers remain challenging to schools, such as chronic illnesses, transportation struggles and family demands, the report said.

The report, known as the 2026 Attendance Playbook, organizes suggested inventions into tiered levels of support, including universal prevention measures for all students at the first tier and intensive interventions for individual students at the third and top tier.

The first tier of interventions, for example, focus on preventing absenteeism by building engagement with students and families and through rigorous and relevant instruction, in addition to other measures like effective communications, and safe and reliable transportation. Offering access to summer and after-school programs is one approach to improving attendance during the regular school day and year, the report said.

The third tier, which provides most intensive support, could include customized interventions to meet each student's housing, health and other needs. Suggested strategies for addressing student housing needs include outreach to local education foundations for resources and the use of Title I, Part A homeless set-aside funds to increase staff capacity and services.

“To reduce today’s elevated levels of chronic absence, educators and their partners can accelerate their progress by building off what works and not reinventing the wheel," said Hedy Chang, CEO and president of Attendance Works, in an Oct. 26 statement.

#943. Scientists discover Arctic sea ice loss could be far worse than predicted

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Arctic could lose roughly one million square kilometers more winter sea ice than scientists previously predicted, according to new research that draws on evidence from a warmer Arctic 6,000 years ago. By using ancient climate records to refine modern computer models, researchers have produced more reliable estimates of how much ice may disappear in the coming decades.

The findings, published in Nature Geoscience, could also have important consequences for winter weather in the United Kingdom and other parts of Europe.

Much of the attention surrounding Arctic ice loss has focused on the dramatic retreat of summer sea ice. Winter ice, however, has received considerably less scrutiny, even though climate models show their greatest disagreements when predicting how much ice will cover the Arctic during the colder months.

Why Arctic Winter Sea Ice Matters for Global Weather

Winter sea ice influences weather far beyond the Arctic by controlling how much heat and moisture escape from the ocean into the atmosphere. Declining ice cover has been associated with colder winters and increased snowfall in the United Kingdom, northern Europe, and North America.

Recent observations have also highlighted the Arctic's changing conditions. In March 2025, winter sea ice reached the lowest annual maximum extent recorded since modern monitoring began. The Arctic matched that record again in 2026.

To improve predictions of future ice loss, scientists at the University of Cambridge and Mexico's Centro de Investigación en Matematicas (CIMAT) turned to evidence preserved beneath the Arctic Ocean.

The team assembled the largest database of its kind containing a chemical marker produced by algae that live within sea ice. The evidence came from sediment cores deposited during the mid-Holocene, approximately 6,000 years ago, when small changes in Earth's orbit helped create a warmer Arctic with less extensive ice cover.

These ancient conditions offered a valuable opportunity to investigate how Arctic sea ice responds to warming and to test whether today's climate models accurately represent that response.

Ancient Ocean Sediments Reveal What Climate Models Missed

The researchers examined concentrations of a biomarker known as IP25, which is produced by algae associated with sea ice. They combined these measurements with other chemical markers left behind by organisms living in open water, allowing them to reconstruct Arctic ice conditions thousands of years in the past.

Next, they compared those reconstructions with simulations generated by 27 climate models for the same ancient period.

Using a statistical method called Bayesian weighting, the researchers assigned greater importance to models that more closely matched the historical evidence. Models that performed less accurately received less influence over the final predictions.

Because the underlying physical processes controlling sea ice remain consistent across different climate conditions, the team could apply these same weights to simulations of both the recent past and the future.

The comparison revealed a significant problem: collectively, the climate models predicted more winter sea ice than the ancient evidence supported.

"When we apply the model weights to historical simulations, we completely eliminated the positive bias," said lead author Crystal Fu, from Cambridge's Scott Polar Research Institute. "The future projections similarly end up being lower than previous estimates, by about a million square kilometers."

More Accurate Arctic Ice Forecasts Reduce Uncertainty by 85%

Scientists have collected substantial evidence about Earth's ancient climates, but translating that information into reliable forecasts remains difficult. Both historical reconstructions and future climate simulations contain uncertainties that complicate direct comparisons.

"The scientific community has amassed lots of paleoclimate records over recent decades, but it's remained a challenge to directly link these records to future projections, since there is uncertainty at both ends," said Fu.

The team's new approach substantially narrowed those uncertainties, reducing the range of possible outcomes in some projections by as much as 85%.

"By using evidence from the past, we can now produce more reliable maps of where Arctic winter sea ice is likely to disappear," said co-author Marco Aquino-López, of CIMAT. "These give us a much clearer picture of what the Arctic might look like in the coming decades, and how much uncertainty remains."

Although the warming that occurred during the mid-Holocene was driven by changes in Earth's orbit rather than the human activities responsible for today's warming, Fu emphasized that the response of sea ice to rising temperatures follows the same basic physical principles.

"It doesn't matter if the warming is natural variation or caused by human activity: if it warms, sea ice is going to melt," she said.

Arctic Ice Loss Could Have Wider Consequences for Europe

The findings may have implications that extend beyond the disappearance of Arctic sea ice itself.

When the researchers applied their improved model weighting to projections of precipitation, they found indications that current climate models may also underestimate future snowfall in southeastern Greenland and rainfall across the Mediterranean region.

Greater snowfall over Greenland could affect the stability of the Greenland ice sheet, while increased Mediterranean rainfall could influence future flooding risks in southern Europe.

These potential effects suggest that the consequences of a warming Arctic may be more extensive than existing predictions indicate. The revised simulations offer researchers a clearer foundation for investigating those risks and understanding how changing Arctic conditions could influence weather elsewhere.

Diez links the offerings to the Totonac culture, which flourished in Mexico’s Sierra Norte region and along the northern Gulf Coast. “Totonac ceramic offerings and a sculpture fragment dating from AD600 to AD900 suggest that later groups may have regarded Teteles's former inhabitants as their ancestors,” he says, pointing to the possible origins of Totonac culture.

The structure identified as a solar observatory could point to early Maya-Huastec connections previously suggested by linguistic studies. “The observatory resembles E-Group complexes (structures associated with solar observations) at Maya sites like Uaxactún in Guatemala,” Diez says. “It is the first of its kind identified in northern Puebla, though La Venta's Olmec complex may be older.” He adds that its alignments could relate to the Mesoamerican agricultural calendar.

Diez also emphasises the astronomical significance of the site's main 12m-tall pyramid: “It may be aligned with Canopus, the second-brightest star in the night sky, associated with the beginning of the Mesoamerican calendar in February.” He notes that although scholars have studied this alignment in Mesopotamian and Egyptian structures, its significance in Mesoamerica remains underexplored.

The archaeologist Ivan Šprajc—an archaeoastronomy expert at the Research Centre of the Slovenian Academy of Sciences and Arts in Ljubljana (who was not involved in the project)—places Teteles within a broader tradition of earlier ceremonial complexes he has studied. “Previous archaeoastronomical studies using Lidar technology have documented similar and earlier complexes along the southern Gulf and Pacific coasts,” he says, “some dating to the late Early Preclassic period”, before 1000BC.

Šprajc is cautious about using the term “observatory”. “The common practice of referring to these complexes as observatories is not entirely justifiable,” he says. “Although they contain astronomical alignments, their primary functions were ceremonial or residential, and such alignments were commonly incorporated in very different types of structures throughout Mesoamerica.” He adds that solar alignments tied to the ceremonial agricultural cycle were common, though the calendar year's start shifted over time.

Further research, including comparative studies and artefact analysis, will certainly reveal more about these discoveries in the future. “Plans are underway to open the site and a museum to the public,” Diez says, although an exact timeline is pending.

#490. The Sky Today on Monday, October 26: Observe the October Hunter’s Moon

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

October’s Full Moon occurs at 12:12 A.M. EDT, shining brightly in both the morning and evening sky. The October Full Moon is also called the Hunter’s Moon, which follows the Full Harvest Moon we saw in September.

Observing the Full Moon can actually be a bit challenging. At Full phase, the Sun appears directly overhead from the nearside and it is lunar “noon,” which means shadows are at their shortest and the landscape does not appear as three-dimensional as we peer up at it. However, that doesn’t mean there’s no observing to be had!

First, though, if you plan to use a telescope, consider a Moon filter to cut down on the glare. If you don’t have one, you can observe while wearing sunglasses to get the same effect — just make sure not to slip them over your eyes until you’re about to step up to the eyepiece. Using higher magnifications will also reduce the amount of bright light streaming into your eye, as greater magnification reveals less of the lunar surface in a single field of view.

Although many features are not at their best during a Full Moon, the rays of ejecta left behind as impactors slammed into the surface to create craters can be viewed during a Full Moon. In particular, the large crater Tycho in the lunar south has a huge, well-defined system of rays you can explore, some of which stretch hundreds of miles from the crater itself. Other craters that have stunning rays you can seek out during Full Moon include Copernicus, Proclus, Aristarchus, Stevinus, and Byrgius.
