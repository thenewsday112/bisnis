#466. Bulgaria seeks suspects in Germany after Starlink sabotage

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A Bulgarian court has issued arrest warrants for two young German nationals suspected of setting fire to Starlink antennas. The case is reminiscent of another in Poland, which Warsaw says was orchestrated by Russia.

Bulgaria on Monday issued European arrest warrants against two 19-year-old German nationals, saying it suspected their involvement in the sabotage of a series of Starlink antennas near the capital Sofia.

"The evidence gathered makes it possible to conclude that there are reasonable grounds to suspect the involvement of the two defendants," the court in Sofia rules, granting a request from prosecutors.

Ukraine's military uses Starlink's military-grade satellite internet service Starshield for battlefield communications amid Russia's invasion.

Bulgaria does not directly border Ukraine, but it is on the Black Sea, one of the flashpoints of the conflict. A similar case in Poland, which does directly border Ukraine, led to a total of four arrests last week, also in Germany.

The two teenage suspects are accused of deliberately setting fire to the antennas on the evening of October 9.

Of 18 in a mountainous region south of Sofia, three were destroyed entirely, two were partially burned and three more were damaged, according to prosecutors.

Prosecutors say the suspects entered from Serbia in a car with German license plates, claiming to be tourists, staying just a few hours around the time of the alleged crime.

Plamen Tonchev, the head of Bulgaria's domestic intelligence agency DANS, said he was working on the assumption that it was deliberate sabotage and said other such acts could not be ruled out in future.

News of the arrests follow just a week after a total of four people were arrested in Germany, accused of sabotaging a Starlink satellite communications station in central Poland in late September.

German police detained three resident men and a woman, two of them Turkish nationals, early last week.

Polish officials said they believed that Russia was behind the sabotage attempt, with Prime Minister Donald Tusk calling it an act of "hybrid warfare."

The domestic intelligence agency also claimed that Russia had cooperated with organized crime elements in Germany on the alleged operation.

Meanwhile, in the Baltic, Lithuania's prime minister on Sunday said that Russia was likely behind an attempted arson attack on a company hosting communication hardware for Starlink satellites.

#455. Brutal, revolutionary empire builder: Genghis Khan's legacy

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Berlin is host to a major exhibition on the founder of the Mongol Empire and his successors. Here's how Genghis Khan disrupted the course of history.

Genghis Khan is one of history's most radical figures. A blood-thirsty invader whose conquests caused the deaths of an estimated 40 million people, he was also a visionary political thinker who established the largest contiguous land empire in history.

The founder of the Mongolian Empire died in 1227. Ahead of the 800th anniversary of his death, Berlin's James-Simon-Galerie explores his legacy and the "Mongol Century" (1250–1350) in the exhibition "Genghis Khan and the World of the Mongols."

The ruler remains "incredibly relevant" to this day, says Matthias Wemhoff, the director of the exhibition project.

"What the era of Genghis Khan — the 'Mongol century,' if you will — vividly demonstrates is how quickly established structures can collapse, how rapidly vast empires can vanish from the map and how swiftly the entire landscape can be utterly transformed," the archaeologist tells DW.

The charismatic leader's unprecedented impact on the world also evokes present-day disruptive individuals who are triggering sudden, unpredictable societal shifts, says Wemhoff: "There's always been this question: Are world politics shaped by individuals or rather by groups and sociological processes?"

In this case, the answer is clear, says the museum director, who also co-wrote a 2026 book on the nomadic leader.

"If Genghis Khan hadn't existed, the course of world history would truly have been entirely different — so much of it is inextricably linked to his person," Wemhoff says.

Genghis Khan was born as Temujin around the year 1162. His father, a Mongol chieftain called Yesugei, died when he was still a child. His family was abandoned by its tribe. To secure his familial position, Temujin killed his older half-brother, Bekter, when he was still a young teenager.

Thanks to his charismatic personality, he built alliances and found followers who helped him become the sole ruler on the Mongolian steppe. He formally adopted the title of Genghis Khan in 1206.

At its peak, the Mongol Empire covered approximately 24 million square kilometers (about 9.3 million square miles), stretching from China and Korea to parts of Eastern Europe and the Middle East, and accounting for over 16% of the Earth's total land area.

Wemhoff lists various innovations under his rule: For one, Genghis Khan transformed warfare. Commanders were promoted for skill, discipline and loyalty rather than based on aristocratic rank or tribal loyalties. Soldiers were organized into decimal units of 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000.

Despite being illiterate by modern standards, he also pushed for the introduction of the first written script for the Mongolian language, which allowed to build a state bureaucracy, track population and tax registers, record decrees, issue legal decisions and handle diplomacy.

A set of laws were established that replaced fragmented tribal customs with a unified legal system.

Trade was simplified through his adoption of a paper currency.

A policy of religious freedom was also established across the Mongol Empire, allowing all individuals to practice their chosen faith without state coercion.

Before the Mongol Empire, the Silk Road was fragmented by local conflicts and heavy taxes. Mongol rule placed the entire route under a single authority, making long-distance travel vastly safer.

The Mongol Empire is known as a period of relative political stability, economic growth and cultural exchange, an era historians call the "Pax Mongolica."

But "it all came with incredible levels of cruelty. The millions of people who fell victim to the Mongols — the mass executions, the abuse of women — this happened on a scale previously unheard of," notes Wemhoff.

The exhibition opens with a raid that happened after Genghis Khan's death, but took place just 260 kilometers (around 160 miles) away from Berlin: the 1241 Battle of Legnica, in Silesia, Poland.

It embodies the brutality and intensity of the Mongols' methods.

There came the "highly mobile, agile Mongols on small horses — lightly armored in lamellar gear, wearing helmets that left the face exposed, and wielding bows capable of firing 20 or more arrows in a single minute," points out Wemhoff.

European knights, heavily armored and mounted on large, massive warhorses, didn't stand a chance against their enemies.

"When a hail of arrows rained down on them, the horses eventually went down, and the knights ended up lying on the ground, stuck on their back, much like a June bug," he adds. "The two styles of combat were simply mismatched."

The Mongols' western campaigns shifted historic power centers in a way that still impacts world politics today.

For example, after the Mongol invasion of Kievan Rus', which was the first East Slavic state, its thriving capital Kyiv was reduced to a depopulated ruin. Moscow became a key regional power, partly because it chose to cooperate with the Mongols rather than pursue resistance.

"In our Western European collective memory, the Mongol invasion plays hardly any role. It's something of an anecdote," says Wemhoff, noting that the era of Genghis Khan and his immediate successors is barely mentioned in standard Western European school curricula.

Germans are mainly familiar with the name of the empire's founder through the 1979 Euro-disco pop band Dschingis Khan, which represented West Germany at the Eurovision Song Contest with a campy song of the same name that reduced complex history to extreme tropes.

But in eastern Europe, the Mongol invasions "changed everything fundamentally," says Wemhoff. "And so fundamentally that we still feel the consequences of it today."

The exhibition "Genghis Khan and the World of the Mongols" runs from October 21, 2026, to April 18, 2027.

#441. Why US investors are snapping up Germany's famed Mittelstand

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Increasing amounts of US capital are flowing into Germany's traditional industrial firms and family-owned businesses. As many business owners struggle to find successors, more companies are becoming acquisition targets.

What do the long-established German companies Birkenstock, Viessmann, Linde and ebm-papst have in common? All have fallen into American hands.

Traditional German family-owned businesses and small-to-medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) have become increasingly attractive to US companies and investors.

Last year, US investors were involved in 76 acquisitions and investments in German industrial and manufacturing companies, according to an analysis by financial data provider PitchBook. The transactions were valued at about $29.5 billion (€26.3 billion) in 2025, nearly double the 2022 total. As of August 19, 2026, the figure stood at $23.9 billion.

"From what we are seeing in the market, the interest from US investors in German companies is definitely still there," said Samar-Ahmad Albrecht-Mahmood of Veracity Capital Investments, which advises companies looking to invest in Germany.

German companies, particularly those in the industrial and manufacturing sector, remain attractive, "because many of them are highly specialized, have strong customer relationships and very good technical know-how," he told DW. "In many cases, these are businesses that have built up a strong position in their niche over decades."

At the same time, many German business owners are reaching an age where they are thinking about selling their business, Albrecht-Mahmood underscored, pointing out that "there is often no successor within the family."

"This brings companies onto the market that might not have been available for sale a few years ago," he said.

Notable takeovers this year include ebm-papst, the Mulfingen-based manufacturer of electric motors and fans, which was bought by US company Madison Air for $5.4 billion in August.

In June, the long-established Augsburg-based company Everllence, formerly MAN Energy Solutions, was sold to Bain Capital, with the US investment firm acquiring a 51% stake in the company.

According to Die Deutsche Wirtschaft (DDW), a German business information network focused on the Mittelstand (medium-sized and family-owned companies), no country owns more German firms than the United States. Of the total 7,861 companies identified in the database "German Companies Under Foreign Ownership," 1,750 are currently held by US investors.

PricewaterhouseCoopers (PwC), one of the world's leading auditing and consultancy organizations, also underscored the growing US investor interest in acquiring German companies. In a report, titled Destination Germany, PwC said that while the number of acquisitions and equity investments has not increased, the financial volume of the deals has jumped.

The report highlighted, in particular, the acquisition of BASF Coatings by the US investment firm Carlyle, in partnership with the Qatar Investment Authority (QIA), for around €7.7 billion.

The deal was struck on October 26, 2025, and closed on June 30 of this year.

According to a recent survey by the American Chamber of Commerce in Germany, about 62% of the US companies active in the country are expecting business conditions to improve over the next three to four years.

Meanwhile, some 52% of them are planning to expand their operations in Germany, up from 49% in 2024.

At first glance, however, the increase in US acquisitions and equity investments does not seem to align with the decline in US investment flows to Germany last year.

According to the Bundesbank, significantly less investment capital flowed from the US to Germany in 2025 than in the previous year.

While foreign direct investment in Germany has risen overall — from €57.4 billion in 2024 to a total of €86.1 billion last year — investment from the US amounted to only €11.8 billion, nearly 44% less than in 2024.

In contrast, investment from the United Kingdom rose to around €26 billion.

"I would say the trend is continuing, although perhaps not simply in terms of higher deal numbers every year," Albrecht-Mahmood said. "The general interest in good German industrial companies, especially strong Mittelstand businesses, remains high."

Relatively low valuations also play a role, the expert argued. "Compared to the US, we still see situations where German companies are valued more moderately, particularly in the SME segment. This can make acquisitions quite attractive for international buyers."

#334. Trump, Putin and Xi at one table, without Ukraine. What Moscow wants from the proposal and why Beijing might agree.

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Moscow is pushing for a summit with Donald Trump and Xi Jinping to discuss ending the war in Ukraine, without Kyiv or the EU. What does Russia hope to gain, and why might China be willing to take part?

The administration of US President Donald Trump has stepped up diplomatic efforts this autumn to revive stalled negotiations aimed at ending Russia's war in Ukraine.

Washington has increased pressure on Kyiv and its European partners while seeking to draw Moscow back to the negotiating table through a series of concessions, including a deal involving Russian diesel exports to the US and the temporary easing of some sanctions.

Yet the Kremlin appears in no hurry to resume meaningful peace talks. Moscow is ready to hold negotiations with Ukraine at a technical level next month, the Kremlin said after the meeting between US special envoy Steve Witkoff and Russian President Vladimir Putin in St. Petersburg on October 21.

Putin agreed to resume the peace talks mediated by Washington after November 10, his aide Yury Ushakov said after the meeting. He added that the next round of negotiations could take place in Abu Dhabi and would focus on "building the security system for Russia and Ukraine after the end of the war."

According to his aide, Putin also shared some thoughts with Witkoff on the possible trilateral summit involving US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping. Ushakov pointed out that Beijing is already preparing the meeting.

Moscow proposed to hold a three-way meeting on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit, scheduled for November 18-19 in Shenzhen, China, The Wall Street Journal reported earlier this week. According to WSJ sources, Putin hopes to reach an agreement with Trump and Xi on ending the war in Ukraine on terms favorable to Russia.

Moscow's proposal for the three-way summit involving Russia, the United States and China — but excluding Ukraine and the European Union — offers a glimpse of the negotiating format the Kremlin would prefer. Russia insists that Ukraine must be prepared to make territorial concessions before negotiations can move forward. Putin has also repeatedly stressed that he is willing to negotiate only if Russian interests are taken into account.

According to the WSJ, Putin views the proposed gathering in Shenzhen as a potential new Yalta Conference, referring to the 1945 meeting of the leaders of the United States, Great Britain and the Soviet Union to discuss the defeat of Nazi Germany and the postwar order in Europe.

The newspaper reported that Chinese officials had indicated through diplomatic channels that Beijing would not object to the three-way format if Trump agreed to participate. Trump, whose relations with Kyiv have deteriorated significantly in recent weeks, has also expressed openness to such talks.

Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy pointed out during his visit in Oslo on October 20 that talks concerning the future of his country will be acceptable only if Ukraine gets a seat at the negotiating table.

Temur Umarov, a researcher at the Carnegie Russia Eurasia Center in Berlin who specializes in China-Russia relations, is not surprised by the Kremlin's proposal. He told DW that Moscow wants to feel respected by Washington and treated as a major international power.

The previous US administration was unwilling to discuss questions affecting Ukraine's sovereignty with Russia without Kyiv's participation. Now, Umarov said, the Kremlin believes such negotiations may have become possible.

Moscow apparently hopes that any agreement reached at the proposed meeting would prompt the White House to pressure Ukraine and the EU into accepting its terms. But what role would China actually play?

Umarov believes Beijing would participate largely as a symbolic partner, rather than as an active mediator responsible for the outcome. "Beijing has no intention of taking responsibility for Ukraine's future," he said.

Simply attending the summit would not impose any additional obligations on China. "It is hardly surprising that the Kremlin insists on this format," Umarov explained. "Russia wants to demonstrate that it is on an equal footing not only with the United States, but also with China. Yet, objectively speaking, Moscow is not the senior partner in that relationship."

Umarov believes China has little direct interest in such negotiations. Even if the leaders issued a joint statement, Beijing would seek language that was as vague and noncommittal as possible, he said. That would allow China to present almost any subsequent development in the war as consistent with the understanding reached at the summit. For Umarov, such flexibility would be essential to Beijing's participation.

However, Yurii Poita, a senior associate researcher at the Mercator Institute for China Studies (MERICS), offers a different assessment. He believes China and Russia have overlapping strategic interests, particularly in their efforts to promote an alternative security architecture across Eurasia.

Both countries, he argues, would benefit from a reduction in Western military presence in Europe and Asia and from a weakening of NATO's influence. "If Russia and China manage to convince the United States to reduce its military presence in Europe and military assistance to Ukraine, Russia would gain additional advantages on the battlefield," Poita told DW.

He added that such a development would further undermine the EU's confidence in the United States as a NATO ally. Poita believes Moscow and Beijing would seek precisely these outcomes in any negotiations with Washington.

Michael Kimmage, director of the Kennan Institute in the United States, also doubts that Beijing is preparing to take a more active diplomatic role in ending the war.

Speaking to DW, Kimmage said China may see the conflict as destabilizing and would prefer to maintain trade relations with Ukraine and other countries in the region. But he believes another priority outweighs those concerns. "More important to China is the political survival of Vladimir Putin," he said.

According to Kimmage, Russia provides China with several geopolitical advantages, including low-cost energy and a foreign policy partner that rarely stands in Beijing's way.

He believes China will continue indirectly supporting Russia's military efforts to prevent a Russian defeat. An outright Russian victory may not necessarily be Beijing's objective, he said, but ensuring Putin does not lose is crucial. For that reason, Kimmage does not expect China to pressure the Russian president into ending the war if Putin is unwilling to do so.

Neither Ukraine nor the European Union would be part of the proposed talks in China.

Kateryna Krasina, a junior researcher at the Kyiv-based New Geopolitics Network, believes their exclusion could reveal how Beijing actually views their international influence.

In her assessment, China's approach suggests that it does not regard Ukraine as an independent player in the negotiations and considers Europe relatively weak.

Kimmage, however, argues that excluding Europe from the summit would not necessarily diminish its influence over the course of the negotiations.

He acknowledged that European governments may have little power to prevent a meeting between Trump, Putin and Xi. But he pointed to European efforts surrounding the 2025 Alaska summit between Trump and Putin, which he believes helped prevent outcomes that would have been unfavorable to Europe.

Kimmage said Europe's influence has grown through financial assistance to Ukraine and extensive defense industry cooperation, particularly involving Germany and other European countries.

He argued that European support has played an important role in Ukraine's ability to resist Russian advances. "And that support equals leverage," Kimmage said.

He believes Russia, China and the United States may all be interested in portraying Europe as a secondary power, but that such a portrayal would overlook Europe's actual influence.

"The optics and the reality are not quite the same," he said, drawing a distinction between the appearance of great-power diplomacy resembling the Yalta Conference and Europe's role in supporting Ukraine.

He believes that any diplomatic agreement between the US, Russia and China would face the reality on the ground.

"China understands that whatever the outcome of negotiations with the United States and Russia, the reality on the ground will be very different and will depend above all on Ukraine," he said.

"Whether the Kremlin likes it or not, Ukraine has agency of its own. Neither the United States nor Western countries can fully determine what happens on the battlefield through diplomatic pressure alone."

#313. Can Germany revive its economy before the far-right AfD gains ground?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Conflicts, rising costs and Chinese competition are testing Germany's economic model like never before. Can new growth sectors create jobs and ease voters' economic fears, thereby helping contain the far-right AfD?

Germany's export-led economic model faces unprecedented strain just as support for the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) party reaches a new peak.

Chinese rivals are steadily eroding the market share of major German industrial players, while persistently high energy prices, elevated labor costs and excessive red tape are adding to the structural crisis.

After two years of contraction, real GDP edged up only 0.2% last year, according to the Federal Statistical Office, Destatis. Exports to China fell 9.7% in 2025, withGerman vehicle and part shipments plunging by a third.

While there are signs the German economy is turning the corner, the government's latest forecast of 1.3% growth in 2026 is still modest compared with the pre-COVID boom years.

The AfD is currently polling ahead of all other political parties in Germany. The country's many economic challenges have been a major factor in the right-wing populists' rise.

Germany's domestic intelligence agency sees reliable indications that the AfD is right-wing extremist in its entirety.

The party has vowed to put Germany back on a growth path by ending the transition to renewable energies, lowering taxes and renegotiating European Union treaties, while also reforming welfare and sharply restricting immigration.

As Chancellor Friedrich Merz's coalition searches for a fresh growth strategy to fend off the rise in populist politics, the government has set out its high-tech agenda focusing on six priority sectors.

These are artificial intelligence (AI), quantum computing, microelectronics, biotechnology, clean energy (including nuclear fusion) and climate-neutral mobility.

Irene Bertschek, head of the Digital Economy research unit at the Leibniz Center for European Economic Research (ZEW), argues that Germany's research in these "key enabling technologies is very strong, particularly in the area of climate-neutral technologies."

Yet, the results are often commercialized abroad, Bertschek told DW, with the United States leading in AI and China closing the gap quickly.

A report published Monday by the Made for Germany initiative — a group of 147 companies and investors — builds on the government's plan, estimating that these and other new technologies could attract up to €4.1 trillion ($4.6 trillion) in investment by 2035.

Realizing that potential, however, will depend less on today's industrial giants, like Volkswagen, Siemens and BASF.

"It will not primarily be the established companies that conquer these new fields, but innovative newcomers — startups," Oliver Falck, director of the ifo Center for the Economics of Innovation and Digital Transformation, told DW.

The task, Falck said, is to create the right conditions for startups to emerge, including proximity to top universities, access to private capital and enough freedom to experiment.

He argues for state financing during the early-stage "valley of death" — the risky phase between research and commercial viability when private financing is often scarce.

There are clear signs this shift is already happening. Data from Deutsche Bank and startupdetector, a German startup database and market intelligence platform, show more than 3,000 startups were founded in Germany in the first half of 2026, up 88% from the same period last year. One-third of the new ventures have an AI focus.

Venture-capital investment soared 44% to €5.8 billion in the first half of 2026, with firms such as Neura Robotics and drone manufacturers Stark and Quantum Systems benefiting.

US investors, meanwhile, are increasingly buying up Germany's famed Mittelstand — its traditional small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) — with an eye on their specialized technology.

"The German Mittelstand is highly innovative. Now it is essential to invest in key technologies such as artificial intelligence and to tap into the potential that arises at the intersection with established technologies," Bertschek stressed.

The expert, who is also chair of the German government's Commission of Experts for Research and Innovation (EFI), thinks SMEs should make better use of their huge amounts of production data and collaborate with AI startups to help those young firms grow.

Another idea for accelerating the shift to new high-tech sectors comes from Sven Gabor Janszky, chairman of the Leipzig-based futures-research institute 2b AHEAD. He argues that Germany needs special economic zones to help catch the next wave of economic growth.

"For a few specific technologies, you lower the regulations, you recruit ambitious workers and pump money into these zones," Janszky told DW.

This, he added, would follow the examples of Silicon Valley and Chinese mega-cities Shenzhen and Shanghai's Pudong district, and echoes the conditions that helped rebuild the German economy after World War II.

In Janszky's view, these zones would help Germany position itself as a first mover in emerging fields rather than remaining tied to older industrial strengths.

"EU regulations may try to prohibit this move, but Germany will have to overcome them so we can grow the economy again," he added.

The sharp rise in Germany's defense spending will also help several of the high-tech sectors now being prioritized.

According to the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS), the defense budget is expected to reach around €120 billion in 2026 — roughly 2.6% of GDP. The government wants this figure to hit 3.5% by 2029.

The resulting procurement will provide a major boost to the production of drones, sensors, communications systems and other dual-use technologies that have both civilian and military applications.

Yet, with major rival China benefiting from much lower energy and worker costs, along with huge government subsidies, Germany will continue to face similar price disadvantages when exporting its new high-end tech. But that doesn't have to spell failure, said Falck, the ifo expert.

"Take the semiconductor industry as an example. It is probably unrealistic to aim for leadership along the entire value chain. But it is enough to lead at individual choke points," he pointed out.

"And that is exactly where Germany is strong: as a supplier of equipment for state-of-the-art chip fabs and as a supplier to the world's AI gigafactories, with power semiconductors."

In other words, with a first-mover advantage, decades of high-quality production data and a focus on specialized niches, Germany can remain a major force in advanced industrial and engineering technologies.
