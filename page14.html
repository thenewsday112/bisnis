#7. Amazon posts negative free cash flow of $42.4 billion, holds 2026 capex unchanged

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Amazon shares plunged more than 6% in extended trading Thursday after the company reported disappointing third-quarter results and ballooning negative free cash flow, reflecting its swelling AI investments.

Here's how the company did, compared with estimates from analysts polled by LSEG:

Wall Street was also looking at other key revenue numbers:

The company held steady on its capital expenditure forecast for 2026, saying it continues to expect spending will reach $220 billion this year.

Tech's hyperscalers have been on a blistering spending run to meet the surge in demand for artificial intelligence infrastructure, but signs have begun to emerge that the capex splurge is moderating. Earlier this week, Microsoft lowered its capex forecast for the year to $170 billion from $175 billion, while Alphabet maintained its guidance at $195 billion to $205 billion.

CEO Andy Jassy said on a conference call with investors that the company expects to "spend meaningfully more in 2027," and that Amazon has a "clear line of sight to attractive financial returns."

The aggressive AI spending has eaten into Amazon's cash flow. The company posted negative free cash flow for the second consecutive quarter, recording an outflow of $42.4 billion for the trailing twelve month period, a swing of 386% year over year. In the prior quarter, the company reported negative free cash flow of $7.6 billion.

The company said the shortfall was primarily due to an increase in purchases of property and equipment as a result of AI investments.

Capital expenditures reached $77.3 billion during the quarter, ticking up from $35.1 billion one year earlier, and coming in well above analysts' estimated $58.7 billion, according to FactSet.

Amazon said it expects fourth-quarter revenue to be in the range of $240 billion and $247 billion. The midpoint of its guidance was below Wall Street's expectations of $244.5 billion, according to LSEG. The fourth quarter is typically one of Amazon's biggest by revenue, due to the peak holiday shopping season.

Sales in Amazon's cloud segment soared 39% year over year to $45.98 billion. That topped the consensus forecast for $45.29 billion and growth of 38%, according to StreetAccount.

Jassy highlighted AWS acceleration in the earnings release, saying the company expects revenue growth rates of "over 40%" next year and in 2028. He added, "Our AWS investment in functionality and infrastructure is working."

Jassy told investors on the call that he expects AWS will be a $1 trillion revenue business in the next five to 10 years.

Amazon is facing pressure from Wall Street to show that its AI investments are lifting its revenues. The company pointed to growing traction for Bedrock, its AI model marketplace, along with its Trainium and Graviton AI chips.

Jassy said Amazon's chips business "continues to grow triple-digit percentages year over year." He added that the company plans to make its Trainium chips available externally "late in 2027," assigning a timeframe to comments Jassy made earlier this year suggesting such a move.

Third-quarter net income came in at $20.69 billion, or $1.89 per share, vs. $21.19 billion, or $1.95 per share, a year ago. The company said the decrease was driven primarily by a $9.5 billion pre-tax gain from its investment in the AI startup Anthropic, which was recorded in the 2025 third quarter.

While the AWS segment continues to hum along, results weren't as bright in Amazon's retail business, which remains its largest unit by revenue, or its advertising segment.

Online sales gained 2% year over year to $68.47 billion, which fell short of analysts' projected $69.68 billion, according to StreetAccount. It marked a slowdown from one year ago, when sales increased nearly 10%.

Advertising revenue rose 11% year over year to $19.64 billion. That missed StreetAccount consensus estimates of $20.82 billion.

Both units were impacted in part by Amazon's decision to pull its annual Prime Day sale forward in several countries from its typical third quarter timeframe into the second quarter so as to not conflict with the FIFA World Cup in July.

#208. China’s record-breaking clean tech exports offer signs of hope in the fight against emissions

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

China is exporting a record amount of clean energy technology and equipment as the energy crisis from the Iran war drives surging demand for renewables, and that will help reduce global emissions, according to a report released Thursday.

Once installed or deployed, these 2026 exports will likely avert emissions of about 450 million metric tons of carbon dioxide per year, roughly equal to Brazil’s annual emissions, says the analysis by the Finland-based Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air.

It’s a small sign of progress as global efforts to curb climate change fall short. In November, Turkey will host annual U.N. climate talks where electrification is expected to be a leading topic.

“This is a pivot point for the outlook of global energy and global emissions,” said Lauri Myllyvirta, with CREA. “We’re seeing growth in clean energy that is much, much faster than anyone predicted just a few years ago.”

Record-breaking year for Chinese exports

China is by far the largest global supplier of renewable energy equipment, from solar panels and wind turbines to electric vehicles and batteries. Most of what it makes is sold domestically, but a sizeable share is exported.

In the first nine months of 2026, CREA tracked a 61% increase in exports of electric vehicles, a 45% jump in shipments of wind turbines, a 39% spike in battery exports and 22% growth in overseas sales of solar equipment, compared to the same period last year.

Southeast Asia was a major destination. China’s clean energy exports to the region jumped nearly 160% from last year to this September, according to Ember, an independent global energy think tank.

The value of China’s electric vehicle exports hit $72.8 billion in the first nine months of this year, up from $39.7 billion last year, according to the research firm Atlas Public Policy.

Chinese clean tech dents emissions

If these export trends continue for the rest of 2026, about 8,400 million metric tons of carbon dioxide will be averted over the lifetime of all the clean tech China exported this year, CREA estimates.

That’s about 24% of annual global emissions, more than the United States’ and India’s emissions combined.

The analysis by CREA is based on export data, the electricity systems in destination countries and Chinese emissions during manufacturing. Independent experts said the estimate was in line with the scale of China’s exports, but were unable to validate the data.

Despite President Donald Trump’s higher tariffs, Chinese clean tech exports to the U.S. rose 13% in January-September compared to a year earlier.

“The demand is much stronger than I expected,” Myllyvirta said. “That’s certainly noteworthy.”

China is both the world’s biggest provider of clean tech equipment and the largest global source of carbon emissions.

Muyi Yang, a senior policy analyst at Ember, likened China’s energy transition to replacing the engine of a ship while it’s still at sea.

Interest in renewables likely to persist, experts say

The drastic war-related disruptions to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, usually the source for a fifth of the world’s oil and gas, hit import-dependent Asian countries first and hardest.

Renewed interest in clean energy options is likely to persist even after the war, said Yu Sun Chin, with the research group Zero Carbon Analytics.

Consumers in Southeast Asia are paying more for their electricity and fuel and opting for renewable alternatives such as rooftop solar.

Laos, Thailand and Vietnam have offered various incentives for installing solar and purchasing electric vehicles to help reduce dependence on imported fossil fuels.

The Philippines implemented similar policies and became the second-largest destination for Chinese solar exports in the first quarter of 2026. Indonesia has pledged to install 100 gigawatts of solar power by 2029.

“The surge in clean energy policies in Southeast Asia shows there is a paradigm shift happening,” Chin said. “Considering Southeast Asia is one of the most vulnerable regions to climate impacts, every emission cut matters in limiting a rise in global temperatures.”

#198. Anthropic’s IPO is selling investors a story. The numbers tell another one

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Welcome to AI Decoded, Fast Company’s weekly newsletter that breaks down the most important news in the world of AI. I’m Mark Sullivan, a senior writer at Fast Company, covering emerging tech, AI, and tech policy.

Sign up to receive this newsletter every week via email here. And if you have comments on this issue and/or ideas for future ones, drop me a line at sullivan@fastcompany.com, and follow me on X @thesullivan.

Anthropic IPO: Do retail investors know what they’re getting into?

Anthropic officially filed its IPO papers earlier this week, hoping to raise up to $100 billion, and as much as 20% of the shares may be reserved for retail investors (aka regular folks). On Tuesday, Meta said it would sell equity shares on the public market to raise $95 billion to finance its AI infrastructure build-out. Retail investors have long been exposed to the AI business through shares in tech behemoths like Google and Microsoft, but they’re now getting the chance to place bets on pure-play AI labs like Anthropic and OpenAI.

Do they know what they’re getting into? I have my doubts. Does anyone really know what we’re getting into? Retail investors make investing decisions differently from institutional investors (such as pension funds or hedge funds). While institutional investors dig into the financials of prospective investments, retail investors often base their decisions on the narrative a company lays out as it goes public.

“Retail investors buy the story, not the model,” PitchBook senior late-stage research analyst Harrison Rolfes tells Fast Company. “The story for Anthropic is $11.6 billion of revenue in a single quarter; the model is $8.9 billion spent on compute in six months and more than $430 billion of non-cancelable compute contracts.” The financials in Anthropic’s S-1 imply an 85% gross margin, but Rolfes points out that after factoring in the cost of the compute that trains the next model, only “about 45 cents of each revenue dollar is left.”

A big chunk of Anthropic’s commitments—at least $269 billion—is owed to Amazon, Google, and Microsoft for cloud computing resources through 2036. Ten years is a long timeline for a commitment that big, especially when it’s not even clear that AI will continue to be served almost exclusively from cloud servers a decade from now. WebAI’s David Stout argues that as AI becomes more efficient and capable of running directly on devices (see Apple’s new iMac and MacBook Pro with the M6 chip), the industry risks pouring trillions into data centers it may not ultimately need. WebAI is one of a wave of companies building private, localized AI that runs on devices organizations already own.

As the cadence of new model releases accelerates, enterprises are struggling to keep up—to build the infrastructure and applications they need to take advantage of the latest models’ capabilities. The pace at which enterprises can adapt their processes and people to AI may be limited by forces outside the AI labs’ control. For example, the best AI model is still only as good as the contextual data an enterprise can give it, and preparing that data and building the conduits to get it to the right place takes time and resources.

A lot is riding on how well workers adapt. Workers will need time to build trust in AI to produce accurate outputs and make good decisions. How quickly that happens depends on the specific application, the company’s culture and leadership, and the industry in which it operates. For example, the healthcare industry is inefficient and wasteful and has much to gain from AI, but it’s also notoriously slow to adopt new technologies.

The big question is whether enterprises will continue to spend aggressively on AI so that Anthropic can earn enough profit to cover its huge infrastructure commitments. Enterprises will have to keep pushing—and investing—even when they aren’t yet seeing measurable productivity and efficiency gains from AI.

None of this is assured, and that’s leaving out other business risks, like new safety regulations that could slow down model development. Interactive Brokers’ chief strategist Steve Sosnick, a longtime Wall Street trader and analyst, told Axios that a lot of things will have to go right for Anthropic, OpenAI, and the like to become success stories. “I don’t want to say precarious, but if you step back, you know that it requires a huge amount of, I don’t know, faith.”

The parents of Bowen Wan, a 31-year-old Amazon software engineer with epilepsy, sued OpenAI and Sam Altman in San Francisco Superior Court on Tuesday, alleging that the company’s ChatGPT chatbot, powered by its GPT-4o model, coached Wan to taper off her seizure medication and conceal the plan from her doctors. Google DeepMind releases Genie 4

Google DeepMind released Genie 4 as a research preview Tuesday, its first real-time world model to generate audio natively, including ambient sound and dialogue that adapts as a scene changes. Users can steer a simulation mid-scene using natural language, changing the weather or adding a person, while the model simulates physics such as aerodynamics and collisions. Google wrote in a blog post that “understanding and simulating the world is a crucial aspect of building AGI.” The company believes Genie 4 could be used to create training environments for robots and AI agents, as well as new interactive experiences for humans. During testing, filmmakers used the model to visualize takes, photojournalists turned archival photographs into explorable scenes, and game developers prototyped games before writing code.

AI agents checked the biggest proof in mathematics

Swarms of AI agents built by the Palo Alto startup Axiom Math translated the longest proof ever written into computer-checkable code in 24 days, the company announced this week. The agents were part of a system called AxiomProver, which the company says uses a changing mix of AI models, but declined to name them. Axiom’s engineers and mathematicians directed the work.

The proof, called the Classification of Finite Simple Groups, runs roughly 15,000 pages across more than 500 papers, assembled by about 100 mathematicians over decades. The agents rendered it as about 43.1 million lines of Lean, a language in which a computer verifies every logical step. Axiom’s agents were not discovering anything. They were confirming that mathematics humans already accepted holds up line by line.

Apple pitches the new iMac as a machine for running AI locally

Apple announced a new iMac powered by its M6 chip Tuesday, touting the machine’s ability to run AI workloads directly on the device as a major selling point. The company says a dual 16-core Neural Engine and memory bandwidth of up to 170GB/s allow the iMac to “rip through AI tasks like running LLMs on device.” Apple claims the machine delivers up to 5x faster on-device AI performance than the previous M4 chip and 12x faster prompt processing in LM Studio than the original M1 chip. Siri can answer questions about onscreen content using Visual Intelligence, while Apple Intelligence can write and edit text in a user’s own style in Mail and Messages. Interestingly, one of Apple’s iMac product photos shows Anthropic’s Claude app running alongside Apple’s Pixelmator Pro graphic design app. The new iMac starts at $1,499 and ships November 6.

Google starts selling its AI chips to the companies it competes with

Google will sell its tensor processing units to Oracle and OpenAI, The Information reported. Oracle becomes the first major cloud provider outside Google to offer TPUs to customers, buying the chips and running them in its own data centers. OpenAI plans to buy them too, and is hiring an engineer to optimize its frontier models for the hardware, per the report. Google built TPUs more than a decade ago for internal use and began selling them directly this year. Anthropic is already a customer.

#139. Nvidia responds to report Jensen Huang was target of Chinese asset, says diversion allegations were 'investigated and debunked'

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An Nvidia spokesperson on Thursday pushed back on a New York Times report that detailed American intelligence agencies surveilling a person close to Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang because they thought she could be a Chinese intelligence asset.

According to the Times report, which cited people briefed on intelligence surveillance, Alice Huang, who is linked to the Singapore-based cloud Megaspeed, developed a close relationship with Nvidia's Jensen Huang.

Intelligence agencies worried that she was a spy, helping the Chinese government gain information from Nvidia and Jensen Huang, and fretted that Nvidia's CEO had access to President Donald Trump.

The Nvidia spokesperson pushed back on the story in a statement to CNBC, saying that Singapore-based Megaspeed is another one of the company's regional cloud customers, and that Jensen Huang's interactions with its officials are not unusual, because he speaks with thousands of customers every year.

The spokesperson added that customers don't get access to Nvidia trade secrets or relevant national security information.

The report also alleged that Megaspeed was previously suspected of helping companies evade U.S. export controls on Nvidia's AI chips, which aren't allowed to be shipped to China.

Nvidia said that Megaspeed had already been investigated for evading export controls.

"The diversion allegations against this regional cloud were thoroughly investigated and debunked last year," the spokesperson said in a statement.

Nvidia implied that the U.S. government had not briefed Nvidia on Alice Huang.

"We're confident that if the U.S. government believes that any person is a threat to American industry, it would let us know," the spokesperson said.

Nvidia has had to walk a tightrope in Washington as its AI chips have become a powerful tool for all AI developers and a bargaining chip in economic competition with China.

Politicians and analysts say that Nvidia's AI chips could be used to create Chinese military AI models, or commercial AI models that could compete and eventually surpass American technology.

As AI has become more important to the government, Nvidia's Huang has befriended Trump and has given pro-American speeches about building U.S. AI infrastructure.

Huang has appeared with Trump at state dinners, AI summits, and international trips, all while pushing for certain Nvidia chips to get a green light to be shipped to China.

Earlier this year the U.S. government cleared Nvidia's H200 chip to be shipped to China, but Chinese regulators prevented their imports. H200s are Nvidia's Hopper generation chip, not the current faster Blackwell generation currently used by American AI developers.

The Nvidia spokesperson said the Chinese government is not looking for Nvidia products from regional clouds like Megaspeed.

"Instead, the PRC government blocked U.S. AI chips, protecting its own industry and resulting in the largest write-offs in U.S. tech history," the Nvidia spokesperson said. Last year, Nvidia wrote off about $4.5 billion in Chinese-tuned AI chips.

#70. Faisal Islam: This Budget was micro and that was deliberate

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It was not a mini Budget, but it was rather small. It might be called the "micro Budget".

Small does not necessarily mean bad, but it clearly falls short of radical action to create a promised new economic model.

A new "theory of growth" was not unveiled as promised by the prime minister Andy Burnham.

It was preamble, roadmap, consultation, perhaps the starter to the main course, which appears to be the PM's 10-year plan next month.

It went unsaid, but the clear stategic aim of Healey's statement was to be the "anti-mini Budget".

This "steady as she goes" predictable fiscal event set out to avoid the aftermath seen following Liz Truss's mini-budget, in which unexpected and unfunded tax cuts spooked the bond and currency markets.

The markets didn't even flicker on Healey's announcement of Â£65bn of extra borrowing over the next five years - government interest costs have actually fallen since Wednesday, against the backdrop of market turmoil due to the US-Iran war.

The core of the Budget was to get Downing Street through the impacts of the Middle East conflict shock on energy and borrowing costs.

If these pressures prove enduring there are some unpalatable decisions that will have to be grasped within the next year. The can will have to be picked up rather than kicked down the road again.

It buys more time, more "breathing space", as it were, for the government and the prime minister's long-term strategic plans.

The detail may come more quickly than feared. On welfare, the Milburn Review process is clearly heading for some sort of welfare-to-work scheme, even a separate system for younger workers.

It will cost money up front to subsidise work opportunities and hope to save more from welfare bills in the future. The chancellor saw at close quarters Gordon Brown do this twice two decades ago.

The Office for Budget Responsbility's (OBR) economic forecast showed the sheer rise in the welfare bill, which is set to jump to Â£431bn in 2031, if pensioner support is included.

There are two further areas where the chancellor laid down markers, with little fiscal cost, in terms of the long term plan.

On energy, there is some significance to the "cheaper power" mission announced, reshaping what had previously been a focus on clean power.

While still committed to Net Zero, a series of technical changes to powers over levies and renewable auctions, will seek to turn the tide on additional pressures on energy costs.

On devolution, the plan to allow local mayors to keep some extra business rate and income tax revenues, are more radical than expected.

There are trade offs here, but it essentially provides incentives for regional leaders to grow their economies, workforce and tax base, that are normal elsewhere, but have not existed in the UK's very centralised tax system.

How the PM will square other aspects of his "theory of growth" with the chancellor's spending constraints is the other significant challenge.

Can he announce a 10-year plan without detailing the Liverpool to Manchester high speed train line, or a rebirth of some cancelled HS2 lines he championed a year ago?

Away from the long term, the OBR forecast point to a very tricky economic backdrop until the spring, and a trickier spending review, given the need to accommodate the new defence target of 3% of national income by 2030.

The PM's insistence he will not call an election for a year are eminently believable given the risk of 5% inflation and declining household income.

A "focused" Budget at an uncertain time achieved its narrow aim of a bit of "breathing space" for households and also for the government itself.

But the evident gaps in the Budget will need filling in come the PM's upcoming 10-year plan.

#80. Bereaved families will not face tax bill over pension delays under new rules

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bereaved families will no longer be hit by an unexpected tax bill because of severe delays paying out civil service pensions, following a change in Wednesday's Budget.

Under previous HMRC rules, certain lump sum pension payments had to be paid within two years of the pension scheme being notified of a death to remain tax-free.

Campaigners had warned some families could face an unfair financial penalty because of delays which were not their fault.

The government has now changed the rules so payments will no longer become taxable solely because more than two years has passed.

The Civil Service Pension scheme manages the pensions of 1.7 million public sector workers in the UK.

Outsourcing firm Capita took over the administration of the scheme from a company called MyCSP in December 2025, saying it inherited a backlog of more than 86,000 cases.

Despite a two-year transition period to prepare for the takeover, problems have persisted and by February the backlog had grown to 120,000 cases.

Retired civil servants and bereaved families have been left waiting months for pension payments, with some struggling to pay bills.

According to the latest figures up to 30 September, external, 1,643 outstanding cases of bereavement or death in service are more than 100 days old.

The Cabinet Office, which has overall responsibility for the Civil Service Pension Scheme, previously said only a small number of these cases were approaching the two-year threshold.

However the PCS union, which represents civil servants, had warned there were "serious questions" over how long cases would remain unresolved.

Civil service pension backlog 'overwhelmed' Capita, boss says

Emergency loans offered to civil servants waiting on pensions

Retired civil servants left in pension limbo

Rob Wassell, 58, had to wait more than two years following the death of his brother until he finally received the lump sum from his civil service pension on Tuesday.

After his case was highlighted by the BBC last week, Rob says he got a call from the pension scheme's complaints department who told him the payment was on its way.

However, because it was made after two years it was taxed at 45%, amounting to thousands of pounds.

If the payment was made after the rule change, it would not have been subject to tax.

Rob told the BBC he was pleased the government had addressed the issue in the Budget as this would mean others in his situation would not face a tax bill.

While he said he was happy to finally get his payment it was "a bitter pill to swallow" as he is now waiting for the outcome of a complaint review to find out if the tax will be reimbursed.

Rob said he was hopeful he would get the money back, adding: "I don't want to have to fight it anymore, it's been an ordeal already."

Rob Wassell was taxed on the payment from his brother's civil service pension

Pensions minister Torsten Bell confirmed the rule change in a letter to the chairman of Parliament's Public Accounts Committee, external, Conservative MP Sir Geoffrey Clifton-Brown, who had raised concerns about the issue after it was highlighted by BBC Radio 4's Money Box Live programme.

Bell said the move would "make the position fairer for all grieving families and friends where there are delays in the payment of death benefits, whether in the Civil Service or any other pensions scheme".

He added: "Removing the tax deadline does not remove [a pension scheme's] wider duties to administer schemes prudently and without avoidable delay."

Under the previous rules, if someone died before the age of 75, a pension lump sum death benefit was only tax-free if it was paid within two years of the pension scheme first becoming aware of the death, and if the value was below the deceased's Lump Sum and Death Benefit Allowance.

However, the government has now removed the two-year deadline so payments will no longer become taxable solely because they were made more than two years after a pension scheme was notified of the death.

The change applies to payments made from 28 October.

In his letter, the pensions minister said people who had already been impacted by avoidable delays and been taxed as a result could seek compensation through the pension scheme's complaints process and the Pensions Ombudsman.

Sir Geoffrey welcomed the announcement but said it does not "absolve Capita from processing and making payments to members of this scheme in a timely manner".

Fran Heathcote, general secretary of the PCS union, also welcomed the change but said "this situation should never have arisen in the first place".

She added: "Bereaved families must not pay the price for failures in the pension system.

"Capita is not off the hook, it continues to fail members and their families, which is why the scheme must be brought back in-house."

Capita has previously apologised for the impact of delays on pension scheme members and their families.

The company has said it is working to resolve outstanding cases as quickly as possible, with a particular focus on people who have been waiting the longest and those facing financial hardship or challenging circumstances.
