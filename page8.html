#616. Free hearing bus helping 2,000 kids faces closure as funding ends

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Eli Wright (middle), Ericka Wright (left) and Jody Wright have benefited from the service.  (ABC Western Plains: Jen McCutcheon)

More than 2,000 children receive free hearing tests in western NSW each year through the Hear Our Heart Ear Bus Project.

A test helped diagnose Nyngan teenager Eli Wright with a cyst in his ear that was millimetres from his brain.

The mobile children's ear clinic is facing closure if $400,000 in funding is not secured by the middle of next year.

Jody Wright knows just how lucky he is to have a healthy teenager at home.

His son Eli had chronic middle ear problems as a child, and Mr Wright said a chance screening by a mobile hearing service in Dubbo, in western NSW, saved the boy's life.

Eli Wright has had three surgeries to remove a growth from his middle ear. (ABC Western Plains: Jen McCutcheon)

Eli was diagnosed with a cholesteatoma, a cyst that grows behind the eardrum in the middle ear, which can be fatal if left untreated.

"With his third operation … they cleaned the growth out, but it was millimetres from his brain," Mr Wright said.

"We thought we were going to lose him at times, but he's pulled through.

But the free service is at risk of closing.

The charity behind it has lost its main sponsor and needs to find up to $400,000 a year to keep running.

The Hear Our Heart Ear Bus Project offers free hearing tests in the western region. (ABC Western Plains: Jen McCutcheon)

The Hear Our Heart Ear Bus Project began in late 2011 as part of the Dubbo District Deaf Club.

The makeshift mobile children's ear clinic travels about 9,000 kilometres each year to more than 40 stops in Dubbo and western NSW, providing free hearing tests to pre-school and school-aged children.

Project director Donna Rees said in the past five years, "over 10,000 tests have been conducted, and half of those have been Aboriginal children within the region".

The tests have led to 310 children being referred to ear, nose and throat specialists, and 51 being fitted with a hearing device.

Previously, the allied health service had significant philanthropic backing through bequests.

The Hear Our Heart Ear Bus Project makes 40 stops a year around western NSW. (Supplied)

The Hall Foundation has been the principal sponsor since 2018, but made its last contribution in July.

The project is looking for new supporters to cover running costs of up to $400,000 a year or it risks closing by the middle of next year.

"The funding is desperately needed to continue funding the staff," Ms Rees said.

"We've literally got 10 fingers in 10 pies or more."

The Western NSW Local Health District declined to comment on the service.

Without the service, many families would have to travel to Dubbo for private screening.

"We'd never have been able to afford help, there's no way," Mr Wright said.

The Hear Our Heart Ear Bus Project only has funding secured until mid-next year. (ABC Western Plains: Jen McCutcheon)

The aim of the bus is to reduce rates of middle ear infection, or otitis media, and hearing loss among Aboriginal and non-Aboriginal children.

The condition is a major cause of long-term ear and hearing problems in Aboriginal children, with about 8,800 children affected in 2022-23, according to the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare.

University of Newcastle professor and Worimi man Kelvin Kong is one of Australia's leading ear, nose and throat specialists.

He has spent his career improving access to culturally safe health care for Indigenous communities and said services like the ear bus were vital to help close the gap.

"It is so crucial that our children are hearing because if they're not hearing, they aren't learning.

"Hear Our Hearts do such an invaluable service to the community and they've been doing it for such a long time … they are incredible."

Kelvin Kong has a particular interest in treating ear diseases in Indigenous children.  (Supplied)

In the case of the Wright family, it was not just Eli who received help.

"Sebby, my 7-year-old, he's got grommets through the bus and not only them two but my wife as well; she's got grommets and hearing aids from them," Mr Wright said.

"We probably would never have gotten it without the bus, we are very grateful."

Mr Wright said since the screenings, both Eli and Sebby were making inroads at school.

"They were having trouble in school, but now they are actually learning and they're coming home happy that they've gone to school so we're getting on with life now," he said.

Ms Rees said without the bus, there would be a lot of children sitting in school with hidden hearing loss undetected and undiagnosed and not meeting their potential with educational outcomes.

#11. How AI and 'doorbell cameras' can reduce growing risk of human encounters with polar bears

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

The new tool is among several solutions being developed to protect both people and polar bears as the impacts of climate change increase the likelihood of dangerous encounters

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Artificial intelligence-powered "doorbell cameras" are being trialled by an international team of specialists to alert local communities to approaching polar bears amid the growing threat of human conflict.

Created by the conservation charity Polar Bears International, the initiative is part of a broader push to safeguard both human lives and wildlife as global warming escalates the frequency of hazardous interactions.

Known as Bear-vision, the technology evolves from earlier "Bear-dar" systems, which integrated radar with video equipment to spot movement across the Arctic landscape and track nearby animals.

According to the organisation, Bear-vision provides a cost-effective and accessible warning tool designed to notify residential properties and commercial premises whenever a bear draws near.

Researchers are setting up the initial Bear-vision unit at a waste site in Churchill, a northern coastal town situated along Hudson Bay in the Canadian province of Manitoba.

This deployment is intended to assist local residents in overseeing the site during a period when polar bears congregate to await the freezing of Hudson Bay so they can hunt seals, a gathering that also draws thousands of visiting tourists.

Krista Wright, executive director of Polar Bears International, said: “We’re grateful to the Churchill community, who we’ve been honoured to work with over the decades on human-polar bear co-existence, ensuring that conservation benefits not just wildlife but the community too.

“We’re experiencing first-hand how co-existence is becoming part of everyday life in more places.”

Elsewhere, the conservation group is exploring other novel methods to safely ward off bears, including drones.

Polar Bear International and Brigham Young University are collaborating with Churchill’s Polar Bear Alert Programme to test drones as an affordable and non-lethal way to move bears away from communities safely.

This deterrent research project is also testing ultrasonic sound, conductive materials, and a new odour-based bear spray formula.

Polar Bear International said the doorbell camera technology was being tested at the Churchill waste dump alongside to keep polar bears away from the facility.

It comes after a fire destroyed the site in 2024, which led to bears pawing through the rubbish to find food and causing concerns around hungry polar bears not only ingesting toxic rubbish but then being attracted by the smell of food waste.

The team has also installed new electric fences, deployed its Bear-dar technology and sent a group of its experts from Churchill to tour a similar waste management facility in Greenland.

The annual Polar Bear Week 2026, organised by Polar Bear International, begins this Sunday to mark the seasonal gathering of the animals.

The group highlighted that melting sea ice driven by climate change now leaves these animals stranded waiting on land for approximately one month longer than their grandparents did.

This means measures to ensure safe co-existence between the town and the animals are becoming increasingly important, it added.

#51. Counterfeit beauty products found to contain deadly chemicals after major raid

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

Perfumes, skincare and cosmetics seized by Trading Standards have been found to contain potentially deadly chemicals

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Counterfeit beauty products have been found to contain a plethora of deadly chemicals following testing conducted by Trading Standards of goods seized.

Perfumes, skincare and cosmetics have become among Trading Standards’ most commonly seized counterfeits, with almost half of all goods found to contain harmful substances.

One skincare product was found to contain 13,200 parts per million of mercury, significant levels of lead in lipstick, eyeshadow and face products.

Nicotine products were found in perfume and fake fragrances as well, which were comprised of around 80% methanol.

According to the latest Intellectual Property Office (IPO) Counterfeit Goods Tracker, an estimated 20% of 18-24-year-olds who bought beauty products in 2025 bought them knowingly, a huge increase from 6% in 2023.

The Police Intellectual Property Crime Unit and Greater Manchester Police seized 1,666 counterfeit hair products in July from a warehouse in Manchester, including hair extensions, shampoo and conditioners.

It followed a raid in Bolton, in which Trading Standards officers seized £2 million worth of counterfeit goods, including perfume, cosmetics, clothing and vapes at a production facility in a commercial property.

The IPO’s Face the Fakes campaign is working with content creators and the beauty industry to raise awareness of the potential harms of counterfeit beauty products.

Independent toxicologist Rani Ghosh said: “When you hear that mercury has been found in face cream, lead in make-up or nicotine in perfume, it raises serious questions about what is going into these products and how they are being made.

“Some reactions to unsafe cosmetics may be visible, such as irritation, swelling or changes to the skin. But what concerns me as a toxicologist is what you might not see. Substances such as mercury and lead can affect the body internally, and those effects may not be immediately obvious.

“Counterfeit beauty products can look incredibly convincing. You cannot tell from the packaging what chemicals are inside, how the product has been made, or what risk it might pose. That is why knowing where your beauty products come from matters.”

Helen Barnham, head of enforcement engagement at the IPO, said: “These findings show why counterfeit beauty products can pose such serious risks to consumers.

“Products can look remarkably convincing, but behind the packaging they can contain dangerous substances that have no place in the cosmetics people use every day, and can be made in appalling, unhygienic conditions.

“Counterfeiters exploit the names and reputations of brands that consumers know and trust, while bypassing the rigorous safety standards that legitimate businesses work hard to meet.

“With our latest research showing a sharp rise in counterfeit beauty buying among young adults, we want to make sure people understand the potential risks and know how to protect themselves. Our Face the Fakes campaign is giving consumers practical advice to shop more safely, including checking who is actually selling a product and choosing trusted sources.”

Dr Emma Meredith, director-general of the Cosmetic, Toiletry and Perfumery Association (CTPA), said: “There is a rigorous safety framework behind every legitimate cosmetic product sold in the UK.

“Ingredients are carefully controlled, products have to be made in clean and hygienic conditions, and they must undergo a safety assessment. Companies have clear legal responsibilities for the products they place on the market.

“A huge amount of work happens behind the scenes to make sure people can use their cosmetics with confidence. Counterfeit products can bypass those safeguards, which is why knowing who you are buying from and choosing trusted sources really matters.”

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new survey conducted by the BPS found nine in ten psychologists are concerned over their patients’ use of AI

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Psychologists have sounded the alarm over AI chatbots providing mental health advice to people, dubbing the information “unqualified and harmful”.

A survey conducted by the British Psychological Society (BPS) found that psychologists have noted a 49% increase in the number of patients seeking support from chatbots in the past year.

The poll, involving 197 participants comprised of psychologists, counsellors and psychotherapists, found that seven in 10 are somewhat or very concerned about the use of AI for mental health advice.

Nearly 88% (nine in 10) sounded the alarm that patients are relying too heavily on AI, while around 76% of survey participants said the technology is negatively affecting the mental wellbeing of their clients.

Some 80% told the survey they are concerned clients are becoming overly reliant on the technology, while around three quarters (76%) said it is negatively affecting client wellbeing.

Dr Laura Dean, president of the BPS, said: “This research shows there is real cause for concern for those that use AI for mental health therapy, and that concern is coming directly from psychological practitioners on the frontline providing vital in-person support.

“The risk of unregulated open access AI chatbots providing unqualified and harmful advice remains very real.”

Dr Dean warned that the integration of AI into healthcare “can only work with the right safeguards that protect both psychological professionals and the people they support”.

She added: “Although AI offers many benefits, it cannot replace the vital human element in mental health care.

“More research and insight from psychological practitioners, and people seeking support using AI tools, is needed to understand how and where human-led therapeutic services and AI can be safely integrated to deliver the right care and outcomes.”

One practitioner surveyed told the BPS: “I am concerned about not understanding exactly what AI does, how it works, and how it comes up with its answers.

“I am also concerned about people turning to AI because it feels “safer” (avoiding the relational risk of building trust with another human being), and we therefore increase the isolation and lack of social skills and contact in society, which is directly linked to quality of life and wellbeing.”

Another called for “accountability”, adding: AI often is seen as “free” but because of this, like with many social media platforms, the companies that run them have free rein to do as they please with little to no legal responsibilities.”

The BPS poll comes after a survey of 2,000 children aged 11-17 by Barnardo’s and the National Counselling and Psychotherapy Society (NCPS) found more than four in 10 (43%) had used AI for advice or emotional support.

Rosie Weatherley, information content manager at the charity Mind, said: “These survey findings echo what we are hearing from people and seeing through emerging evidence – that even casual use of generative AI can cause harm. So much is unknown about this new and ever-changing technology, particularly when it comes to the impact of long-term use.

“We’re supportive of digital tools that help people navigate information to make more sense of mental health and the services they can access, but these must not become a replacement for great quality care, where people feel properly heard and seen.

“We know that people still value human support over AI tools and we must not lose sight of this.”

#20. Can testosterone improve menopause symptoms? Here's what the experts say

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

Social media has been rife with claims testosterone helps with menopause, but a new study finds insufficient evidence

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Testosterone has shown “insufficient” evidence to support social media claims that it can improve certain symptoms linked to the menopause, according to a new study.

Social media has been rife with claims that testosterone can improve brain fog and memory symptoms in menopausal women.

However, experts say that while anecdotal information exists around the effects of testosterone, there is currently not sufficient evidence to make any definitive claims.

According to University College London (UCL), prescriptions for testosterone have “increased across NHS and private clinics in the UK”, which are largely “reflecting growing interest in its role during menopause”.

The team reviewed five studies which looked at testosterone can improve learning and memory in post menopausal women.

Some 244 women with an average age of 57 involved in the review were given either a placebo dummy drug or testosterone at varying levels.

The studies took place over four to 26 weeks. The research team did not find statistically significant improvements for cognitive function among participants taking testosterone.

“The findings should be interpreted as insufficient to establish an effect, rather than as evidence of no effect,” the authors wrote in the journal Reproduction And Fertility.

Professor Aimee Spector, senior author of the study and director of the UCL Menopause Mind Lab, said: “Social media is flooded with claims that testosterone improves memory and brain fog, and we hear of many women taking testosterone specifically to manage these symptoms during the menopause.

“While there may be some anecdotal evidence for this in perimenopausal women, we cannot claim testosterone improves cognition during menopause without further evidence.

“More research is needed to understand any links between testosterone and brain health.”

Corresponding author Dr Alexandra Faulkner, from UCL’s division of psychology and language sciences, added: “Our review found that the available evidence is limited and insufficient to determine the effect of testosterone therapy on cognition in women during the menopause transition and post menopause.

“This reflects a lack of sufficient evidence rather than evidence of no effect.”

Around 13 million women are estimated to be peri or menopausal in the UK. Menopause happens when a woman’s periods change and eventually stop due to lower hormone levels.

It is thought that testosterone levels do not acutely change during menopause, but instead begin to decline from a woman’s mid-20s to early 30s, the UCL team said.

The NHS website states that testosterone gel or cream can help to improve low libido for some women in the menopause.

The hormone can be obtained on prescription from a menopause specialist, the website adds.

The research team called for more work to examine the impact of testosterone on cognition during the menopause.

Experts at Cardiff University are currently studying whether adding testosterone to standard hormone replacement therapy (HRT) can reduce menopausal symptoms beyond its effect on libido.

The trial, which will involve 400 women and is due to conclude in 2029, aims to publish findings on whether testosterone can impact cognition, exercise, motivation and energy levels among menopausal women.

Earlier this year it emerged that women are posing as men to buy testosterone from online pharmacies to get hold of prescriptions.

Menopause specialist Dr Michelle Olver told the BBC that she had seen women with up to 12 times the safe level of the hormone after online purchases.

#143. Erin Patterson to die in prison after losing bid to overturn mushroom murder convictions

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Victorian court of appeal rules Patterson should be jailed for life with no parole over deadly beef wellington lunch that killed three people

Erin Patterson mushroom murders appeal – live updates

Erin Patterson has lost her bid to overturn her convictions and been resentenced to life behind bars with no chance of release, as Victoria’s highest court handed down its decision on two appeals regarding her triple murder case.

The court of appeal ruled on the two appeals on Friday, after justices Lesley Taylor, Stephen McLeish and Peter Kidd heard arguments from her legal team and prosecutors in August.

Patterson, 52, did not attend the 15-minute court hearing in person, and showed no emotion on the video feed as McLeish outlined why her appeal had failed, and how the director of public prosecutions had succeeded in seeking a longer sentence.

Patterson was last year sentenced to life imprisonment, with a non-parole period of 33 years, for the murders of her estranged husband’s parents, Don and Gail Patterson, and his aunt Heather Wilkinson, after she hosted them for lunch.

She was also convicted of the attempted murder of Heather’s husband, Ian Wilkinson, who survived the beef wellington laced with death cap mushrooms after spending weeks in hospital.

McLeish said the court had granted leave to appeal on the first two grounds of Patterson’s appeal, but dismissed the appeal, and dismissed the five other grounds raised by her lawyers.

He said the court granted the DPP appeal against the sentence, however, as it was clear the original sentence did not properly reflect the seriousness of her offending.

McLeish said Patterson sustained an intent to murder all four people for three weeks, from the time they were invited to the lunch, during the meal, and during the “protracted suffering and ultimate deaths that resulted”.

The offending was “carefully planned and deliberate”, he said, and involved an “elaborate series of deceptions” including claims that she had cancer, denying she had foraged mushrooms, and lying about the source of the mushrooms.

“This offending was extraordinarily cold and calculated, and stands to be denounced in the strongest possible terms,” McLeish said.

When the judges left court, Patterson also stood, took a book from beside her, and walked off-screen.

In the courtroom, family members hugged and were emotional, and detectives involved in the case looked elated. Nobody commented outside court.

Patterson now must apply to the high court for leave to appeal, should she wish to continue fighting her conviction or new sentence.

She is one of only a handful of Victorian offenders sentenced to life without parole, and one of the only women in Australian history to be given the sentence.

In August, the court heard Patterson should have been given no possibility of ever being released from prison, or a non-parole period so lengthy that she was likely to die behind bars.

Her lawyers argued seven grounds for her appeal, including that allowing jurors to stay in the same hotel as police and the prosecution while they deliberated over their verdicts in the triple murder trial represented a “catastrophic” failure of the justice system.

Richard Edney, for Patterson, had said the failure to properly separate the jury during their deliberations was a fundamental irregularity that had denied her a fair trial.

“What we say has occurred here in the applicant’s trial was an inexplicable and perhaps still unexplained failure of a process,” Edney told the court in August.

They also argued that the trial judge, Christopher Beale, erred in ruling that photos and videos related to mushrooms and found on an SD card in Patterson’s home was inadmissible, as it tended to support her evidence that she foraged for mushrooms.

During the hearings, Brendan Kissane KC, the DPP, said that even though Patterson would be aged in her 80s by the time she was eligible for parole, her sentence was still too lenient.

When asked whether a longer non-parole period would essentially damn Patterson to dying behind bars, and had “some artifice of it being life with life”, Kissane told the court: “It has its harsh aspect to it, of course, we accept that.

“But in our submission that is not a matter that is outweighed by the seriousness of the offending.”

Republicans are facing a difficult political environment ahead of November’s midterm elections, driven in large part by President Donald Trump’s historically low approval ratings.

With the pivotal elections approaching, Trump’s approval rating dropped to 30 percent earlier this month, according to polling from the Pew Research Center. The slump marks the lowest point of his second term and represents the lowest level any sitting president has reached heading into the midterms since Harry Truman in 1946, as reported by TIME.

“This is historically low approval ratings for a president,” Peter Enns, a professor in the department of government at Cornell University and co-founder of the survey research company Verasight, told TIME.

Data indicates that much of the public discontent with the administration stems from economic performance. An Associated Press-NORC Center for Public Affairs Research poll released earlier this month found that only 26 percent of U.S. adults approved of how Trump was tackling the economy, and just 17 percent approved of his handling of the cost of living.

Foreign policy has generated similar pushback. The same AP-NORC poll showed that only 28 percent of respondents expressed support for Trump’s handling of the war in Iran.

“Part of the reason Trump’s approval ratings are so low is he’s pushing policies and pushing positions that are very unpopular, and so the conflict in Iran is a great example; the public understands that this was Trump’s decision, and they understand this connects to extremely high gas prices,” Enns told TIME. “It’s very unusual for a President to position himself on the side of so many unpopular positions.”

These voter priorities could prove decisive at the ballot box. Pew Research data shows that 84 percent of registered voters rank the economy as a “very important issue” heading into the midterms, while 57 percent say foreign policy is “very important” to their vote.

Midterm elections are routinely difficult for the party controlling the White House, as voters frequently express frustration with the sitting administration. This dynamic is a “well-known phenomenon” resulting from dissatisfaction among both opposition voters and a president's supporters who feel policy progress has lagged, Enns told TIME.

“That doesn’t mean everybody’s changing their vote,” Enns said. “It just means the backlash, the tides, are working against the incumbent president.”

Costas Panagopoulos, a professor of political science at Northeastern University, told the magazine that voters often sought “partisan balance in Washington,” particularly when one party held unified control over the executive and legislative branches.

“There’s simply nobody else to blame for government actions and current policies if voters are unhappy with those besides the Republican Party [right now],” Panagopoulos said. “It’s very easy for [voters] to point the finger to the Republicans if they’re not happy with what they’re seeing.”

Though historical precedents usually favor the opposition party, rare exceptions have occurred. Following the September 11 terrorist attacks, President George W. Bush maintained a 63 percent approval rating, allowing Republicans to gain seats in both the House and Senate during the 2002 midterms.

Current polling metrics favor Democrats in voter engagement. A recent PBS News/NPR/Marist poll showed that 91 percent of Democrats expressed enthusiasm about voting in the midterms, compared to 86 percent of Republicans. Democrats have also shown strength in competitive state races, including in Texas, where Democratic Senate candidate James Talarico holds a narrow lead in polls over Republican candidate Ken Paxton.

“I think we can expect Republicans to be facing an uphill battle at the polls,” Panagopoulos said. “We can expect that they will likely lose seats in the House and also the Senate. And the question is, will they lose enough seats to lose the majority in either or both those chambers?”
