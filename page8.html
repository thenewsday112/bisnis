Sarah Wild is a freelance journalist in Canterbury, UK.

Tollulah Oni, right, running with teammates during an event at the International Conference on Sustainable Urban Mobility in Lagos, Nigeria, in 2025.Credit: LAMATA/The 2025 International Conference on Sustainable Urban Mobility Run

In 2016, public-health physician Tolullah Oni was jogging through the streets of Dakar when she saw someone doing push-ups in the middle of a busy roundabout. Cars were puffing fumes around him as the Sun baked down. As a physician, she knew that this had health implications.

More than half of the global population now lives in urban areas, but many cities are not conducive to human health and well-being. Oni, who is a researcher at University of Cambridge, UK, describes herself as an urban epidemiologist whose patients are cities. She works with colleagues around the world to diagnose and treat ‘sick’ cities with the goal of improving the health and quality of life of their inhabitants. This involves monitoring several variables, such as air quality and temperature, and their impact on the city’s residents. Those data can inform advocacy and interventions.

Frustrated by a lack of infrastructure on the ground in such cities, and by the struggle to connect researchers and residents, she set up the non-profit organization UrbanBetter in 2019 to support her research and advocacy. By providing training on data collection and analysis, the organization helps citizens and local researchers to diagnose their own cities and to use that evidence to lobby for change. Its research arm, the UrbanBetter Academy, has a centre in Cambridge, and its civic arm, called Cityzens Hubs, operates in 11 cities in Africa, as well as in Bogotá and Bangkok.

Oni, who was born in Nigeria, identifies as African diaspora, which she classifies as a “sixth distinct region of the African continent”. Now based in the United Kingdom, she recognizes the friction that comes with trying to treat cities that she does not live in. Oni tells Nature how she wants to create lasting virtual infrastructure through UrbanBetter that channels the diaspora’s intellectual capital back into the continent and heals sick cities.

Cities can’t tell you that they’re sick, so you must bring the symptoms to light first. What are the lungs or air quality doing? What is happening in the green spaces? How is the city metabolizing energy? What is happening to its waste? If you don’t have the data, you don’t know whether the city is sick and in a lot of our cities, we’re not even measuring those things.

It is important that the people who are identifying the symptoms are on the ground in that city, rather than me, a professor, sitting thousands of kilometres away in Cambridge. People there are breathing the air and moving through the city. We have to equip local people with the skill set to diagnose a city. The diagnostic tests can be done by sensors and their data can be mapped.

In cities, we need treatments to be grounded in lived realities. Outsiders can assess a city and propose a solution, backed by evidence, but it might not work for many reasons. We need to include residents. They can identify priority areas and advocate for change. For example, in Nairobi, a group of Cityzens collected air-pollution data and convened their own community assembly in 2025 to identify potential solutions, such as cleaner public transport, adding trees and green spaces and reducing open burning of rubbish. These points fed directly into the government’s Nairobi City County Air Quality Action Plan.

Developing treatments for a city is a collective effort and accountability is spoon-feeding that medicine in the form of small, incremental changes so that the city can get better.

I set up UrbanBetter in 2019 as an experimental platform that would situate research differently by combining it with action and advocacy. We started with a US$300,000 unrestricted grant from the funding organization Unorthodox Philanthropy, which is sponsored by the Lampert Byrd Foundation based in San Francisco, California.

Each of UrbanBetter’s hubs has different strengths — Lagos works on physical activity, air pollution and heat, whereas Bogotá has done work on transport, cycling and car-free days. This means that when another city wants to work in a new area, they don’t have to reinvent the wheel.

Ahead of the 2025 Lagos City Marathon, the local Cityzens Hub partnered with World Athletics’ project Running for Clean Air. The marathon takes place in February, which is among the hottest times of year in Lagos, and the city has high levels of air pollution.

Throughout 2025, a group of residents ran the marathon route each month, starting in the January before the marathon. They wore air-quality sensors and collected geotagged data with the UrbanBetter app. Some runners found that there was a 60% reduction in air pollution on the actual marathon day because cars were barred from the roads. These findings have informed a shift in transportation-policy implementation in the city, which is now targeting net-zero emissions by 2050. Government initiatives include increased cycling and walking, an overhaul of public transport and increased air-quality monitoring.

The Cityzens Hub then identified heat as another major issue for city dwellers, so they reached out to the UrbanBetter Academy for help analysing complex data. In 2025, researchers from UrbanBetter, local partner institutions and the Lagos Transport Authority received a £2-million [roughly US$2.6-million] grant from the biomedical funding charity Wellcome to investigate heat exposure and health risks in Lagos in a project called Hotsteppers. This is how UrbanBetter works — that research question about heat came from people on the ground raising the issue.

The ability to create protein scaffolds that impart enantioselectivity to simple, readily available metal catalysts could redefine the landscape of asymmetric catalysis. Artificial metalloenzymes (ArMs), hybrid catalysts that combine a synthetic metal cofactor with a protein scaffold, offer a potential means to achieve this goal, but scaffold discovery has remained constrained by trial-and-error approaches and the functional limits of extant proteins.1–5 This challenge is acute for metal polypyridine photocatalysts, which are widely used in visible-light chemistry but have not enabled highly enantioselective photocatalysis within protein scaffolds. In this study, we show that generative protein design6–8 can create de novo scaffolds that selectively bind these photocatalysts and that directed evolution converts the resulting artificial metalloenzymes into efficient, highly enantioselective photocatalysts9. The evolved proteins support diverse light-driven reactions, high catalyst turnover and catalyst recycling, and can function with commercially available racemic metal complexes. Spectroscopic studies show that the protein environment extends the photocatalyst excited-state lifetime and organizes substrate near the cofactor, altering energy transfer and controlling stereoselectivity. These findings establish de novo protein design as a route to functional scaffolds for synthetic metal complexes that lack natural protein partners and provide a strategy for combining protein design and evolution to control non-natural catalytic chemistry.

For more than two millennia, Archimedean screws have been used for water pumping and, more recently, as turbines in hydroelectric power stations[1]. Their helical-blade structure, essential to their function, is also found in chiral molecules and nanostructures, where injected electric current has been proposed to generate torque-mediated rotation[2,3]. Here, we present a torque-driven molecular machine with a rotor actuated by electric current and conductance enhanced by its rotation. Suspending a helicene molecule between two electrode tips, we show that an applied voltage induces cyclic molecular motion with 100% directional preference that can be inverted by the voltage’s polarity. Above a voltage threshold, the current through the rotating molecule is significantly enhanced, and the enhancement onset is tuned by stretching or compressing the helical molecule. Such effects were absent in previous helical molecular junctions, likely due to structural and operational differences[4–6]. Calculations show that molecular rotation by current-induced forces explains both the observed cyclic motion and current enhancement. This mechanism predicts a transition from stepwise to continuous rotation at a threshold voltage, yielding a current increase as rotation allows adopting highly conducting configurations not accessible at rest. Our work introduces a prototype for a new class of torque-driven molecular machines with perfect rotor unidirectionality and significant current modulation.

The challenges associated with this new way of doing maths are already evident. Many AI-generated results are not described clearly enough to meet quality standards for publication. Some researchers using AI tools simply move on to the next problem once they have a minimally presentable proof, leaving results that can be difficult for other mathematicians to assess. And it still remains unclear how extensively AI models are drawing on the previous work of mathematicians without providing proper attribution.

The centuries-old practice of openly sharing maths problems is under serious threat because mathematicians are increasingly fearful that disclosing their research directions will lead to their work being ‘scooped’ by a rival who is equipped with a frontier AI model. Graduate students and junior mathematicians used to develop their skills and gain early recognition from their peers by solving such problems. Perhaps the most counter-intuitive consequence is that human understanding of mathematics is falling behind, because AI systems generate results faster than mathematicians can digest and absorb them. Confined to pure mathematics, this ‘proof indigestion’ might be a minor concern. But AI-generated mathematical models deployed in the real world without a matching understanding of their mechanisms could have serious consequences. For example, complex AI-generated models are already used to some extent to determine who qualifies for a bank loan and to measure financial risk in markets. If the complexity of these models outpaces the capacity for humans to understand when they might malfunction, the risks of unforeseen mistakes will grow, affecting the general public.

These negative impacts are not inevitable. Used creatively, and with the right incentives, AI tools could enhance human understanding. The speed at which regions of the brain mature during early adolescence can forecast the emergence of mental-health conditions up to four years before symptoms develop, according to a study of nearly 12,000 young people in the United States.

By tracking the brains of adolescents over six years in nearly 30,000 scans, researchers mapped how the brain’s outer layer, the cerebral cortex, thins as children grow into teenagers. The team observed that sensory areas of the cortex thinned, or matured, first, followed by the regions responsible for complex thought.

The findings, posted on 11 October on the preprint server bioRxiv1 ahead of peer review, challenge the theory that psychiatric disorders are caused by defects in specific brain circuits. Instead, they indicate that vulnerability to mental-health symptoms could involve disruption of the brain’s developmental timeline and could be set in motion by genes that are active before birth.

The work “provides one of the clearest pictures yet of how the cortex develops from childhood into adolescence”, says Avram Holmes, a systems neuroscientist at Rutgers University in New Brunswick, New Jersey, who was not involved in the study. “It makes you think about the timing of brain development, not just the location [in the brain], when we look at psychiatric symptoms,” he says.

But he cautions that the population-level patterns found in the study — trends that become visible only when averaging data across large numbers of people — are subtle and cannot yet be used in the clinic to forecast the mental-health trajectory of an individual child.

During childhood and adolescence, the cerebral cortex thins. Neuroscientists attribute this process to the pruning of unused connections between neurons, which helps the brain to operate efficiently.

Researchers have long suspected that disruptions to cortical thinning contribute to mental illness, because psychiatric conditions such as depression are often diagnosed during late adolescence, when the thinning process is winding down. But proving that link has been difficult because brain-imaging studies have historically relied on small sample sizes or single snapshots in time.

To overcome these hurdles, study co-author Jacqueline Clauss, a child psychiatrist at the University of Iowa in Iowa City, and her colleagues turned to data from a massive project that is tracking nearly 12,000 children from around the age of nine years. Participants’ brains are scanned every two years, and their parents complete surveys annually about their children’s mental-health symptoms.

When analysing the data, Clauss and her colleagues found that rather than thinning at a steady rate, brain regions change following a distinct trajectory that fits an S-shaped curve. Thinning of a region of the cortex initially accelerates, then proceeds steadily before decelerating as the region nears maturity.

In launching the trial, Prime Medicine joins a host of companies aiming to engineer cells directly in the body, rather than using the standard method of extracting a person’s cells, editing their DNA in a laboratory and then reinjecting the altered cells. Rewriting DNA in the body, or in vivo, is appealing because manufacturing and administering the treatment might be much cheaper and faster than making a bespoke therapy for each person from their own cells.

A few months ago, I started a PhD at a university in North America. I have a disability that affects both my physical and my mental capacity, and its impact on me fluctuates. The more I can rest, the fewer symptom flare-ups I experience.

Unfortunately, my laboratory has a severe problem with overwork. Most students work 50–60 hours a week and sometimes more. We’re constantly asked to take on new tasks for the projects of our principal investigator (PI), often with tight deadlines. I’ll go into the lab intending to work on my experiments and end up spending all day on my PI’s tasks.

My PI, to whom I have not disclosed my health status, has repeatedly expressed disappointment in my work ethic. I have worked in other labs with different supervisors without facing issues (during my master’s degree, for example), and I know I’m not the only person struggling in this environment: my lab-mates are also burnt out.

Many of my issues are tied to being unable to take breaks while working long hours, often 12–16 hours per day for several days in a row. In previous jobs in which my colleagues and I worked overtime, we could take the equivalent amount of time off either the following week or as holiday time. But in my current lab, we’re expected to come back to work immediately. Sometimes we’re called in on weekends. I can barely keep up because I’m given no time to rest. A task that would take me an hour now takes longer because I’m fighting through pain. When I asked for time off, I received a firm no.

I’m nervous about what I might face in the rest of my programme. I know that I can have the success I want if I find the right environment. I plan to seek accommodations by contacting someone at my university. But should I disclose to my PI? I fear it might make things worse, and that I will face disability stigma. What can I do to make sure myself and other students are well cared for and protected? — A disabled graduate student

“Disability stigma contributes to power imbalances between graduate students and advisers,” says Bonnielin Swenor, director of the Johns Hopkins Disability Health Research Center in Baltimore, Maryland. That stigma is evident from discrepancies in earnings1 and grant outcomes2 between disabled and non-disabled researchers in science, technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM) fields, according to her research on US scientists. Understandably, then, “taking action can feel intimidating, and many disabled scientists fear retribution”, she says.

“What you describe is not a personal failure of work ethic,” emphasizes Deepti Sharma, a management researcher at Ahmedabad University in India. You’re in a dysfunctional work environment, in which “your disability made the dysfunction visible to you earlier and more painfully than it did for others”.

Nature’s careers team asked Sharma, Swenor and two other researchers for advice on how disabled graduate students and other people facing similar challenges can protect themselves.

Rebecca Williams, an environmental-law researcher and disability officer at the University of Glasgow, UK, says that there should be staff members who can offer support to students. She recommends investigating what resources are available at your university. These could be from a graduate-student union, a director of graduate studies or university disability services.

In many regions, there is a legal duty not to discriminate against people with disabilities, Williams adds. She also suggests checking whether your university offers any grants or other financial support for the resources that you might need. One source of guidance could be the Job Accommodation Network, a US website providing information on workplace accommodations.

Sharma distinguishes between formal and personal disclosure: “Registering with your university’s disability-services office is a formal, documented process that establishes the accommodations you are entitled to.” At most institutions, the office should inform supervisors only of the accommodations required, not the diagnoses that prompted them. “Personal disclosure to a PI is a separate, discretionary act,” says Sharma, who strongly encourages formal registration as a first step. “Regardless of what you decide about your PI, informal goodwill evaporates under pressure, while documented accommodations do not.”

Williams recommends pausing before deciding whether to approach your PI: “You may want to wait until you have a better sense of your new PI’s supervisory style before you decide how much or little you want to disclose beyond the accommodations that you need.”

To make best use of institutional resources, all four disability advocates stress the importance of documentation. “Keep written records such as e-mails confirming verbal instructions and notes on refused leave requests,” advises Sharma. “Patterns documented over time carry far more weight with a department than any single complaint.” Doing this could also help other students who face similar issues with your PI.

Swenor notes that “keeping detailed records of e-mails and interactions in which disability discrimination occurs is often necessary. As with any form of discrimination, this information provides the needed support if or when you choose to file a complaint or grievance,” she says. “It is also essential to understand your rights and legal protections.”

But, she cautions, keeping a record is only one step. “Formally documenting your needed accommodations does not eliminate bias or discrimination,” she says.

Sharma suggests “building a support structure outside your supervisor’s control: a thesis committee member you trust, a mentor in another department, peers in your cohort”. This support structure could include your head of department or, if it exists, a staff disability network that can connect you to supportive staff members or graduate students who might have relevant lived experience, says Williams.

“Disability in academia is often about finding ‘your people’, who understand what it is to manage health difficulties,” notes Williams, who has written about her own experiences of dealing with chronic illness in academia. “Sometimes finding people that ‘get it’ will help you feel less alone during the process of your PhD, and gauge what a realistic daily or weekly PhD routine might be for you.”

The personalized vaccine, called intismeran, is not designed to prevent skin cancer, or melanoma, from developing in the first place, but to stop it returning in people who have already undergone surgery to remove tumours.

Anticipation for the trial results has been building since August, when the vaccine’s developers — Merck, based in Rahway, New Jersey, and Moderna, which is headquartered in Cambridge, Massachusetts — announced early positive results. Data from the phase III trial will be presented at the European Society for Medical Oncology meeting in Madrid on 24 October. The intismeran trial enrolled 1,137 people with moderate to advanced melanoma across 157 centres. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either a tailor-made vaccine (intismeran) alongside the immunotherapy drug pembrolizumab — which is already used to treat melanoma — or pembrolizumab plus an inactive placebo. Trial leader Georgina Long, an oncologist at Melanoma Institute Australia in Sydney, says participants have so far been followed for around 25 months and will continue to be monitored for several years to determine whether the vaccine improves overall survival. She adds that the therapy did not result in any major harmful side effects.

Roughly 15 years ago, people with advanced melanoma typically survived for only months. Today, nearly 55% are effectively cured with targeted drugs or immunotherapies, says Long. Jessamy Tiffen, a cancer geneticist at the Centenary Institute in Sydney, says that the trial results are exciting, but that only time will tell how effective the treatment is beyond two years. “Cancer cells also have an insidious ability to adapt and evade whatever treatment we throw at them,” she says. Furthermore, some people did not respond to the vaccine, she says.

Long says that her team will conduct a statistical analysis to identify who is most likely to benefit from the treatment.

To develop intismeran, Long and her colleagues sequenced DNA from a sample of each participant’s tumour to identify mutations in the cancer cells. These mutations can give rise to proteins called neoantigens, which help to distinguish cancer cells from healthy ones.

Blood, sweat and tears in biotech — the Theranos story

Blood, sweat and tears in biotech — the Theranos story

More than a decade after the company’s spectacular collapse, a new generation of diagnostics firms still grapples with the fallout. During the 2010s, Theranos said that its device could run hundreds of tests on only a few drops of blood, drastically reducing the amount of fluid needed for routine diagnostics. The company’s success soared as investors backed the technology, but US federal juries later ruled that Theranos had misled those financial supporters about its device’s capabilities, and two of its executives, including Holmes, were sentenced to more than a decade in prison.

Today, health-tech companies are still developing devices similar to the ones that Theranos aspired to make. The firms say that Theranos’s demise underscored the importance of transparency in their pursuit. “Trust in diagnostics has to be earned through evidence and not promises,” says Dena Marrinucci, co-founder of Truvian Health, a diagnostics company in San Diego, California.

Theranos left “a crater in the whole field of monitoring and diagnostics”, says Paul Yager, a bioengineer and diagnostics developer at the University of Washington in Seattle. For years after its collapse, he says, researchers pitching blood-testing technologies faced the same opening question in every meeting: “How is what you’re trying to do different from what Theranos did?”

Standard diagnostic tests, which can measure everything from the levels of basic minerals — or electrolytes — to hormones in the blood, typically require several vials of fluid to be drawn from a person’s arm to ensure a large, homogeneous sample. The results can be used to diagnose and track various health conditions.

Theranos, which was based in Palo Alto, California, claimed that its Edison device could execute hundreds of diagnostic tests on just a few drops of blood from a finger prick. But behind the scenes, the company was having a hard time making the platform work as advertised, and instead resorted to diluting patient samples and running them on standard blood-testing machines from other companies, in some cases producing skewed measurements.

Theranos, the failed blood-testing company founded by Elizabeth Holmes, claimed that its device could run hundreds of tests on a few drops of blood.Credit: HBO Documentary Films/Everett Collection

Although many factors probably contributed to Theranos’s failures, says Eric Olson, founder of Babson Diagnostics in Austin, Texas, one of them was using blood from a finger prick rather than an arm vein. This introduces the potential for contamination of the sample. For example, sometimes squeezing the fingertip to extract blood droplets can cause blood cells to burst as they emerge from the skin, leaking debris into samples and causing erroneous measurements.

Tiny blood volumes also run into a statistics problem, Yager says. Measuring an abundant molecule in the blood, such as glucose, requires only a small droplet because a person’s bloodstream is chock-full of the sugar. But many other molecules that are biomarkers of disease, such as viral particles from an HIV infection, circulate at much lower concentrations. In a few microlitres of blood from a finger prick, those molecules might barely register on a device’s detector.

Despite those challenges, the biotechnology industry hasn’t given up on Theranos’s goal. That is because making rapid blood monitoring more accessible remains an urgent problem, Yager says, especially for people who live in rural areas that are far from centralized laboratories, and for older and disabled people who struggle to travel to medical appointments.

An individual with cancer in France has become the first person known to contract the Shamonda virus, after an outbreak in livestock in Europe that was first identified in July. The man, aged in his sixties, died on 9 October, 16 days after he started showing neurological symptoms, including vision loss and confusion, according to a case report published in Eurosurveillance this week1. Infectious-disease researchers say little is known about the virus or how it affects the body.

The man lived in a rural area in the Rhône–Alpes region, where Shamonda virus has been detected in cattle. He was probably infected after being bitten by midges, the report states.

It is also possible that he contracted the virus by eating contaminated food or through a blood transfusion or transplant, although the report’s authors say these routes are less likely. “Investigations are ongoing to rule them out,” they write. Researchers say that the risk of the virus being transmitted between people is low.

Shamonda virus belongs to a family of viruses called orthobunyaviruses, which are known to infect animals such as cows and deer, and are transmitted by mosquitoes and midges, says Krispin Hajkowicz, an infectious-disease researcher at the University of Queensland, Australia. There are no vaccines or antiviral treatments for orthobunyaviruses, Hajkowicz says.

More orthobunyaviruses are being detected in people, says Daniel Rawle, a virologist at the QIMR Berghofer Medical Research Institute in Brisbane, Australia. Climate change and greater contact between wildlife, livestock and humans are some of the factors that have increased the risk of people becoming infected. Another element is that a growing number of people are immunocompromised, and so more at risk of being infected and developing life-threatening symptoms. Although no human-to-human transmission has been recorded for these viruses, during an outbreak of an orthobunyavirus called Oropouche in Brazil in 2024, transmission was reported between pregnant women and their fetuses.

The individual in France, who had leukaemia and was severely immunocompromised, started having seizures shortly after the onset of symptoms and went into a coma, requiring intubation and mechanical ventilation. When his symptoms did not improve after treatment with antibiotics, an antiviral drug and high doses of corticosteroids, he was taken off life support.
