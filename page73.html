Technologies developed to remove space junk and fix broken satellites can be repurposed to build fleets of bodyguard spacecraft to protect important assets from attacks.

Technologies developed and tested in orbit to remove space junk and fix broken satellites can be speedily repurposed to build fleets of bodyguard spacecraft to protect important satellites from adversary attacks, space clean-up company Astroscale thinks.

Astroscale made history with its 2024 up-close in-orbit inspection of a discarded Japanese rocket stage during the pioneering ADRAS-J mission in low Earth orbit (LEO), the region of space below altitudes of 1,200 miles (2,000 kilometers).

The firm's ELSA-D mission, which ran from 2021 to 2024, was the first commercial endeavor to demonstrate orbital capture technologies in LEO. Now, this technology can come in handy as Western space assets face increasing threats from Russia.

Speaking on the sidelines of an event in London earlier this month, Alex Godfrey, Astroscale's head of defense, said the company could have a protector spacecraft ready for launch in under three years.

"In in-orbit servicing, we have to achieve very high levels of agility to be able to dock and close to a distance of zero meters to refuel something or remove it from orbit," Godfrey told Space.com. "With space control, you're rarely getting quite as close, so in some ways it's simpler because you are further away."

Space control — a military term for ensuring the space around Earth remains orderly and peaceful — is quickly becoming a hot talking point. Godfrey's remarks came just as the world witnessed in shock as Russia made close-up maneuvers around a radar satellite owned by Finland headquartered Earth-observation company ICEYE. The October incident, described by experts as the first rendezvous of two active satellites in low Earth orbit conducted without the consent of the target, raised concerns Russia may be preparing to damage the satellite, which is part of a fleet providing critical intelligence to Ukraine.

U.S. Under Secretary of State Thomas G. DiNanno described Russia's actions on X as "threatening behavior" and "consistent with on-orbit weapons operations."

The ICEYE incident is only the latest in a long string of activities conducted by Russia in space designed either to eavesdrop on or interfere with the activity of critical Western space assets. The Luch-1 and Luch-2 satellites have been stalking Europe's and America's communications satellites in geostationary orbit — the busy region 22,000 miles (36,000 km) above the planet where satellites have a fixed view of a portion of the globe—since 2014. Germany's military space command chief Major General Michael Traut admitted earlier this year that the Luch spacecraft have been spying on European strategic communications in space for years.

A bodyguard satellite would make sure no such intruder gets dangerously close. Godfrey said that the cost of a bodyguard mission would be on the order of a single percent of the value of a military-grade geostationary communications constellation such as the U.K.'s £8.4-billion Skynet fleet.

"To build a bodyguard satellite is absolutely within the capability and the budget of a single country like the U.K.," said Godfrey. "It would be similar to a carrier strike group where you have a [naval aircraft] carrier, but that carrier never leaves the port on its own. It has an escort of much smaller, lower-cost systems that move around to protect it."

Although Astroscale, headquartered in Japan but with significant presence in the U.K., is a leader in the commercial orbital rendezvous and proximity operations technology, no Western players currently match the deployed capabilities of Russia and China. Europe, in particular, is under-equipped for defense and military operations in space, according to the Secure World Foundation's Global Counterspace Capabilities Report. With Russia's accelerating orbital forays, the clock appears to be ticking.

Godfrey proposes a pan-European fleet of orbital bodyguards patrolling clusters of European assets to ward off future Russian attempts to jam or damage the satellites.

"The UK, for example, has multiple assets in different positions around the geostationary ring," Godfrey said. "That is difficult to protect with a single spacecraft. So what I encourage nations to think about is how to form a geo-patrol force through collaboration. If France has assets in a similar location to the UK and maybe Germany, then it makes sense for one spacecraft to protect two or three allied spacecraft, and then, in another region, another country takes responsibility for the protection."

Nick Shave, the UK managing director at Astroscale, told Space.com that the company sees interest in its technologies and is "in discussions" with "companies and customers across Europe" although it has not signed any contracts to develop bodyguard satellites.

"We have spent a good amount of time talking over the last few years, but now we have to go and really deliver," Shave said. "The UK government is promising funding for space control development from 2030 onwards. So what needs to happen now is really preparing the ground so that we've got the right capabilities, the right investment and the right demonstrator capability, by 2030."

In the meantime, Russia's COSMOS 2612 stalker satellite keeps lingering 298 miles (480 km) from the ICEYE intelligence satellite in LEO, keeping the Western space community on edge.

#1799. Autonomous AI: How to Get AI to Manage Itself

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Wondering how to set up autonomous AI agents that complete work in the background without constant babysitting? Want a step-by-step process for training AI to run your workflows on its own and even manage other AI agents?

In this article, you’ll discover how to build AI playbooks you can trust to run autonomously, remove yourself from the review loop, and set up an AI middle manager that oversees your other AI agents.

Building an agent doesn’t make AI autonomous. According to Rachel Woods, AI can only run in the background without supervision once it can be trusted to do the work the way it’s supposed to be done. Without that trust, an agent runs off doing random things, and someone has to go back and undo that work.

The fix is to document the work step by step in what Rachel calls a playbook. When AI follows a playbook exactly, the result is predictable, and that predictability is what makes it possible to stop checking the work. Across industries and roles, Rachel hasn’t found a type of work that can’t be playbooked, and on an average day, she gets about 8 hours of autonomous work from AI.

An AI playbook is a step-by-step process that AI follows to complete one specific type of work the way its owner wants it done. Most people haven’t yet worked out the structure of their own work, so figuring out that structure is the hard part. For example, connecting AI to an email account and asking it to get to inbox zero produces work that has to be checked tag by tag, with constant feedback that often contradicts itself.

​Rachel’s email inbox triage playbook, which runs every hour and keeps her at inbox zero, started with a playbook outline.

The most difficult part of the process is setting the bookends of a playbook. The common trap, and one that also arises when delegating to people, is defining something too large, such as having AI run an entire morning or building a single playbook for a whole marketing campaign.

A marketing campaign, for example, includes many deliverables: briefs, emails, social media posts, landing pages, follow-up lead magnets, and often several channels. One playbook for all of it won’t produce value or earn enough trust to run autonomously until every piece works correctly.

The better approach is one playbook per deliverable, each working independently, with an overall playbook that ties them together. Rachel calls this stacking, and it’s also how AI ends up managing itself. Her morning routine playbook triggers other playbooks and collects their outputs, a setup built brick by brick rather than overnight.

Pro Tip: The outline comes first, and the detailed instructions get filled in later, rather than writing the whole playbook from start to finish like a book written from chapter one.

The trigger, inputs, instruction steps, and output description components apply to outlining any type of work.

The CRAFT cycle is Rachel’s five-step process for building an AI playbook and improving it until AI can run it without review. CRAFT stands for clear picture, realistic design, AI-ify, feedback loop, and team rollout. The same five steps apply to every playbook and are repeated to improve it over time. The cycle avoids the all-or-nothing outcome that a new playbook tends to trigger: either reviewing its output forever or deciding it doesn’t work and abandoning it.

The first CRAFT cycle is useful even for workflows that someone still runs and reviews every day. A second cycle on the same playbook is what makes the work autonomous.

​The first version usually keeps a human in the loop. Running it shows what that person checks for and which mistakes the AI makes, and those observations reveal where the playbook needs to be more specific.

​The second cycle starts with a new, clear picture built on what was learned, then changes the playbook and removes the human from every step, where the AI now has what it needs to succeed.

​These cycles go quickly once a person gets into the rhythm of running them.

​Clear Picture: The playbook gets written in its most ideal form, documenting the full process being handed off. The outline is the starting point, with more specific, nuanced instructions added to steps that need them, especially when the work itself is nuanced.

​Realistic Design: The first version gets scoped down. Steps that will be easy for AI go to AI, and a human stays in the loop on the harder ones. The goal is a playbook that’s working at some level, so there’s something to learn from and improve. A roadblock isn’t a reason to abandon the playbook. AI keeps improving, so a step that isn’t realistic now may be in six months, and AI can handle the rest of the process in the meantime.

​AI-ify: The playbook gets set up in an AI tool and put into use. This is where its owner starts confirming the AI can do what’s expected.

​Feedback Loop: Rachel considers this the most important step. Running the playbook shows both where the AI does the work correctly and where it doesn’t, and both need a response. AI is predictive and guesses at what’s wanted unless it’s told otherwise, so good behavior isn’t guaranteed to recur.

​For example, if the AI adds a useful summary to the top of a document without being asked, the fix is to add “include a summary at the beginning” to the playbook, or to tell the AI it got that right and have it update the playbook. If the summary was a waste of space, an instruction to never include one goes in instead.

​Each round of feedback adds the specificity that lets the AI follow documented instructions rather than guess.

​Claude can edit its own skills, so it can apply changes from the feedback loop directly. That self-editing needs constraints. Rachel’s team uses a major-and-minor feedback framework: the AI can make minor changes on its own, but major changes get discussed first. The alternatives are to review every change or to build a playbook for updating playbooks. Without one of these safeguards, the AI can go off the rails.

Team Rollout: This is the most overlooked step, and it’s where the return on investment comes from because a playbook without a formal rollout sits on the shelf.

​Rachel’s favorite change management tactic here is habit stacking. Applied to playbooks, habit stacking means defining exactly when in the broader workflow each playbook gets used. Everyone using it also needs training on how to start it, how to review its work, and what the expectations are.

A playbook is a method, not a type of AI tool, so it works in any tool that can follow instructions. Rachel runs playbooks on ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok. Many of her students run them in Microsoft Copilot, and Gemini and Airtable work, too.

​The easiest starting point is to save playbooks as Claude skills, along with any other skills or saved instructions.

​The most durable approach is what Rachel calls “own the playbook, rent the tech.” Playbooks get written and stored in Google Docs, Google Drive, Notion, or another document storage system rather than inside an AI tool, which turns each one into an independent asset. Why? Keeping playbooks only inside an AI tool is like a team member keeping important work only in a personal drive. Stored separately, the same playbook can run in any AI tool.

​Rachel’s team stores its playbooks in a mix of Google Drive and GitHub. Google Drive is the easier option, since Claude can be told where to find specific playbooks, which keeps them up to date. GitHub suits more advanced users and lets team members on separate accounts access the same playbooks and collaborate on improving them.

An AI middle manager is an AI agent that oversees other AI agents and the playbooks they run, taking over the management work a person would otherwise do. Once several playbooks are running, the next step is to hand that management layer to AI as well.

​The skill set used here is the same one used to manage people: breaking work down so it can be delegated, managing outcomes, and sitting on top of the system rather than doing the work in the weeds. Rachel says anyone who hasn’t managed before needs to develop it.

​Managers also build a process of their own, such as morning huddles, keeping tasks on track, and checking in with specific team members, and that process is itself a playbook. After managing a set of AI playbooks for a while, the management layer can be playbooked too, and that move up a level can repeat.

​Rachel calls this a clear and direct path to having the equivalent of a whole role, or even a whole team, doing work underneath one person.

Rachel uses two methods to get AI agents to coordinate, both of which rely on defined playbooks rather than open-ended instructions.

​Use Subagents: In Claude, one agent can spawn subagents that run playbooks and report back to the main agent, which then manages them. This is the more advanced option.

​Use a Task Management Tool: For a company already using Asana, ClickUp, or a similar tool, a manager agent can assign work to other agents there, just as a manager assigns work to people. The other agents monitor the tool and are triggered by activity inside it, and the manager agent follows up to ensure tasks are completed.

​The choice depends on the use case. Team systems benefit from the task management method because the audit trail shows what the AI did, didn’t do, and got wrong, which is why Rachel’s company uses Asana for them. For a personal routine, that’s overkill. Rachel simply talks to her main agent, which she has named Pepper.

​Pro Tip: Rachel says making up custom vocabulary for AI is a sign of reaching a new level with it. Specific terms make the AI less confused and reduce how much it has to guess. Because she uses the word “feedback” constantly, she calls the playbook for overall feedback and observations about her business and systems her “nitpicking” playbook.

#1604. Cybersecurity incidents hit over 50% of schools and districts in past year

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Some 87% of school leaders reported phishing or email compromises, a report from top cybersecurity groups found.

Some schools tap MS-ISAC for free cybersecurity services including threat intelligence and incident response. During the 2025-26 school year, over 11,600 K-12 cybersecurity incidents needed escalation and response among the 1,764 K-12 organizations using one or more of MS-ISAC’s services.

On top of that, 13% of schools or districts receiving MS-ISAC monitoring services experienced a credential compromise or exposure, according to the CIS and MS-ISAC report.

“Building resilience requires not only strong security tools but also ongoing awareness, phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, and a culture that empowers users as the first line of defense rather than treating them as the weakest link,” the report said. “The most targeted system in K-12 today is the human.” The report was conducted in partnership with the Consortium for School Networking.

The report relied its findings on two datasets: MS-ISAC’s K-12 users of cybersecurity services and a survey of 426 K-12 cybersecurity and tech leaders that CIS conducted between August and September.

Artificial intelligence is also adding a more complicated layer to K-12 cybersecurity, especially when it comes to risks.

“Schools without clear guardrails may expose sensitive information, introduce unapproved tools into their environments, and become more vulnerable to increasingly convincing AI-enabled phishing and impersonation attacks,” the CIS and MS-ISAC report said.

Concerns also persist that third-party software providers are leaving school systems’ data vulnerable to breaches and cyberattacks, especially as more districts embrace AI tools and cyber criminals increasingly rely on AI to find and exploit software vulnerabilities.

As school districts continue to rely on third-party online vendors, education is among the top sectors to experience a cybersecurity incident through a third party service, according to a report released by Moody’s Ratings in October.

Some 57% of Moody’s debt issuers surveyed in the education sector reported suffering a third-party cybersecurity incident since 2020 — compared to 40% reported across all industries. Healthcare and insurance were among sectors that saw slightly higher rates than education, Moody’s found.

Across all sectors, Moody’s said, third-party cyber incidents increased from 19% to 39% between 2020 and 2025. While more organizations keep expanding the number of software providers they use, it’s likely that third-party cybersecurity risks will continue to grow, Moody’s said.

Recent examples of K-12 challenges with third-party cyber incidents, Moody’s said, include several large ed tech platforms targeted by ransomware attacks, such as PowerSchool in 2024 and Instructure’s Canvas in 2026, which left millions of teacher and student sensitive data exposed nationwide.

As schools' risk for both third-party data breaches and compromised credentials heightens, so do districts' struggles for resources to fend against them. For instance, CIS and MS-ISAC found in their survey of K-12 cybersecurity and tech leaders that 56% reported their cybersecurity programs were underfunded and 70% said they had to delay cybersecurity projects, technology investments or staffing because of budget issues.

#481. Smithsonian scientists just recreated the first 2 billion years of the Universe

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Scientists have created one of the largest and most detailed simulations of the early universe ever developed, offering a remarkable new way to explore how the cosmos evolved after the Big Bang. Known as "Lumina," the simulation was built by researchers at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian to help astronomers interpret observations from powerful instruments such as the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and Nancy Roman Space Telescope.

The research, published in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society, reconstructs the universe's first 2 billion years by modeling the interactions between ordinary matter, dark matter, and radiation. By comparing Lumina's predictions with observations from JWST, Roman, and other telescopes, scientists can evaluate how well existing theories explain cosmic evolution and identify discoveries that might challenge their understanding of the universe's fundamental laws.

Astronomers have spent centuries studying the sky and developing explanations for the formation of stars, galaxies, and other cosmic structures. Modern supercomputers now make it possible to turn these ideas into detailed simulations that predict how the universe should behave. When telescope observations agree with those predictions, they strengthen confidence in current theories. Unexpected differences, however, may point toward physical processes that scientists have yet to fully understand.

"With JWST, the early universe has become an exciting new frontier in astrophysics," said lead author Oliver Zier, an ITC Fellow at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian. "To understand what we are seeing with this telescope, we need to translate our theories into detailed predictions. After several years of work, with plenty of ups and downs, it is incredibly rewarding to see the simulation results and watch our predictions come to life in video."

Recreating the Universe's First 2 Billion Years

Lumina is the largest cosmological radiation hydrodynamics simulation ever produced. In simple terms, it uses powerful computers to calculate how gas, dark matter, and light influenced one another as the young universe developed. By following these interactions across the first 2 billion years after the Big Bang, researchers can examine how the earliest galaxies formed and how their activity changed the enormous clouds of gas surrounding them.

The simulation models a volume of space more than 100 times larger than those covered by earlier comparable projects. Its virtual universe spans a cube approximately 1.5 billion light-years on each side, providing a view of cosmic evolution on a scale comparable to the vast regions that today's most advanced telescopes can study.

Another major advance is Lumina's treatment of helium reionization, a process in which radiation strips electrons from helium atoms. For the first time, a simulation of this kind follows that transformation as it unfolds while simultaneously accounting for how it affects the surrounding universe. This allows scientists to study the relationship between evolving galaxies, radiation, and the gas that fills the space between them.

"We feel incredibly fortunate to be building simulations like Lumina," said Aaron Smith, an assistant professor of physics at the University of Texas at Dallas and an author on the paper. "For the first time, we can follow along as both hydrogen and helium changed into their modern form across such an enormous volume of the universe, while still resolving the galaxies and other luminous objects driving that transformation."

A Massive Supercomputer Powers the Virtual Cosmos

Recreating such an enormous portion of cosmic history demanded extraordinary computing resources. The team used more than 15,000 graphics processing units on Frontier, one of the world's fastest supercomputers, housed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee.

That processing power enabled the researchers to simulate an exceptionally large region of the early universe without sacrificing the fine detail needed to investigate individual galaxies and other important structures. As a result, Lumina can capture the broader evolution of the cosmos while also tracking processes involved in the formation of stars, galaxies, and black holes.

Testing the Mysteries of the Early Universe

Beyond recreating cosmic history, Lumina provides a powerful new framework for investigating how the universe developed. Researchers plan to use it to explore the movement of gas between galaxies, the influence of radiation left over from the Big Bang, and the emergence of regions where stars formed at especially high rates.

By placing these predictions alongside observations from the James Webb Space Telescope, the Nancy Roman Space Telescope, and other instruments, scientists hope to build a more complete picture of the universe's earliest chapters. Any unexpected differences between the simulated cosmos and the real one could provide valuable clues, potentially revealing where current theories need to be refined or reconsidered.
