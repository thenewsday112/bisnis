#3826. Apple’s Very Colorful iMac Gets the M6 Treatment

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apple’s Very Colorful iMac Gets the M6 Treatment

The iMac has been a core part of Apple’s Mac family for nearly 30 years, with the first model arriving in 1998. The newest iMac has arrived, powered by Apple’s latest chip, the M6.

Apple describes its new iMac, which sports the same thin, 24-inch design as the first Apple Silicon-powered iMac released in 2021, as “the world’s best all-in-one.”

Underpinning that claim is the move to M6, Apple’s first 2-nanometer chip, which it launched in August inside the new Mac mini. For those who like desktop computers but don’t want to fuss with an external display, the iMac has long been a compelling choice, one of the few options on the market, in fact.

As Apple explains, the M6 offers greatly improved performance across the board, thanks to Apple’s fastest CPU core to date, a more powerful GPU, and better memory performance. The new Dual 16-core Neural Engine also delivers up to twice as fast AI compute as the previous generation, ensuring better performance with AI models and LLMs. That, alongside the faster graphics, is good news for photographers and video editors.

Compared with Apple’s first M-series iMac, which had the original M1 chip, the new iMac with M6 delivers up to 3.3 times faster graphics performance for photo editing tasks in Affinity and up to twice the performance of the M4. The iMac skipped the M5 generation.

The updated iMac features Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6, plus 2.5Gb Ethernet that delivers up to 2.5 times faster wired connectivity than the previous generation.

“This means faster transfers of large files, smoother streaming of high-resolution content, and even better performance for gaming and demanding business workflows,” Apple says. The internal SSD also offers up to twice as fast read and write speeds.

The new iMac features up to four Thunderbolt 4 ports, and users can connect up to two external 6K displays to go alongside the built-in screen. Speaking of the screen, the iMac still sports the same 4.5K display with up to 500 nits of brightness. It also keeps the same 12-megapixel Center Stage camera. On the sound side, it has two force-canceling woofers, a pair of tweeters, and supports Dolby Atmos.

“iMac is the world’s best all-in-one, and today it raises the bar yet again,” says Shelly Goldberg, Apple’s vice president of Hardware Engineering.

“With the blazing performance and AI capabilities of M6 supercharging every workflow and the next generation of Apple Intelligence with Siri AI — combined with its stunning 24-inch Retina display, its gorgeous design in seven vibrant colors, and macOS 27 — the new iMac has everything users need to run their businesses, manage their lives, and bring their ideas to life,” Goldberg continues.

The new Apple iMac with M6 comes in seven colors, blue, purple, pink, orange, yellow, green, and silver, and ships with color-matched accessories. The new iMac starts at $1,499 with a 10-core CPU and 10-core GPU. This base model ships with 16GB of unified memory and 256GB of storage. An upgraded configuration with 12 CPU and GPU cores starts at $1,699.

Customers can upgrade to up to 32GB of unified memory and up to 2TB of internal storage. A $200 nano-texture glass upgrade is also available. Users can also get the iMac with a VESA Mount Adapter at no additional charge.

Preorders start October 28 at 1:10 PM ET (10:10 AM PT), and the new iMac starts shipping November 6.

#662. Fish are appearing where they’ve never been recorded before in Japan

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists analyzing seawater DNA across Japan have uncovered surprising changes in where fish species are appearing, raising new questions for fisheries.

Go fishing off the Japanese coast today, and you might pull up something your grandfather never encountered. Fish typically found in warmer southern waters are turning up farther north.

The people whose livelihoods and cuisines depend on the sea are already noticing. The ocean is reshaping itself around them.

Yet, most of the world’s tracking tools have been too slow, too expensive, or too patchy to keep up.

A new study from the Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology (OIST) offers the most detailed picture yet of this shift.

Using environmental DNA, the researchers identified more than 100 fish species appearing further north than any previous record suggested.

The work is the largest marine eDNA study ever conducted in Japan. It draws on 2,672 water samples collected at 316 locations along the Japanese coastline between 2017 and 2024.

Environmental DNA, or eDNA, works on a simple premise. Every organism sheds genetic material into its surroundings – through skin, scales, mucus, feces.

Collect a sample of seawater, extract and sequence the DNA, then compare it against a reference database.

The result is a snapshot of the species whose genetic traces are present in the water.

This procedure uses a fraction of the time and cost of traditional survey methods.

Citizen science networks, fieldwork surveys, and specimen collection all have their place. Still, each comes with its own blind spots.

Observations can be difficult to verify, traditional surveys can be expensive, and predictions do not always reflect conditions in the water.

Japan is one of the few places where this approach could be applied at a national scale.

The method relies on MiFish primers, genetic tools developed by Japanese researchers to identify fish DNA in water samples. Extensive reference DNA databases help researchers match those sequences to individual species.

Study lead author Ayşe Haruka Oshima Açıkbaş is an OIST PhD graduate.

“Researchers have put tons of effort into sequencing the local marine fauna, increasing the coverage and accuracy of species detection within Japan,” Açıkbaş said.

The team combined nearly 1,000 newly collected eDNA samples with data from previous sampling efforts.

They built a seven-year dataset that allowed them to map fish distributions across Japan’s coastlines with unprecedented resolution.

Among the fish species detected, 126 appeared at latitudes farther north than previously recorded.

The Japanese scad (Decapterus maruadsi) and the blacktip grouper (Epinephelus fasciatus) are both important to local fisheries. Others carry ecological consequences that go beyond the catch.

The black rabbitfish (Siganus fuscescens), also appearing further north, is associated with “isoyake.” That’s a phenomenon in which grazing pressure destroys coastal seaweed beds, with cascading effects on other ecosystems.

“Fishermen across Japan are already finding differences in the types of fish they reel in compared to a few decades ago,” said senior author Timothy Ravasi, the head of the Marine Climate Change unit at OIST.

“This is a problem because the people in these areas may not be used to buying, cooking, or eating these new kinds of fish, and at the same time, they can’t make the dishes that might be culturally or historically significant because those fish aren’t there anymore.”

“And a sudden influx of new types of fish means you need new techniques, tools, and knowledge to catch them, which is especially hard on small, family-based fishing businesses.”

Japan’s combination of a highly developed marine science infrastructure, a dense network of coastal monitoring stations, and decades of fisheries data made this kind of study possible.

The eDNA technique scales well and the researchers expect the value of the dataset to compound as collection continues.

For now, the study provides something that policy discussions around fisheries management and coastal conservation have largely lacked: a fine-grained, empirically grounded map of where fish actually are, rather than where models predict they should be.

The 126 species detected beyond their previously recorded northern limits offer evidence of changes already underway in Japan’s seas, although individual detections do not necessarily establish permanent range shifts.

The authors expect these findings to shape future coastal and fisheries policy in Japan.

The longer-term goal is to move from tracking species presence to modeling the processes driving their movement.

That means understanding not just that fish DNA is appearing in new places, but why it ends up where it does, and where it is likely to turn up next.

As the database grows and predictive modeling catches up with the descriptive work, the technique could become a standard tool for monitoring range shifts in marine ecosystems far beyond Japan’s coastline.

The study is published in the journal Diversity and Distributions.

#2968. Exped Ultra 10R Sleeping Mat Review: This Is One of the Warmest Backpacking Sleeping Pads Available

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The Exped Ultra 10R Sleeping Pad is an ultralight, all-season inflatable backpacking mattress. Designed for maximum warmth in extreme temperatures (down to -40° Fahrenheit), its intended use if for winter backpacking, high mountain treks and alpine expeditions — and also for those of us who get really, really cold at night no matter where we’re sleeping.

The Ultra 10R’s weight-to-warmth ratio is outstanding: the pad has an R-value of 10.2, and the rectangular-shaped medium size (perfect for most of us) weighs less than 2 pounds. This is the warmest sleeping pad in the Exped Ultra Series.

The Exped Ultra 10R Sleeping Mat is intended for cold weather adventures. We’re talking winter backpacking in the Boundary Waters, but also mountaineering and alpine adventures, and anything high altitude like the Himalaya.

And that is exactly where I brought the Exped to test in the field: I trekked in Upper Dolpo, an extremely remote and very high arid desert in Western Nepal. Temperatures would drop into the single digits at night, but more critically, the wind was fierce and the rocky ground was cold. Even in May, it snowed at night, but I slept blissfully unaware of all that on my personal cloud of comfort.

Synmat Plus is Exped’s trademark insulation technology. It combines recycled synthetic microfiber with layers of reflective foil to create an efficient insulation that makes the pad uber warm and lightweight. I was floored by how quiet the pad was, not once crinkling as I moved around at night.

Schnozzel Pumpbag and FlatValves: The included pumb sack, paired with the separate inflate and deflate valves, make filling and emptying this beast easy and quick, even in thin air. That’s because you never have to blow into it with your mouth. You simply grab the ambient air in the bag and squeeze it in. While that technique takes a little practice, it would normally take me five bags of air to get the pad taut. In the morning, I simply folded the mat in thirds and rolled it up to press out the air.

Comfort Cradle: The idea of this trademarked system is that the side rails are larger than the inner air chambers and they center you on the mat. As a restless sleeper who never ends up in the same spot by morning, this was essential for restful sleep.

Bluesign approved fabrics: The fabric in Exped Sleeping Mats are sourced from a supply chain that eliminates hazardous substances, conserves water and energy and protects factory workers. Also, the mats are made with recycled synthetic insulation that is more sustainable and environmentally conscious.

One thing I have had trouble understanding is just exactly what R-value is and how we can know whether this mat will keep us warm when it’s minus 40°.

Essentially, R-value measure a material’s thermal resistance, its ability to resist heat flow. It’s important to know because when we’re sleeping out in the wild, especially an extreme environment like the Himalaya, we want to ensure we don’t lose body heat. To test a sleeping mat’s R-value, Exped sends it to a lab for a rigorous, standardized test developed by ASTM International.

Here’s what that looks like: the mat itself is sandwiched between two metal plates in a temperature-controlled chamber. The top plate is heated to a normal body temperature, while the bottom is chilled. Over a period of time, testers measure how much electrical energy the top plate requires to maintain its temperature.

I can’t speak personally to this mat’s insulating performance at the lowest rated temperature (minus 40) since I only saw temperatures between 5 and 10° Fahrenheit. Still, 5 – 10˚F is very cold indeed to sleep on the ground, and I stayed warm.

The key with a high R-value pad is that it works in tandem with your sleeping bag, ensuring that you can achieve the bag’s stated level of warmth without all the heat trapped by the bag draining away into the ground. For comparison, on a different Himalayan expedition, I slept on a lighter pad with a R-value around 4. Even though my sleeping bag was rated to 20 degrees, I needed to cuddle up with hot water bottles at night to stay warm.

When I got the pad all blown up and set into position, I have to be honest: it didn’t look particularly fancy. It’s a dark gray, rectangular pad that has a whiff of old-school feel (e.g., heavy and cumbersome). It also looked thin with that water-bed flavor that would either leave me uncomfortbaly wedged into a crevice or thrown off the pad completely by morning.

The reality was quite the opposite. While I want to make the caveat that I tend to prefer harder mattresses, I felt both cradled and supported on the Exped. Each vertical chamber is welded, keeping the pad from feeling like a bouncing balloon. The outer chambers have just a tiny bit more loft to keep you centered. Maybe most importantly, the pad remained absolutely silent while I faffed about in my sleep. Just a note here, many a camper sleeping near me has complained about my noisy sleep habits from noisy sleep pads over the years.

Another noteworthy comfort feature is the rectangular shape, which made this pad feel luxurious and more apt to hold my body while I slept compared to a more minimalist, mummy-shaped pad. Have you ever found it hard to keep your arms on your sleeping pad? They tend to flop off or feel unsupported. As a side-sleeper, I have often had my arms go numb overnight while backpacking. In contrast, the Exped Ultra 10R felt similar to my own cozy bed at home and my arms had plenty of support.

The Schnozzel pump bag is unassuming yet very effective

I admit it took a bit of practice to figure out how to fill the Schnozzel pump bag (though very fun to say Schnozzel pump bag over and over and elicit a giggle.) The Schnozzel pump bag is a multi-purpose dry sack that inflates the air mattress quickly. It works by your manually scooping ambient air into the bag, sealing the roll-top and then squeezing the air into the sleeping pad. This eliminates the need for bulky air pumps and prevents moisture from your breath from causing mold inside the pad.

I never bothered sealing the roll-top. Instead I raised the bag or pointed it towards the wind if my tent fly was still open, then sent the air inside. Exped does sell separately a tiny, 3.5 ounce, rechargeable electric pump. I used it and it works like a dream. But honestly, this is not like filling a paddle board. If  even I can fill the pad with my lousy Schnozzel skills, there’s really no need to take another piece of gear.

A caveat here: a trek in the Upper Dolpo region of the Himalaya requires a guide and is more feasible with the support of porters and a cook. As a result, I never had to carry the Exped Ultra 10R in my pack.

A little taller than a one-liter water bottle, the pad is not tiny, but I would liken its size to a small tent. Should you be using a pulk or sled while skiing or snowshoeing, or if gear is being transferred over many trips to high camps, the pad’s size is negligible. I was able to fit into the stuff sack not only the mattress, but also the Schnozzel pump bag and the very comfortable, attachable Exped pillow.

If you you could experience how a Nepali porter grabs a duffel, joins it with two other duffels by tying them tightly together, then attaches them via a tumpline to his forehead and carries them up and over 17,000-foot passes, you would be very humbled indeed.

The young man who carried my gear (who was well paid and given a good tip for all his work) would drop my bag on the ground as he sat down, unconcerned about what might tear or get damaged inside. One evening, I received my bag muddy and damp when he slipped at a water crossing. Nothing was damaged.

The Exped Ultra 10R Sleeping Mat is made of 20-denier ripstop polyester, which is light and packs down small while also holding up its strength. It’s comfortable, but tough. The lamination itself is water-resistant and airtight, which should enhance its longevity. I slept on it, sat on it, ate dinner on it a few nights, all over a 20-day trek and never saw one blemish.

Perhaps most notable about this Exped pad (and really all of Exped’s products) is the brand’s desiccation to sustainability. It finances certified climate protection to offset all emissions from the production and transportation of its products.

For over a year, Exped has intentionally manufactured all of its products without added PFAS and, when possible, from recycled materials.

Most critically, Exped focuses on using Bluesign approved textiles, the strictest standard for not only environmental but also consumer protections.

The Exped Ultra 10R has very little competition in its category: backcountry sleeping pads with an R-value this high are rare. Among the few pads at this warmth level that do exist, the Ultra 10R’s warmth-to-weight ratio compares favorably.

Weighing less than two pounds and offering an R-Value of 10.2, all at a comparable price point, purchasing this particular sleeping pad is money well spent. Throw in its generous size and unbeatable comfort level, non-crinkly materials, and ease of inflation, and the Exped Ultra 10R Sleeping Mat gets my highest recommendation.

#3627. Downsizing and debt swaps: Pace Gallery files latest UK accounts

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In the wake of public retrenchment over the summer, Pace Gallery’s UK registered company, which serves as the parent company for all of the firm’s operations outside the US and Tokyo, has filed its 2024 accounts with Companies House.

The accounts—which were due at the end of December 2025 and filed ten months late—report earnings for The Pace Gallery Limited (“the company”) and various subsidiary undertakings in Asia and Europe (“the group”).

The group incurred a pre-tax loss of $2,429 in 2024, compared to a profit of $6.8m in 2023. Such a drop, the directors’ report states, reflects a "continuation of the sector-wide contraction in global and UK art markets as well as a continued reluctance by some clients to buy which became most pronounced in 2024 and continued through 2025 and into 2026". These market conditions led the directors to "initiate cost saving measures across the group, but despite these measures, difficult market conditions continued".

Total group turnover stood at $67.7m in 2024 (2023: $104.9m), of which some $48m was contributed by its subsidiaries. Only $4.1m of this was sold to the UK (2023: $10.7m) while $60.5m went to the rest of the world.

Net assets amounted to $2.5m as of the end of 2024 (2023: $4.5m), of which $9.4m (2023: $17.3m) was payable to the US parent company, The Pace Gallery LLC. Debts and other amounts owed falling within one year stood at $49m, compared to $50.8m at the end of 2023.

Although the filings refer to 2024 results, late filing meant they also outline cost-saving measures implemented this summer. “Throughout 2025 the group developed a comprehensive plan to refocus the gallery's mission by streamlining strategy, reprioritising relationships with artists and collectors, and reducing operating expenses,” the report says. This plan was first announced by Pace’s chief executive Marc Glimcher in May, when he told The New York Times that the gallery was cutting 50 artists and 50 staff. Such a diminished artist roster, the filings say, “reduced the number of full-time support roles and resources” needed, hence the concurrent staff cuts.

The group’s global footprint has also been scaled back, the filings say, “which decreased the number of exhibitions the group is engaging in, as well as lowering overheads” (the gallery’s Hong Kong space was shut in October 2025). Such consolidation “will allow the group to focus its resources, better support its artists and collectors, and return to a more efficient and profitable approach,” the filings say. Furthermore, only last month it was reported that Pace is seeking a tenant to share its enormous eight-storey New York headquarters in order to share operating costs.

The Companies House filings mention the partnership with Emmanuel Di Donna and David Schrader to form Pace Di Donna Schrader Galleries (PDS), first announced in December 2025, with the purpose of expanding the group's secondary market business. Along with the “counter-cyclical investment” in the form of a new gallery opened in Berlin in 2025 as “a foothold in Europe”, the directors believe the company is “well placed to take advantage of any improvement in market conditions in 2026 and beyond, despite the ongoing uncertainties associated with geopolitical tensions and inflationary increased in operational expenses.” The report concludes that with the above changes, the directors “have a strong belief in the future of the company and group.”

The 2024 filings also show that severance pay of $1m was paid to one key member of departing management staff, though the gallery declines to identify the employee. Advances of $5.7m were paid to artists (2023: $2.9m), of which $306,235 (2023: $9.4m) have been written down as irrecoverable, whether due to the saleability of the works held on consignment or the saleability of the artist's works more generally.

The Pace Gallery Limited, it should be remembered, is a subsidiary of the US parent company, The Pace Gallery LLC (controlled by Marc Glimcher), which—together with Marc and his father Arne Glimcher—underwrites it. Therefore, these filings only give a partial picture of the broader corporate structure and its international operations.

Nonetheless it is interesting that the filings reveal a planned debt for equity swap as the UK registered entity, The Pace Gallery Limited, is unable to repay its debt of $19.2m to the US parent company, The Pace Gallery LLC. This unpaid group debt will be turned into shares by the end of 2026. “The proposed debt-for-equity conversion, part of a broader global financial management strategy, reflects a capital structure decision within the Pace group and should not be interpreted as a loss or a liquidity issue for the UK business,” a Pace spokesperson tells The Art Newspaper. The company reviews how its subsidiaries are financed “to ensure that their balance sheets appropriately reflect the long-term nature of the capital invested in those businesses” the spokesperson says. In this case, the parent company has decided that some of its investment in the UK operation “is more appropriately reflected as equity rather than intercompany debt”, they continue: “The proposed transaction is intended to strengthen the UK company's balance sheet and align its capital structure with the long-term needs of the business.”

The share issuance will not make any material difference to the running of the UK group, the spokesperson says, and on the planned move of the London gallery’s premises from Hanover Square to a smaller space on nearby Grafton Street in Mayfair, there is no news yet.

#1228. This Colorado Mountain Escape Is Home to a Plum Kitchen, “Quilted” Bedroom, and the Occasional Grizzly Bear

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Designer Emily Tucker designed her family’s rustic retreat with hosting in mind.

The first guest to make himself at home at designer Emily Tucker’s Colorado mountain retreat was a bear. While the property was still under construction, the curious visitor took to wandering through the unfinished rooms, even leaving a few paw prints on the architectural drawings. It was a fitting introduction to life at Camp Grizz, Tucker’s family getaway in Ward, Colorado, where the surrounding wilderness has always had a say in things.

Tucker and her family purchased the wooded parcel, which backs up to a national forest, in 2020. The existing cabin offered rustic accommodations (read: no running water), but the family had bigger plans for the property. “The goal was to build a house as modest as possible, but able to fit two families at a time,” says Tucker, who regularly hosts friends and their children for weekends away. Building on a landscape composed almost entirely of granite, however, was no small feat. With no existing infrastructure to tap into, everything from the well to the driveway had to be created from scratch. “It was a beast,” the designer adds.

Tucker envisioned interiors that felt “colorful, warm, Scandinavian-ish, but with a lot of collected touches,” taking inspiration from the mismatched charm of a patchwork quilt. That influence appears everywhere, from the primary bedroom’s pieced-together grasscloth walls to the checkerboard bathroom tile and eclectic mix of textiles. Designing her own home also offered Tucker the opportunity to indulge ideas that hadn’t quite found an audience with her clients. Chief among them? An eggplant-purple kitchen, complete with plum cabinetry and glossy zellige tile. “I had proposed purple kitchens and mudrooms for many clients who had all said no,” she laughs.

But Camp Grizz’s greatest luxury has little to do with its finishes. “My favorite thing about the house is the way we feel in it,” Tucker says. “It’s cozy, layered, collected, and holds our family and friends for celebrations, relaxing, and just enjoying life’s small moments.”

A chance discovery in a stair-parts catalog led Tucker to the charming Swiss-style balusters that distinguish the staircase. The decorative stenciling came later, inspired by a trip to London during construction. Tucker and her son worked out the pattern together on a spare baluster before local decorative painter Annie DeCamp brought their design to life.

A mix of existing furnishings (a “hodge-pod of things I already owned,” says Tucker) gives the living room its collected, unfussy character. Flanking the wood-burning stove, a pair of floating live-edge wood benches act as a happy departure from Tucker’s original plan for concrete seating. “When the opportunity came up to use the live-edge wood slabs, it seemed the perfect fit,” she says.

“The vintage Thomas Moser chairs were purchased before we even broke ground on the house; I love these chairs and always have,” Tucker says of the seating in the dining nook. “The classic but modern, pared-back Americana design was perfect for the space.” Nearby, trim clad in Rattan by Benjamin Moore unites the open floor plan.

After years of pitching purple kitchens to clients without success, Tucker finally took the plunge herself. “Once I landed on eggplant purple, I couldn't get it out of my head,” she says. Benjamin Moore’s Plum Royale cabinetry meets glossy Purple Iris zellige tile from Zia Tile, with soapstone countertops providing a moody counterpoint. Behind the saturated facade, clever cabinetry conceals appliances and pantry essentials, leaving the countertops streamlined and clear.

For her own bedroom, Tucker gathered leftover Phillip Jeffries wallcoverings from previous projects, supplemented them with her installer’s remnants, and pieced together a one-of-a-kind patchwork-style wall treatment. The room’s generous ceiling height also gave her the chance to indulge another longtime wish: a canopy bed, which she dressed with modified Anthropologie curtains.

A length of cowboy-print fabric gifted to Tucker by a client became the unexpected starting point for the bunk room’s spirited palette. Picking up on the textile’s red accents, she painted the built-in bunks in Benjamin Moore’s Caliente and layered them with a deliberately mismatched assortment of Pendleton blankets and pillows from Target, St. Frank, and IKEA.

The home’s patchwork inspiration finds another expression in the bunk bathroom, where blue and green tiles from Wow Tile form an intentionally irregular checkerboard. Leftover green-veined marble flooring from another project adds another layer of color.

Salvaged architectural pieces are always going to be popular, like doors or fireplace surrounds for example, but Baxter is really drawn to antique pedestals and plinths at the moment. “A pedestal gives one object, like a vase or a sculpture, its own focal point and gives a neglected corner or window bay some personality,” she explains. #444. Scientists Studied 23,000-Year-Old Human Footprints—and Reconstructed a Lost World Around Them

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The footprints in what is now White Sands National Park were left when Earth’s environment was remarkably different, scientists learned.

The ancient human population that traversed what is now White Sands National Park in New Mexico encountered a shockingly different environment when they were there between 23,500 and 21,700 years ago.

Back in the present, the same research team that pioneered the dating of preserved footprints in the sand in 2021 took the study a step further, analyzing the sediment surrounding the prints to discover a rapidly changing environment, one the team called “an abrupt environmental change at White Sands,” in a new study published in Science Advances.

By analyzing 20 stratigraphic horizons with in situ human footprints, megafaunal trackways, and linear features created by historic wheels, the team used radiocarbon dating of conifer pollen, combined with ages of aquatic seeds, to refine their dating timeline. Researchers believe the evidence indicates a “complete overturning of the environment from a perennial lake to a playa [dry lake bed]” from the height of the ice age.

“This well-dated and highly resolved paleoclimate record,” the study authors wrote, “provides compelling evidence that humans in North America experienced marked environmental changes during the Last Glacial Maximum.”

The researchers now believe that their data, published in both the new Science Advances study on climate and a Fossil Studies study on the footprint record, not only strengthens the dating of the footprints but provides snapshots of life around the lake through information on temperature and rainfall. “Together, these allowed us to reconstruct the landscape through which people were walking,” researchers Matthew Bennett and Sally Reynolds wrote in a post published by The Conversation.

Lake Otero occupied much of the basin at the start of the timeline, with the mountains around it full of conifer forests and temperatures about 4 degrees Celsius colder than today. Then temperatures began to rise; the lake shrunk, forests declined, and oak and sagebrush crept in. “As a result of this warming, the basin became progressively drier as evaporation increased,” they wrote. “Superimposed upon this longer-term change were much sharper climate swings.”

A drought impacted things heavily and “this dry period coincides with a rapid climatic change recorded thousands of miles away in Greenland ice cores.” The earliest footprints occur around the climatic transition, with footprints continuing at intervals as the lake retreated. “Our new evidence therefore shows that people visited this landscape repeatedly while experiencing substantial environmental change,” the team wrote.

The researchers used agent-based computer modeling to explore what the footprints represent in human movement, revealing complex behavior patterns. “Our virtual world consisted of people moving around a fluctuating lake,” the team wrote. “When they crossed mud, they left footprints. Those footprints gradually disappeared unless changing water levels crated the right conditions for them to be buried and preserved.”

In other words, sometimes human activity around the lake left virtually no fossil record, and other times, plenty of preserved footprints remained. “The footprint surfaces occur as irregular pulses that tend to cluster around periods of climatic and hydrological change—just the pattern predicted by the model,” the team wrote.

That data now gives a much fuller view of ancient human history and some of the oldest in North America.

#1958. Another Trump term?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

President Donald Trump and his policies were a consistent and high-profile theme during the Supreme Court’s last term. The justices weighed in on several of Trump’s signature initiatives, from sweeping global tariffs and his efforts to limit the availability of birthright citizenship to his attempts to fire members of the Federal Trade Commission and Federal Reserve’s Board of Governors – as well as various important immigration cases. And that’s all before you get to the court’s interim docket, where Trump chalked up a number of significant victories.

As it currently stands, the Supreme Court’s 2026-27 term is much less Trump-centric than its predecessor, featuring two challenges to Trump’s immigration policies and a dispute over access to information about Trump’s U.S. Department of Government Efficiency, also known as DOGE. But that could change soon: between Nov. 6 and mid-January, the justices are likely to consider a number of petitions for review filed by the Trump administration or Trump himself. Given the Office of the Solicitor General, which represents the federal government before the Supreme Court, has a high success rate – usually somewhere around 60% – for petitions that it files with the justices, Trump could once again end up dominating the term.

Immigration is already a hot topic on the court’s docket, but two immigration disputes could raise the prominence of the issue even more. When the justices meet for their Nov. 6 conference, one of the cases that they will consider is Mullin v. Refugee and Immigrant Center for Education and Legal Services, a challenge to a Trump administration policy of quickly removing immigrants who cross the border illegally without giving them the chance to seek asylum.

Two federal courts in Washington, D.C., agreed with the challengers – a group of immigrants and three immigrants’ rights groups – that the policy violates federal immigration law. That prompted U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer to come to the Supreme Court in August, where he argued that the decision by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit “threatens to dismantle” what Department of Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin “describes as an ‘indispensable’ tool for controlling the border.”

Last month, the Trump administration asked the justices to weigh in on another dispute arising from its immigration agenda – this time involving the government’s efforts to detain and deport several students and academics because of their support for Palestine. The government’s petition came in the case of one of those detainees, Badar Khan Suri, an Indian national who came to the United States for a postdoctoral fellowship at Georgetown University. The Trump administration has told the justices that they should also consider granting the petition for review filed recently by Mahmoud Khalil, an Algerian national who was born in a Palestinian refugee camp in Syria and became a lawful permanent resident of the United States in 2024. The government contended that both men should be deported based on determinations by Secretary of State Marco Rubio that their conduct – alleged support for the Islamic militant group Hamas, in Suri’s case, and participation in “antisemitic protests and disruptive activities” for Khalil – compromised U.S. foreign policy.

Attorneys for Suri and Khalil went to federal court in New Jersey and Virginia, asking judges there to review the legality of their detentions on the ground that (among other things) they had been detained in retaliation for their speech. As their cases come to the court, the question that the justices have been asked to decide is whether federal trial courts even have the power to consider such challenges, or whether (as the Trump administration argues) those claims are instead barred by federal immigration law, which generally requires all challenges “arising from” deportation proceedings or the decision to begin such proceedings to go first to a federal appeals court, because they are also related to whether one can be deported. The federal courts of appeals have reached conflicting conclusions on the issue, which increases the likelihood that the justices will agree to weigh in.

Suri’s response to the government’s petition for review and the government’s response to Khalil’s petition are both due on Nov. 20. The justices will likely consider both petitions at a private conference sometime early next year.

In June, the Supreme Court granted the federal government’s petition for review in Genalo v. Black, agreeing to address whether there is a point at which a noncitizen has been detained so long that he or she has a constitutional right to a bond hearing and, if so, what standard the government must meet for him or her to remain in detention. That case was scheduled for oral argument in October, but it was dismissed in September because it was moot – that is, no longer a live controversy.

The Trump administration returned to the court later that month, asking the justices to grant a petition for review involving the same questions, which Sauer called “exceptionally important.” The case was filed by a citizen of the Dominican Republic, identified in court filings only as D.C., who has spent most of his adult life in the United States and has three daughters with a U.S. citizen. D.C. was detained on the same day that he finished serving a prison sentence, and the government sought to deport him.

In March 2026, when he had been in immigration custody for almost six-and-a-half months, U.S. District Judge Paul Engelmayer agreed with D.C. that he was entitled to “an individualized bond hearing, with constitutionally adequate procedural safeguards.” The Trump administration is now appealing that decision to the Supreme Court without waiting for a federal appeals court to rule on the case. Sauer told the justices that “granting immediate review would allow the Court to consider” the questions it had already agreed to decide in Black during the 2026-27 term.

D.C.’s response is currently due on Dec. 8; if that deadline remains in place, the court would likely consider the government’s petition sometime in early to mid-January.

In Trump v. Thakur, the justices will consider a petition for review stemming from the 2025 decision to end research grants from the Environmental Protection Agency, National Science Foundation, and National Endowment for the Humanities for researchers at the University of California. The researchers went to federal court, where they argued that the termination of those grants violated (among other things) their rights to free speech and fair treatment, as well as the federal law governing administrative agencies.

A federal district court issued a preliminary order requiring the government to reinstate the grants. The government went to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit, which upheld that decision with regard to the claims on the basis that the administration had ended the grants because of the recipients’ “perceived expression of DEI, DEIA, or environmental justice viewpoints.”

The court of appeals rejected the government’s contention that it could terminate funding for programs that, in its view, are not in the public interest. “This overlooks that there is a critical distinction between creating or ceasing a particular program (or subsidy, or forum), on one hand, and discriminating against disfavored speaker viewpoints within a program (or subsidy, or forum), on the other,” the 9th Circuit wrote.

The Trump administration came to the Supreme Court in August, asking the justices to weigh in. Sauer told the court that “if left to stand, the Ninth Circuit’s decision will establish precedent forcing taxpayers to waste countless dollars on private projects promoting ideologies that their public stewards reject.”

Last week Sauer asked the Supreme Court to distribute the government’s petition, as well as the researchers’ brief opposing review, on Oct. 28, without waiting the 14 days normally prescribed by the court’s rules. This would allow the justices to consider the case at their Nov. 13 conference.

In United States v. Jackson, the Trump administration has asked the Supreme Court to rule on the legality of Trump’s appointment of an acting U.S. attorney in Nevada. Sauer told the justices that the appointment of Sigal Chattah (as well as others around the country) was not only lawful, but “a critical tool that the Executive Branch has used without meaningful objection for 25 years.”

Under the Federal Vacancies Reform Act, the federal law that governs how the executive branch can temporarily staff vacant positions that require presidential appointment and confirmation by the Senate, interim officers can serve for 120 days. Under that law, then-Attorney General Pamela Bondi appointed Chattah as the interim U.S. Attorney for the District of Nevada, effective April 1, 2025, after Jason Frierson, who was nominated by then-President Joe Biden, stepped down.

With only a few days remaining before her interim appointment was set to expire, Chattah resigned, and Bondi designated her as the first assistant U.S. attorney. In that post, Bondi said, Chattah would have the power under the FVRA “to serve as Acting United States Attorney upon a vacancy in that office.” Bondi also gave Chattah the powers of a U.S. attorney for legal proceedings in Nevada.

Three men who had been indicted on felony charges in Nevada after Chattah’s designation as the first assistant U.S. attorney sought to have, at a minimum, Chattah disqualified from appearing in the case as the “Acting U.S. Attorney.” The lower courts agreed that Chattah was not eligible to serve as the acting U.S. attorney, and that she could not play any role in the prosecution of the defendants’ cases.

In a 34-page opinion, Judge Eric Miller of the 9th Circuit wrote that the “text and structure” of the FVRA are “unambiguous: The statute does not permit the executive to fill an existing vacancy by designating a new first assistant.” Instead, he reasoned, the FVRA “provides that a first assistant automatically succeeds to a vacant office” only if the first assistant was first assistant when the principal office became vacant. The automatic-succession rule, he concluded, “does not apply to a first assistant” – like Chattah – “who never served under a validly appointed official.” Moreover, Miller continued, the attorney general cannot achieve the same result “by delegating all of the functions and duties of the U.S. Attorney to someone else.”

In a second petition, United States v. Office of the New York State Attorney General, the Trump administration has appealed a ruling against it by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 2nd Circuit in a dispute over the appointment of John Sarcone as the Acting U.S. Attorney for the Northern District of New York. When Sarcone obtained grand jury subpoenas for information from the New York attorney general’s office relating to its investigations of the National Rifle Association and Trump, the office sought to quash the subpoenas on the ground that Sarcone had not been validly appointed.

A federal district court granted that request, and the 2nd Circuit upheld that decision. Sauer came to the Supreme Court in September, where he told the justices that Jackson was a better case in which to consider the validity of Trump’s appointments because the grand jury that issued the subpoenas in the New York case had been discharged and, as a result, the subpoenas were no longer enforceable. He urged the court to hold the New York case until it acts on Jackson.

The defendants challenging Chattah’s appointment have until Nov. 5 to file their brief opposing review; the response of the N.Y. attorney general’s office is currently due on Nov. 2.

There are several other notable petitions pending.

In May 2025, the Supreme Court cleared the way for the Trump administration’s request to temporarily enforce a ban on transgender servicemembers. In a different case just over one year later, a divided panel of the D.C. Circuit upheld part of an order by a federal judge in Washington, D.C., barring the Trump administration from expelling several challengers to the policy from the military. (At the same time, that panel lifted the part of the order that would have required the military to admit transgender people who want to join the armed forces.)

In its petition for review in United States v. Talbott, filed in August, the federal government asked the Supreme Court to review and eventually reverse the D.C. Circuit’s decision. Sauer called it “gravely erroneous” and argued that its “reasoning cannot be squared with this Court’s precedents or deference to the military.”

The response from the challengers is currently due on Nov. 2.

In Department of Health and Human Services v. Texas Medical Association, the Trump administration has asked the Supreme Court to weigh in on whether and when a federal court can throw out an agency’s action or rule in its entirety – a concept known as “universal vacatur.”

The question comes to the court in a case that began as a pair of challenges (that were later combined) to rules issued to implement parts of a 2022 law that was intended to protect patients from surprise healthcare bills. A federal district court in Texas ruled for the challengers – the Texas Medical Association, a hospital, and four air ambulance companies – but a three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit reversed that decision in part.

The case then went to the full 5th Circuit, which threw out some of the challenged provisions. The Trump administration came to the Supreme Court this month, asking the justices to take up the dispute. It pointed to the Supreme Court’s 2025 decision in Trump v. CASA, holding that federal courts do not have the authority to enter so-called universal or nationwide injunctions, which prohibit the government from enforcing a policy anywhere in the country. The same logic underlying that case – the idea that courts “can grant ‘complete relief’ to parties before the court but cannot, as a rule, reach beyond them” – should apply to such regulations, Sauer contended.

The challengers’ response is currently due on Nov. 13, although that deadline may be extended.

Petitions filed by Trump’s personal lawyers

Although the federal government filed all of the petitions discussed above, Trump is also seeking Supreme Court review in three petitions filed by his personal lawyers. The most recent petition is also the one that the justices could consider first: Trump v. Clinton, the president’s bid to revive his lawsuit against Hillary Clinton (and several others, including the Democratic National Committee) alleging “a coordinated scheme … to undermine his candidacy and Presidency through fabricated accusations of Russian collusion.”

U.S. District Judge Donald Middlebrooks dismissed the lawsuit, finding (among other things) that Trump had brought his claims too late, and that many of the statements alleged to have been made by the various defendants were “plainly protected by the First Amendment.” Middlebrooks also sanctioned Trump and his attorney, Alina Habba, ordering them to pay nearly $1 million.

On appeal, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 11th Circuit largely upheld Middlebrooks’ decision and affirmed the sanctions orders. The full 11th Circuit rejected Trump’s request for reconsideration, prompting Trump – represented by Richard Klugh – to come to the Supreme Court on Oct. 9. He asked the justices to weigh in on two questions: whether the time to file Trump’s claims should have been extended because of his unique role and responsibilities as president, and whether a court can fine the president and his lawyers for making the “substantial, unresolved argument” that the time to file Trump’s claims should have been extended.

Through her longtime attorney, David Kendall, Clinton waived her right to file a response to Trump’s petition. The case has been distributed to the justices for consideration at their Nov. 6 conference. The court is extremely unlikely to grant review without first calling for Clinton to file a brief opposing review.

In Trump v. CNN, the president is seeking to reinstate his defamation case against cable news giant CNN. Trump contends that when CNN used the phrase “the Big Lie” to describe his insistence that he had actually won the 2020 election, which had been stolen from him, it was attempting to link him with Adolf Hitler and Nazi propaganda – which, Trump said, was defamation.

A federal judge in Fort Lauderdale, Florida, dismissed Trump’s case. In an eight-page, unsigned opinion, the 11th Circuit upheld that ruling.

In his petition for review, which was filed in August, Trump contended that “CNN falsely and maliciously portrayed” him “as a knowing liar who fabricated objections to the 2020 election, lacked supporting evidence, and deliberately deceived the American people.”

And in July, Trump filed the second of two petitions arising from lawsuits brought by E. Jean Carroll, who had alleged that in 1996 Trump had sexually abused her in a dressing room at a New York department store. The lawsuit leading to the second petition claimed that in June 2019, Trump had defamed her by calling her a liar – saying, among other things, that Carroll “was trying to sell a new book” and that her accusations were “totally false.” A jury awarded Carroll $83.3 million in January 2024.

The 2nd Circuit upheld the award in 2025, finding “ample evidence that Trump was recklessly indifferent to Carroll’s health and safety” and that “Carroll was subjected to ongoing and prolific harassment as a result of these statements, including a multitude of death threats and other threats of physical injury.” The full court of appeals in April declined to rehear the case.

Represented by a private lawyer, Robert Giuffra, Trump argued in his petition for review that in the wake of the Supreme Court’s 2024 decision in Trump v. United States, in which it held that presidents generally have broad immunity for their acts while in office, the $83.3 million award cannot be allowed to stand. “The threat of even a single damages judgment based on official acts will render all Presidents ‘unable to boldly and fearlessly carry out [their] duties for fear that [they] may be next,’” Giuffra wrote.

The federal government filed its own petition backing Trump, in which it (like Trump) urged the Supreme Court to also decide whether the court of appeals should have granted then-Attorney General Bondi’s request to recertify that Trump made his statements as part of his role as president and therefore the federal government, rather than Trump, should be the defendant in Carroll’s case.

Carroll’s response to both petitions is currently due on Oct. 30.

Shopping for snowboards is like dropping a cliff: It’s equal parts exciting and nerve-wracking. But if you do your homework before you send, chances are you’ll ride out just fine. Here are a few tips to help you stick your landing:

Demo boards from your local shop or at resort demo days, swap with your friends, and do whatever it takes to ride as many boards as you can. Whether you’re an old head or relatively new to the game, testing a diverse range of boards will expand your snowboard lexicon. You can read reviews of the best snowboards all day long, but nothing informs your decision-making like time on snow. One word of advice: Complete newbies should learn to turn properly on a board they’re comfortable with before going on demo sprees.

Profile refers to the curvature that’s visible when you lay a board flat on the ground and look at it from the side. It is a critical element of board design, as it dictates how and where a board interacts with snow. There are two primary profiles to be familiar with: camber and rocker.

Sizing a board can be just as tricky as picking one in the first place. Sizing depends on a number of factors, both in terms of the board itself and the rider who’s rocking it. One outdated sizing method is comparing board length to rider height, which doesn’t account for weight or board width. Instead, focus on these considerations.

Women’s snowboards are generally smaller and more flexible compared to men’s snowboards. Other than those key attributes, the biggest difference between men’s snowboards and women’s snowboards often comes down to graphics, which might impact the aesthetic of your kit, but not the way a board performs on the hill. But don’t let gender dictate what boards you can and can’t ride. Many of our male and female snowboard testers ride unisex boards, which are becoming increasingly popular as brands turn away from largely unhelpful gender constraints. Instead, focus on factors like flex, board length, recommended rider weights, board width (especially regarding your boot size), and other riding features.

Library & Archives, Toledo Museum of ArtCourtesy of the Toledo Museum of Art

The Toledo Museum of Art (TMA) has launched the initiative Africa Art Library to donate books to libraries on the continent. The museum donated around 100 books to the Koyo Kouoh Library at the Gihanga Institute of Contemporary Art in Kigali, Rwanda, earlier this year and a second set is headed to the Nubuke Foundation in Accra, Ghana, this month.

The subjects of the books cover Africa and African diasporic art as well as art history, criticism, museum education and other relevant topics, and have been selected by the receiving libraries from the TMA’s library inventory.

The idea grew out of conversations over the past four years among leaders of the TMA and African art residency spaces, foundations and cultural institutions, during which books for their libraries were among the top requests.

During the process of the museum’s current reinstallation—the first in 40 years—the team realised that “like so many Western institutions, [the TMA] did not have adequate representation of the breadth and depth of artistic traditions on the African continent, including and especially Modern and contemporary art histories”, Adam M. Levine, the museum’s president, director and chief executive officer, tells The Art Newspaper.

The TMA team travelled to African countries and bought artworks for its collection by the likes of Jimoh Akolo, the late Nigerian artist renowned for his contributions to modern Nigerian art. He was also a member of the Zaria Art Society, a group of local artists known for their rejection of Western art-focused curricula by their mainly British lecturers, who chose to embrace indigenous Nigerian art in the late 1950s to early 1960s.

“Our objective was not just to go to these places to try and find artworks but to figure out how we could partner and support these ecosystems without being extractive. Without perpetuating sort of colonialist or imperialist logic,” Levine adds.

Prior relationships and engagements with people on the ground helped select the receiving partners for the pilot Africa Art Library initiative, supported by the Accra- and London-based Gallery 1957 and its founder, Marwan Zakhem.

Alexandra Nickolaou, a fellow in African and African Diasporic Art at the TMA, is leading on the Africa Art Library project and views it as a long-term initiative. She hopes to bring other museum libraries across the US on board as partners and aims to expand and add more recipients in other African countries.

“I think [the initiative] is a really fantastic way to think about partnerships with the continent [...] making sure that these partnerships are not just strengthening repatriation efforts but also creating good lasting partnerships outside of that in other ways that have to do with art education and not just art itself,” says Nickolaou.

Over the past year, the TMA has also partnered with Nigeria’s Yemisi Shyllon Museum of Art for cultural exchanges that boost museum expertise and access, and with the Ethiopian Heritage Authority to return objects to Ethiopia. The TMA is set to reopen its galleries in autumn 2027.
