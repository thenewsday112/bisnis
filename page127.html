#1328. 7 Things Designers Wish More People Knew Before Getting a Kitchen Island

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Pictured above: A warm, texture-rich kitchen with geometric barstools, designed by City Hill Design.

Having an island in the kitchen is definitely a perk—it offers more prep space, provides a casual spot for people to sit, and it creates an architectural point of interest. Designers love them because they create a focal point in the room, and for homeowners, they often help create a better flow.

However, as essential as islands are to large kitchens, there are also some factors that affect the usability of these features that designers say people don’t think about until after they’ve already committed. While these details might not be deal breakers, they can definitely make using your kitchen island more inconvenient and annoying.

We reached out to a handful of interior designers to ask them what things they wish homeowners knew about islands that they likely don’t think about. Though not every kitchen will have an island, anyone who’s putting one in their home should know the seven things listed below before committing to a design.

Just because you can put a sink or a cooktop on your kitchen island, doesn’t mean you should, according to these designers. The most obvious reason why? No one wants to sit next to a pile of dirty dishes.

“When a sink is located on the island, dirty dishes tend to pile up and clean dishes are often left out to dry, creating both visual and actual clutter,” Micaela Quinton of Copper Sky Design + Remodel says. “This inevitably limits how you can use the island, especially when entertaining.”

The island tends to be the focal point of the kitchen, and installing these types of features into the countertop creates an interrupted stone, visually disrupting the space. “I love an island that's completely uninterrupted by appliances or plumbing,” designer Lindsay Angyus of KSA Design Group adds. “It gives you this wonderful expanse of countertop for cooking, entertaining, spreading out a project, or just gathering around.”

For Gaelle Dudley of GLDESIGN, a sink actually isn’t a deal breaker, but a stovetop is. A hot, flat cooktop will always inevitably get messy, and it’s dangerous for little kids who might not be paying attention to where they’re resting their hands.

When it comes to island seating, there are two things to remember: barstool height and layout. Quinton explains that with an island that’s the same height as the other counters, probably around 36 inches tall, then you should specifically be shopping for counter-height barstools. For a taller island, you’d obviously want to buy taller counter seating. Either way, when deciding on the correct height, make sure to consider the amount of space for your legs so they don’t bump the countertop every time people sit down.

There are a few factors when it comes to proper layout too. Catherine Balding of Balding Design Build says that her team plans on 24 to 30 inches of space per person or barstool. The stools, and the people occupying them, need breathing room for comfortability, so if your counter seating area is only 60 inches long, then you should only fit in two barstools. “Ideally, we create a design that accommodates seating for the number of people who gather regularly on any average night at the home, so we use these measurements in connection with that information to create the ideal size island,” Balding adds.

According to Angyus, a lot of homeowners get caught up in the number of seats they can put at an island counter, though they should be thinking about how those people are going to interact with each other once they’re sitting. “Four stools lined up in a row might technically accommodate four people, but it doesn't exactly encourage conversation,” she says. “Whenever possible, I like to create seating around a corner or at adjacent sides so people can actually face one another.” Just don’t squeeze in another stool for the sake of getting another seat—no one wants to eat their dinner with their elbows tucked into their ribs.

The island is the centerpiece of the kitchen—it shouldn’t look like any other cabinetry. Designers say you should treat it like the statement piece that it is.

“An island can be one of the most architectural and sculptural elements in a home,” designer Heather Weisz of HW Interiors says. “Whether it’s an unexpected shape, a beautiful stone waterfall, a furniture-inspired detail, or an incredible combination of materials, there is so much opportunity to create something truly special.”

Balding points out that the furniture-style islands require thoughtful planning though. “A key design detail that makes an island look like a furniture piece are the beautiful, airy cabinet legs and/or feet,” she explains. “This detail requires open space at the bottom of the cabinetry that tends to reduce storage space.” While storage space can be accommodated elsewhere in the kitchen, it can be harder to add on later in the project and should be thought about toward the beginning.

Just like how some rooms are better with smaller footprints, the same goes for the kitchen island. “I've seen enormous islands that look impressive but are incredibly impractical to actually use,” Angyus says. “If you can't comfortably reach across it to clean the countertop, or you're constantly walking around it to get to the other side, you've probably gone too big.”

The island needs to work with the kitchen, not become an obstacle simply because you wanted something showy. A freestanding table or two smaller work areas can make more sense, depending on the space.

As we often say, lighting is the jewelry of the room, and in the kitchen, the impact of proper lighting above the island can’t be overstated.

“Go bold but timeless, and always keep in mind the light the pendants cast in the evening because at the end of the day, they will set the tone for what is also most likely your living room parallel to the space,” Dudley says. For a nine-foot-long island, for example, she likes to use three pendant lights or two oversize ones.

This actually doesn’t have to do with aesthetics but with building codes. “Electrical building codes for kitchen islands have been changing a lot lately,” Quinton explains. “Years ago, they said all islands had to have side outlets. Now in our area, the electrical code says you cannot have an outlet on the side of the island anymore due to safety concerns about young children pulling appliance cords and potentially bringing small appliances down on themselves.”

Unless you forgo outlets on the island completely, which isn’t always the most convenient, then integrated or pop-up outlets are the only options. Quinton adds that pop-up outlets can get expensive though, with some people calling them out as crumb catchers. Ultimately, it’s up to the homeowner to choose whether they want an integrated outlet that’ll cause an interruption in the stone, a pop-up outlet that’ll catch crumbs, or no outlet altogether.

Clearance around your barstools isn’t the only thing you have to think about when planning out your island; the clearance around the actual centerpiece is just as important.

“Homeowners are often tempted to make the island as large as possible, and when initially looking at floor plans, the walkways can look larger than they might feel in real life,” Balding explains.

She recommends a space of 42 inches minimum between the island edge and the perimeter countertop edge, increasing that to 48 inches if there are regularly going to be two cooks in the kitchen. This allows people to move past each other comfortably when cooking together or hosting, especially when opening appliances and drawers.

Are leggings pants? It’s a divisive question. I say yes. As someone who’s been testing activewear for years, I can assure you that today’s leggings are a far cry from the flimsy, nearly see-through pairs many of us grew up with. I have a drawer overflowing with them, and plenty get just as much use with an oversized sweater or blazer as they do at the gym. After years of testing different fabrics and fits, I’ve also figured out which leggings actually work with the rest of my capsule wardrobe.

I find nylon—which has a smoother, more matte finish—looks more luxe than other synthetic fabrics, while minimal seaming (especially a pair without a front seam) makes them look more sleek. Those details make a huge difference when I’m pairing leggings with the dressier pieces in my closet. Once you find the right pair, styling leggings comes down to pairing them with pieces that either add structure (like a blazer or boxy zip-up) or add a dressy element (like a heeled boot or tailored trench coat).

Ahead, I’m sharing the ways I actually wear leggings in my everyday life, plus the tops, jackets, shoes, and accessories I reach for most. Whether you’re firmly on team “leggings are pants” or need a little convincing, these outfit ideas should give you a few new ways to wear the pairs already sitting in your drawer.

You can opt for a heeled boot or a flat riding boot for this outfit; it depends on how dressy you want to get. Once you find the right boot (I like a black boot with black leggings), layer a blazer over a blouse or sweater. I like the way a turtleneck or mock neck balances out a more casual bottom like leggings, but a semi-structured blazer also does a lot of the heavy lifting here. If you work in a more casual setting, this can totally be an office outfit (I recently wore this to a team lunch)—but I also love this outfit for holiday get-togethers and dinners with friends.

For this combo, start with a zip-up hoodie layered over a plain white T-shirt or tank. A zip-up with a boxy, structured cut and a fabric with a bit more heft to it is key to prevent the outfit from looking frumpy. From there, grab a pair of chunky sneakers, like the New Balance 9060, and scrunchy crew socks. It might not seem like much, but this outfit nails that clean-girl vibe and is my ideal errand-running, movie theater, or mall ensemble.

Leggings are basically synonymous with comfort, and you can dial into that without going full gym-mode or couch potato by adding the right cozy pieces. Grab a pair of scrunchy or cable-knit socks, a pair of slippers (like my go-to Ugg Cozy Slipper), and an oversized sweater. You can also layer on a fleece vest or triangle scarf for extra warmth. I frequently wear this outfit on work-from-home days when I don’t want to change out of my pajamas or for casual hangs with friends on a chilly fall day.

A trench coat is my secret weapon for dressing up pretty much anything in my closet, leggings included. Start with your favorite (or a long wool coat) and toss it on over a sweater and a pair of basic leggings. Then step into a Chelsea boot—I went with my Blundstones. I added a pair of white quarter-length socks to visually break up my leggings and black shoes, but no-shows work too. This is an ideal rainy-day outfit or just great for transitional weather when you don’t want to be dressy or too casual.

#1357. The 25 Best Movies on Apple TV

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mountain climbers, Formula 1 racers, and the Beastie Boys make up some of Apple TV’s best movies.

Is Apple TV one of the best entertainment streamers? It’s up for debate, but the tech giant’s catalog of original shows and movies makes a really strong case on its behalf. Shows like Widow’s Bay and Pluribus have seized the attention of Esquire’s staffers. But what about its movies? While Apple TV’s movie offerings have been more of a mixed bag, there are still quite a handful of films that are worth putting on for movie night.

Since Apple launched its streaming service in 2019 as Apple TV+ (rebranded to just “Apple TV” in 2025), the Cupertino-based brand has become a powerhouse in the streaming wars. While everyone in Hollywood was playing catch-up to Netflix, Apple TV’s library of television shows earned it prestige once associated with HBO. Not every show from Apple TV has been a slam dunk, but its output-to-hits ratio is unusually strong. So, what about the movies?

While there are plenty of Apple TV movies that can’t shake off that ineffable, know-it-when-you-see-it “streaming movie” stink, there are just as many that delight and amaze. Here are 25 of the best movies you can stream on Apple TV.

More than just a rock-climbing movie, Tenzing puts a well-deserved spotlight on the first-ever summit of Mount Everest—and the men who made the impossible happen. Genden Phuntsok and Tom Hiddleston star as Tenzing Norgay and Sir Edmund Hillary, who in 1953 etched their names into the history books when they ascended to the top of Mount Everest.

After director Joseph Kosinski took to the skies in 2022 with Top Gun: Maverick, he teamed up with Brad Pitt to conquer Formula One in F1: The Movie. Awkward branding aside, this pedal-to-the-metal sports drama stars Pitt as an aging racer who makes a comeback to help an underdog team win championship glory. It’s a movie about talented men who are a bit sad and live their lives on the edge of victory and death. If that isn’t catnip to Esquire readers, I don’t know what is.

Horror director Scott Derrickson takes a detour into romance with his action-thriller The Gorge, starring Miles Teller and Anya Taylor-Joy. Set in a remote and government-classified gorge, two elite operatives from opposite sides of the world take watch in their respective towers. Over a long year, the two form a bond, but their budding romance is threatened when the mysteries of the gorge begin to crawl out. An action-horror-thriller-romance, The Gorge defies genre labels to be a perfectly fun popcorn flick.

Now here’s a story I got to tell... Whether you’re a fan of the legendary Beastie Boys or not, you have to see them unpack their career highs and personal lows in this riveting “live documentary” by Spike Jonze. Mike D and Ad-Rock take the stage of the Kings Theatre in Brooklyn to recount their origins, successes, and failures as unlikely hip-hop icons in this “adaptation” of their 2018 memoir Beastie Boys Book. More than just another music doc, Beastie Boys Story is an autopsy of masculinity, vulnerability, and growth through the eyes of two guys (RIP Adam Yauch) who sabotaged the color lines of rap music forever.

Death has never been so damn—steamy? In this delightful rom-com drama from director David Freyne, Miles Teller plays a deceased man who reunites with his beloved wife (Elizabeth Olsen) at the Junction—a way-station for the dead until they decide their permanent destinations. Things get complicated fast when Olsen’s Joan also reunites with her long-dead first husband (Callum Turner), who has been waiting for her for a literal eternity. Who will Joan pick? Essentially a modernized version of Defending Your Life, Eternity tugs at your heartstrings and tickles your funny bone, sometimes all at once.

Causeway stars Jennifer Lawrence as Lynsey, a war veteran who suffers a traumatic brain injury while working in Afghanistan. She’s sent home to recover and struggles to reacclimate to “normal” life. Well, what is normal after you’ve quite literally been to war and back?

In Cha Cha Real Smooth, a college graduate named Andrew becomes a bar mitzvah party starter. Due to his new job, he crosses paths with a young mother and her autistic daughter, Lola. When Andrew breaks Lola out of her shell, he begins a complicated relationship with her mother, Domino.

America’s dad, Tom Hanks, ranks up from Captain Sully to Commander Krause in this adaptation of the 1955 novel The Good Shepherd by C. S. Forester. Set during World War II at the Battle of the Atlantic, Hanks stars as a U. S. Navy commander on his first assignment: leading a convoy ship across the Atlantic and defending against U-boats. (Oh, you thought your first day on the job was brutal?)

After Martin Scorsese’s dalliance with Netflix, the acclaimed filmmaker went to Apple TV for his 2023 epic, Killers of the Flower Moon. Based on the 2017 book by David Grann about the real-life Osage murders, Scorsese’s movie stars Leonardo DiCaprio (a frequent Scorsese collaborator) and Lily Gladstone in this sprawling period crime drama about wealth, power, and who, exactly, is allowed to profit from stolen land.

On the Rocks stars Rashida Jones and Bill Murray as Laura and Felix Keane, a dysfunctional father-daughter duo. When Laura thinks her husband is having an affair, she confides in her father. His suggestion? Investigate! Together, they embark on a stakeout, which proves to be a lot funnier than they imagined.

Sharper is an A24-produced film about money, greed, and power. Told from the perspectives of five different people, the plot follows a Manhattan-based con artist who targets billionaires. Aside from its gripping storyline, Sharper includes fantastic performances from Julianne Moore, Justice Smith, Brianna Middleton, and Sebastian Stan.

When Cameron (Mahershala Ali) is diagnosed with a terminal illness, he’s given the opportunity to cheat death. So long as he withholds the information from his family, Cameron can continue “living” through a clone. The bot looks exactly like him and is programmed to hold the same memories and personality. It seems like a solid solution—but as the real Cameron grows sicker, he wonders if he made the right choice.

The criminally underrated Tetris stars Taron Egerton as Henk Rogers. After discovering Tetris in 1988, Henk realizes it can be the next big thing (no kidding!). In order to mass-produce the game, Henk travels to the Soviet Union to hunt down the inventor Alexey Pajitnov. He hopes that they can market the product together—but as it turns out, obtaining the rights to a video game is harder than you’d imagine.

Okay, yeah, I know your high school English teacher forced you to read Macbeth, but I promise the story is worth revisiting. This iteration stars Denzel Washington as the brooding lord of Scotland and Frances McDormand as his wife, Lady Macbeth. In his quest for power, Macbeth descends into madness, causing a wave of destruction. (And prompting a valuable life lesson.)

The Best Picture–winning CODA stars Emilia Jones as Ruby, the only hearing member of her family. She spends her mornings helping out with the family fishing business, but at school, Ruby explores her own passions. When she joins the choir, Ruby’s teacher encourages her to take the hobby seriously and pursue it as a career, but she fears it’ll distance her from her family.

What happens when you let a group of teenage boys form a pseudo-government? Would you expect a Lord of the Flies situation or something a bit more sophisticated? Boys State puts that notion to the test. This documentary follows a group of boys in Texas who are tasked with creating a pseudo-government. The result gives a peek at what democracy looks like to kids today.

From the producers who created Boys State, Girls State bravely showcases how teenage girls view the future of America. The film embeds the viewer within a group of Missouri-based kids who, over the course of a week, build a government from scratch. In the process, they run for office and discuss societal issues, all while building the foundation for a potential career in politics.

Academy Award nominee Lily Gladstone stars as Jax in Fancy Dance. The film tracks Jax’s plight to find her sister after she goes missing. During the search, she takes custody of her niece Roki and helps her train for a powwow.

Anthony Mackie and Samuel L. Jackson star as Bernard Garrett and Joe Morris, respectively, in The Banker. Set in the 1960s, this story follows two Black men who swindle their way into a career in real estate. Their scheme allows them to segregate neighborhoods and sell homes to Black families—but when people catch on to the ploy, both Bernard’s and Joe’s livelihoods are put at risk.

The Velvet Underground is an exceptional music documentary directed by Todd Haynes. The film dives into New York’s punk scene in the 1960s, when a band called the Velvet Underground took shape. With a mix of archival and expert interviews, the movie shines a light on a band that pioneered alternative music. Oh, and did we mention Andy Warhol managed them? Come for the music-history lesson and stay for the star-studded cameos.

At seventeen years old, Billie Eilish reached a level of fame that even the most talented artists dream of. So how did she do it? The World’s A Little Blurry is a music documentary that tracks Billie’s rapid ascent, from the release of “Ocean Eyes,” her debut single, to the creation of her first album, When We All Fall Asleep, Where Do We Go? With appearances from her parents and brother-turned-producing-partner Finneas, this documentary gives a brilliant look at the life of a teenage superstar.

When Flora (Eve Hewson) learns that her son, Max (Orén Kinlan), has gone off the beaten path, she tries to rehabilitate him with music. It’s a last-ditch effort—one more strike and the kid goes to juvie. At first, her plan fails, but soon after, Flora and Max reconnect through a dusty old guitar and a DJ set. The result is a heartwarming and hilarious story about parenthood and connection.

When was the last time you watched a hand-drawn movie? During the pandemic, Apple released Wolfwalkers, a gorgeous animated feature based on Celtic folklore. Directed by Ross Stewart and Tomm Moore, the movie follows Robyn (voiced by Honor Kneafsey), a young hunter who befriends a free-spirited girl (Eva Whittaker) whose tribe can turn into wolves. A gorgeous storybook-style fantasy, Wolfwalkers’ emotional storytelling and themes of friendship transcending prejudices make it a true gem in the Apple TV library.

In Raymond & Ray, Ewan McGregor and Ethan Hawke star as—you guessed it!—Raymond and Ray, two estranged brothers who are united by their father’s death. To their surprise, the man’s final wish was for his sons to dig his grave. While struggling to accomplish the task, Raymond and Ray unpack their family trauma and find their way back to each other.

We all know what happened in 2020. We don’t need to relive it. But in case you were curious how the natural world adapted to a world without people (temporarily, anyway), the Apple TV documentary The Year Earth Changed, narrated by David Attenborough, captured for posterity how wildlife adapted to widespread lockdown. Deer in empty streets, penguins on the roads, and clearer skies without traffic smog—what might our world look like if we all slowed down?

The natural first step beyond a line is to introduce polynomial features. Then we encounter a new limitation: polynomial flexibility is global, so changing one coefficient can reshape the curve everywhere. Splines offer another route by introducing flexibility locally.

That gives us two very different questions to address: which observations should the estimator trust, and what shapes should the model be capable of representing?

The estimator controls trust. The basis controls shape.

With the representation problem in place, we can now return to the first question: which observations should the estimator trust?

Measurements can go missing because of dropouts, failed recordings, or skipped readings. Missing values may be removed, imputed, or handled through probabilistic methods such as Expectation–Maximization [6]. Here, we deliberately use the simplest option—prefiltering measurements containing non-finite values—because our interest lies in the effect of outliers on curve fitting. This should not be interpreted as a general recommendation for handling missing observations.

Once the explicitly missing measurements have been handled, a more difficult problem remains: what should we do with observations that are present but unreliable?

Outliers can appear because of faulty readings, interference, or unusual operating conditions. Unlike missing values, they are present and finite, and may initially look perfectly legitimate.

This creates a chicken-and-egg problem: we need a reliable model to determine which observations are inconsistent with the underlying relationship, but those same observations can distort the model used to identify them.

Robust estimators address this problem by limiting the influence of observations that are inconsistent with the fitted relationship.

Changing the basis does not require us to redesign the robust estimator.

Huber regression reduces the influence of large residuals [1]. RANSAC searches for a consensus among the observations [2]. GNC-GM and GNC-TLS gradually transform easier optimization problems into more aggressive robust objectives [3]. ASOR adaptively estimates how strongly each observation should contribute to the fit [4].

The two mechanisms therefore solve different problems:

Local support controls where model flexibility acts. Robust estimation controls how strongly unreliable observations act.

Random replacement outliers are difficult, but a coherent competing relationship creates a different problem.

At low and moderate contamination, the robust estimators can still recover the nominal curve. As the two structures approach equal representation, however, the problem becomes ambiguous and different Monte Carlo realizations may favor different relationships.

Once the competing curve becomes the majority structure, the robust estimators generally follow it.

This is not simply an optimization failure. A single-model estimator has no way to know which of two internally consistent relationships is the physically correct one without additional information.

Robust estimation can suppress observations that disagree with the dominant model.

It cannot decide which of two coherent models should be trusted.

Taken together, these experiments show that changing the basis affects more than the shapes a model can represent. It also changes how much data the estimator needs, how expensive consensus-based methods become, and how contamination propagates across the fitted curve.

Robustness is not the same as model flexibility.

The practical workflow is therefore straightforward:

Choose a basis that can represent the relationship of interest.

Center and scale predictors when polynomial powers become poorly conditioned.

Remove identifiable missing rows before fitting when complete-case analysis is appropriate.

Use robust estimation when finite observations may still be contaminated.

Use enough observations to support the chosen basis.

Treat extrapolation more cautiously than interpolation.

#1369. How to Winterize a Garden: Ready Your Plants to Weather the Cold With These Expert Tips

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

As fall winds down, you’ll pull sweaters out of storage and crank the thermostat, but what about your outdoor plants? Knowing how to winterize a garden is key to welcoming spring with an abundance of thriving greenery—as opposed to a sad brown patch of lawn. If frost, snow, rain, and freezing temperatures are in the forecast, know that your outdoor spaces don’t have to face the elements without a little assistance.

To prepare your garden for winter, you’ll need burlap; straw; garden tools like pruning shears and a pruning saw or lopper; a compost pile or ready source of nutrient-rich matter; and, ideally, some winter-ready plants that can take the place of flowering annuals and edible plants.

One more item to add to your list (in potentially large quantities): mulch. “One of the biggest things we do is mulch, and then mulch some more,” says Christina Stembel, founder and CEO of D2C e-commerce flower company Farmgirl Flowers. “We bring in what feels like an absurd amount of mulch every fall and use it just about everywhere. It helps insulate roots, suppress weeds, protect the soil through rain [and snow], and eventually adds organic matter back into the ground. We mulch our garden roses, peonies, perennial beds, [and more].”

Amy Enfield, who has more than 30 years of experience in the lawn- and garden-care industry and is currently a senior horticulturalist at Miracle-Gro, offers thoughts on a mindful first step ahead of digging in: “Before pulling out tools or buying materials, walk your garden and take stock of what you have. Note which plants are annuals that will not return, which are perennials you want to protect, where the soil looks tired or compacted, and which areas might benefit from a cover crop over winter. This assessment keeps you from doing more work than necessary or missing something important.”

Burlap adds a layer of protection against the elements for this outdoor plant in the wintertime.

“Do not begin with applying mulch,” says Jose Lopez, the founder and owner of Williams Landscaping Inc. “First, walk through your fall garden to pull up dead foliage. Pull any foliage that shows signs of disease as well. Next, apply appropriate care to the plants that will remain. Finally, cover your soil. If you do not complete these first three steps, you’re simply burying the problems of the growing season.” To cover that soil, there are three primary materials, each of which plays a different role, Lopez says. “Compost supplies nutrients to the soil. Mulch serves as insulation [and you can] use the leaves that are readily available, shredding them for mulch. Straw has no place in most flowerbeds but is suitable for vegetable gardening.” Just be mindful of where you are placing covering materials, and “do not layer large amounts of wet leaves over a newly planted shrub because the water can cause root rot at its base,” Lopez notes.

Raised garden beds tend to be used for annual flowers and edible plants, and these typically need to be removed, says Dr. Enfield. “Leaving their debris in the garden over winter creates a cozy habitat for overwintering pests and fungal disease.” Pull any spent annuals and finished vegetable plants, disposing of or composting them. “Diseased plant material should go in the trash rather than the compost pile,” Dr. Enfield says.

Once the beds are cleared, Dr. Enfield says to “amend the soil before it freezes. Working organic matter, like compost, composted manure, or a bagged soil into garden beds in fall gives it the entire winter to work into the soil structure, so it is ready to support planting in spring. Spread three inches over cleared beds and work it into the top six inches of native soil before winter sets in.”

Some plants are tough enough to make it through the winter without much support, but others can benefit from some TLC in the autumn. For those plants that aren’t hardy enough to withstand winter in your climate, you’ll need to break out some of the aforementioned materials: burlap, straw, frost cloth, and horticultural fleece. Amanda Webb, head gardener at Windy Hill Farm and Preserve, notes that “burlap is particularly useful for protecting exposed evergreens from winter wind, while straw can provide insulation around the crowns of marginally hardy perennials. I prefer breathable materials over plastic, which can trap moisture and create problems when temperatures fluctuate.”

Shrubs and other borderline hardy plants also need to take cover from the cold. “Some plants, particularly broadleaf evergreens like rhododendrons, hollies, and boxwoods, as well as any shrubs or perennials that are on the edge of their cold hardiness zone, benefit from physical protection over winter,” says Dr. Enfield. Burlap is the most commonly used material for this purpose; it can be wrapped loosely around individual plants or used to construct a simple windscreen that protects evergreens from the desiccating winter winds and sun, which can cause browning and leaf scorch even in plants that are otherwise cold-hardy. “Avoid wrapping plants in plastic, which traps moisture and heat and can do more harm than good. Burlap breathes, which is what makes it the material of choice.” For young or newly planted trees, wrapping the trunk with tree wrap or burlap protects against sun scald, “which is the cracking of bark caused by rapid temperature changes on the south and southwest sides of the trunk during winter thaws.”

Frost protection will help shield these potted plants from freezing temperatures.

Gardening doesn’t have to stop in winter; you just have to focus on plants that thrive in the cold. “I can’t stand seeing a vegetable bed sitting bare all winter,” says Mirela Bajic, senior garden designer at House Designer. “If you’re not growing anything in it, put something in for the soil’s sake. Winter rye is brilliant for that, [as] the roots hold everything together through the bad weather and then you just cut it back come spring.” She adds that many plants are more than rugged enough to make it through frosty, even snowy seasons. “Kale, cabbage, leeks, chard, [and more] all keep going through the cold if you let them.” Winter-ready crops like these help hold the soil together, provide some lovely greenery amid the gray backdrop of wintertime, and can even be used in hearty cold-weather meals.

It’s imperative that you tend to your property’s sprinkler systems and hoses, says Webb. “Drain irrigation systems and hoses if freezing temperatures are expected,” she says, or you risk catastrophic damage once water in the lines freezes and expands.

First, you’ll need to shut off the water supply to your sprinklers and hoses, removing the latter and putting them away for winter. Then, blow out sprinkler lines using compressed air to expel any residual water from the pipes. You should use a compressor set to 80 PSI or lower to avoid damaging sprinkler heads in the process. Once the lines are clear, leave the water shut off at the source inside your home (or garage or greenhouse or wherever the main water connection point is found) while leaving external valves partially open so last drips can fall and trapped water has a place to expand if it freezes.

Just because it’s too cold outside to grow colorful flowers or tasty tomatoes, peppers, or herbs doesn’t mean you can’t keep the growing going on indoors, and no dedicated greenhouse is needed, either. With a self-watering hydroponic growing system like a Gardyn Studio 2, you can grow full-sized plants indoors, including the same flowers and edible plants you’d place in a raised garden bed outdoors. If indoor space is at a premium, you can also use a smaller countertop system like a LetPot that can be used with a smaller number of flowers or food plants.

All those leaves you painstakingly rake or blow into a pile need not be bagged up and sent off to the dump; you can recycle it all to make your own mulch, which can preserve and nurture your garden throughout the winter. It’s best to use a leaf shredder to break down fallen leaves into smaller bits, rather than simply spreading collected leaves out atop your beds. You can also use a lawn mower to break down leaves, though this will dull the blade with extended use. If you have a wood chipper, you can use branches and brambles to make mulch as well. Even grass clippings can make great mulch, provided you dry it in the sun first and mix it with some thicker material, like those shredded leaves or chipped branches.

You don’t have to remove every dead flower, be it a truly finished annual or a dormant perennial, says Stembel, of Farmgirl Flowers. “One thing I’m trying to get better at is not cleaning everything up just because it looks messy. There are flowers that went from stunning to eyesores everywhere, seed heads, and so many perennials that are better off being left alone for a while so the foliage can die back naturally and the plants can store energy for next year. A lot of those seed heads and stems also feed birds and give beneficial insects places to overwinter.” Sometimes, the best thing you can do to winterize your garden is to leave it alone.

You should plant bulbs in the autumn when soil temperatures are consistently holding at around 55 to 60 degrees Fahrenheit, and about six weeks before you expect the first frost. “[It’s] my absolute favorite fall ‘chore,’ planting the bulbs,” says Stembel. “This fall we’re planting thousands of daffodils, tulips, and drumstick alliums around the farm. I know thousands sounds excessive [but] bulbs might be my favorite kind of delayed gratification. By spring, I’ll mostly have forgotten that we trudged outside on cold, rainy fall days and dug what felt like endless trenches to plant all of them until the little green sprouts start popping through the soil. Those first flowers bring us so much joy right when we need it most [after] months of cold, gray weather. In fall, I wonder why we planted so many, but by spring I’ll wonder why we didn’t plant more.”

The team carried that momentum through the 2025–’26 season, helping the United States capture the women’s Nations Cup for the first time ever. Awarded to the nation whose skiers accumulate the most World Cup points over the course of the season, the title reflected the depth of the U.S. roster, particularly in the technical events. Shiffrin and Moltzan combined for 16 World Cup podiums in slalom and GS, while O’Brien, AJ Hurt, Elisabeth Bocock, and Katie Hensien contributed valuable points.

Now comes the question: Can the U.S. women do it again at this year’s World Cup opener, Oct. 24-25 on Sölden’s Rettenbach Glacier?

The core of last year’s Sölden contingent is back, with the women who scored World Cup points at the opener returning to take another shot at the Rettenbach Glacier.

Leading the charge is Shiffrin, who is putting renewed emphasis on giant slalom this season. Long dominant in slalom, she’s turning her attention to the event that upended her career two years ago. A crash in the 2024 Killington GS left her with a serious abdominal puncture wound. Returning to the discipline meant working through post-traumatic stress, but Shiffrin steadily rebuilt her confidence and climbed from 30th in the GS standings to fourth last season.

Now, she’s ready to see how much further she can go.

“I really wonder where I can take GS,” Shiffrin said earlier this fall. “I have a ton of questions about it in my own mind, and I want to find out.”

Moltzan also finished last season on a high note, bookending her World Cup GS campaign with runner-up finishes in Sölden in October and Åre, Sweden, in March. Of her 10 career World Cup podiums to date, five came last season, four of them in GS.

At 32, and entering her eighth full season on the World Cup circuit, Moltzan is looking for a fresh source of motivation. She made a major equipment change, joining VAN DEER-Red Bull Sports, the ski brand founded by eight-time overall World Cup champion Marcel Hirscher. The move offers her a chance to help shape the next generation of women’s slalom and GS skis.

For Moltzan, the motivation goes beyond a new logo on her skis. “My route of inspiration is trying to develop this whole brand-new line of women’s equipment.”

She spent the off-season testing skis to find the right set-up. While her ambitions remain high, she’s approaching the challenge with a little more patience.“I was truthfully not intending to switch ski brands this year after such an incredible season,” Moltzan said. “But the opportunity to develop and create a line of skis that I have a lot of influence on is something I’m really excited about.”

O’Brien, 28, is chasing a milestone of her own: her first World Cup podium. She recorded a career-best fifth-place finish in GS before the 2026 Olympics, then delivered one of the Games’ standout GS second runs, posting the fastest time in the second run to climb from 29th to 20th overall.

AJ Hurt, 25, who made her second Olympic team last winter, also returned to the GS top 10 last season after rehabbing a hip injury suffered when she straddled a gate in training in the early season.

Katie Hensien, 26, will be looking to recapture the form that earned her a career-best fourth at Sölden in 2024. She too was injured early last season, fracturing her tibia after straddling a gate in training. Although Hensien qualified for the 2026 Olympics, she had to withdraw as she continued to recover.

Then there’s Elisabeth Bocock, 21, one of the team’s rising young talents. She made her World Cup debut in 2023 and announced herself a year later by finishing the first run of the Killington GS in eighth place. Last winter, she appeared on track to earn her first Olympic team selection, but a string of DNFs in her primary event, giant slalom, ultimately derailed that bid. “If you’re having a good day in training, if, say, you beat Paula or Nina, and you know that they win [World Cup] runs, you could be beating the entire world,” Bocock said. “But also, if you’re not skiing well, you can put it in perspective, like, okay, the people who are the best in the world are beating me. The result was a summer of adapting on the fly rather than stringing together long blocks of ideal training. “There wasn’t a massive block of good training days,” Bocock said, “but I’m still really excited. I think we got what we needed.”

Not every day had to be about gates and split times, though. Moltzan remembered the powder skiing in Chile as a welcome change of pace. “We free skied powder for a couple of hours, then went to a place called Tío Bob’s, which is the après-ski spot in Portillo. #2414. "The District Court—or Perhaps, Being Charitable, Its AI Agent—Ignored and Manipulated Governing Law, Apparently to Satisfy a Policy Agenda"

A short excerpt from the long opinion in Jackson Fed. of Teachers v. Fitch, decided yesterday by Fifth Circuit Judge Jerry Smith, joined by Judge Kurt Engelhardt:

In April 2025, Mississippi enacted House Bill 1193 (H.B. 1193), a law designed to keep Mississippi public education funds from being spent on diversity, equity, and inclusion ("DEI") initiatives and similar controversial programs. The law prevents government-run institutions from requiring activities such as diversity training or pushing DEI ideology, so defined, in the course of government-funded business.

H.B. 1193 does four main things. First, it prevents educational institutions from mandating DEI programs and diversity trainings in a variety of contexts. Second, it stops public educational institutions from punishing students for their views on DEI. Third, it prohibits regulated institutions from endorsing or promoting DEI through course materials. Finally, it prevents institutions and their educators from otherwise engaging in DEI methods or actions, preventing bad actors from circumventing prohibitions and frustrating its purposes. The statute also creates an administrative process to handle claims that a given educational institution is not in compliance. H.B. 1193 excepts several activities from its sweep, including scholarly work, the activities of students and their groups, and creative engagement.

Plaintiffs sued, claiming (among other things) that the law violated the First Amendment, but:

The proceedings in the district court were a botch job, to say the least. Judge Henry Wingate issued a temporary restraining order ("TRO") enjoining enforcement of H.B. 1193 that contained several factual assertions nowhere to be found in the record, or indeed, in any proceeding of the case. Those included mention of plaintiffs and defendants not in the complaint, supposed "declarations" from non-existent or never-present individuals, false quotations purporting to be taken from the text of H.B. 1193, and several erroneous claims that the law had already led to the demise of apparently fictitious programs.

After this came to light, Judge Wingate replaced the TRO without explaining.

Forging forward despite the bungled TRO, Judge Wingate issued a pre-enforcement preliminary injunction. The various state defendants appeal.

At oral argument in this court, it was discovered that even the "corrected" TRO—which was incorporated by reference into the preliminary injunction order—still contained hallucinated information (namely, a citation to a purported Fourth Circuit case, "Cousins v. School Bd. of City of Norfolk," that does not exist).

This district judge carelessly exercised his Article III power by issuing sloppy edicts based on falsehoods and fabrications. Upon discovering some of these falsehoods, Judge Wingate dug in his heels and claimed that the hallucinated claims had no bearing on its reasoning; he offered no explanation as to why he included such apparently irrelevant material in the original TRO or why the TRO nevertheless was justified in its absence.

Moreover, the district court got the law wrong. The TRO and preliminary injunction order rested on impoverished legal analysis that barely paid lip-service to the precedents of the Supreme Court or this court, seeming at times purposely to misconstrue the law by manipulating the words of statutes and cases, without regard to their texts' meaning as a whole. The district court—or perhaps, being charitable, its AI agent—ignored and manipulated governing law, apparently to satisfy a policy agenda.

Because of the extraordinarily grave errors by the district court, we vacate and remand for assignment to a different district judge. Per the Supreme Court's decision in Moody, we instruct the new judge to conduct proceedings consistent with federal law as interpreted by the Supreme Court and this circuit….

The original TRO—the one actually issued on July 20, 2025—named as plaintiffs eleven individuals and organizations who had never been party to the case; named as defendants two officials who were not actually sued; claimed, without evidence, that three post-secondary institutions had already cancelled DEI initiatives or programming because of the act; further declared, again without evidence, that staff at another school had been told not to discuss certain topics, including "historical interpretations involving systemic racism"; misquoted H.B. 1193 as applying to "race or sex stereotyping," "race or sex scapegoating," "critical race theory," and "institutional bias," despite none of these terms' appearing in the act's text; and referenced purported declarations of four individuals who do not appear in the record at all and may not even exist….

On July 22, 2025, defendants filed a motion to clarify, pointing out these errors. In response, the court, without explanation, replaced the original TRO with a seemingly corrected version. Though that document was docketed on July 22, it was backdated to July 20. Defendants requested that the original order be restored to the docket to keep the record whole and requested that the court explain the inaccuracies in the original order. The district court denied that motion, claiming the factual inaccuracies were merely "clerical."

In response, U.S. Senator Chuck Grassley accused the district court of using artificial Intelligence ("AI") to prepare the TRO, with the substantive errors' arising from AI-generated hallucinations. Fifteen days later, Judge Wingate admitted the errors, claiming that a law clerk had used AI to assist drafting an earlier version of the TRO that was docketed by mistake. His letter stated that he had removed the original TRO from the docket to avoid confusion and that the errors were clerical, in his view, because they did not relate to "core legal reasoning or the ultimate judgment on the merits" but instead to "failure to confirm the accuracy of certain information."

The seemingly corrected TRO did not explain how or why the factual inaccuracies were not necessary to the reasoning of the original TRO. The merits analysis failed to cite a binding authority from after the year 2000. It claims that H.B. 1193 constitutes viewpoint discrimination, without any analysis whatsoever of a state government's authority to regulate its own speech. And the document did not correct all of the errors: At Fifth Circuit oral argument, it was discovered that the doctored TRO cited to "Cousins v. School Bd. of City of Norfolk, 503 F.2d 422, 426–27 (4th Cir. 1974)." That case does not even exist; it is an apparent AI hallucination….

Mums fall into two categories: hardy garden mums (C. × morifolium) which can survive winter outdoors in zones 4-9, and florist mums which are grown as temporary decorative annuals. Regardless of which variety adorn your porch or backyard beds, October care determines whether your plants fade fast or stay vibrant into late autumn.

Fortunately, mums are tough, resilient plants. With a few simple steps this month, you can nearly double the time your mums light up the fall garden. Following these 5 essential October tasks ensures a spectacular seasonal finale, while safeguarding your plants for a triumphant return next spring. Here’s how to keep these happy flowers blooming for as long as possible both this year and the next…

It’s important to know what you can and can’t do for chrysanthemums this month. Depending on your USDA hardiness zone and local weather, different mum varieties sit at varying stages of growth mid-autumn. Early-blooming cushion mums may already be finishing their first flower flush, while late-blooming heirloom varieties like Hillside Sheffield Pink or Clara Curtis are just starting to open their daisy-like blooms.

Managing in-ground plantings and container mums requires a clear understanding of what not to do in October. While it is tempting to plant potted nursery mums into garden beds during autumn sales, October is too late in most northern regions for roots to establish before the ground freezes. Unless you live in a mild southern climate where the first hard freeze is at least 6 weeks away, keep any late-purchased potted mums in their containers rather than risking root death in frozen soil.

Similarly, October is not the time to divide mum clumps: doing this now would be the equivalent of a death sentence. Splitting root crowns in autumn would disrupt the plant's winter survival mechanisms. This month, put a note on the refrigerator to divide mums in early spring when green shoots appear. Dividing in spring every few years helps to keep clumps healthy and vigorous. As for what you can do to help your mums now, these tasks below are really easy to do and won’t take long at all.

Keep an eye on flower and stem quality, water management, container essentials, and crown insulation. If you need to check soil condition before carrying out these tasks, grab a soil meter. Deadheading is often viewed as a way to encourage fresh flower buds. But that’s not the only reason for deadheading mums. While snipping off spent blooms now may not force a new flush of blooms, it stops the plant from expending energy on seed production. By regularly snapping or snipping off brown, wilting flower heads just above the nearest leaf junction, you force the plant to redirect all its energy downward into root growth and crown development.

Building a robust root system now is key to helping garden mums survive winter freezes and break dormancy in spring. Make deadheading a quick weekly routine for the next few weeks. Pinch off faded blooms with your fingers. Alternatively, remove them with snips or spring-action blades like Fiskars Micro-Tip Pruning Snips, available from Amazon which are great for clean cuts that won’t tug or damage stems. Keeping spent flowers removed also improves airflow around remaining buds and prevents gray mold (botrytis) from taking hold in damp fall rain.

Consistent moisture is essential for keeping mums hydrated and blooming through October. While fall weather brings cooler temperatures and frequent rain, seasonal rainfall may not be sufficient to meet the needs of dense, heavily budded mums, especially container-grown specimens. Container mums have tightly packed roots that dry out rapidly in fall. Check potted soil daily by inserting a finger an inch (2.5cm) into the pot. If it feels dry, water thoroughly until fluid runs from the drainage holes. For ground-planted mums, check soil moisture every couple of days.

Your job as a mum mom is to check frequently. When watering, direct it to the base of the plant rather than overhead. Keeping foliage and open petals dry prevents fungal pathogens like powdery mildew, rust, and leaf spot from ruining your fall display. Every couple of weeks, add a diluted seaweed or kelp drench. GS Plant Foods Organic Liquid Kelp, available from Walmart, adds a light mineral boost to help stressed mums maintain vibrant flower color for longer. Once the soil freezes solid, stop watering in-ground plants.

October is not the time to do a hard pruning of your mums. You should snip off any diseased or rotting foliage. Cut off yellow foliage and leaves close to the base of the plant that are likely to harbor fungal diseases. If you need a comfortable set, Corona Dual Link ComfortGel Bypass Pruners from Lowe’s are excellent value and highly efficient. However, leave the main stems intact. Performing a hard prune in fall is one of the most common reasons garden mums fail to return in spring.

Allow the woody stems to stand uncut through the fall and even the winter. These stems trap snow that helps to insulate the roots and protect the crown. Furthermore, standing stems provide valuable winter habitat for beneficial garden insects. Wait until early spring, when green shoots peek through the soil, to cut the old, woody stems back to ground level.

In-ground garden mums planted in the ground can survive much colder weather than container mums. Potted mums, however, face a bit more of a challenge. Soil in above-ground pots can freeze solid quickly, subjecting pots to freeze-thaw cycles. If you want your potted mums to survive winter, protect them before hard freezes arrive. In mild regions (zones 7-9), move your containers to a sheltered spot against a warm, south-facing house wall and wrap the pots in burlap or bubble wrap.

In colder northern regions (zones 3-6), transition dormant potted mums into an unheated garage or shed once night temperatures drop consistently below freezing. Use the excellent Bloem 12-Inch Heavy Duty Terracotta Plant Caddies, available from Walmart, to move heavier mum containers for overwintering. Their caster wheels make light work of moving heavy pots. The goal is to keep the soil cold and dormant (around 32-40°F / 0-4°C) without exposing roots to sub-zero winds. Water your stored pots lightly once a month to prevent roots from drying out completely.

Applying a protective blanket of mulch over garden mums is an essential fall task, but timing is everything. Applying mulch too early in October, while soil is still warm, can trap heat and moisture against the crown of the plant, encouraging fungal rot and encouraging pests to nest. Wait until after the first hard freeze has killed off top growth and the ground begins to cool significantly before adding your mulch.

Spread a 2-4-inch (5-10cm) layer of loose, airy organic material (such as clean straw, evergreen boughs, or pine needles) around the base of each plant. You can also use an organic wood mulch. Our team loves Back to the Roots Organic Premium Wood Mulch, available from Amazon for a dye-free bark mulch that insulates crowns against freeze-thaw cycles and helps prevent frost heave. Loose materials insulate the crown without packing down into a heavy, waterlogged mat. Just don’t use heavy packed leaves which can suffocate the plant.

Taking a little time to care for your mums this month pays massive dividends now and next year. By maintaining consistent moisture, deadheading spent blooms, leaving main stems intact, and applying a protective layer of mulch after the first hard frost, you maintain strong, healthy plants for seasons to come. Give your mums the attention they deserve this month, and enjoy their lush blooms year after year.
