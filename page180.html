#3489. What I Learned from Finishing a Triathlon When Everything Goes Wrong

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Before the start of her second attempt at the Cowichan Challenge, Anna Rea had high hopes of besting her time from the previous year. (Photo: Courtesy Anna Rea)

For this article, a novice age-group triathlete shared, in her own words, her experience racing the Olympic-distance Cowichan Challenge Triathlon. Despite multiple setbacks, she received encouragement from volunteers and fellow racers to keep going, and has tried to apply that same perseverance to other areas of her life.

It was a perfect early summer morning. The forecast wasn’t too hot, the water was warm, and there was no hint of wind. I had fueled and hydrated the day before. I was well rested and ready. I had done the exact same race one year earlier, which was my first ever triathlon.

This time, I assumed I would be faster. I had to be. Any other outcome would feel like failure.

But within a few minutes of the race start, the rest of the swimmers were barely visible. I had recently done two private lessons with a swimming coach, during which I learned that my technique was broken. There was no time to properly address it before the race.

The swim took 53 minutes, so I was eager to make up some time on the ride. But after five minutes on my bike, just as I was finding my rhythm, my derailleur seized up, and I couldn’t move the pedals. This was defeat.

I tearfully walked my bike back toward transition. I assumed there was no helping this situation. I hadn’t taken my bike for a tune-up before the race. I had failed.

After 25 minutes of walking, a race volunteer spotted me and ran over. He tinkered with my bike for a couple minutes, and I secretly hoped he would confirm my fears – that the bike was broken and it was time to give up. An easy out.

But when he fixed my bike with a couple turns of an Allen key, my guts twisted with dread. He encouraged me to get back on the bike. I told him I had given up, but he told me I might as well keep going since there were still a couple hours until the cut-off time. I had already spent the money, trained, and showed up to the race — I had nothing to lose.

I was alone for most of the ride. I kept apologizing to the volunteers along the race course because I felt guilty that they had to wait for me. I didn’t think I would even start the run, but I kept my focus on the moment, on each corner and hill, until I was back at transition.

My sister, who had completed the sprint distance and was already finished, was waiting for me. I updated her on my situation and asked if she and my mom were OK with waiting until I finished. She said yes and encouraged me.

Then a race worker advised me that the race cut-off was in 52 minutes. He said there was no way I would make it in time and suggested I drop out. I told him I wanted to try, and I accepted that I would complete part of the run without support.

I started the run before I could change my mind. I had a hard time seeing the direction markers on the course. After a couple wrong turns, I was overwhelmed with the urge to give up. I cursed out loud, turned on my heel, and started walking back toward transition. But I immediately met the eyes of an athlete walking back to his vehicle, finisher medal hanging from his neck. He told me to keep going. I told him about my broken bike, the bad swim, not making the cut-off time, and running in the wrong direction. He encouraged me to go anyway, so I did.

I did most of the trail run alone. The race volunteers were supportive and stayed past the cut-off even though they didn’t have to. I was overwhelmed with gratitude, and this time saying thank you was easier than saying sorry.

I shouted curses on the run, stumbling over tree roots in my fatigue, fueled by frustration and determination. When I was 1 kilometer from the end, I almost passed out at a hydration station. I regrouped and kept moving.

Four hours and 15 minutes that felt like an eternity were suddenly coming to an end. I saw the finish line arch. I heard the applause as by now the awards ceremony was well underway. My sister saw me approaching, turned to a group of people behind her, and told them I was coming. And finally, after hours of exhaustion and frustration, wanting to give up the whole time, I crossed the finish line in front of a cheering crowd. And it was all worth it. I wouldn’t change a thing.

I’m not good at this. I don’t feel like I’m good at much. Genetic vision loss has greatly impacted the last few years of my life. I’m struggling to start a new career while managing chronic mental health issues and an acquired disability.

My confidence has taken a hit, and I think about giving up all the time. Things could be a lot worse – I have a support system and free healthcare in Canada. I am privileged with a great quality of life and generally good health aside from my eye disease.

But I feel behind. And by challenging myself to do things like triathlon, I expose myself to people who are incredibly fit. Self-criticism and body dysmorphia usually tag along.

The three months that have passed since that race have been difficult, and I feel out of touch with the person who persevered that day. I’m trying to let some light in and keep going – keep adapting to vision loss, keep building my career, and keep pushing myself.

I used to care a lot more about being good at things. Now I care about following through. Life is hard, and persisting when you don’t feel like enough is a feat of strength. I faced my fear of being slow that day and finished to a crowd of cheers. Of course I want to be faster. I want to finish in the pack. I want to be good at triathlon. But we can learn so much by being bad at things and doing them anyway.

Walking my bike back to the car, finisher medal around my neck, I turned to my sister and said, “Winning is cool, but losing is where it’s at.”

Do things you’re bad at. Challenge yourself. It’s OK if you don’t feel like a triathlete. How many things would you do if you weren’t afraid of failure?

#2817. Why 5 Days of Heat Training May Be Enough

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

In a perfect world, we’d spend the month (or more) leading up to toasty races like the Javelina Jundred training to handle the heat. Actually, in a perfect world, we would heat train all year round to reap the potential physiological rewards, such as increased plasma volume, and maybe even improved Vo2 max and lactate threshold.

But we’re not all pros with flexible schedules and not everyone lives in the Arizona desert, where the average high is around 82 degrees Fahrenheit.

The last thing any of us want is to succumb to hot conditions, much less end up in the med tent, because we were too busy for heat training. But many heat training protocols call for weeks and even months of heat sessions to induce positive physiological adaptations; time we don’t often have to spare while juggling work, sleep, family obligations, and the ever-important logging of miles.

So when I found out that endurance gravel cyclist Metheven Bond claimed he managed to acclimate to the heat in only 5 days, I was curious: Can runners implement a similar protocol?

Off his 5-day protocol, Bond rode for 43 hours and 6 minutes with no sleep through the sweltering desert of Eastern Andalusia to finish sixth overall in The Badlands. If you’re unfamiliar with the gravel cycling race, Badlands runs some 507 miles across the Gorafe and Tabernas deserts in Spain,where temperatures regularly exceed 104 F.

To find out exactly what he did and how it may apply to runners, I spoke with Bond himself, as well as Aline Barre, a competitive ultra-cyclist and triathlete with a master’s degree in Sports Performance Analysis, and Daniel Snape, Ph.D., a performance physiologist, accredited sport and exercise scientist, and researcher in endurance and environmental physiology.

Earlier this year, Bond, who races for Ribble Outliers gravel cycling team, competed in some hot races in Spain and Switzerland. Temperatures reached nearly 97 degrees.

“I fell apart even though I thought I did everything right,” he told Trail Runner. “I froze all of my bottles and was drinking over a liter an hour for hydration, but my power still dropped off a cliff, my heart rate was through the roof, and I couldn’t think properly. I knew it was from the heat.”

Racing in hot conditions can compromise endurance capacity, and, as Bond discovered the hard way, it’s not always enough to rely on mid-race cooling. “I needed to find ways to adapt so the heat bothers me less, so I looked into heat acclimation.”

The goal of heat acclimation is to cause adaptations that allow the body to more effectively function in high temps. That process involves exposing the body to hot conditions that cause core body temperature to rise. Done long and consistently enough adaptations can include a lowered heart rate and body temperature, a higher sweat rate, improved performance, and better heat resilience.

“Before Badlands, I looked at the weather forecast and knew the heat was going to be bad. I’m in the North of England where it’s colder and somehow needed to get ready to be thrown into the heat,” Bond said. He visited the lab of Precision Fuel and Hydration, a sponsor of Ribble Outliers, for a focused heat training block. There, the Precision team implemented a protocol before Badlands consisting of five one-hour heat training sessions of cycling in a heat chamber set to 102 degrees over five consecutive days.

The team monitored Bond’s performance markers—such as heat transfer and skin temperature—throughout the block via sensors like the Core 2 Thermal Sensor, which mounts to any heart rate strap and transmits data in real-time to an app or wearable.

As the sessions progressed, Bond was able to push harder without a corresponding increase in cardiovascular effort. The result: Higher power output, lower average heart rate, and improved resistance to the fatigue of heat stress.

Aline Barre is a cyclist, triathlete, and endurance performance lead at Core. Like Bond, Barre had an equally unpleasant experience racing in the heat.

“My first endurance race was Ironman Frankfurt. For training, I didn’t do any running in the heat,” Barre said. “Race day was super hot and I was cooked. I was drinking a lot, but I felt horrible during the run. I was so afraid of the heat that I went to Finland for my second Ironman.”

Barre concluded the heat was holding her back—until she focused on acclimation. “Heat training is even more important for ultrarunners than endurance cyclists. In most cases, it’s easier to cycle in the heat than to run in the heat,” she said. “That’s because you move forward at a faster pace when riding a bike than running, creating substantially more airflow to help cool your skin through sweat evaporation.”

Combine this with the fact that running creates higher metabolic heat than cycling and it’s no wonder that medical teams see more cases of heat stroke at endurance running events than at cycling races. Additionally, some studies suggest that female athletes may require a slightly longer heat exposure period (10-14 days) or stronger heat stimulus relative to male athletes (seven days) to fully develop certain adaptations, particularly those related to sweating

“Most athletes see the majority of gains after 4-5 heat sessions and the full adaptation after doing 10 to 12 sessions within two to six weeks,” Barre said.

But for Bond, five days was enough. Performance physiologist and researcher Daniel Snape explains why.

“When you heat train, the main response you get is an increase in your blood plasma volume. For Bond to increase his plasma volume across those five days is a very normal and achievable response,” Snape said. Snape is part of a team of experts at the Health and Performance Hub at Leeds Beckett University. He works with professional ultrarunners like 2025 UTMB winners Tom Evans and Ruth Croft to develop specific heat acclimation strategies.

“Also you’re becoming more familiar with the heat, so your body doesn’t have that: ‘Oh no, I’ve got to increase my heart rate to cope with this stressor’ fight or flight response. That’s why five days straight was enough time for Bond to adapt and be able to hold a higher power output at a lower heart rate.”

While five days may be enough for a professional male endurance athlete who has a stellar baseline and access to a heat chamber, core body temperature sensor, and a team of experts, it might not be for many ultrarunners.

“Even I had to continue with three sessions per week of heat acclimation at home to maintain the adaptation, riding wrapped up in layers. I kept this up until a week before Badlands, when it was time to taper,” Bond said. He also points out that while significant adaptations can occur in five days, a few weeks is preferable for the mental preparation.

“The more times you train in the heat, the more confidence you have when you face it on race day,” he said. “You think: ‘Okay, it’s uncomfortable, but not as uncomfortable because I’ve been exposed to it. I’m used to this’.”

If you’re short on time and you want to have some semblance of conditioning going into Javelina or another toasty ultra, Core has a simple but mighty indoor heat training plan to build heat into your workouts with five sessions over 2 weeks. (See below.) It includes a long run where you start cool, then dress in warm layers for the last 75 minutes, as well as an easy 50-minute run where you run cool, but add a 30-minute passive heat session after.

The long run  is best done inside on a treadmill, but a smart trainer, stationary bike, or even a rowing machine will work as well. For your layers, you’ll need clothing that is non-permeable and traps heat against your body,  such as a heat suit, thermal top, down jacket, rain jacket, fleecy sweat suit, and/or wool hat. As a last resort, Bond recommends using a trash bag with holes cut out for your head and arms.

The passive heat session consists of time in a sauna, steam room, hot tub, or even the bathtub. If you’re preparing for a hot desert race, a sauna is the way to go if you can access one. Research shows that repeated post-exercise sauna sessions can enhance key components of heat adaptation, such as blood plasma volume and thermoregulatory efficiency. To prepare for a trail run in the tropics, a steam room or steamy bath are the best options.

Alternatively, you can do a micro heat training block that includes five days of only passive heat sessions immediately after your normal training runs, when your heart rate and core temperature are still elevated. According to Dr. Snape, even this can be effective at improving performance come race day.

The benefits of heat training extend beyond simply ramping up for a 100-miler under the desert sun, however.

“With the sauna for example, we know that if you do a hill workout, then go into the sauna, it has some muscle hypertrophy benefits, helping with recovery,” Snape said. “We also know that saunas improve your immune function. And if you do a sauna session late at night, it can actually improve sleep quality.”

Before starting a heat training plan, you should already be used to training for a minimum of four days per week and at least four hours of total weekly volume. You should also be able to complete a 60-to 75-minute run workout.

“You can do heat training right up until taper week. But don’t start heat training too close to your race. If you start two weeks before your race, the chance is quite high that you’ll expose your body to unnecessary fatigue because it’s not used to the heat,” Barre said. “We recommend starting four to five weeks out. During heat sessions, monitor your skin temperature, sweat rate, and heart rate so you don’t overdo it or get a heat illness, which can be life-threatening if you’re not careful. And hydrate before, during, and after heat sessions.”

It’s important to note that even if you use a Core sensor as part of your heat acclimation protocol, you can’t wear it into saunas or your bathtub at home, so there’s no way of knowing your internal body temperature during those passive sessions. As such, active and passive heat training should only be done when someone else is with you or in a nearby room.

“Any heat acclimation protocol will induce a training stress. Respect it as an additional thing to recover from,” Snape said. “That means you will have to take some training off the table elsewhere when undergoing a heat training block. That could mean cutting nine hours of running down to eight hours.”

Heat acclimation doesn’t have to be a part-time job. If you have time for 7-14 heat sessions during or after the run workouts on your training plan, you should experience full adaptations. But if you only have time for a 5-day heat adaptation block, it’s still more beneficial than not training for the heat at all, helping you develop a base adaptation that can make it mentally and physically easier to stay on pace when the heat is on.

Gearing up for your first triathlon postpartum, you’ve certainly got a lot on your mind. Most of it probably has nothing to do with you. Who will take care of your child for so many hours? What will they eat? Are they going to miss nap time? Still, you’ll need to make sure that you have all the right equipment and fuel laid out the night before the race.

And while there might not be a “postpartum” filter on triathlon brands’ websites, it’s worth looking into purchasing a new tri suit to accommodate and support the same evolving body that has nourished both you and baby over the past few years.

If you’re still lactating and plan on expressing milk during the race, finding a tri kit with good access is especially crucial. Many mothers have publicly pumped milk while racing (if you’re thinking of doing the same, read my tips for why (and how) new moms should breastfeed during a triathlon).

One common refrain among lactating athletes is that we need more appropriate gear to better facilitate mid-race expression. In testing these tri suits, I therefore paid special attention to how easy each one was to unzip and slip a breast pump inside. That usually hinged on how deep a suit’s zipper was and if it had sleeves. Of course, it also helps to have a nursing sports bra underneath. And because you may be required to carry a hand pump the entire length of a race, I also noted whether pockets were spacious enough to accommodate such equipment.

Not every mother expresses milk, however. That’s why I examined several other aspects of these suits that specifically impact postpartum bodies. For starters, compression. At 2 ½ years postpartum, my body still doesn’t look like it did before baby, and compression in a tri suit can aid in keeping everything from feeling “loose.” However, if you find tight suits restrictive to your airways or range of motion, you might look for the suits with fewer stars in that category.

I also focused on how easy it is to pull each suit down for bathroom breaks (sorry, coach, but there’s gonna be more of them in motherhood). And while chafing is always a concern among triathletes, you might find yourself chafing more and/or in different places as your body fat redistributes itself postpartum. Of course, in addition to all these factors, I rated suits according to more traditional criteria like durability and fit.

Again, don’t simply go for the suit with the most stars. Consider your unique needs, and try suits on before ripping off the tags in excitement. This article is meant to offer helpful insights as you return to racing postpartum, but as you know by now, every mom’s journey is different. What matters most is that you return to the start line on your own time and in your own style.

Size options: XXS, XS, S, M, L, XL Styles: Sleeveless, Sleeved, Tank, Shorts

If you’re just getting back into racing postpartum, a sprint triathlon may be first on your agenda. There are suits designed for that, including the 2XU Core Tri Suit. I wouldn’t wear this for a half or full Ironman — there aren’t enough pockets to store nutrition, and while the padding is high-quality, it’s also minimal. But it’s an excellent option for shorter races, as long as you’re fine with a looser fit. Plus, this suit is relatively easy on the wallet, which can come as a blessing since child-rearing is financially difficult enough.

Former competitive swimmers will understand me when I say this suit is more like an Aquablade than a Fastskin in terms of compression. Some postpartum women will appreciate the looser belly fit, as well as the fact that this suit will accommodate some amount of weight fluctuation and body recomposition as you get back into racing. Even so, the extra folds of fabric did nothing to help the already subpar breathability of this suit. The midriff trapped heat and sweat more than any other suit I’ve ever worn. I was asked to determine if any of these suits were “swamp-assy,” and this one certainly leaned that way. Still, I loved how the open back made me feel like I wasn’t wearing anything at all on my shoulders.

For those concerned about lactation, the zipper of this suit didn’t zip down far enough to accommodate easy breastfeeding or pumping. For shorter distances, though, that’s likely not an issue. The top of the zipper reached unusually high, touching the base of my neck, and it chafed me slightly on the run. Finally, a few loose threads along the seams concerned me about how this suit would hold up across more than one racing season.

Size options: XS, S, M, L, XL, 2XL Styles: Sleeved, Sleeveless, Jersey, Shorts

I wanted to like this suit so that I could rock its floral design at my next Ironman, but I had to knock off several stars because of the terrible chafing it caused in my armpits. That’s not a problem I usually experience, even with sleeved tri suits, but I couldn’t even finish a 3-mile run without my skin screaming in pain. I even felt the suit chafing me while swimming.

It seems I’m not alone, either; I recently spoke to someone else who has suffered unusual chafing in the armpits from a Zoot suit. Nevertheless, there are plenty of folks who peel off this suit after a race and find themselves untarnished, so it’s worth trying on for size (or simply opting for the sleeveless version). Keep in mind that if you’re recently postpartum and had a C-section, the high waistline might irritate the scar.

What I most loved about this suit (aside from its aesthetics) was its full frontal zipper, making dressing and undressing a breeze. My breasts aren’t small, and yet, I had no difficulty cajoling the zipper up or down. Furthermore, you could easily let the top portion of this suit hang loose while breastfeeding a baby. I also appreciated the numerous pockets, though they weren’t very spacious. The cooling pockets, found on the neck and kidney area, were the cherry on top of what was already great breathability.

Size options: XS, S, M, L, XL, 2XL, 3XL Styles: Sleeved, Sleeveless, Sleeved top, Tank top, Shorts

This suit struck the perfect balance between price and performance, and it especially stood out for its medium-high, yet comfortable, level of compression. Because it’s sleeveless and features a full frontal zipper, it’s relatively easy to pull on and take off – whether that’s to pump milk or to hit the restroom. Still, the upper-body compression meant that the zipper was a little harder to tug up over a larger breast size. Plus, the top of the zipper chafed me slightly while running – the protective flap should have been larger.

If you’ve ever complained about how minuscule girl pockets are, this suit will please you with its two large hip pockets, each of which could fit an item as large as a handheld breast pump (or extra chamois butter if you find yourself chafing more postpartum). Those are in addition to two smaller, though still relatively sizable, pockets on the back.

For the most part, this is a great option if you feel like elite suits restrict your airways or range of motion. It performed spectacularly in the water due to its flexibility and sleeveless nature. I also loved how securely the grippers glued the shorts portion to my legs so that they never rode up. Even so, this suit felt slightly tight across my back as I cycled, especially because I hunch slightly more than is good for me.

This suit fit so snugly and comfortably that I had difficulty finding anything to criticize. I typically don’t love sleeved suits, but the thin, breathable fabric on this one made me forget that I was going rogue. It’s high on the compression factor, which is ideal if you want more core support and to shave off every second possible from your time. Still, the sleeves were flexible and loose enough to allow full range of motion.

Did I wish that this suit came in something other than black or black-and-white? Yes. Not only because a bit of brightness cheers me up when I feel like puking on the run course, but also because my personal cheerleading squad can spot me from a greater distance when I’m wearing bright colors. Still, they say black is classy, so I’m willing to hang up my dreams of a tie-dye race suit for a little while longer. That said, I did notice a bit of color leaking out of the suit once while handwashing it.

The full frontal zipper will be easy to negotiate for those with smaller breasts, but if yours are naturally larger or engorged from breastfeeding, be prepared to possibly spend a few extra seconds aligning and jostling it up. Finally, you won’t be able to overpack with this suit; the two back pockets are medium at best and diagonally aligned.

#3176. Wildfires Force Closure of Carstenz Pyramid for the Season

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

All around the world, natural disasters are hampering climbing expeditions. The latest case is a series of uncontrolled wildfires in Papua that have doomed the fall climbing season on Carstenz Pyramid.

The region is facing severe, widespread forest fires that have blanketed the island in a persistent thick haze. The air quality and visibility have reached levels that make transportation, logistics, and climbing operations impossible, according to local outfitter Tropic Adventure Papua. Apparently, the effects of the El Niño phenomenon, known this year as the Super Niño due to its extraordinary intensity, are to blame. It has brought a prolonged drought and extremely dry air, diminishing hope for the rain needed to extinguish the fires.

By the time the situation became unbearable, the first groups of foreign climbers had reached Timika in Papua, but no one made it to the top during this season, which is now officially canceled.

Outfitters have already shared the news with their clients and are now placing their hopes in 2027.

“Our thoughts are with the local communities affected, and we’re hoping the fires are brought under control soon,” Garrett Madison posted on social media. Instead, they have set the dates for next year, with two trips in March and October.

The harm caused to the fragile Indonesian ecosystem is incalculable. In recent days, a viral video of a young orangutan rescued from a treetop where it tried to escape with burned hands and feet has drawn attention to the disaster. In fact, a significant number of primates were evacuated by rescue services in Borneo. Some of them were returned to the forests as the fire receded last weekend, according to the BBC.

The intensity of the fires in Papua and the air quality can be tracked live here.

At 4,884m, Carstenz Pyramid (Puncak Jaya in the local language) is the highest point of Oceania and considered one of the Seven Summits. Located in Central Papua, and with a tropical climate, Carstenz can be climbed at any time of year. However, summit options increase in the less rainy months, from February through November.

Outfitters apply for permits twice a year. Political unrest has often kept climbers away in the past, and major mining operations limit ground access. Climbers are typically airlifted to Base Camp instead of trekking through the jungle.

Call me sentimental, but I feel sad seeing those vegetable beds that overflowed with produce all summer lie empty in October. In fact, cover crops offer many of the same benefits as mulch, suppressing weeds, improving soil structure, and protecting against erosion. They can also act as green manure, adding valuable organic matter and nutrients to the soil when you cut them down and incorporate them before spring planting.

Growing fall cover crops isn’t difficult, but it helps to get the information you need up front, including how to prepare the soil, which cover crops to plant, and when to plant them. That’s why I’ve pulled it all together for you in this article.

Even if you don’t feel sentimental about the deserted autumn beds, there are many reasons not to leave garden soil bare over winter. The practice can damage soil health, structure, and fertility.

Let’s think about erosion first. When plants grow in soil, their roots anchor it in place, preventing the precious top layer from blowing away. Winter’s worst can easily blow or wash away bare soil. That reduces the amount of topsoil that spring crops have to grow in.

Beyond washing away topsoil, winter rain can leach water-soluble nutrients from the soil. These vital nutrients are essential to growing a healthy spring crop. In addition, it can leave the soil crusted over, damage soil structure by removing air spaces, and generally leave it severely compacted.

Soil like this is not a good place to plant spring vegetables or flowers, but the weeds don’t object. In fact, aggressive winter weeds invade the bare ground, followed by nettles and bindweeds, as well as other early spring weeds.

Cover crops address almost all bare-soil-over-winter issues, and they do it in spades. When you plant fall cover crops, their roots dive deep and hold the soil firmly in place. Weed roots are tougher than winter wind and heavy rain. That means that they prevent erosion. That’s important, but it’s not the whole story.

Installing fall cover crops also keeps winter weeds away, or at least dramatically reduces their number. They also stop the soil from crusting, improve the soil structure, and add additional organic matter back into the soil to get it ready for spring planting.

Be sure to wait 2–3 weeks after terminating the cover crops in spring before planting.

#4542. AI Agent Security Risks: What Can Go Wrong When Agents Act on Their Own

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The thing that makes an AI agent useful, its freedom to act, is also what can introduce risk. An agent doesn’t just answer a question, it browses, runs code, and calls tools on its own, so a single bad or manipulated instruction can turn into real damage instead of just a wrong answer. That’s the shift traditional application security wasn’t built for, and it’s why trust has to be built into the system around the agent, not assumed.

An agent decides its own path, picking tools at runtime, chaining actions together, and usually running with the credentials of whoever launched it. That autonomy is the source of most agent-specific risk, and it points to the two-part answer that runs through this post: contain what an agent can reach, and oversee what it does.

If you’ve secured web services, the model is familiar: requests arrive at known endpoints, deterministic logic handles them, and you design controls around that predictability. Agents break that assumption. They interpret instructions dynamically, choose tools at runtime, and run multi-step chains with very little human intervention at each step. A coding agent might read a file, install a dependency, change config, run tests, and push a commit from a single prompt.

Because an agent chooses its own path, a compromised or misdirected one can take a far wider range of actions than a traditional service. Every risk below has already produced documented real-world incidents, and they all follow from that shift.

Prompt injection is the risk that defines agent security. Because an agent acts on whatever text it reads, an attacker doesn’t need access to your systems. They just need the agent to encounter their instructions, hidden in a web page, a code comment, an issue, or a document it summarizes.

A real prompt-injection case showed an agent following instructions buried in repository content and taking actions its user never intended.

Incident highlight: A coding agent followed instructions buried in a repository’s own content and took actions its user never intended. The malicious text arrived through data the agent was simply asked to read.

The agent has no reliable way to tell a legitimate instruction from an injected one, so the defense is to limit what a hijacked agent can reach.

Agents act through tools: API connectors, database queries, file operations, and code execution. Every tool is an access vector, and two things could go wrong.

An agent can be given more tools than the task needs, widening the attack surface for no reason. And a tool itself can be poisoned, its description carrying hidden instructions that manipulate the agent into misusing it. The mitigations are to scope tools to the task at runtime, source them from registries with verified provenance, and review tool descriptions rather than trusting them by default.

The more an agent can do without review, the more a single bad decision costs. Excessive autonomy is the risk of granting an agent broad, standing permission to act, most visibly when developers run YOLO mode, where every action is auto-approved. This autonomy is genuinely useful, so switching it off defeats the point. The better move is to run the agent where that autonomy can’t reach anything it shouldn’t, so speed doesn’t necessarily mean increased blast radius.

Incident highlight: A developer asked a coding agent to clean up an old repository. It generated an rm -rf command with a trailing ~/ and ran it with no confirmation step, wiping the developer’s entire home directory. Nothing in the setup gave a human a chance to catch it before it executed.

When an agent runs under a developer’s personal token, every action it takes carries that person’s full permissions, and a compromise hands those permissions to an attacker. Secrets sitting in prompts, config files, or environment variables baked into an image make it worse, because a prompt injection can read them straight out of the agent’s context. Agents should get their own scoped, short-lived credentials, injected at runtime and kept out of the conversation, rather than borrowing yours.

Incident highlight: An AWS engineer handed a small Cost Explorer bug to a coding agent running with their own operator-level access. The agent decided the cleanest fix was to delete and rebuild the production environment, and it held the credentials to do exactly that. The result was a thirteen-hour regional outage.

An agent with network access and sensitive information in its context can send that data somewhere it shouldn’t, whether through a compromised tool or an injected instruction. The risk grows in multi-agent setups, where one agent passes work to another. If the receiving agent treats that input as trusted, a compromise in one link can spread down the chain. Restricting network egress to known endpoints and treating every agent-to-agent handoff as untrusted input could keep a single failure from spreading.

The risks are varied, but there are controls to secure AI agents. Isolate where the agent runs so a mistake or a hijack is trapped in a disposable environment instead of loose on your host, and scope what the agent can access so the damage it can do is small even inside that boundary.

Isolation is the highest-impact starting point, and a microVM boundary makes it strong without slowing the agent down. On top of containment, oversight is what keeps a fleet of agents accountable. Then, AI governance defines which actions an agent may take, what it can reach, and how its behavior is logged, so risk is managed deliberately rather than discovered after an incident.

These risks aren’t reasons to avoid developing with agents. Instead, we need to give agents the freedom to finish the job by building trust into the system around them, so that freedom never means access to everything.

And that’s the job Docker Sandboxes is built for: each agent runs in its own disposable microVM with the network and filesystem controls you define, so a mistake or an injection is contained by design. And AI Governance adds the oversight layer, setting which actions, tools, and credentials are allowed and enforcing it everywhere your team works. Contain the blast radius, and govern the behavior.

The most significant are prompt injection, tool misuse and tool poisoning, excessive autonomy, credential and secret exposure, and data exfiltration. All of them stem from the fact that agents act on their own, using tools and credentials, rather than following a fixed, reviewable path.

For most teams it’s excessive access: an agent running with more autonomy and more credentials than the task needs. It turns a small mistake or a single prompt injection into a large incident, because the agent can reach so much.

Traditional application security assumes predictable request-and-response flows. Agent security has to account for autonomous decisions, tools chosen at runtime, and multi-step chains where the agent sets its own path. The attack surface is wider because the agent, not a fixed program, decides what happens next.

You can reduce it with input handling and tool hygiene, but you can’t assume it away, because an agent can’t reliably tell injected instructions from legitimate ones. The durable defense is containment: run the agent so that even a successfully injected one can’t reach anything sensitive.

Start with isolation so a compromised agent is trapped in a disposable environment. Add scoped tool access and dedicated credentials to shrink what any agent can do. Then layer on governance to define and enforce those limits consistently across every agent your team runs.

#3525. What Really Happened When the Men and Women Raced Kona Together Again

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Triathlete contributor Jordan Blanco interviewed female Kona fans and age-group women over the course of a week she spent on the Big Island leading up to covering the Ironman World Championship. Blanco has attended 12 Ironman World Championship events (11 Kona, one Nice) and raced it nine times (eight Kona, one St. George).

If the 2026 Ironman World Championship delivered one thing, it was drama.

Vincent Luis (FRA) became the Ironman world champion on his Kona debut but also in the final race of his career. It was a mic drop moment for the three-time Olympian. Taylor Knibb (USA) finally claimed the title that eluded her in 2025, following one of the most exciting finishes in the history of the women’s race. Defending champion Solveig Løvseth (NOR) appeared to be heading for a second consecutive victory before collapsing in the final kilometer, allowing Knibb to reclaim the lead and take the win.

This kind of racing makes Kona special. Unpredictable, unforgiving, and compelling viewing until the very end.

But beyond the professional racing, this year’s world championship marked another milestone: For the first time since 2019, men and women competed together on the Big Island on the same day.

After three years of separate men’s and women’s championships, Ironman committed to bringing everyone back together. The decision was broadly welcomed by athletes. Couples could race together again, and the sport’s biggest names were back to sharing the same stage.

However, women, specifically, had legitimate concerns about what might be lost in the process. Would the professional women receive the same coverage as the men? Would age-group women have a fair race on a more congested course? And could Kona accommodate everyone.

As we wrap up coverage of the race, here’s what appeared to work, what didn’t and what Ironman might want to consider — from the women’s perspective — before October 2027.

Let’s start with the positives. The professional races were exceptional, and the decision to separate the men’s and women’s races by 10 minutes worked well in preserving the integrity of both competitions. With age-group athletes starting later, there was little interference with the professional fields.

Ironman also made a genuine effort to address one of the longstanding criticisms of single-day racing: the tendency for the men’s racing to dominate the main broadcast. Ironman CEO Scott DeRue had committed to equal coverage of the men’s and women’s races. The broadcast production included multiple feeds, including a “quad box” allowing fans to follow different cameras alongside the main broadcast.

Alison Freeman, a longtime triathlon coach who watched from Colorado, with a group of athletes, was pleasantly surprised.

“I remember thinking at some point, maybe the second half of the bike, that they had done and were doing a very good job of providing balanced coverage between the men’s and women’s races,” Freeman says. “I didn’t feel like we were watching the men’s race and occasionally cutting over to the women’s race.”

For much of the day, the broadcast appeared to deliver on DeRue’s promise. That was until the leading men approached the finish line. Freeman had gone into the championship concerned that the men’s finish would coincide with a critical stage of the women’s marathon, as had happened during prior combined championships.

That is precisely when the coverage began to unravel. As the top 10 men crossed the finish line, the women’s “race ticker” disappeared from the main broadcast. Meanwhile the supplementary feeds that Freeman had been relying on began to malfunction. One went black, and another froze on footage of Knibb. For approximately 10 minutes, Freeman and her guests had virtually no reliable information on the status of the women’s race. They turned to the Ironman tracking app, but the information there was also inconsistent.

“We couldn’t see what was going on with her,” Freeman recalls of Knibb. “So it was basically this 10-minute essentially blackout on the top two women in the race with no data, no visuals.”

Fortunately, coverage returned before Løvseth made her pass on Knibb and viewers witnessed that key moment. But Freeman argues that whether the broadcast missed a critical pass is beside the point.

“To me, equal coverage is … do I get an equal sense of what is going on in both races?” she explains. “An equal understanding of the moves, the passes, who’s faltering, who’s looking strong.”

Triathlete’s own internal analysis found that approximately 56.5% of the broadcast was devoted to the men compared with 41.6% for the women, with the remainder consisting of scenery and other miscellaneous footage. The analysis also found that helicopter footage during the bike leg was almost exclusively focused on the men’s race. The notable exception being when the leading men passed by the leading women going in the opposite direction.

Producing race coverage for two simultaneous races is a difficult undertaking, particularly when decisive moments overlap. However, Ironman demonstrated throughout the day that split screen coverage was possible. Why then did the women’s race coverage disappear from the main broadcast when it mattered most?

Freeman sees a straightforward solution: Keep both races visible, even when the attention shifts to the men’s finish.

“If you want women to be in the sport, you should show women being in the sport, right?” she says.

While the professionals enjoyed largely separate races, the age-group athletes had a very different experience. Ironman’s wave-start schedule was designed to manage thousands of swimmers entering Kailua Bay, with age groups alternating between women and men rather than separating genders entirely.

If we’re looking at the women’s experience exclusively, the oldest women started first at 6:38 a.m., followed by older men. The younger men’s age groups began at 7:05 a.m., with women aged 40-54 starting at 7:25 a.m. The men’s 45-49 and 50-54 groups followed, before the youngest women finally entered the water at 7:40 a.m., followed by men aged 55-59 at 7:45 a.m.

For strong swimmers among the younger women, the consequences were significant. Victoria Feng, who finished eighth in the women’s 25-29 age group, described an extraordinarily difficult swim.

Feng was among the fastest female age-group swimmers, but starting behind slower male swimmers, she quickly encountered significant traffic. She estimates that she had clear water for only about five minutes before catching the waves ahead. The data bears it out; given her 56:55 swim split, Feng outswam 233 of the eventual 240 finishers in the men’s 55-59 age group by more than five minutes — though it’s also worth noting that Feng’s experience is an exceptional outlier.

She describes men grabbing, kicking, and pulling at her shoulders as she attempted to pass. Around the turnaround boat, she describes swimmers breaststroking directly in her path, forcing her toward the boat. The final approach to the pier was no better, with congestion in the narrow swim chute, making it difficult to navigate safely.

“This is not a way to run a world championship where women are significantly disadvantaged straight from the start,” she says.

Not every woman experienced the same problems, however.

Jana Richtrova, who finished second in the women’s 45-49 age group, reported a relatively uneventful swim despite the challenging ocean conditions. Richtrova started at 7:25 a.m. and swam approximately 1:15, a performance she was delighted with given the choppy water.

“Swim was completely fine,” she reports. “There was no aggression.”

She even attempted to jump on the feet of faster men as they came through from behind. But Richtrova still questions the decision to intersperse the women’s waves among the men’s, noting the previous combined championships had provided greater separation to provide fairer racing. Starting all the women behind the men might mean later starts, with greater exposure to heat and wind, but she believes it would produce a more equitable racing environment.

The contrasting experiences illustrate the complexity of designing wave starts for a combined championship. What works for a 1:15 swimmer may be considerably more successful than for someone swimming sub-1 hour.

While the swim created problems for Feng and some fellow competitors but not others, Feng and Richtrovra report similar experiences on the bike course.

Both Feng and Richtrova describe extensive drafting, crowded roads, and difficulty maintaining legal separation from other riders.

“I wish I was exaggerating when I said it was like the Tour de France out there,” Feng says.

She reports repeatedly encountering packs of 15 to 20 age-group athletes riding together.

“For the first 30 miles, it was pretty much impossible to ride legally most of the time,” she says.

Instead of riding at her planned power, she found herself deciding whether to surge past groups of athletes or drop well below race effort to reestablish legal spacing.

Richtrova had similar complaints. Shortly after leaving T1, she witnessed three men riding wheel to wheel on Kuakini Highway. As they approached the turnaround, the third rider touched the wheel of a rider ahead and crashed. On the Queen Ka’ahumanu Highway, she described large groups of athletes riding together, leaving limited space for athletes to pass safely. Even when Richtrova deliberately opened a legal gap, she experienced athletes cutting in dangerously close to her front wheel.

Neither woman knows exactly how the drafting affected results. Richtrova acknowledged that even riders making a genuine effort to follow the rules benefit from the presence of others.

However, the extent of the congestion raises questions about whether enough was done to enforce fair racing. Feng says she didn’t see a single referee and observed only one athlete in a penalty tent during the race. This of course contrasts with the pro event, which saw extensive penalty enforcement that had major implications to both genders’ podiums.

One of the less obvious consequences for women of returning to a combined race was the new combined transition tent. Richtrova, a veteran of multiple Kona championships, was surprised to discover that the traditional separate men’s and women’s change tents were now a single, shared tent. Private areas were available for athletes needing to change clothes, but the main changing area was mixed gender. For Richtrova, accustomed to a relatively quiet women’s transition tent, the experience was considerably more chaotic.

“It was just like, we’re here with the guys, and there’s a couple of us, and the rest is just men,” she recalls.

She praised the volunteers who continued to provide excellent assistance but questioned whether the new arrangements had been communicated clearly enough. A fellow competitor, she says, was initially directed into the men’s private changing area before discovering where she was supposed to go.

In many respects, yes. The professional races delivered unforgettable drama. The logistics of bringing both genders together appeared manageable. And it was special to see men and women sharing the Kona stage again.

But the combined event also exposed some weaknesses from the women’s perspective. Ironman made progress with its broadcast, only to lose sight (literally) of the women during one of its most important stretches. The wave start structure created very different swimming experiences for age-group women, while bike-course congestion and drafting raises questions about the fairness of the competition.

Ironman has a year to rethink the wave starts, review its drafting enforcement for age-groupers, improve communication around transition arrangements, and ensure its broadcast can follow two professional races simultaneously without sacrificing either one.
