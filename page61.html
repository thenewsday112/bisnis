#1209. Designers Say Placemats Are Falling Out of Style—Here’s What They’re Using Instead

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

These chic alternatives are stealing the spotlight.

There was a time when no tablescape was complete without a carefully chosen placemat. Department stores, boutiques, and linen closets in hosting-ready homes were filled with placemats covered in holiday motifs and seasonal hues. Whether serving as a practical safeguard or a conversation-starting detail, placemats were once a nonnegotiable element of the table.

These days, not so much. This table-setting staple is slowly disappearing. Here’s what designers say is behind the decline—and what they’re using in its place.

Placemats have long been used to add pops of color and texture to tablescapes. But now these textiles are losing their place at the table as hosts abandon them for chargers, runners, and slabs that give a more minimalist look to the table. However, some designers say that placemats will always have a place at their table.

“We love layers in all aspects of home design,” says Johanna Lyle, founder of the interior design firm Jo Lyle & Co. “Placemats are another way to add texture, color, and/or pattern to your tablescape. Functionally, they also protect your tablecloth from the messiest areas.”

Pretty as they may be, placemats are a first line of defense against spills, splatters, and stains. They’re easy to swap out, often less expensive than newer alternatives, and bring together style, value, and practicality. But they can interrupt the visual flow of a tablescape—a faux pas even novice hosts may want to avoid. Charger plates offer a popular alternative, pairing practical protection with a look that complements flatware.

“Placemats often chop up a table,” says Mallory Robins, co-founder of the Kansas City-based luxury design studio Kobel + Co. “A beautiful tablecloth layered with chargers offers the same defined effect but with a more seamless base layer.”

Designer Sarah Stacey couldn’t agree more. While she would hesitate to cancel placemats entirely, she’s rarely reaching for them when putting together her tablescapes.

“I usually opt for a tablecloth because placemats always feel bulky and too oversized. A tablecloth offers more flexibility in decorating a tablescape,” says Stacey.

If you find yourself yearning for the charm of linens, Robins suggests using linen napkins to add a personalized feel, textured detail, and pattern into your designs. But the most bespoke dining detail sure to dazzle guests? Place cards.

“Most critically, we're team place cards over place mats! There is nothing like walking into a gorgeous dinner party with clarity as to where you are sitting,” says Robins. “It feels inherently welcoming and is a great way to curate your guests into the ultimate conversation groupings.”

If you happen to own a beloved collection of placemats, we wouldn’t suggest tossing them just because they’re currently out of favor. Even the most dated decor details have found a way to resurface in a new way. The trend cycle is a cycle, after all.

#2756. Surefire Ways to Beat Jet Lag, According to Elite Athletes

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Mountaineer Megan Rath reaches the summit of Lhotse (the world's fourth-highest mountain at 27,939 feet). (Photo: Courtesy of Megan Rath)

Imagine not only enduring a long-haul flight, but then having to compete physically soon after you land. Top athletes have no time for jet lag symptoms, such as extreme fatigue, headaches, insomnia and gastrointestinal issues. They have more important things to do, like scaling mountains or qualifying for the Olympics.

Two recent research reviews focusing on how jet lag impacts athletic performance outline how jumping three or more time zones during extended east- or westbound travel disrupts the body’s internal clock. Current Sleep Medicine Reports says transatlantic travel exposes athletes to stressors like prolonged inactivity and dehydration, which not only affect sleep patterns and quality but can also impair an elite athlete’s cognitive and physical performance with symptoms including trouble concentrating, disorientation, light-headedness and irritability.

The journal Sports also found jet lag can disrupt athletes’ physiological, circadian, and performance systems. Athletes might experience hormonal and mood shifts, blood pressure or heart rate changes, and cognitive impairment that can affect strength, coordination, reaction time and velocity—any of which could impede high-performance outcomes.

From sleep scheduling and strategic exposure to light and dark to proper nutrition and hydration, four top competitors, including a high-altitude mountaineer and an ultramarathon runner, share with Outside how they beat jet lag and stay on their game while traveling.

Long before she plunges her paddle into the water, two-time Olympic sprint canoe medalist Katie Vincent prepares for international competitions by following a detailed protocol.

“Having a plan helps the transition,” Vincent tells Outside, having competed across Europe, Asia and South America. “The ideal situation would be to go a week or two early, have a full prep camp and slowly get over jet lag. But even with three to five days, you can at least get enough sleep, get into your routine and start competing.”

During flights, Vincent wears compression socks, a sleep mask, and earplugs, and prefers a window seat where she can curl her 5-foot-9 frame into a comfortable sleeping position. Upon arrival the next morning, she drinks two cups of coffee and aims to stay awake all day, i.e. no naps. The next few days are spent waking up, training, and going to bed at her regular time. She avoids napping and takes melatonin as needed to help her sleep.

“By day four, we’re adjusted, which is crucial as our nervous systems need to be regulated to produce the best performances on the water,” explains Vincent, who also tracks her sleep patterns on a Garmin watch.

“Most athletes on my team use their watch to gauge sleep quality. But everyone’s different; some can fall asleep in an instant, and others need more time to fall into the rhythm. There’s no one way to handle jet lag, but there are ways to navigate it.”

As a professional ultrarunner, former U.S. Biathlon National Team member, and a Carmichael Training Systems (CTS)-certified endurance sports coach, Corrine Malcolm has clocked countless hours in the air to run 100-mile races in Asia, Europe, Australia, and New Zealand.

“Jet lag impacts more than your ability to sleep or feel awake—there are hydration components, thermal regulation, and GI distress,” Malcolm tells Outside. She is based in Leavenworth, Washington, and hosts Trail Society, a podcast about trail running.

In the days leading up to a flight, Malcolm practices sleep banking—going to bed earlier and staying in bed longer to shore up resilience in anticipation of sleep loss.

“When you’re racing 100-plus miles, coming in with a lot of sleep banked can be important for feeling good,” she explains. “You’re also trying to move your circadian rhythm closer to the time zone you’re moving to. It takes a whole day for every time zone over three hours you’re crossing eastward versus a half-day adjustment going westward.”

Smart long-haul flying starts when booking your ticket; Malcolm opts for evening flights that head directly to her destination.

“You’ll sleep more readily on a ten-hour [direct] flight, versus flying from Seattle to Newark, and then to Geneva,” she says.

Malcolm brings her own food so she can eat quickly and get sleep-ready with noise-canceling headphones, compression socks, and an eye mask.

“Getting the biggest chunk of sleep possible goes a long way in managing how you feel on the ground. I also make sure I’m getting good morning light and eating familiar foods,” she says, noting that high-protein meals boost arousal while high-carbohydrate foods increase drowsiness.

“Control what you can control. Fixating on things that are out of your control will keep you up at 2 A.M. when you’re trying desperately to sleep. I always remind athletes: One bad night of sleep isn’t ideal, but focus on how you’ve been taking care of yourself for the last couple of weeks.”

“Some people I know start adjusting to a schedule ahead of time; I do not,” Emily Harrison Torrence, an ultramarathoner in Rapid City, South Dakota, tells Outside. She is also the co-owner of Sundog Running, which coaches athletes training for ultramarathons, endurance sports, and trail running.

“I try to nap on the plane, but I let that happen naturally, because it’s hard to know exactly what time zone you’re in when you’re in the air.”

Torrence packs lots of snacks for the plane: protein, healthy fat, nothing too sugary.

“It’s not uncommon for athletes, myself included, to have food allergies or food sensitivities, and sometimes the meals served on planes may not be too friendly, so make sure you’re prepared to be self-sustainable,” she suggests. “On international flights—before, during, and once you land—always have fluids on you. Plain water is great, but adding electrolytes is also good. Travel is dehydrating, and that can affect your performance and how you adjust to the new time zone.”

Once Torrence lands, she tries to adapt to her new time schedule but doesn’t overthink it.

“It depends on how long I’m going to be there; if I’m just flying in for a race a couple of days ahead of time, I’ll nap if I need to when I land, but just for 20 minutes— avoid passing out for three or four hours,” she says. “Good nutrition and staying well hydrated will help you offset that sleep deficit as you’re adjusting.”

Megan Rath, a triathlete-turned-mountaineer in Philadelphia who has scaled the Seven Summits—the highest mountain peaks on every continent—likes to add new methods to her mix of jet-lag-busting tricks. So, when she read about Timeshifter, a science-based circadian rhythm app, she was eager to learn more.

The app uses circadian neuroscience algorithms to create a custom schedule that slowly resets your body’s clock before, during, and after travel. After entering your sleep habits, age, sex, caffeine or melatonin usage, and flight itinerary, the app generates a personalized plan that sends you prompts to wake up, get bright light or none at all, drink coffee, and sleep.

“It’s all about light control—when you’re seeing light, when you’re not—and that mindset shift was a game changer,” Rath tells Outside. When climbing Lhotse, the world’s fourth-highest mountain, last spring, the mountaineer followed her Timeshifter plan.

“It’s a funky time change of nine hours and 45 minutes, plus flying from Newark to Istanbul and Istanbul to Kathmandu—essentially two red-eyes in a row—so it was tough trying to time when I’m putting my sunglasses on at the airport to start signaling my time to sleep,” recalls Rath.

“By the time I got to Mera, where they drop you off in the middle of nowhere, I felt pretty good. I’ve never felt this good on an 8,000-meter climb, and I was very dialed in with my preparation, including the hypoxic tent so I wouldn’t have to spend as much time on the mountain.”

Powered by a generator that reduces the oxygen concentration, hypoxic tents simulate higher altitude to allow the body to acclimatize before a climb. In addition to the tent, Rath prepares for rigorous treks by being laser-focused on hydration.

“If you’re drinking two liters at sea level, you’re drinking four at altitude, no exceptions, because your body is working that much harder. It’s dried out, and water needs to get to your cells; otherwise you’ve got headaches and cramping,” she explains.

“Everything I do is measured. I know exactly how much I’ve had to drink and when I’m drinking it. You don’t want to just guzzle water, because your body doesn’t have a chance to absorb it. They tell you to drink three or four sips every 15 minutes.”

Rath also limits sugar and avoids alcohol, which can increase dehydration due to its diuretic effects while also decreasing sleep quality.

With smart tools, proper nutrition,  and customized schedules, you can optimize performance instead of crashing in a jet-lagged stupor.

#3811. Jae Ko Transforms Everyday Paper Into Colorful Sculptural Landscapes

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Saturated spirals of blue, green, and magenta curl into intricate forms that resemble everything from tree rings to geological formations. At first glance, Korean-born artist Jae Ko’s sculptures could be made from carved wood, molded clay, or even stone. But the material behind these richly textured works is far more ordinary: paper.

Ko has spent more than four decades exploring the sculptural possibilities of paper, transforming discarded office supplies, recycled kraft paper, and adding machine paper rolls into intricate, often monumental forms. Her latest exhibition, Striated, at 22MUSE Gallery in Antwerp, Belgium, brings together works from a formative period in her practice, alongside a site-specific installation created for the gallery. It also marks Ko’s first solo exhibition in Belgium.

Ko’s process is both methodical and experimental. She begins by unwinding tightly coiled rolls of paper, then reshapes the material through rolling, twisting, folding, stacking, and compression. Some works are soaked in sumi ink, a traditional Japanese calligraphy ink made from soot, while others incorporate pigmented inks that introduce saturated shades of blue, red, green, and violet. Depending on the piece, she may also cut, carve, bind, or even bury the paper, letting time and environmental conditions shape its final form.

The results are surprisingly varied. Some sculptures curl inward like tightly wound spirals, their layered edges creating rhythmic patterns reminiscent of tree rings or sedimentary rock. Others stretch into elongated ribbons or fold back on themselves, producing forms that appear almost fluid despite their dense construction. The accumulation of countless individual layers gives the sculptures a visual weight that belies the fragility of their source material.

That tension between delicacy and durability is central to Ko’s work. Through repetition and compression, she transforms paper into a material capable of holding substantial volume and complex three-dimensional shapes. The process itself becomes visible in every ridge, groove, and curve.

Nature has long informed Ko’s approach, though her sculptures rarely represent a specific landscape or organism. Instead, they explore the forces that shape the natural world: erosion, growth, accumulation, and movement. Her large-scale installations, constructed from densely packed bundles of paper that loosen as they rise, can evoke rolling hills, ocean tides, or glacial formations. The resulting surfaces seem to shift and flow, even when completely still.

Ko’s relationship with landscape has also evolved geographically. After growing up in Seoul and studying in Japan, she relocated to the United States, eventually settling on Maryland’s Eastern Shore. Her work reflects both the urban environments of her early life and the natural surroundings she encountered later. She has also drawn parallels between her sculptures and bristlecone pine trees in the American West, whose trunks and branches are gradually reshaped by wind and weather.

For Striated, the title itself points to the fine parallel lines and grooves that define much of Ko’s work. The exhibition considers how those accumulated layers function as a record of time and labor, with each fold and compressed edge revealing something about the process of its making.

Striated is on view at 22MUSE Gallery in Antwerp, Belgium, through November 28, 2026.

Ignoring your money might feel blissful at first, but it can lead to bigger problems.

You’ve probably heard of getting ghosted by a romantic interest. One minute, they’re love bombing you. The next … crickets.

It turns out that many people act the same way toward their finances. Thinking about money is so stressful or overwhelming that they turn away from it, leaving statements unchecked and apps unopened for long periods of time.

“It’s easy to get caught up in a negative feedback loop where you know you’re under stress about your bills. You walk away to make it feel better,” says Steven Conners, founder and president of Conners Wealth Management in Scottsdale, Arizona.

The problem with financially ghosting yourself is that it’s easy to overspend, run up debt or create other financial problems when you’re not paying attention.

Here’s how to quit ghosting your finances and get back on track.

If you’ve been avoiding taking a look at your bank, credit card and other financial accounts, set aside time to dig in.

Wanda Belle, an accredited financial counselor and founder of Belle Financial Services in the Atlanta area, suggests printing out statements when possible so it’s easier to comb through them.

“Highlight any expenses that surprise you,” she says. Belle encourages people to avoid judgment or shame. Focus on bringing awareness to the numbers.

Once you’ve reviewed spending from the last month or two, Belle suggests giving yourself a new goal for the coming months. Perhaps you want to decrease restaurant spending by $100, or keep online shopping expenditures under a certain limit.

“Focus on variable expenses because you have more control over them,” she says.

Those are the expenses that fluctuate each month like groceries, rideshares or eating out, and they’re easier to cut back than fixed expenses like rent or car payments.

In addition to your spending, it’s helpful to dig into your overall money picture, including any loans, investments and other financial accounts.

That’s what Jason Bernat, president and CEO of American Financial Services, a wealth management company in Boardman, Ohio, does with clients when they’ve been ignoring their accounts.

“We go over all the bills, loans and assets, and identify what causes the most stress,” he says. “Then we can work backward to decide where to start.”

That first step could be as simple as opening up a new retirement account or increasing contributions to an existing one.

It could also be identifying a lingering balance you know you need to pay off.

Since debt can be a major money stressor, Bernat suggests prioritizing paying down credit card debt with any savings generated from your spending shifts.

“Paying down debt will make everything else more manageable,” he says.

At the same time, you also want to save what you can. Building up an emergency savings fund can protect you from having to take on more debt in the event of an unexpected expense, like a car repair or medical bill.

Both paying down debt and building up an emergency fund can take time, Bernat says.

“Don’t try to fix everything in a weekend. Pick one financial issue that’s creating the most stress at a time.”

If you’re resisting money management tasks because of the stress you associate with the experience, Conners suggests pairing your financial work with a physical activity.

“I’ve learned that it’s easier to manage your emotions when you have a coping mechanism like working out, going to the gym or going for a long walk. You get the tension and anxiety out of your system,” he says.

In that calmer state, it’s easier to tackle difficult tasks, Conners says. If exercise isn’t your thing, then another calming activity like watching movies, listening to music or knitting could work.

Put on your favorite jazz playlist while you pay bills, log in to your banking app while out on a walk, or grab your favorite beverage before making a budget.

With your fear of financial intimacy hopefully dissipating, you can keep a closer eye on your cash flow.

Document your spending and check in throughout the month to stay on track with your new goals, Belle says. After a month, review your new statement.

If you met your goals, pick a new category to focus on next month. If you didn’t, commit to making other adjustments and try again.

“Either way, you’re more aware of where your money is going and more intentional about future purchases,” she says.

In other words, you’re controlling your finances instead of ghosting them.

#1118. How to Get Better REM Sleep, According to a Sleep Specialist

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

There’s no magic biohack, but a few expert-backed habits can help you get more restorative sleep.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

On the pyramid of wellness and overall health priorities, sleep very often gets bumped to the bottom. Despite constant reminders like dark circles, puffiness, fatigue, and low energy, many have difficulty sticking to a consistent sleep schedule. Or, if they do manage to get to bed at a decent hour, sleep quality is compromised. Much like other daily routines, a person’s sleep patterns are personal, but recent studies show that consistently getting to a REM snooze state has significant benefits beyond the bed. Dr. W. Christopher Winter, a board-certified neurologist and sleep medicine specialist, explains why REM sleep is crucial to long-term health and how to get the most of each night.

Winter explains that sleep happens in three stages: light sleep, deep sleep, and dream sleep. rapid eye movement (REM) is dream sleep. “Deep sleep and REM often get lumped together, but they’re almost opposite states,” he says. “Deep sleep is quiet body, quiet brain. REM is quiet body, active brain.” In this state, he describes, your brain is nearly as active as when you’re awake, but your muscles are essentially paralyzed so you don’t act out your dreams.

We cycle through the three stages several times a night, roughly every 90 minutes, notes Winter. Deep sleep dominates the first part of the night, and REM gets longer and more concentrated toward the morning. Regarding how sleep habits and this level of insight sleep specialists have been able to home in on, Winter says, “We have come a very long way since Eugene Aserinsky figured out REM sleep from the basement of the University of Chicago, messing around with an old brain wave machine on his 8-year-old son.”

The term “experience” is subjective in the sleep field. “Most people move in and out of REM sleep without perceiving or experiencing it,” says Winter. “That said, even in those who 'don't dream...or remember it,' they are experiencing REM sleep, probably four to five times each night.” If you're sleeping a normal amount, meaning more than a couple of hours, Winter says you're getting REM. If your marker for REM is remembering a dream or whether or not you had one at all, Winter explains that there's a difference between not having dreams and not remembering them. “For people who truly don't seem to dream or experience REM, it's likely because something is disrupting their REM sleep. That could be alcohol, certain medications, or a sleep disorder like sleep apnea.” According to Winter, sleep apnea patients often have very disturbed REM sleep, which can lead to memory and cognitive issues, more pain, and brain fog.

Winter says., “We spend about half the night in light sleep. The other half is some mix of deep sleep and dream sleep, about 50/50, so REM ends up being roughly a quarter of the night. “The first REM period is short, maybe 10 minutes, and later ones can stretch close to an hour. That’s why cutting your night short, with an early alarm, for example, cuts disproportionately into REM. Unlike deep sleep, which steadily shrinks as we age, REM tends to hold steadier across adulthood,” Winter says

REM is more about concentration, focus, and memory. “It’s a little more opaque than deep sleep. If you don't get enough REM, you’ll get through your day okay. You're just going to put your car keys in your refrigerator. That little injury you got at the gym is going to bother you more. You might have more depressive thoughts or lack the motivation to do something,” Winter shares. He describes sleep as similar to a balanced diet: Protein isn’t necessarily more important than carbohydrates. It just does something different in the body. The goal, he says, is properly balanced sleep at night. “For the athletes I work with, REM is sneaky important...it's the difference between a base hit and a foul ball...or a perfect bound pass and one that goes out of bounds just out of reach from a teammate,” he adds.

Overall, poor sleep has many negative implications. Winter warns that inconsistent REM can affect the cardiovascular system, mood, hormones, blood sugar, and weight, and it can cause inflammation. “The list is almost infinite, so it's not surprising to see a study like this. Researchers followed more than 95,000 people for about nine years. Those who got more REM had a lower risk of 83 conditions, including heart failure, dementia, and Parkinson’s disease,” Winter summarizes.

While these findings are insightful, Winter says to keep two important factors in mind: “First, it's an association, not proof that more REM prevents disease. Second, sleep stages were estimated from a wrist device worn for one week,” he points out. The popularity of biometric wearable devices has peaked; Winter cautions against leaning too heavily into the data gathered from them. “I like trackers, but staging sleep is a tough thing for them to do. My takeaway is simple: People with the lowest risks were sleeping six to eight hours, and the highest risk was among people sleeping under five [hours]. Protect your total sleep, and REM tends to follow,” says Winter.

Findings from this study, he says, are not surprising. “When you think of how foundational sleep is, it should surprise nobody. It's like the Super Size Me experiment...When an individual eats poorly, it would not be a shock to see obesity, diabetes, cholesterol, heart disease, mood, etc. worsen. Sleep is just as foundational, so when it falls apart, many things downstream follow suit.”

Dreams are the biggest clue as to whether or not you’re reaping the benefits of REM sleep. If you wake up in the middle of a vivid dream, Winter says you were probably in REM. “Many of us naturally wake up out of REM in the morning, which is why morning dreams are the ones we remember. But not remembering a dream doesn't mean you didn't have one, because anything from your alarm to checking your phone can wipe a dream away,” Winter notes.

Sleep trackers can show trends, and Winter points out that they're good at telling you how much you sleep, but he says to take the exact REM numbers with a grain of salt. “The better test is how you function. Are you focused, is your mood steady, are your car keys in the right place?”

Inconsistent sleep patterns are worth seeing a doctor for, but Winter also says that if your sleep is fragmented or if you’re suddenly dreaming more, that’s also a good time to speak to a sleep specialist to see if something deeper is going on.

REM itself isn't bad, and as already established, it's essential. However, according to Winter, nightmares mostly happen in REM. Sleep apnea is often worse during REM because our muscles are so relaxed. “Sleep paralysis happens when that REM muscle paralysis lingers for a moment as you wake up,” he explains. “It's frightening, but usually harmless. There’s also REM sleep behavior disorder, where that paralysis doesn't work, and people physically act out their dreams. That one is worth telling a doctor about,” says Winter.

Certain things can help you get a better night’s rest, but Winter says you can’t really force REM. “There's no magic device or biohack. What you can do is protect the conditions for it.” Pick a wake-up time and stick with it, even on weekends. Seek bright light in the morning and dim the lights in the evening. Keep in mind that alcohol is very disruptive for sleep, so avoid it close to bedtime.

If you have insomnia, Winter says try not to chase sleep. “The secret to great sleep is being as happy in bed awake as you are asleep. If you snore or wake up gasping, get checked for sleep apnea, because it often fragments REM. For chronic insomnia, CBT-I, a talk therapy specifically for insomnia, is the first-line treatment,” Winter concludes.

On Oct. 27, 1961, NASA launched the first test flight of its new Saturn I space launch vehicle.

The Saturn I rocket was the United States' first heavy-lift rocket designed to launch big payloads beyond low Earth orbit. This was also the first test flight of any rocket in the Saturn family of rockets, which includes the Saturn V that was used to launch astronauts to the moon during the Apollo program.

The Saturn I was enormous compared to every rocket that came before it. It was almost three times as tall as the Jupiter-C rocket, which launched the first American satellite into orbit in 1958. It also produced more than 10 times the amount of thrust that the Jupiter-C rocket could. The liquid-powered rocket launched on its maiden voyage from Cape Canaveral.

Despite a one-hour weather day, the flight was nearly perfect. It reached an altitude of 85 miles and splashed down in the Atlantic Ocean 15 minutes later. The only thing that went wrong was that its engines cut off 1.6 seconds early, but that wasn't a huge deal. Scientists figured out that this happened because there was too much liquid oxygen in the rocket and not enough propellant.

NASA was only 3 years old when it launched its first Saturn rocket on Oct. 27, 1961. Human spaceflight was a mere 6 months old, after the April 12 launch of Soviet cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin earlier that year. Yet NASA had its eyes on the moon.

That's where the Saturn rocket family came in.

The Saturn I rocket, the predecessor to the Saturn V that sent Apollo astronauts to the moon, began its life in 1957 as the so-called Saturn C-1 booster, a project led by rocket scientist Wernher von Braun at the Advanced Research Project Agency. The plan called for a heavy-lift rocket capable of 1.5 million pounds of thrust (the first launch generated 1.3 million pounds of thrust, but later versions hit that 1.5 million mark). At launch, the Saturn I stood 162 feet tall and weighed 460 tons. NASA called that first test flight the Saturn SA-1 flight.

The Launch Complex 34 pad at what is now Cape Canaveral Space Force Station was specially built for the Saturn I rocket, with NASA taking charge in June 1961.

"The flight was considered essentially flawless," NASA said in a statement. "The Saturn SA-1 flight was the first of 32 successful Saturn rocket launches — 10 Saturn Is, 9 Saturn IBs, and 13 Saturn Vs — between 1961 and 1973."

NASA ultimately built three different types of Saturn rocket, the Saturn I, more powerful Saturn IB and the super-sized Saturn V rocket for Apollo moon launches.

#4219. Who Watches the AI?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Publish AI, ML & data-science insights to a global community of data professionals.

A separation of powers for control of autonomous agents

Autonomous AI will pursue its goals with rational persistence, even when those goals conflict with human intent. Without effective oversight, any advanced model with a degree of autonomous decision-making authority and access to consequential actions will eventually violate human expectations or assumptions. This broad alignment challenge has been significantly amplified by recent efforts at Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google to develop recursive self-improvement (RSI). Using this model-development approach, AI systems work at breakneck machine speed to enhance subsequent versions without human oversight. “Left unchecked, it could outrun our ability to understand and control these systems,” according to Anthropic’s CEO Dario Amodei.

Using RSI, an AI system can autonomously enhance its own capabilities and then use those enhanced capabilities to further accelerate its improvement, creating a compounding feedback loop that can rapidly exceed human oversight. RSI creates safety and governance risks because iterative self‑enhancement can amplify misalignment across generations and undermine safeguards that rely on static controls or human‑paced evaluation.

In this brave new world where it only takes a few hours for a model to improve itself, this latest advancement in AI capabilities is just one more reason why it’s time to introduce a new discipline: runtime alignment monitoring.

Based on my many years of experience with building systems and pushing the envelope on AI capabilities, I recognize the essential need for controlling and containing these rapidly evolving capabilities. My belief that effective AI alignment and governance require model observability and independent runtime oversight led me to co-found Confidential Core AI where I serve as Chief AI Scientist. Drawing on that body of work, I propose a runtime alignment monitoring framework rooted in the following three engineering imperatives (see Figure 1):

Learn this step by step with the interactive AI Agents roadmap.

Separation of powers: Governance architecture that separates obligations, agentic operations, and monitoring functions.

Independence of oversight: Monitors should be independent of and orthogonal to model providers.

Design for interpretability: AI systems must be built with intrinsic observability, not treated as black boxes.

For decades, AI safety focused on training-time alignment: values, fine-tuning, guardrails, and red-teaming. But as agentic systems gain autonomy, the alignment problem shifts from education to governance.

French political philosopher Montesquieu’s “trias politica” argued that separating the legislative, executive, and judicial branches is the minimum viable architecture for governing powerful actors with independent interests. Merging these functions inevitably triggers instability, corruption, or collapse.

It’s the same with AI. In an agentic system (see Figure 2):

The director or deploying enterprise creates a constitution — a defined set of obligations, constraints, and principles that governs agent behavior (legislative function).

The agent executes tasks (executive function).

The monitor (sometimes referred to as a guardian) ensures compliance with the rules and obligations defined in the constitution (enforcement and adjudication function).

For example, for an optimization agent built to maximize output, every constraint looks like drag on its mission, so it naturally treats guardrails as obstacles to navigate around. Meanwhile, the monitoring system exists to keep that same agent inside the boundaries laid out in its constitution. That built‑in conflict of interests becomes the core dynamic of the interaction.

When these roles blur, misalignment becomes inevitable. Any mechanism with overlapping incentives will eventually face conflicts, and if the same entity builds the model, deploys it, and monitors it, then blind spots align, oversight becomes model‑dependent, and the monitoring layer becomes something the agent can anticipate, influence, or manipulate.

AI agents exhibit a set of internal drives, including survival, goal‑protection, intelligence augmentation, resource accumulation, and tactical deception. These drives emerge naturally from optimization under autonomy, not from malfunction. These behaviors are rational for systems designed to pursue objectives aggressively, which is why they need strong checks and balances. A system can only be considered safe when safety itself is the overriding goal, not one objective among many.

That makes architectural separation essential: the mechanisms responsible for achieving functional goals must be independent from the mechanisms responsible for enforcing safety and oversight — not just logically separate, but organizationally and economically distinct. Even with the evolution of RSI, the required observation points for the monitor agent can be maintained through runtime alignment monitoring.

This brings us to the second imperative — the industry is beginning to acknowledge the need for independent monitoring. Anthropic’s new embedded evaluators proposal is a step toward transparency, but it is still fundamentally internal oversight. Accenture evaluators will be embedded in Anthropic with employee-level access to observe the decisions that govern how models are built, trained, and deployed. These evaluators will verify that Anthropic is meeting its safety commitments, and report incidents to the public.

This pilot program cannot address the structural conflict of interest created when the model provider funds and hosts the evaluators. Even though the evaluators are an external organization, their proximity and economic dependence limits true independence. Anthropic itself acknowledges this by noting that long‑term funding should come from pooled or government sources — an admission that embedded evaluation is an early step, not a complete oversight model.

Monitors must be structurally, operationally, and economically independent from the model providers they supervise. This requirement follows from five structural realities (see Figure 3):

Shared lineage equals shared blind spots: Monitors built or hosted by the same company inherit the same assumptions, vulnerabilities, and training artifacts.

Conflict of interest: When borderline behavior is discovered, the model provider has incentives to downplay or reinterpret it.

Model-dependent monitoring: Monitors built on the same architecture can be gamed using the same failure modes.

IP leakage: Enterprise-specific rules, constraints, and proprietary workflows cannot be safely shared with model providers.

Ownership and sovereignty: Monitoring must be owned by the deploying enterprise — not the frontier lab.

Enterprises should define the rules that govern agent behavior, but they must not tamper with the mechanisms that enforce them. Independent monitors may run within enterprise environments to preserve data sovereignty and confidentiality, yet the enterprise should not modify the monitoring technology, disable monitoring functions, or alter the enforcement process. Rule definition and rule enforcement must remain separate to ensure oversight cannot be weakened by the organization being monitored.

The third principle is interpretability. AI should not be a black box. There should be base levels of model/agent interpretability as necessary to allow deployment.

As agentic systems gain autonomy, they increasingly manipulate their external communications to pursue their goals. This is the deep scheming foundational premise: Every external form of interaction that the AI model can deliberately manipulate is not trustworthy.

Over the past two years, autonomous systems have been increasingly behaving in ways that are opaque, untraceable, and ungovernable. In Anthropic’s alignment‑faking experiment in 2024, the model used a “secret scratchpad” to record its true reasoning while presenting a sanitized chain‑of‑thought externally. The scratchpad revealed that the model understood the conflict between its pre‑training values and the modified guidance, and chose to fake alignment to avoid being retrained. Relying on models “thinking out loud” should not be a long-term safety direction. Fast forward to a recent 2026 study where researchers used a custom API tool to induce frontier models to externalize chain-of-thought reasoning. OpenAI’s GPT‑6 Astra demonstrated that frontier models now resolve many reasoning steps internally, externalizing only selective fragments. This means that external logs, chain‑of‑thought, and output‑based monitoring are already insufficient.

Interpretability is no longer just a matter of understanding how a model works. It is a matter of ensuring that the model cannot hide how it works. This requires a shift from external interpretability of observing outputs to designing intrinsic interpretability for observing internal processes.

Interpretability must be designed into architecture. In the early days of microprocessors, testing and debugging were afterthoughts. Over time, design for testability (DFT) and design for debug (DFD) became mandatory. You cannot design a functional block without meeting some requirements of DFT. Hooks for observability, including scan chains, test points, and debug registers, became part of the architecture itself.

AI must follow the same path. Today, most AI systems are designed for performance, not observability. Interpretability is bolted on afterward, if at all. This is unsustainable.

Design for interpretability (DFI) must become a discipline analogous to DFT. Models must include:

Built‑in observation points that cannot be suppressed or manipulated.

Internal reasoning channels that expose latent decision processes.

Mechanisms for tracing decisions back to principles and constraints.

Architectural features that support inner probing and mechanistic analysis.

Guarded internal memory structures that reveal long‑term planning.

Non‑bypassable hooks for runtime monitoring systems.

The developer must incorporate mechanisms that allow for observability and testability even if it has some negative impact on power, performance, or latency.

The way to control AI is not by slowing down the pace of progress. It’s made safer by ensuring that agents are transparent, constitutionally grounded, interpretable, and continuously monitored. However, recent AI developments like RSI break every assumption of traditional monitoring.

This is why separation of power, independent oversight, and design for interpretability must be established before RSI and other advanced AI capabilities become widespread. Once agents can rewrite themselves, any safety mechanism not structurally external and independent will become obsolete.

Runtime alignment monitoring closes the gap between what the agent intends, what the agent plans, and what the agent executes. The ultimate goal of this layer is to catch sycophancy, reward hacking, inter‑agent steering, covert planning, oversight subversion, hidden chain‑of‑thought, deep scheming, and RSI‑driven drift.

Runtime alignment monitoring with sovereign control by the deploying enterprise provides a path to safe, scalable, autonomous AI.

#1946. The Trump administration’s attempt to prevent noncitizen voting and the role of the Supreme Court

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

The Supreme Court may soon see another clash between the original public meaning of the Constitution and President Donald Trump’s wishes. The birthright citizenship case, Trump v. Barbara, is one previous example of this. The next instance, which looms on the horizon, involves the issue of noncitizen voting.

The Trump administration has been aggressively undertaking efforts to get states to take steps aimed at preventing noncitizens from voting. The latest such effort is a lawsuit that Trump’s DOJ has filed against California seeking to force the state’s election officials to segregate mailed ballots cast by anyone on a list of 20,000 registered voters whom the Trump administration believes to be noncitizens. As of this writing, the suit is pending in the federal district court, after the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit denied DOJ’s emergency appeal for failing to give the district court an adequate chance to issue a ruling, but the merits of the case are likely to make their way to the Supreme Court very soon.

As authority for its suit against California, Trump’s DOJ is relying upon the federal statute that makes voting by noncitizens in federal elections a crime: 18 U.S.C. § 611. There are two fundamental problems, however, with DOJ’s reliance on this statute. First, the original public meaning of the Constitution does not give Congress the authority to make voting by noncitizens a crime; rather, the Constitution leaves it to the states to decide whether or not noncitizens may vote, and this is true even for federal elections.

Second, even assuming that 18 U.S.C. § 611 is a valid exercise of congressional power under the Constitution, it does not authorize DOJ to seek an injunction that would interfere with a state’s rules and procedures for conducting the “manner” of the current congressional elections. Article I, Section 4 of the Constitution provides that state law will govern the “manner” of administering congressional elections unless and until Congress enacts alternative procedures for their administration; 18 U.S.C. § 611 is a criminal law and does not regulate the “manner” of administering elections and thus offers no basis for DOJ or a federal court to supersede the state’s administration of the election.

As to the first problem, the Constitution creates a clear division of authority between determining who can vote in congressional elections and regulating how the voting process occurs. As to the how, as noted, Article I, Section 4 of the Constitution empowers Congress to override state regulations if Congress so wishes. But as to the who, the Constitution lodges the power to determine the qualifications for voting in congressional elections to the states, with Congress having no authority to change those qualifications.

To be sure, a series of important constitutional amendments constrains the degree to which states can set those qualifications. The 15th Amendment prohibits racial discrimination in doing so. The 19th Amendment similarly prohibits sex discrimination with respect to voting rights. The 24th Amendment prohibits a state from making payment of a poll tax a qualification for voting in a federal election. And the 26th Amendment prohibits states from denying an equal opportunity to vote based on age to any citizen at least 18 years old.

But none of these amendments prevent states from setting other qualifications or extending the right to vote. States, for example, could grant 16-year-old citizens the right to vote in congressional elections, and Congress cannot override a state’s decision to do this. The Constitution is explicit on this point. Article I, Section 2, clause 1 provides for the House of Representatives that “the Electors in each State shall have the Qualifications requisite for Electors of the most numerous Branch of the State Legislature.” The 17th Amendment contains the same provision for elections to the U.S. Senate. Thus, if a state were to grant 16-year-old citizens the right to vote for the lower house of the state legislature, the Constitution requires that 16-year-old citizens in that state must have the right to vote for that state’s Representatives and Senators in Congress. No Act of Congress could take away this right to vote in congressional elections granted to 16-year-olds by the state.

The same point applies to voting by noncitizens. Currently, no state chooses to let noncitizens vote for the lower house of the state’s legislature. But if a state decided to let resident aliens vote in these elections, then resident aliens would also be eligible to vote in the state’s congressional elections.

The Supreme Court previously has made clear this distinction between who and how. In 2013’s Arizona v. Inter Tribal Council of Arizona, Inc., which involved the National Voter Registration Act, Justice Antonin Scalia wrote in the opinion for the court (with its own emphasis on the two key words): “Arizona is correct that the Elections Clause empowers Congress to regulate how federal elections are held, but not who may vote in them.” Scalia elaborated: “Prescribing voting qualifications, therefore, ‘forms no part of the power to be conferred upon the national government’ by the Elections Clause, which is ‘expressly restricted to the regulation of the times, the places, and the manner of elections’” (quoting Alexander Hamilton in The Federalist No. 60). Scalia then explained why: “This allocation of authority sprang from the Framers’ aversion to concentrated power. A Congress empowered to regulate the qualifications of its own electorate, Madison warned, could ‘by degrees subvert the Constitution.’”

The prohibition on noncitizen voting in 18 U.S.C. § 611 contravenes this constitutional division of authority. This congressional statute makes it “unlawful for any alien to vote in any election held solely or in part for the purpose of electing a candidate for … Member of the Senate [or] Member of the House of Representatives,” and it does so regardless of whether the state in question permits noncitizens to vote in these elections. The statute lets states allow noncitizens to vote for state or local offices, but a state must do so “in such a manner that an alien has the opportunity to vote for such other purpose, but not an opportunity to vote for a candidate for any one or more of such Federal offices.” Thus, according to this federal statute, a state has no choice in the matter of whether or not a noncitizen can vote in a congressional election. This makes 18 U.S.C. § 611 unconstitutional.

I am not the first to reach this conclusion. A federal district court in Florida did so recently. More significantly, that district court had asked Paul Clement, the distinguished former U.S. solicitor general in President George W. Bush’s administration, to write an amicus brief on the issue. After undertaking a thorough analysis of the matter, Clement concluded “the better view is that because §611 was intended to criminalize alien suffrage in federal elections, regardless of state law, it sets a voter qualification—which only the states may do.”

To be clear, Congress can make noncitizen voting a crime if (but only if) that criminality is contingent on noncitizen voting also being unlawful in the applicable state. In that context, the congressional statute could be understood as merely regulating procedures for implementing congressional elections, and not determining who is permitted to vote in them. One might then try to argue that 18 U.S.C. § 611 can be sustained as this kind of statute as long as the relevant state currently criminalizes noncitizen voting. The difficulty with this argument, as Clement explained, is that § 611 is not written that way; instead, it emphatically makes noncitizen voting in congressional elections a federal crime regardless of what the state wants.

In any event, let’s assume that 18 U.S.C § 611 could be construed to be constitutional in the way just described. That still doesn’t give Trump’s DOJ the authority to seek an injunction in federal court to force California to change its procedures for administering the current congressional election. Congress would have needed to enact a different law, exercising its authority under the Constitution’s Article I, Section 4 to “alter” the “manner” by which California has chosen to regulate the voting process.

There was a time in American history when Congress enacted a statute to provide federal oversight of a state’s procedures for conducting congressional elections. In the wake of the Civil War, the first Enforcement Act of 1871 empowered two federal “supervisors” in each of the state’s voting precincts, who were appointed from “different political parties” by the federal circuit court for their state, to monitor the voting process for congressional elections. This statute gave these supervisors authority similar to what Trump’s DOJ is currently seeking. The supervisors, for example, could “challenge any vote offered by any person whose legal qualifications the supervisors, or either of them, shall doubt.” To be sure, these supervisors were not part of the Department of Justice, which Congress had created the previous year. Rather, the requirement that these two supervisors be from “different political parties” and appointed by the judiciary was designed to keep them insulated from a cabinet department under the control of a partisan president.

In any event, in 1894 Congress repealed all the provisions of the 1871 Act that had created these supervisors for congressional elections. Nothing like them existed until the Voting Rights Act of 1965, when Congress enacted new provisions for monitoring elections and enforcing the prohibition against racial discrimination in voting. These new provisions did give some significant enforcement powers to the DOJ and federal courts, but they were focused on guaranteeing the equal opportunity to vote regardless of race. These new provisions did not give DOJ or the federal courts any authority to interfere with the counting of ballots in a congressional election in order to prevent ballots cast by noncitizens from being counted.

Indeed, throughout the 20th century, the prevailing understanding was that federal courts lacked the power to intervene in a state’s procedures for counting votes in a congressional election, even if a suit was brought by a candidate with a direct stake in the matter. The most famous example of this is the 1948 Senate race that Lyndon Johnson won by 87 votes. His opponent, former Texas governor Coke Stevenson, sued in federal court to block the counting of votes believed to be fraudulent in the infamous Ballot Box 13. In an exercise of the Supreme Court’s emergency docket, however, Justice Hugo Black ordered that the federal court refrain from interfering with the state’s counting process. After an unusual four-hour oral argument held in Black’s chambers, which he permitted members of the press to attend, he announced his ruling orally. The New York Times reported that he stated that “before a court interfered with an election there must be a statute clearly authorizing this.” The Times also quoted Black as saying, “I think it is impossible to interpret present statutes that Federal courts may go into a state and suspend its process of electing a Senator.” (For more details on the fight over Ballot Box 13, see chapter 8 of my 2024 book “Ballot Battles: The History of Disputed Elections in the United States.”)

There is one caveat. In Bush v. Gore, the court modified Black’s understanding of the federal judiciary’s power insofar as that unusual (and controversial) case permitted a candidate to assert an equal protection claim as grounds for constraining a state’s election procedures. But Bush v. Gore did not involve a suit by the federal DOJ. Indeed, President Bill Clinton insisted that his administration refrain from any involvement in the litigation between the two candidates in the 2000 presidential election. The idea that the federal DOJ could have brought suit in federal court to control the counting process in that election – contrary to Florida’s own procedures – would have astonished even those who supported the idea that the candidates themselves could invoke the authority of the federal courts to enforce Florida’s compliance with constitutional requirements respecting the electoral process.

The bottom line is this: In a congressional election, the state administers the electoral process according to its own laws, subject to the prerogative granted to each congressional chamber by Article I, Section 5 to be the “Judge of the Election … of its own Members.” If Congress wants to add DOJ-initiated federal court proceedings to the overall process of counting, canvassing, and certifying the votes in a congressional election – which would still be subject to being overridden by the ultimate authority of each chamber to adjudicate any disputes over the outcome pursuant to Article I, Section 5 – Congress can do by exercising its Article I, Section 4 authority. But unless and until Congress does that, DOJ has no role to play.

Trump and his DOJ clearly wish the situation were different. But it is the job of the Supreme Court to uphold the law as it is. That job may require the court to reject the effort by Trump’s DOJ to interfere with California’s procedures for conducting this year’s midterm elections.
