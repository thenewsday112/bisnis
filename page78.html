InfoQ Homepage News Developers Reporting Double-Digit Weekly Time Savings from AI Fell from 68% to 26%

Oct 27, 2026 3 min read

The share of developers reporting ten or more hours saved each week through AI fell from 68% to 26% in a year, according to Atlassian’s State of Developer Experience 2026. Adoption over the same period became close to universal, with 95% of developers and 96% of engineering leaders saying their organization actively uses AI in engineering.

The report draws on 1,155 individual contributors and 405 engineering leaders surveyed during 2026. Atlassian is unusually direct about what that means, stating that its findings "reflect self-reported experience and perception, not system-level telemetry such as deployment frequency, cycle time, or revenue impact," and that associations in the data "should not be interpreted as causal proof."

Taroon Mandhana, Atlassian’s CTO for AI and Teamwork, summarised the shift as one where "the bottleneck has moved to managing AI’s work."

Local benefit has not gone away. Eighty-seven percent of developers say AI helps them ship faster and 80% say it helps them deliver more without longer hours. What has not followed is relief from everyday obstacles. Only 28% report noticeably less friction than before AI, while 50% report about the same amount, which the report describes as friction "of a different type than before AI."

Some of that difference is AI itself. Forty-nine percent of developers now spend four or more hours a week managing AI tools, and 65% say they spend time correcting AI-generated work that missed the original intent. Both figures run higher among engineering leaders, at 57% and 72%. Twenty-nine percent name reviewing or managing AI-generated code as a source of friction outright.

The rest of the list has barely moved in three years. Finding information leads at 51%, followed by meetings at 50%, context switching at 45% and technical debt at 44%. Finding information also topped the 2025 list and placed second in 2024. Coding has never appeared on it.

Measurement is where the report is most pointed. Sixty percent of developers say AI use is treated as a productivity signal in their organization, 41% say they sometimes use AI more than necessary because they feel expected to, and only 42% of leaders say they have the right metrics to separate real gains from activity.

Responding to Mandhana, Shan Huang, who builds production AI agents for enterprise workflows, connected two of those figures:

If people are using AI more than necessary because they feel expected to, part of that 49% spending 4+ hours a week managing AI output is not a tooling problem, it is a metric problem. Call it performative throughput: the usage gets counted as productivity, and the cleanup lands on somebody else’s calendar.

Taylor Johnson of Athena Intelligence proposed a replacement in one line:

AI activity is not impact; measure accepted work, not usage.

Organizations with a stated position are rare and appear to fare better. Seventeen percent of developers and 23% of leaders report a clear, actively implemented AI strategy across the software delivery lifecycle. Among developers who do, 41% report noticeably less friction than before AI, against 22% of those describing an inconsistent or poorly communicated strategy. Andrew Boyagi, Atlassian’s head of DevOps evangelism, calls that gap the differentiator, though the report itself cautions that the association is not causal.

One trend has run the same way for three years. The share of developers saying leadership does not fully understand the challenges they face was 44% in 2024, 63% in 2025, and 66% in 2026.

Cam Turner, a designer and former AWS staffer, put the practical question that neither the report nor the thread answered: "68 to 26 in a year says the work moved from making to reviewing. Is anyone designing the review step yet, or are teams just absorbing it?"

Two caveats sit on the headline figure. This is vendor research from a company selling developer tooling, landing on a conclusion that favors formalizing AI delivery. The comparison base also changed, from 3,500 developers and managers across six countries in 2025 to 1,560 respondents this year, and neither edition publishes fieldwork dates.

Self-reported hours are soft in both directions, which commenter Harshit Jain raised directly: is the drop about review load, or about higher expectations? The report does not separate them.

#4512. IBM admits mainframes need to be modernized and will use AI to do the job

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Promises to build ‘IBM Z Atlas’ tool that will tidy up messy big iron and enable AIOps

IBM touts its Z series mainframes as the most reliable and cost-effective environments for certain workloads, but on Monday also acknowledged that the platform can drift towards hard-to-manage complexity that reduces resilience and therefore needs some help from AI.

Big Blue admitted that not all mainframe estates exist in a state of perfect bliss in a Statement of Direction for a forthcoming product called “IBM Z Atlas” that the document describes as “an AI-native solution that uses system intelligence to ground AI skills and agents in the context of key workflows like planning and validating changes or modernizing applications and infrastructure.”

“It intends to map a client's entire z/OS stack across infrastructure, middleware, and applications, and then use that system intelligence to reduce the manual effort of tasks like patch management, regulatory response, and sharing knowledge across every team, from systems programmers to application developers.”

Big Blue also hopes the tool helps mainframe techs and ops teams “better understand how components connect, interact, and depend on one another.”

“IBM Z Atlas intends to eliminate the leading cause of unplanned z/OS outages – changes that aren't proven to be safe – by mapping how a change impacts the z/OS stack, testing it, and validating it,” the Statement of Direction adds. “This design accelerates clients' modernization efforts by keeping experts in control and giving every practitioner, in operations or development, the same shared Z system visibility to reduce risk.”

IBM reckons Z Atlas will help mainframe shops do four things:

Understand systems and dependencies before making decisions or initiating changes

Assess potential impacts, risks, and operational considerations

Accelerate planning for modernization, deployment, and provisioning

Provide trusted context for AI-native experiences and automation

While IBM clearly sees AI has a role in helping its customers to manage mainframes, it appears not to be going all-in on AIOps – the emerging practice of allowing agents to either keep tech infrastructure humming, or autonomously find issues and develop proposed fixes.

“IBM intends for AI-assisted insights and recommendations to preserve appropriate human review and decision authority,” the Statement of Direction reads.

The multiple mentions of modernization caught your correspondent’s eye, because IBM’s many rivals often suggest the best way to modernize mainframes is by trashing them and moving the applications they run to another platform. In recent years, vendors have suggested using generative AI to rewrite mainframe apps in other languages, an idea that analyst firm Gartner thinks is highly overrated.

IBM clearly wants modernization to mean making mainframes easier to live with so that customers persist with the platform, a critical outcome as the company’s revenue spikes when each new generation of Z-series machines hits the market. Indeed, IBM CEO Arvind Krishna last week said the current Z17 generation of mainframes has outsold all others by $1 billion.

That kind of result well and truly puts to bed the “death of the mainframe” narrative that, like next year becoming the Year of Linux on the Desktop, is a prediction that never goes away and never comes to fruition. With Z Atlas, IBM seems to be trying to ensure rumors of the mainframe’s demise are even more exaggeratedly false. ®

#510. Scientists discover a surprising clue to why some brains resist Alzheimer’s

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Some people have the brain changes associated with Alzheimer's disease but never develop dementia. While others experience worsening memory and thinking problems, these individuals remain mentally sharp despite signs of the disease in their brains. Scientists have long struggled to explain why.

Now, researchers at the Netherlands Institute for Neuroscience have uncovered a promising clue. Their findings suggest that a rare group of brain cells, known as immature neurons, may behave differently in people who resist Alzheimer's symptoms. Rather than simply replacing damaged cells, these neurons could help protect the brain from the harmful effects of the disease.

This remarkable ability is known as cognitive resilience. It describes how some people maintain their memory and thinking abilities even when their brains show signs of damage or disease.

"Around 30 percent of older adults who develop Alzheimer's disease never experience its symptoms," says Evgenia Salta, senior author of the research. "We really don't know why. That's a big mystery, and a very important one."

Understanding this mystery could transform how scientists approach Alzheimer's, shifting attention from what causes brain cells to deteriorate toward what allows certain brains to remain healthy and functional.

One possible explanation for cognitive resilience is that some brains have a greater ability to repair or protect themselves as Alzheimer's progresses.

"Perhaps they can add new brain cells to a network that is degenerating," Salta says.

This possibility involves a biological process called adult neurogenesis, which refers to the production of new nerve cells (neurons) in an adult brain. Neurons are specialized cells that transmit information throughout the nervous system, allowing us to think, learn, remember, and respond to our surroundings.

Scientists have established that adult neurogenesis occurs in several animal species. Whether it continues to happen in the adult human brain, however, has been a subject of scientific debate for decades.

If the human brain can produce new neurons later in life, those cells might help maintain important connections as aging and disease damage existing networks. However, finding immature neurons does not necessarily prove that they were recently formed.

To investigate this possibility, Salta and her colleagues examined donated human brain tissue obtained through the Netherlands Brain Bank, a research resource that collects and preserves brain samples.

The researchers compared three groups: people without detectable brain pathology, patients who developed Alzheimer's dementia, and individuals whose brains showed Alzheimer's pathology but who remained cognitively resilient.

The team concentrated on a small region within the brain's memory center, an area believed to be among the few locations where new neurons might develop.

Finding the relevant cells was far from straightforward.

"These cells are extremely rare, so we had to develop new ways to find them," Salta says. "We really zoomed in on the exact spot where we expected them to be."

The researchers also developed new approaches to analyzing their data, allowing them to identify the cells more precisely without relying heavily on assumptions drawn from animal studies.

Scientists Find Immature Neurons in Brains Over 80

The search revealed something remarkable. Immature neurons were present across all three groups, including in the brains of older adults.

These cells resemble neurons that have not yet fully matured, suggesting that the aging brain may retain cellular features associated with development and adaptability.

"Even at an average age of over 80, we still found these immature neurons in all groups," Salta says.

But the most intriguing discovery came when researchers compared the brains of people who had developed dementia with those who had remained mentally sharp despite Alzheimer's pathology.

Initially, the scientists expected resilient individuals to have substantially more immature neurons. If these cells help replace damaged neurons, having greater numbers might explain why some brains withstand the disease.

Instead, the difference in cell numbers was much smaller than anticipated.

That unexpected result pointed toward another explanation. The important distinction might not be how many immature neurons a brain contains, but how those cells respond when the brain comes under attack.

A Hidden Defense Against Alzheimer's Damage

When the team examined the activity of immature neurons more closely, they identified striking differences between the groups.

In cognitively resilient individuals, the cells showed signs of activating biological processes associated with survival and protection against damage.

"In resilient individuals, these cells seem to activate programs that help them survive and cope with damage," Salta says. "We also see lower signals related to inflammation and cell death."

Inflammation is part of the body's normal defense system, but when it becomes persistent in the brain, it can contribute to damage and interfere with communication between neurons. Cell death, meanwhile, gradually disrupts the networks responsible for memory and thinking.

The findings suggest that immature neurons in resilient brains may be better equipped to withstand these harmful conditions.

More surprisingly, their role might extend beyond protecting themselves.

"It might not be (only) about replacing lost neurons," Salta explains. "It could be that these cells support the surrounding tissue and help the brain stay functional and 'youthful'. They may act as a sort of fertilizer in a garden that has started falling apart."

In other words, rather than serving only as replacements for dying neurons, immature cells could potentially help maintain the environment surrounding existing brain cells.

Such support might allow neural networks to continue functioning even as Alzheimer's related damage accumulates.

Why Scientists Are Cautious About the Discovery

Although the results offer an intriguing explanation for cognitive resilience, Salta emphasizes that the researchers have not established exactly what these immature neurons do.

Their conclusions about the cells' possible protective roles are based on patterns observed in brain tissue, not direct experiments demonstrating those functions.

"We assume the cells' function based on the data, but we cannot confirm it in this type of study," she explains.

That distinction is particularly important because research on adult neurogenesis has attracted considerable public attention, sometimes leading to claims that go beyond what scientists have demonstrated.

The presence of immature neurons does not establish that new neurons are continuously being produced in older humans, nor does it prove that these cells are responsible for preventing dementia.

Other biological mechanisms almost certainly contribute to cognitive resilience as well.

"This is one piece of a very large puzzle," Salta concludes. "There will never be just one factor that explains resilience."

Could Alzheimer's Research Shift Toward Protecting the Brain?

The findings raise a broader question about aging: Why do some people's brains remain relatively stable while others experience progressive cognitive decline?

"Somewhere along this trajectory, there's a kind of decision point," Salta explains. "Some people remain stable, others develop dementia. We want to understand what drives that difference."

To explore this further, the researchers plan to investigate how immature neurons communicate and interact with neighboring brain cells. Understanding those relationships may reveal whether the cells actively contribute to resilience and how their protective responses might work.

The research also reflects a growing change in how scientists think about Alzheimer's disease.

Traditionally, much of the field has focused on understanding the processes that damage the brain, including the accumulation of abnormal proteins and the loss of neurons. Increasingly, researchers are also asking why some brains remain functional despite those changes.

By identifying the mechanisms behind this natural resistance, scientists hope to uncover new ways to preserve memory and cognitive abilities as people age.

"Cognitive resilience is extremely exciting," Salta says. "If we understand what protects these brains, it could eventually lead to new therapeutic strategies."

Although practical treatments remain a distant possibility, the discovery suggests that even in old age, the human brain may possess protective abilities that scientists are only beginning to understand.

The Instax Mini Evo 2 Has a Bigger Display and Expanded Field of View

Fujifilm has announced the Instax Mini Evo 2, which upgrades the rear LCD, adds a new “Expanded View” framing feature, and introduces an “Immersive Movie” option that captures up to four seconds before the photo is taken.

As a reminder, the Instax Evo line is Fujifilm’s hybrid digital and analog system which uses a digital sensor to capture content and a built-in printer to print those photos onto two-by-three-inch Instax instant film. Photographers can review and edit images digitally on the camera before pulling the physical print lever to output a tactile physical photo. In the case of the Instax Mini Evo 2, Fujifilm notes that the integrated printer delivers rich 600 dpi resolution for sharp output.

The upgrades are small, but they significantly enhance day-to-day usability. The rear LCD display has been expanded from three inches to 4.5 inches to make it far easier to compose a shot and navigate menus. The camera also simplifies shot composition via the new Expanded View feature. While it does not increase the actual field of view of the captured image, it displays a wider area beyond the frame edges. Fujifilm states this helps photographers view more of the surrounding environment to compose “with greater intention.”

Fujifilm also added a new Immersive Movie mode that records the four seconds leading up to each photo. A microSD card, which is not included with the camera, is required to utilize this video feature.

“The instax Evo line remains an incredibly popular part of the instax™ product family, so it made perfect sense to take everything about the original Evo that made it great and enhance it even further,” Bing Liem, division president, Imaging Division, Fujifilm North America Corporation, says. “Evo 2’s new features give creators even more options for both still photography and video, creating a meaningful, immersive creative experience.”

The camera retains the popular 10 lens effects and 10 film effects from the previous generation, allowing photographers to combine them for 100 distinct visual style combinations. Additional built-in features include a macro mode for close-up photography and a front-facing selfie mirror. The Instax Mini Evo 2 also provides adjustable exposure compensation, white balance presets, and flash controls operated via tactile, analog-inspired dials. For versatile shooting setups, the camera can also be controlled remotely via the companion Instax Mini Evo smartphone app.

The Fujifilm Instax Mini Evo 2 hybrid instant camera is expected to be available by the end of November for $290.
