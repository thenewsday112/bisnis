#3096. Adventure Links of the Week

📅 Oct 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When we’re not outdoors, we get our adventure fix by exploring social media and the web. Here are some of the best adventure links we’ve discovered this week.

First Highline on Trango Tower: On August 17, Lukas Irmler and Antonia Ruede-Passul of Germany completed the first-ever highline walk on Trango Towers in Pakistan’s Karakoram. They climbed Great Trango Tower to acclimatize before tackling Nameless Tower.

Setting up the 21m highline required fixed ropes and two summit ascents of Nameless Tower. The successful crossing saw Ruede-Passul break her own women’s highline altitude world record, previously set at 5,860m on Ojos de Salado in Chile, while Irmler surpassed 6,000m for the first time.

Following in the footsteps of Norway’s Great Adventurers: Steffen Skjottelvik set out to cross Canada, by foot, dog sled, and canoe, accompanied only by his beloved sled dogs. The Norwegian began his journey in 2023, but when one dog fell ill, he paused in the remote Cree community of Peawanuck. Here he found his home and a way of life that fitted him perfectly. He also fell in love with a local woman and became a second son to the community elders.

His original plan to travel by sled across the Hudson Bay coast changed after wolves killed one of his lead dogs. He decided to canoe the coastline in summer instead.

In 2025, he set off with his malamute Togo and a husky named Bay, facing polar bears, powerful tides, dense bogs, clouds of biting flies, and river crossings. On August 15, exhausted after weeks of difficult travel, Steffen could finally see his endpoint — the town of York Factory across the Hayes River. All he had to do was cross it. Togo survived and made it to shore, but Steffen’s body was found downstream 10 days later.

Where Was ‘Tenzing’ Filmed? Apple TV’s Tenzing recreates the historic 1953 first ascent of Everest, this time with Sherpa Tenzing Norgay at the center of the story. The filmmakers used real alpine environments across Nepal and New Zealand rather than green screens.

Nepal provided locations for Tenzing’s early life in the Lower Mustang, as well as the scenes in Darjeeling, Kathmandu, and Everest Base Camp. New Zealand’s Southern Alps, particularly the Aoraki region, doubled for Everest’s glaciers and icefalls, with the Darwin Icefall standing in for the notorious Khumbu Icefall.

Photographer Encounters Magnificent White Stag: Wildlife photographer Brian Johnston has captured incredible images of a rare white stag, believed to be an albino red deer. Johnston and a friend spotted the stag one windy morning during the rutting season, but it detected them and ran off.

Returning the following day, they found it asleep in a field, apparently exhausted after a night of rutting activity. Eventually, it stood up, giving Johnston the opportunity to photograph it.

Shark Attack Survivor Recounts Losing His Leg While Surfing: Australian firefighter and surfer Mel Ismail was surfing at Glenfield Beach on Australia’s west coast on September 14, when he was attacked by a shark. Sitting beyond the breaking waves, watching a pod of dolphins, Ismail initially thought a dolphin had bumped into his leg. Suddenly, he was dragged underwater and realized what was happening.

He desperately tried to push his fingers into the shark’s large black eye, and it released him. Ismail was bleeding heavily but managed to catch a wave toward shore. Another surfer spotted him and helped him onto the beach. Despite losing his leg below the knee, Ismail is determined to return to the water and start bodyboarding as soon as he can.

Canoe Dig It: Sam Dunning discovered freestyle canoeing during the 2020 lockdown after seeing a video of master paddler Marc Ornstein performing an interpretive routine to music. Amazed by the combination of technical skill and on-water choreography, the New York-based actor and filmmaker began writing Canoe Dig It?, a mockumentary inspired by comedies such as This Is Spinal Tap.

Winning the paddling community’s support was not easy, but at a freestyle canoeing symposium in Ohio, Dunning eventually met Ornstein. Ornstein and his wife agreed to be stunt doubles for the film.

Into Thin Air: This is a good background to the stories we’ve done on Kalie McCrystal, the 38-year-old skyrunner who disappeared on the Matterhorn on September 1. She met a Polish climber on her ascent, and theirs is the last footage of her. It shows her climbing towards the 4,478m summit. Shortly afterward, she was spotted descending the Italian side. Then she vanished.

Mountain rescuers believe she fell between 4,100m and 4,250m, but cannot establish exactly where or in which direction she fell. Despite a thorough search, no clothing, blood, or remains have been found. Her mother and sister eventually accepted that she had died on the mountain.

Climber Awarded Carnegie Medal After Rescuing Hang Glider Pilot: Matthew Clark has been awarded the Carnegie Medal for rescuing a hang glider pilot who crashed into a cliff on Vancouver Island, British Columbia.

Clark, a 24-year-old climber and paraglider, had launched from the same spot minutes apart. Clark had just landed when he heard the crash reported over his radio. Reaching the scene with other pilots, he found Parson suspended in his harness around 15m above the ground.

#4005. Scientists uncover 3 key brain pathways linked to lasting depression relief

📅 Oct 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Scientists at Mount Sinai have identified three important brain connections associated with long-term improvement in patients receiving deep brain stimulation (DBS) for severe depression that has not responded to conventional treatments. The discovery could help researchers make this experimental therapy more precise and effective.

Published October 1 in the American Journal of Psychiatry, the research examined the largest group of patients assembled across multiple independent cohorts to date who had received subcallosal cingulate deep brain stimulation (SCC DBS) for treatment-resistant depression.

The researchers found that lasting improvements were consistently associated with electrical stimulation reaching three specific white matter pathways: the cingulum bundle, forceps minor, and medial uncinate fasciculus/ventral amygdalofugal pathways.

These findings strengthen the case for using detailed maps of the brain's connections to determine exactly where electrodes should be implanted, rather than choosing their positions based primarily on anatomical landmarks. Known as connectome-based targeting, this strategy has already helped shape the ongoing Abbott TRANSCEND clinical trial, a randomized, controlled study that uses individualized brain connectivity maps to guide SCC DBS treatment.

"This study underscores the importance of individualized surgical targeting for achieving optimal outcomes with deep brain stimulation for depression," said Helen Mayberg, MD, Mount Sinai Professor in Neurotherapeutics and founding Director of the Nash Family Center for Advanced Circuit Therapeutics at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. "In any randomized controlled trial of a neuromodulation treatment, precise lead placement and confirmation that the intended brain target has been engaged are essential first steps. We still have much to learn about which patients are most likely to benefit from stimulation of this particular target, but developing imaging-based signatures that can match patients to their optimal treatment remains a central goal of our research."

How Deep Brain Stimulation Targets Severe Depression

For people with treatment-resistant depression, standard therapies have failed to provide adequate relief. Researchers are investigating deep brain stimulation as a possible treatment for individuals living with severe, persistent symptoms.

The procedure involves surgically placing electrodes inside the brain to deliver controlled electrical signals to circuits involved in depression. However, positioning the electrodes correctly remains one of the greatest challenges.

Electrical stimulation does not simply affect the brain tissue closest to an implanted electrode. It can also influence larger networks through the white matter pathways that connect different brain regions. As a result, two patients with electrodes implanted in nearly identical locations may experience very different outcomes depending on which connections are actually stimulated.

Earlier research from Mount Sinai and Emory University helped establish the importance of these networks. In studies published between 2014 and 2023 in Biological Psychiatry, Molecular Psychiatry, and Nature, the teams identified white matter pathways associated with successful SCC DBS treatment and developed a method for planning electrode placement using brain connectivity maps.

The latest investigation sought to determine whether those connections were also important in patients treated at other research centers. Scientists also wanted to understand whether differences in the pathways reached by stimulation could help account for patients who did not benefit.

Three Brain Connections Linked to Lasting Improvement

To investigate, the researchers examined information from several clinical studies, including the BROADEN trial and a patient cohort at the University of Toronto, where SCC DBS was originally developed.

They used computer models of the electrical fields generated by implanted electrodes alongside maps of brain connectivity. This allowed them to estimate which pathways received stimulation and compare those patterns across different patient groups.

The results showed a consistent relationship between sustained improvement and stimulation of the cingulum bundle, forceps minor, and medial uncinate fasciculus/ventral amygdalofugal pathways.

One finding was especially notable. Across the study sites, patients who had not responded to treatment after two years showed a lack of stimulation engagement with the left cingulum bundle.

The researchers did not investigate whether moving electrodes to reach this pathway would improve those patients' symptoms. Nevertheless, the pattern offers a possible explanation for why apparently similar electrode placements can produce different results. An electrode might be positioned in the correct general region but still fail to activate all the connections associated with lasting improvement.

"This study addresses an important question in DBS: not simply which area of the brain to stimulate, but which connections within that area need to be engaged," said Brian Kopell, MD, Director of the Center for Neuromodulation at Mount Sinai. "For a network disorder such as depression, millimeters can determine whether stimulation reaches the pathways we intend to modulate. Identifying and reproducibly engaging those pathways gives us a more precise surgical target and a framework for understanding why otherwise similar electrode placements may produce different clinical outcomes."

Toward More Personalized Depression Treatments

Although reaching these three pathways appears important, the researchers emphasize that electrode placement alone is unlikely to determine whether a patient improves.

The investigation concentrated on identifying the brain connections that should receive stimulation. Additional studies are needed to determine which individuals are most likely to benefit from SCC DBS and how electrical stimulation should be adjusted as treatment continues.

There were also limitations to the brain imaging data. Individual diffusion MRI scans, which can provide information about a person's white matter connections, were not available for every participant. Consequently, much of the analysis used generalized maps of brain connectivity rather than maps created specifically for each patient.

For the Emory patient group, the researchers were able to compare results obtained from generalized connectivity maps with those based on individual diffusion imaging. Both approaches produced consistent findings, providing further support for the importance of the identified pathways. However, more research is needed to establish the value of patient-specific imaging.

Future investigations will explore whether additional brain imaging measurements, including white matter integrity and functional connectivity, can help identify suitable patients, improve electrode placement, and guide treatment settings.

"Our findings show precisely engaging these white matter pathways is a critical first step toward sustained response," said Ki Sueng Choi, PhD, Associate Professor, Imaging Core Leader at the Center for Advanced Circuit Therapeutics at Mount Sinai, and corresponding author of the study. "The next step is to characterize abnormal functional and structural brain states along these pathways to build more detailed models for setting patient-specific stimulation parameters, including amplitude, frequency, and pulse width, for precision medicine."

By combining increasingly detailed brain maps with personalized stimulation settings, scientists hope to better understand why some people experience lasting relief while others do not. The findings represent another step toward developing more targeted treatments for severe depression, although further clinical research is necessary to establish how much these approaches can improve outcomes.

I’m a big believer that you don’t have to spend much money to have a beautiful garden. I often repurpose containers and other household items outside, but one of my favorite ways to recycle is to return food waste to the earth. Each fall, I bury kitchen scraps in my empty raised beds using a mix of ingredients I’ve found that makes an ideal free soil improver. The scraps break down in place over winter, adding organic matter to the soil before spring planting.

This on-the-go trench composting method doesn't require a compost bin, but I keep a countertop caddy, similar to this best-selling Oxo Good Grips Bin on Amazon, in my kitchen and add suitable scraps as I go. Every few days, I empty it into a shallow trench in one of my beds, then move on to another spot. I keep going until the ground freezes.

You don’t need every ingredient in my mix; simply leave out anything you don’t have. I can’t recommend adding other ingredients in their place, though. Think of it as an easy, personal way to put kitchen scraps to work in the garden, rather than a precise recipe.

Fruit and vegetable scraps are the main ingredient and make up the bulk volume of the mix. I save things like banana, citrus, and cucumber peels, apple cores, carrot tops, and other uncooked trimmings. As they break down, they return organic matter and some of their nutrients to the soil.

I chop larger or tougher scraps into pieces about 1 inch (2.5 cm) long. Smaller pieces are easier to mix into the soil and should break down more readily. I avoid scraps covered in oil, sauce, or dressing, and I never add any meat, fish, bones, or dairy to the mix.

I add about 1 cup of used coffee grounds to a batch of scraps. Grounds contain small amounts of nutrients and organic compounds, but despite what you may have seen in viral gardening videos, they need time to break down before the bed is ready for planting.

I let the grounds cool first, then stir them through the scraps so they don’t clump together. I keep the amount modest: coffee grounds are best used in moderation.

Eggshells are one of the most widely used food scraps in gardening. For my mix, I save the shells from a couple of eggs, rinse off any residue, let them dry, and crush them as finely as I can – with a blender or pestle and mortar, like this Gorilla Grip design on Amazon.

Eggshells contain calcium, although they break down slowly, so I don’t expect them to act like a quick calcium feed. I add them because they’re easy to save and can eventually become part of the soil.

Many gardeners also sprinkle eggshells around plants in the hope of deterring soft-bodied pests such as slugs. I don’t rely on them for pest control, though, so I crush mine and add them to the mix instead of scattering them around plants.

While not technically a scrap, I add a small handful of plain rolled oats – about ¼ cup – to my recipe. Oatmeal is grain-based, so it’ll break down into organic matter in the soil, adding lots of nutrients. Oats are also believed to help stimulate root growth, as they contain phosphorus and feed beneficial mycorrhizal fungi in the soil.

I add a little cinnamon to my mix for its much-touted properties as a natural fungicide, rooting hormone, and pest deterrent. These uses are popular with gardeners, although I wouldn’t rely on a small amount buried in the soil to solve an existing problem. You don’t need much – around ½ teaspoon is plenty for my mix.

If I have some flat beer left over, I add a small splash – about ½ cup – to my caddy. Beer contains yeast and other organic compounds, which is why some gardeners use it in compost or homemade plant feeds. I don't know whether it makes a measurable difference to my soil, so I wouldn't open a bottle specifically, but it’s a way to use up a little leftover beer rather than pour it down the drain.

Once my caddy is at least half full, I take it out to an empty bed. I dig a trench about 12–18 inches (30–45 cm) deep, add the scraps, and mix them with some of the soil, then I fill the trench back in. I make sure the food waste is fully covered, with at least 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) of soil above it, so that it doesn't attract animal pests.

One key point is that I don’t try to fill the whole bed with one enormous batch. As a rough guide, I use about 1 gallon (4 L) of mixed scraps for each 4-by-4-foot (1.2-by-1.2-m) bed, spread along a short trench. If I have any leftovers, I bury the rest in another empty spot or save it for another trench. Alternatively, buy a cheap compost bin, like the Vevor small compost tumbler from Home Depot, and start building that up as you go.

The exact amount isn’t a strict recipe. I keep the layer of scraps fairly modest, chop everything small, and cover it well. I avoid digging close to established plants, whose roots could be disturbed, and don’t bury food waste in beds where I plan to plant immediately.

Kitchen scraps are moist and break down more easily when mixed with some dry material. If I have them, I add a couple of handfuls of shredded leaves to my trench batch. They contribute carbon and help keep the scraps from clumping together.

I sometimes add a small handful of fresh grass clippings, too. They’re nitrogen-rich, but I use them sparingly and mix them through the scraps and leaves so they don’t form a dense, soggy layer. Both additions are optional home composting extras: I just use what’s available in the garden.

By spring, the scraps will have had several months to break down. When I’m ready to plant, I check the area first. If I can still see recognizable pieces, I leave that part of the bed alone for a little longer and plant elsewhere. If the soil looks settled and the scraps have mostly disappeared, I loosen the surface and get the bed ready for planting.

I don’t expect this one mix to replace compost or a soil test, and I don’t use it as a precise fertilizer. For me, it’s a simple way to make use of kitchen leftovers and add them back to an empty garden bed while there’s time for them to break down before spring.

#3139. The Only Way to Truly Hunt the Fall Migration Is to Follow It

📅 Oct 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 18, 2026 10:18 AM EDT

It’s dark and raining on the Canadian prairie. Most sane people are still tucked in bed or just putting on a pot of coffee. But the group of us getting soaked to the bone setting out decoys and brushing in A-frame blinds in a cut pea field are all certifiable. We’re jonesing for cupped wings, and the only way to get our fix is to wake up before the ducks and geese fly from their roost waters to these grain fields to feed.

The morning is full of the usual anticipation, plus an extra helping. That’s because we’re hunting one of the oldest birds in the world: the sandhill crane, a flying, 4-foot-tall velociraptor with a wingspan of up to 7 feet. At first light their distinctive purrs break the prairie stillness, and we are all now very awake. Their razor-sharp talons dwarf the spurs of any 3-year-old tom. Sandhills have long and pointed beaks, and they aren’t afraid to spear you or your retriever with them.

The first cranes glide into the decoys. Some of us have never hunted them before, but even we can tell that once the sandhills commit, there’s no backpedaling. They are big and clumsy and can’t gain altitude fast enough as we raise our shotguns in unison. Most of the birds tumble from the sky in a tangle of wings and legs, but two sail into the peas. Our guide, Dusty Brown, doesn’t hesitate to stomp through the muddy Saskatchewan stubble toward one of them. The bird spouts a vicious hiss and spreads its wings.

But Brown gives the crane a swift boot right in the chest and it flops back. He finishes it quickly. He has to get the crane to the ground to kill it and protect himself and his Lab, Briley. The second, less experienced crane guide is far more tentative, dancing around the second sandhill like a nervous boxer in the opening round of a fight.

“The first time one stood up and hissed at me, I did the same thing,” says Brown, who has since hunted cranes in Texas, season after season. “I wasn’t sure if it was going to attack me or my dog, and so I was hesitant.”

His experience is the product of chasing the annual waterfowl migration from Canada south to the oil fields of Texas and back north in spring for the conservation snow goose order. The 50-year-old guide has been doing this for more than 20 years. Most core duck hunters undertake similar journeys at some point in their careers, starting in the Prairie Pothole Region in September and calling it in the South come January, stopping at points along the way to intercept ducks and geese.

“When the lakes and rivers freeze up, that’s the end of the season for a lot of hunters because the birds move on,” says Brown. “But for guys like me, it’s just another stop along the way. We never want our season to end. So we hitch the trailer, hop in our trucks, and follow the birds.”

Like so many duck hunters who live a migratory lifestyle, Brown has been infatuated with waterfowl from an early age. A friend’s dad took him on his first hunt, floating a garbage can full of decoys into the marsh, dumping them out, and then coming back for the two boys. They both climbed into the can and used it as a blind while his friend’s father shot ducks. During high school, Brown worked for his dad, slinging pizzas in the bustling flyway of Oregon’s Willamette Valley. He saved all the money he made slicing pepperoni to lease farm ground along the Pudding River. It flooded every year, creating seasonal wetlands for ducks.

The hunting along the river was good enough to last a decade. Brown didn’t start living on the road until his late 20s, when he began buying every goose call he could get his hands on.

“The calls really intrigued me, but I didn’t know anything about contest calling because the world championships in Stuttgart, Arkansas, were 2,000 miles away,” Brown says. “I won the Oregon state goose calling contest in 2001 and became a goose guide at 27 years old.”

That win led to competition calling at contests across the country. Back then you had to listen to live callers to improve (YouTube didn’t exist yet), and that meant traveling from state to state, learning from the best along the way.

By then, waterfowling had Brown firmly in its grasp, and he only wanted to hunt more and see new places. That urge took him up and down the Pacific and Central flyways. The same thing happens to a lot of duck hunters: the early mornings, feathers cutting through wind, tricking birds into the decoys. Some hunters can never get enough, and that’s Brown. He can’t stop. And he doesn’t want to.

“You see a lot of guides who are single because they have devoted their lives to this and a relationship doesn’t play into it,” says Angie Erickson, who hunts with Brown at her Curly Tail camp in Saskatchewan in the fall and spring. “It happened to me. Once you start, it’s an addiction. I hate to call it that, but that’s what it is. I have young twin boys and had to pull the reins in. I was consumed with setting spreads and fooling birds, and I was not spending enough time with my family. When I stopped, it was like going through detox.”

That’s the sacrifice this kind of duck hunting requires. You can lose yourself in the migration, and it pulls many hunters away from the folks they love most. It’s hard to watch hunters with more freedom continue to the next duck camp while you head home and take care of your responsibilities. It can cause jealousy, test friendships, and even end marriages. Brown has seen it happen time and again. It’s the reason, he says, he’s never married and never had children. Because he made his choice years ago, he doesn’t have to choose each season.

Brown won’t be able to pass the knowledge he’s gained as a guide down to a son or daughter, but he does share his expertise with any hunter who’s willing to listen. Unlike some guides who talk at their clients, Brown cares enough to teach his something new. Because odds are they’re not as talented as callers and can’t read birds like Brown.

“Dusty has become one of my best friends in this entire world,” says Hunter Pickett, who began guiding with Brown in Canada and Texas when he was 21. “He’s helped me personally become a better guide and given me the knowledge to kill birds consistently so I can keep doing this for a living. I wake up at 4 a.m. eight or nine months out of the year to go hunt. That’s a great life for a duck hunter.”

Brown’s lessons aren’t patronizing either. When I hunted cranes with him and that handful of other hunters, it felt like old friends sharing a morning together—the way duck hunting ought to always be.

Many guides who travel as much as Brown eventually settle somewhere. They cut back on the number of days they hunt or open outfitting businesses of their own. If Brown has one regret, it’s that he has never successfully worked for himself.

“We never want our season to end. So we hitch the trailer, hop in our trucks, and follow the birds.”

He did try to open his own guide service once in Alberta, with a friend. After years in transit—he’s gone through three Cummins diesel truck engines, which last up to 350,000 miles apiece, in the last two decades—Brown put down stakes. But it was bad timing. Just as the 2001 waterfowl season kicked off, terrorists flew into the World Trade Center. Air travel was suspended for only a few days, but new flight restrictions and fear of another attack caused clients to cancel, and the business went under.

It was a major financial hit for Brown, and it understandably soured him on ever owning another guide service. The COVID-19 pandemic only solidified his stance on never taking that chance again as he watched many Canadian outfitters shutter their businesses due to the extended U.S.-Canada border closure.

“I’ve always worked for someone else,” Brown says. “I think that if I could have started a business here in Oregon, that’s what I would have done. But I just never felt comfortable doing that because we don’t have the bird numbers to make it sustainable.”

Still, not owning an outfit has allowed Brown the freedom to move on when he’s ready for the next adventure. He can’t help that the migration is in his blood. Even at 50, he continues to follow the birds as they make stops along their traditional routes while exploring new spots as migration patterns shift. Brown shows no signs of slowing down.

“I still get excited as we come out of summer and the cooler months are upon us,” Brown says. “You start to see fields full of geese in late August, pay attention to how they act, and I just can’t wait to get on the road—to be there when the first birds come to the grain fields of Canada.”

Is there anything more magical than spotting a bright red cardinal among the branches of your backyard?

Honestly, I'm not so sure that there is; there's a reason we all love these beautifully vibrant birds so much, and it's not just because they've become synonymous with the holidays (a study back in 2022 determined that 34% of the birds featured on Christmas cards are cardinals, if you don't believe me!). They are laden with symbolism, and many believe that if they're the first bird you spot on January 1st, you're owed 12 months of love, hope, optimism, and good luck for the year ahead.

Now, these beautiful songbirds are year-round residents across much of the eastern and central United States, and with the right planting choices, you can make your garden a more inviting place for them to visit. So, how best to go about it?

The secret to charming these birds into your yard? Think beyond bird feeders and give cardinals more of what they naturally need: food, shelter, and places to nest.

Now, cardinals eat a varied diet of seeds, fruit, and insects, and they particularly favor gardens with plenty of dense vegetation where they can hide from predators and forage safely. If you want to make your garden more appealing to cardinals, hawthorn is a great tree to consider. Depending on the species, these trees produce small fruits often called haws, which can provide food for birds into the cooler months. Their dense, sometimes thorny branches also offer useful shelter for wildlife.

Crabapple trees are another excellent option for a cardinal-friendly garden. Their spring blossoms bring seasonal color, while the small fruits that follow can become an important food source for birds in fall and winter.

True lilies are some of the most striking, fragrant, and elegant flowers in the garden. As fall continues, those magnificent blooms may fade, but underground, their leaves and bulbs are working overtime to prepare for winter. October is a critical time when lily bulbs are drawing in late-season nutrients, developing healthy roots, and storing vital energy to push up towering stems next year.

Before diving into autumn chores, make sure you clarify which type of lily you need to care for, and distinguish true lilies (Lilium) from daylilies (Hemerocallis). Daylilies grow from dense, fibrous roots and die back quickly, while true lilies grow from fleshy bulbs. These fall tasks focus on early-blooming Asiatic hybrids, fragrant Oriental lilies (like 'Stargazer'), majestic Trumpet lilies, and robust Orienpet (OT) hybrids.

These simple October tasks outlined here ensure maximum winter survival, smart disease resistance, and better flower development next time. Because most true lilies are cold-hardy, they can remain safely in the ground (just a few tender types, or lilies in very cold climates, benefit from winter storage). By giving your lily beds a little expert attention now, you set the stage for an explosive, colorful display next year.

Before getting stuck into lily care, give some thought to your USDA hardiness zone and climate, as well as specific varieties. Because specific lily classes mature at different rates through late summer, individual plants in borders may sit at varying stages of dormancy in October. Early Asiatic varieties may have dropped their leaves weeks ago, while tall Oriental and Orienpet hybrids might hold green foliage into fall.

In addition to managing existing lily beds, October is a great time to introduce new lily bulbs. Soil temperatures remain warm enough for fresh roots to establish before deep winter freezes arrive. At the same time, growers in severe northern climates (zones 2–3) or those growing tender, exotic species like Formosa lilies can lift and store bulbs for protection.

It’s a good idea to invest in a soil probe that can give you a reliable idea of light and moisture levels in the ground at this time. A diagnostic tool like the Raintrip 4-in-1 Soil Meter, available from Amazon, can give you a heads-up about conditions around your lilies before you start. Checking can help you clarify root moisture, soil pH, and temperature levels before you mulch or plant new bulbs.

Whether you are tending established clumps, adding new varieties, or lifting tender bulbs for winter, these simple October tasks will ensure your lilies transition smoothly into dormancy. Following these quick checks this month gives you the best preparation for healthier plants and bigger blooms next summer.

Removing spent plant material is important for maintaining a tidy bed but also for plant health and preventing infections. Fallen leaves and spent lily foliage can harbor destructive fungal spores, most notably lily blight, as well as other pathogens. Collect and dispose of yellowing or dropped leaves as they loosen from the main stems. Any foliage showing dark spots, water-soaked streaks, or powdery mildew should be bagged and placed in the trash (not the compost pile) to prevent spreading disease.

Additionally, snip off any seed heads that form after flowering finishes. Unless you are intentionally saving seed for propagation, developing seed pods drain massive amounts of energy away from the bulb. Removing seed capsules redirects resources back down into the bulb, strengthening reserves for winter. Always use clean, sharp snips when removing seed heads, and wipe down the blades before and after use. Bonide Captain Jack’s Ready-to-Use Neem Oil Spray, available from Walmart, is great for sanitizing tool blades as well as treating beds against fungal spores.

This is an excellent opportunity to check remaining stems and leaf joints for signs of damage or insect activity, like spots or holes. Aphids, red lily leaf beetles, and scale insects frequently deposit overwintering eggs close to the soil line. Removing old stem tissue and clearing away leaf clutter can eliminate cozy pest harbors. To help you get a closer look, grab a magnifying glass or a loupe like the Carson LumiLoupe 10x Stand Magnifier from Amazon. This can help assess leaves or bulb scales for hidden mites, thrips, or early fungal rot.

If you are dividing or lifting tender varieties for indoor storage, inspect bulbs closely. Healthy lily bulbs should feel firm, plump, and heavy, with crisp white, yellow, or pinkish scales. Discard any that feel soft, squishy, or show signs of blue mold or basal rot. If you notice rot in multiple bulbs, it might be a sign of poor soil drainage. Correct this in October by working some coarse grit or perlite into the planting area.

While it is tempting to clear away all stems early to keep beds tidy, patience is essential – and timing is crucial. As long as a lily stem holds green leaves, it is actively performing photosynthesis, converting sunlight into carbohydrates and pumping energy down into the bulb. So while parts of the plant are working, it’s best to leave lilies as long as they are healthy. Cutting back green stems prematurely starves the bulb, resulting in stunted growth and fewer flowers the following season.

Wait until the foliage has turned yellow, brown or papery before cutting back. Once the stem is fully dead, use bypass pruners to cut the stalk down to about 2-3 inches (5-8cm) above the ground. Leaving a short stem stub helps mark the bulb's location. Dispose of dead stalks promptly, as hollow, decaying stems left standing over winter make ideal overwintering sites for pests and spores. After cutting back, add a gentle organic phosphorus booster to the soil. A little Down to Earth Organic Fish Bone Meal from Lowe’s around trimmed bases can encourage root development.

This is a chore many gardeners can skip, as most lilies are hardy in most areas of North America. Asiatic, Oriental, and Trumpet lilies are hardy in zones 4-9, but if you are in zones 2-3 or growing tender exotic species, it’s smart to lift bulbs in October. Once foliage dies back, carefully lift using a garden fork, taking care not to nick the bulbs. Brush off loose soil and allow the bulbs to air-dry in a cool room for 24 hours. Store them in cardboard boxes layered with slightly damp peat moss, wood shavings or vermiculite. Keep in a cool, dark location, around 40-45°F (4-7°C).

October is also a great time to plant new hardy lilies. Autumn soil retains summer warmth, allowing fresh bulbs to build strong root systems. Plant bulbs pointy side up at a depth 2-3 times the height of the bulb, 6-8 inches (15-20cm) deep. Choose a sunny site with well-draining soil, mixing a little organic compost or bone meal into the bottom of the hole. Add a gentle root tonic rather than a heavy dedicated feed. Go for a light soil drench with a seaweed or kelp tonic like GS Plant Foods Organic Liquid Kelp from Walmart. This is rich in trace minerals to help develop roots.

Bulbs that stay in the ground over winter benefit from a little protection, even if they are hardy in your growing zone. Mulching insulates the soil, moderates temperature changes, and reduces the risk of frost-related damage. Once beds are cleared and trimmed, apply a layer of mulch. If you’re looking for a great mulch for lilies, I recommend Back to the Roots Organic Premium Wood Mulch from Amazon. This dye-free organic bark mulch insulates bulbs, while suppressing early weeds. You can also use shredded bark, evergreen boughs or clean straw.

As well as insulating the soil against temperature swings, mulching prevents damage by freeze-thaw cycles. These can cause frost heaving, which is where shifting soil pops bulbs up to the surface. Wait until the ground cools down (or after the first hard freeze) before applying a 2-4-inch (5-10cm) blanket of your mulch. Don’t pack it too tightly or thickly, though, as this can trap moisture over the bulbs.

Finally, be sure to mark the exact locations of your buried lily bulbs. True lilies emerge relatively late in spring, and it is dangerously easy to forget exactly where they are and to slice through dormant bulbs in spring. Drive durable wooden or plastic garden stakes into the ground besides your trimmed stem stubs or fresh plantings. Taking a quick photo on your smartphone can also help to create a visual map that prevents accidental digging when spring arrives.

These well-timed tasks will help your lily bulbs survive the winter and thrive next spring with vigorous growth and abundant, majestic blooms. The most important priorities are to leave green foliage intact until it dies back naturally, clear away dead plant debris, and protect underground bulbs while ensuring excellent soil drainage for any additions. Take a little time to tend your lily beds well this fall, and you can enjoy an unforgettable display of lovely lilies next summer.

Red, yellow, and orange barricades don't always mean the same thing. Learn what common safety colors signal and when to stay clear.

Yellow caution tape is a pretty familiar sight, whether it’s blocking off a wet floor or keeping people out of a construction area. Orange barriers along the highway and red warning signs at job sites are just as common. But barricade colors aren’t random, and they don’t all mean the same thing.

In the U.S., OSHA has rules for certain workplace safety colors, while ANSI provides guidelines for signs and warning tape. Road construction follows a different set of standards. Depending on the location, the same color might serve a different purpose.

Learn what the most common barricade colors mean, where people are likely to see them, and what to watch out for.

There’s a reason red is a common choice for warning signs. OSHA uses it to identify danger, emergency stops, and fire protection equipment. At a construction site, a red “Danger” sign might warn of exposed electrical equipment or another hazard that could cause serious injury. OSHA’s general-industry rules also call for red warning lights at barricades and temporary obstructions.

Of all the safety colors, yellow might be the one people see most often. It shows up on caution tape, warning signs, and around areas where crews are working. OSHA uses yellow to warn of physical hazards, including falls and falling objects. A damaged walkway or an area undergoing maintenance might have yellow tape around it.

There’s no shortage of orange around construction sites. On workplace warning signs, the color often identifies hazards that could cause serious injury, particularly around machinery or equipment undergoing repairs. Orange also makes road construction easier to spot. Crews use orange barriers and signs to warn drivers about upcoming work, lane changes, or other temporary traffic conditions.

Instead of warning people away from something, it usually helps them find safety equipment. A green sign might point toward a first aid station, identify emergency equipment, or provide instructions for workers. It’s especially useful in workplaces where people need to find these resources quickly.

Blue signs typically provide information about workplace equipment or procedures that don’t directly involve a hazard. For example, a blue notice might explain that a machine is temporarily out of service. Unlike red or yellow warnings, blue notices generally provide information rather than warn of immediate danger.

Purple isn’t a color people encounter very often on construction barricades, but it does have a specific safety use. OSHA requires certain radiation warning signs to feature the familiar radiation symbol in purple or magenta against a yellow background. These signs warn people about radiation hazards, including areas where access may be restricted.

Those orange-and-white striped barricades lining roads and highways aren’t quite the same as the warning signs used inside workplaces. The Federal Highway Administration has separate rules for these barriers, which crews use to close lanes, block roads, and direct traffic around construction.

There’s also a purpose behind the diagonal stripes. When a barricade directs drivers around an obstruction, the stripes slope down toward the side where traffic should pass.
