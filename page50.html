#3759. Inside the Craft of Woven Whisky Branding by Freytag Anderson

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Freytag Anderson built Woven whisky branding around one idea: blending as weaving, from a frosted apothecary bottle to a graphic for every blend.

Freytag Anderson's clients, Pete and Duncan, came with a clear brief: shake up the blended whisky category and make it more open, more human, and far less prescriptive. The studio's answer starts with the name. Woven speaks to blending as threads coming together to make something richer, and the positioning, Experience Whisky, treats the drink as something to try rather than something to be instructed in. The core range stays pared back and quiet, while each limited edition gets its own graphic, so the shelf reads as one brand with many moods. The case page notes an IWSC win for Hemisphere.

The wordmark does most of the talking. WOVEN sits in widely spaced capitals directly on the frosted brown glass. On the core labels the release name sits centered in the middle of the label, Homemade in muted grey green, Superblend in warm cream, Hemispheres in black scattered with fine dots. Below each name, the same small spec columns run across the foot of the label: non chill filtered, Woven Whisky Makers, the whisky type, and the volume. A small zigzag notch cut into the foot of every label echoes the woven thread idea, and the same mark turns up as a wide white zigzag band wrapped around the house blend bottles. The limited editions break the quiet. Each one is numbered and named, Experience N.11 Peachy, N.12 Echoes, N.13 Catalyst, N.14 Kaleidoscopic, and each gets a graphic that belongs to that blend alone: a soft peach gradient, overlapping blue circles, red diamonds, a grid of colored dots. The labels keep the same centered layout and the same small spec columns, so the wild graphics never break the system, and the grey board boxes follow the same discipline, with the spaced wordmark and a single centered label. The box top repeats the line The Worldwide Whisky Makers in a circle around Woven.

Material is doing as much work as print here. The custom bottle is frosted brown apothecary glass, tactile and unlike a standard whisky bottle, closed with a plain black cap on the core range. The grey board gift boxes hold the bottles with a black Experience Whisky inner box, and the tasting set packs three small bottles with a printed leaflet carrying flavor charts for each blend. Nothing on the shelf shouts. The frosted glass, the quiet board, and the restrained labels let the one loud element, the graphic of each limited edition, do all the talking.

#3955. This Vitamin Reduces Depression Symptoms By 50%

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

One vitamin reduced depression symptoms after a month, but the benefit depended on how participants felt at the start.

One vitamin reduced depression symptoms after a month, but the benefit depended on how participants felt at the start.

Taking a high dose of vitamin B6 for a month reduced depression symptoms in people already experiencing them.

The vitamin helps the brain produce chemical messengers involved in mood regulation.

The findings suggest a potential role for B6 in treating depression, although larger clinical trials are needed.

Almost 270 adults, mostly young women, took part in the research.

They were randomly assigned to take either 100 mg of vitamin B6, vitamin B12 or a placebo each day for around a month.

Among almost 60 people with at least mild depression symptoms at the start, those taking B6 improved more than those receiving B12 or a placebo.

Average depression scores fell by about 54 percent in the symptomatic B6 group, compared with 24 percent in the placebo group.

The benefit remained after accounting for differences in how severe their symptoms had been at the start.

The findings come from a new analysis of data collected in the same research programme that previously reported reduced anxiety after B6 supplementation.

Vitamin B6 supports several processes involved in regulating mood.

Dr David Field, the study’s first author, said:

“Vitamin B6 plays a crucial role in the production of several chemical messengers in the brain, including serotonin and GABA, both of which are central to mood regulation.”

GABA helps restrain nerve cell activity, contributing to the balance between excitation and inhibition in the brain.

Increasing B6 availability could boost their production, potentially explaining the improvement in depression symptoms.

Inflammation may also play a role, potentially affecting both depression and how the body uses B6.

Vitamin B6 occurs naturally in foods including poultry, fish, chickpeas, bananas and fortified cereals.

However, the trial tested a high supplement dose, rather than a change in everyday food intake.

High doses of B6 can cause peripheral nerve damage, so the researchers say supplementation at these doses requires medical supervision.

Larger trials now need to establish the most effective dose, monitor safety and determine whether B6 could help alongside existing depression treatments.

The study was published in the journal Depression and Anxiety (Field et al., 2026).

#1150. Raw Denim vs. Regular Denim: Which Is Actually Worth Buying?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Here’s what to know about both kinds of jeans.

Our product picks are editor-tested, expert-approved. We may earn a commission through links on our site. Why Trust Us?

I COME FROM the denim era where streetwear juggernauts like FUBU and Rocawear reigned supreme, along with mall favorites like Abercrombie & Fitch and Hollister. I was in my preteens rocking all these clothing brands and you couldn’t tell me their baggy-stacking-at-the-shoe jeans weren’t the flyest on the market. Since I began working as a fashion editor, I’ve practically worn every pair of jeans in the denim universe. Slim, relaxed, stretch, washed, distressed—you name it, I’ve probably tried it. I’ve even tried my hand at denim-making in Japan, the mecca of old-school, high-quality denim, where I produced my own pair of selvedge jeans. Denim is one of those rare items in a guy’s wardrobe that never goes out of style.

And after years of being in the menswear space, I’ve gotten familiar with the nuances that distinguish one kind of denim from the next. Two worth knowing are regular denim and raw denim. Raw denim usually looks darker, stiffer, and more uniform in color, while regular denim has a softer feel and a more broken-in appearance with visible fading or whiskering from factory washing and finishing. Those differences also affect how each pair feels when you first put it on and how it changes over the years. Here’s what to know about both and which one deserves a spot in your rotation.

Regular jeans are usually made by weaving cotton yarn into denim, then cutting fabric into patterns and sewing them together to create the finished garment. These jeans have already gone through washing or finishing treatments to soften the fabric and give them that broken-in look, so you don’t have to spend weeks waiting for them to feel comfortable. If I want to throw on a pair of jeans and get on with my day, this is the kind I’m reaching for. I also like that you can find them in practically every fit and wash, whether you’re after a clean, dark indigo pair or something faded and lived-in. Most everyday options from brands like Levi’s and Wrangler run roughly $40 to $100, while designer and premium styles can cost considerably more. For me, prewashed denim makes the most sense when you want that comfortable feel from the jump. There’s nothing wrong with skipping the break-in period and getting straight to the good part.

I like raw denim because you get to see how jeans change the more you wear them. Unlike prewashed jeans, raw denim typically hasn’t been washed after the dyeing and manufacturing process, so it typically arrives stiff, dark, and pretty rigid. One thing to watch out for, though, is dye transfer. Indigo can rub off onto lighter-colored clothes, shoes, and other materials, including suede. I’ve had this happen before, so I'd be mindful of what you wear with a fresh pair. But that’s part of the appeal for denim enthusiasts. As you wear it, the fabric softens and develops fades and creases based on how you move, sit, and live in your jeans. You’re essentially breaking them in yourself, which makes the finished look feel uniquely yours. Prices can start around $60 to $100 for entry-level options and climb to $150 to $300 or more for premium denim, particularly Japanese-made styles. I’ll admit, the break-in period takes some patience, but if you enjoy watching your jeans evolve over time, raw denim is worth considering.

Raw denim and selvedge denim get lumped together all the time, but they actually refer to different things. Raw denim describes the fabric’s finishing state: It’s typically sold without a post-production wash that softens the fabric or creates a faded look. Selvedge, on the other hand, refers to how the denim is woven, creating a clean, self-finished edge you can often spot by rolling up the cuff. You can have raw selvedge denim, washed selvedge denim, or raw denim that isn’t selvedge at all. Having made my own pair of selvedge jeans in Japan, I’ve seen firsthand how the construction differs. Once you know what to look for, the distinction is pretty easy to spot.

At the end of the day, I don’t think you need to drop $300 on raw denim to dress well. After years of working in menswear, I’ve learned that a higher price tag doesn’t automatically make something the right choice for your wardrobe. I’ve seen guys look just as good in an affordable pair of Levi’s as they do in premium Japanese denim. What matters more is finding comfortable jeans that fit your body and work with the clothes you already own. I’m always going to appreciate the quality craftsmanship behind a great pair of raw denim, but there’s no single right way to wear jeans. I think every guy should have one dependable pair, whether that’s raw or prewashed. But ultimately, like most things in style, it comes down to personal preference—and knowing what works for you.

#3770. This Mouse Has a Built-In Touchscreen and Customizable App Controls

This Mouse Has a Built-In Touchscreen and Customizable App Controls

Arctic Fox Technologies has announced Super Mouse, a productivity-focused, screen-equipped mouse with built-in, customizable app shortcuts.

As Arctic Fox explains, the computer mouse has employed a relatively consistent form and function for many decades. Peripherals have gotten better and more precise, of course, but the core functionality has remained static. The company hopes to change that with Super Mouse.

Super Mouse is part mouse and part software hub. It integrates software shortcuts, macros, application controls, and other common computer actions directly into the hardware while still serving as a traditional mouse.

“The way people work has completely changed, but the mouse has stayed almost the same for decades,” says Arctic Fox Technologies’ founder and CEO, Sridhar Thirunakara. “We wanted to create a device that keeps the simplicity and comfort people already know while giving them direct access to the tools they use every day.”

The Super Mouse has a built-in 3.1-inch 720 x 720-pixel touchscreen that lets users access a control panel, digital keyboard, and on-screen sliders, such as for photo editing. It includes things like a quick-glance Google Calendar and app shortcuts.

It also includes two customizable physical keys, called Hyper Keys, near the scroll wheel. The mouse also includes a built-in microphone for voice-to-text AI functionality and a gyro for additional control options.

The Super Mouse works alongside a dedicated app, Flow, where users can configure their shortcuts, app integrations, display layouts, and more. There’s no limit to how many apps can have configured shortcuts.

Super Mouse is a wired mouse that connects via USB-C. It works on both macOS and Windows.

The new device is launching first on Kickstarter, with early-bird pricing starting at $199, a 50% discount compared to the expected MSRP. The Super Mouse comes in yellow, white, and dark gray. Arctic Fox says the first units will ship to backers in February 2027 and notes it has been testing prototypes for over a year. However, as always, Kickstarter backers incur risk.

#4383. ZacOS 9 and Mavernix: Two classic Apple desktops find their Wayland to Linux

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Classic Platinum or lickable Mavericks-era Aqua – it's your choice

You wait decades for a Mac-like desktop for Linux, and then two of them come along at once. AI might be involved, but even so, it's still shiny.

ZacOS 9 and Mavernix are two very new, independent, and separate projects, but there is a common thread linking them: they are both brand new efforts to bring vintage Macintosh-like desktop environments to Linux. One is explicitly built using LLMs, and we suspect it was probably involved with the other as well.

"ZacOS" is just a couple of weeks old, and both to forestall what is becoming a very frequently asked question, and also to credit to its inventor Adam Lawson, the project's GitHub page acknowledges two of the more visible LLM coding assistances: both Claude and Copilot are there. In the Hacker News discussion, he acknowledged that he used LLM assistance to make the software, and even his online comments.

It aims to recreate the “Platinum” appearance of MacOS 8 and 9 – as we described last month, one of the few things that Apple successfully salvaged from the Copland project to build a true multitasking successor to MacOS 7.

ZacOS includes a window manager, a filer, a global menu bar, a Control Panel app, a Control Strip, desk accessories and other additional apps, and more. It can network with Macs, and integrate with the existing SheepShaver emulator to run classic Mac apps. We previously mentioned SheepShaver when looking at the remarkable MacOS9.app web-based emulator, and if you don’t remember what it looked like, that’s the easiest way to find out.

The Sourceforge downloads contain a complete pre-installed Debian Linux image. On Github, there are much smaller Debian packages if you want to try installing it yourself. It's an all-new environment, based on Wayland. (Its own icons are based on Lawson's dog, Zac.) As far as we can see, ZacOS doesn't draw on any older MacOS-like code.

That's slightly surprising, because this is not the first project of its kind that we have ever seen. Although ZacOS does look to be one of the more complete ones, there's quite a bit of prior art.

"Once upon a time, a very long time ago now, about last Friday," (as A. A. Milne put it, a century ago this month) – or at least, a few months before OpenAI launched ChatGPT – we mourned the lack of Linux desktop diversity. In that article, we linked to some projects that tried to bring a Mac-like UI to Linux.

That included Ryu Kojiro’s classic-wm, which is a Mac System 6-styled window manager, and also Morgan Aldridge’s mlvwm, which aims at the later grayscale MacOS 7 and MacOS 8 look. Although it targets the modern Unix-based macOS, we also mentioned Classic Mac Finder, which aims to recreate the older Finder on the modern OS.

A more modest but easier approach would start with one of the more versatile desktops out there. As we covered in our two-part workshop last month, that would be Xfce, which can already emulate the macOS desktop layout and with a few extra tweaks make a very usable alternative. We intentionally didn’t look at themes and skins, but they do exist, and one of the most thorough is what “Grassmunk” did with the Platinum9 theme for Xubuntu. Although Windows 95 itself is long obsolete, the Chicago95 theme makes Xfce look remarkably like the classic OS, and Chicago's developers still actively maintain it.

There is a lot of nostalgia for the Classic MacOS look and feel out there, as websites such as MacOS 9 Lives! shows. However, there’s also nostalgia for how Mac OS X used to look, as well. When we looked at how to Bring back your old Mac a year ago, we mentioned Mavericks Forever – a fan site for the last OS X version to have the “lickable” Aqua look.

Mavericks is notable because it was the first release of OS X that was available completely for free, as The Register reported in 2013. It’s not easy to find today, but you can still download it.

The creator of Mavernix demonstrates it on Youtube

We mention this because you will need a copy if you want to try the second new vintage Apple OS remake. It’s called Mavernix, and for now, it’s only available for Arch Linux.

Unlike ZacOS 9, Mavernix is built on top of existing software: its windows are drawn by the Wayfire compositor, and its panels by Quickshell. So, yes, this too is entirely Wayland-based.

The main novelty in Mavernix is that it contains a tool to extract and use the images, icons, textures, wallpapers and so on from a real copy of Apple OS X, so that it can draw a Mac-like desktop using real Mac resources. To use it, you need to have a copy of OS X to hand – which is why we point out where you can get one. The license agreement prohibits running it on non-Apple hardware, but since this does not require running it at all, we don’t see any IP violations here.

Mavernix is by “Pruefsumme”, who is also a YouTuber, and on their channel, they’ve uploaded a demo video of the desktop in use. We can’t deny that it does look pretty.

How well it works, we can’t say, but the Gershwin desktop that we reported on last year is still in active development and may provide a deeper, richer Mac-like UI in time. Its development is also LLM-assisted, but the majority of the tools are human-written code, much of it from the GNUstep project – which started in 1991, making it older than Xfce, KDE, GNOME, and around the same age as the Linux kernel itself. ®

#4224. A fast and secure tar parser for the Rust ecosystem

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Starting with 0.131, uv uses tar-codec to parse source distributions.

tar-codec is roughly twice as fast as our previous tar implementation (astral-tokio-tar) while also reducing uv's exposure to parser differentials and other security risks.

This blog post is about tar-codec itself: what it is, and what compelled us to build and optimize it. Read on if you, too, would like to know too much about tar archives.

What follows is an extended digression on the tar format. If all you care about is tar-codec, skip to it.

As a Python packaging tool, uv is in the business of resolving and installing Python packages. uv requests installable distributions from various sources (principally package indices like PyPI) and installs them on the user's behalf. This is how you go from uv add urllib3 to something you can actually import and use in your own Python codebase.

Installable distributions come in two primary forms:

"Built"2 distributions, also known as bdists or (most commonly) wheels, are essentially ZIP archives containing structured metadata and a fixed layout. Installers handle these by (more or less) plopping their contents into the right destination directory.

Many people think that bdist is short for "binary distribution," since wheels often contain compiled native code. This one common way to use wheels, but wheels can also contain pure Python. They're just another (and frequently better) way to package and install a distribution.

"Source" distributions, also known as sdists, are essentially tar archives. Unlike built distributions, a source distribution is essentially3 just a rollup of a source tree's state at release time.

By definition, source distributions execute arbitrary code at installation time. This makes them very powerful, but brittle (missing build tools or dependencies) and also a security risk (an attacker who controls the sdist can run code without the user needing to import anything).

Wheels are generally produced by running a build backend on a source distribution. Examples of that include uv's own uv_build, maturin, and flit.

Consequently, at a bare minimum, a Python package installer needs to understand at least two different archive formats: ZIP for wheels, and tar for sdists.

We've already talked a bit before about ZIPs on the Astral blog, and we'll certainly be talking about them again soon enough. But today we'll be focusing on tar.

It's fair to ask: why does uv want a new tar parser?

In short: security and performance, but mostly security.

Up until 0.13, uv used astral-tokio-tar for its tar operations, including both parsing and creation (in uv build). astral-tokio-tar is itself a fork of edera-dev/tokio-tar, which is a fork of vorot93/tokio-tar, which is a fork of dignifiedquire/async-tar, which is an async adaptation of composefs/tar-rs.

Around a year ago, we began to receive a number of security reports in astral-tokio-tar. This spurred us into looking more into our compression and archive handling stack, where we found additional problems.

Many of these boiled down to differentials that could cause a full parser desynchronization4: given an identical tarball, uv, pip, and other package tooling could be made to see different contents for the same package distribution. We found this particularly concerning in the context of archives that would appear "safe" to an index (like PyPI) but could extract dangerously when parsed by an installer like uv or pip.

This could the take the form of an "append" differential (where the attacker can append new files or new contents to existing files for a specific victim parser), or even a "full" differential (where the attacker can fully separate the extraction contents for each parser):

In the context of uv, the attacker model for these kinds of vulnerabilities is somewhat subtle: tar is only used for source distributions which, per above and unlike wheels, are assumed to execute arbitrary code at build time. Consequently, Python packaging mostly considers tar differentials to be a minor concern, since any attacker who would use them has a much more direct and official route towards writing arbitrary contents to disk (or executing arbitrary code).

Still, these findings unnerved us, and we embarked on a project to eliminate every differential we could find in astral-tokio-tar.

tar-codec grew directly out of that effort: we ultimately came to the conclusion that the tokio-tar family was too lenient by design, and that retrofitting constraints to it would be more troublesome and error-prone (including for existing non-Astral users of its APIs) than developing our own, ground-up implementation of tar. This would also give us the opportunity to systematically understand the tar format, and ensure that our assumptions around which aspects are (or aren't) differential risks hold true.

Similarly, we determined that our performance goals around tar parsing would be impossible to meet in the existing code base, at least not without refactors that would be tantamount to a rewrite.

It's important to understand that one does not simply build a tar parser from scratch. One must first understand how the tar format evolved into its current state5.

tar is an interesting format. Or more precisely, it's an interesting family of formats.

To summarize, there are three (and a half) variants that matter in practice:

There's UNIX V7 tar, which is the "original" tar. Very few tar implementations write this format by default anymore, but virtually all still read it. For example, Python's tarfile module will happily parse V7 tar files.

The UNIX V7 format is not widely used anymore because it comes with significant limitations on per-member file sizes (approximately 8 GiB) and path lengths (100 bytes).

Then there's the UStar/pax tar family6. This family is widely supported by tar implementations for both reading and writing. As far as tar goes, UStar and pax are what passes for a "gold standard" in terms of clarity of specification and documentation.

At this juncture, the main thing to know about the UStar/pax family is that it's derived from the UNIX V7 format, but with certain restrictions lifted:

GNU tar is documented in the GNU tar manual, but is not standardized per se. The source code of GNU tar (the CLI) is the de facto standard for GNU tar.

Like UStar/pax, GNU tar is derived from UNIX V7 tar. It's also derived from an early draft of UStar.

Also like pax, GNU tar makes file sizes and path/link lengths (effectively) unlimited. It does so in a completely different and much funnier way than pax does.

If you've ever written a parser for a binary format before, then you probably have a preconception for what a binary format should look like. To first, second, and third approximations, binary formats tend to look something like this:

Within this, too, certain idioms appear: binary formats typically use some form of TLV encoding, use different magics to signal different endiannesses, &c.

The tar format does not look like this, regardless of the variant. tar is fundamentally a block format, owing to its origins as a tape archival format8.

As a block format, a tar archive is comprised of one or more fixed-length blocks. There is no file-level header; instead, many of its blocks are headers, while other blocks contain actual member9 data:

In all flavors of tar, all blocks (including the header blocks) are always 512 bytes. Each header block declares metadata for the member that follows it. This includes important things like the size of the member and its filepath.

Member contents don't always fit cleanly into multiples of 512 bytes, so a tar that wants to encode a file member of N bytes will allocate ceil(N / 512) blocks and pad out the remainder of the last block. This doesn't affect extraction, since the reader knows (via the header block) exactly how many bytes out of the allocated space are actual file content.

Conventionally, the padding or "holes" in a tar archive are filled with NUL bytes. However, nothing stops a tar producer from filling them with uninitialized memory, or lines from the Bee Movie, or anything else. A recurring theme with tar (of all flavors) is that it has no opinions about what goes in the holes of the format. This malleability makes it very prone to parser differentials, since it gives the attacker ample space to place surprising data.

tar's block design also makes it relatively space-inefficient10 by modern standards. For example, a 513-byte file occupies an absolute minimum of 1,536 bytes when encoded as a tar member: one 512-byte block for the header, and two blocks for the content (one of which has only 1 "real" byte, followed by 511 bytes of padding). Similarly, a tar archive containing 1000 one-byte files requires a minimum of 1025 KiB of encoding (1 MiB for the headers and contents, and then 1 KiB for the trailing zero blocks).

Our state machine begins with a "classification" stage, which effectively locks the remainder of the parse to a single family (either UStar/pax or GNU16) based on the identity (magic and version) of the first block.

This is done for differential reduction reasons: while researching tar-codec's design, we realized that many other widely used tar parsers attempt to merge states across various tar families, with inconsistent results.

In practice we've seen other parsers do all kinds of things in this state, including attempting to merge the extensions, honoring one over the other(s), attempting to apply the L state to x instead of the file member, and so forth. Each of these is a potentially exploitable differential!

Instead, tar-codec will reject a tar archive if its individual headers don't agree about what family of tar it is. Real-world tar encoders don't mix tar families (there's no good reason to), so this doesn't pose a significant breakage risk.

The GNU part of the state machine is slightly more strict than the GNU documentation implies: the GNU documentation allows stacking of L/K extensions, including multiple of the same kind, in any order.

tar-codec tightens that to allowing L and K in any order, but only one of each per underlying member.

The UStar/pax part of the state machine models local and global extended headers rigidly.

There's a significant amount of complexity beneath this: pax allows x and g anywhere in the tar stream. For example, this is a perfectly valid extension sequence in pax:

This brings us to one of the most painful parts of pax that we glossed over before: extended headers don't shadow each other fully, they compose depending on what state the "stack" of effective pax records is in. The state of any given pax record can be:

Undeclared: no effective pax record exists anywhere in the current stack of local and global extensions, which means that the member's own UStar header's state takes precedence.

Active: there's an effective pax record in the stack, and it has a value.

Tombstoned: there's an effective pax record in the stack, but it has no value (for example, 8 path=\n instead of 12 path=abc\n).

You would think that this would be the same as the Undeclared state, but it's not! pax considers this record explicitly deleted, and prescribes:

If the field is zero length, it shall delete any header block field, previously entered extended header value, or global extended header value of the same name.

This has fascinating consequences for the state machine, because it means that an effective-but-deleted path record means...a file with no path? How are you supposed to extract that17?

Needless to say, tar-codec tries to follow this faithfully. In particular, it attempts to be diligent about rejecting logically-consistent-but-nonsense states that fall out of pax's strange record state machine, including empty paths and global extended headers that try to declare size and other key states for all members.

One of the early design choices we made with tar-codec was to isolate the implementation into a few layers. From lowest to highest:

The tar-framing crate is responsible for taking a stream of blocks and turning it into a stream of frames. This happens in two refinement stages:

The stream or "physical" API honors the big state machine above and yields each block in a given state, one block at a time.

The "logical" API consumes the blocks produced by the "physical" API, and yields them as a stream of assembled members. Each assembled member is essentially its "header" (which is the physical header, merged with any effective extension state that affected the physical framing), a separate extension state for non-framing extensions (such as GNU L and K), and a readable payload for the member's actual contents.

The tar-codec crate takes the "logical" API from tar-framing and turns it into something that can be extracted in memory or onto disk.

Extracting an archive (of any format) onto disk is its own can of worms, with its own differential risks18. Another one of our early observations is that every archive implementation tends to have its own extract-to-disk implementation, with its own edge cases.

We built archive-trait to address that. archive-trait exposes Archive and ArchiveBuilder traits (along with associated types and machinery) that allow any format to expose uniform APIs for reading and creating archives. We then built archive-trait's own filesystem extraction on top of cap-std, giving us the ability to "anchor" extraction into a single directory and avoid a large class of link and path traversal footguns.

Writing a differential-resistant tar parser is surprisingly hard, given how ubiquitous tar files of all flavors are, and how simple the original (V7) format is.

This post has only scratched the surface in terms of complexity. To recap:

Each tar family has its own state machine, which is not always clearly laid out in that family's documentation or reference implementation. It is imperative that these state machines do not mix, but real-world tar parsers regularly allow them to.

Each tar family has its own, non-trivial rules for determining the precedence of state (pathname, linkname, and size) attached to archive members.

Some of this state affects the framing of the parser itself, meaning that faithfully handling e.g. pax archives means handling size and similar records up-front, rather than trying to abstract the parse across individual 512-byte header blocks.

Separately, there were numerous places where we had to compromise on strict pax conformance because of observed behavior from other implementations:

The pax spec is explicit that pax record values must be UTF-8. However, both GNU tar and star historically emitted some vendor-specific records19 as raw binary. See this bug-tar thread for more context.

UStar and pax are explicit that uname and gname should be NUL-terminated in the UStar header, meaning that each can be at most 31 bytes long. However, CPython's tarfile (and probably others) will happily fill either to 32 bytes, only overflowing to the pax record at 33 bytes or more. See CPython#159322 for more context.

More generally, tar implementations will frequently leave mode, uid, gid, and mtime as all NUL, rather than zeroed or similar. This doesn't pose a framing/parser synchronization risk, so tar-codec tracks them as "missing" rather than assigning an arbitrary default value.

Finally, in the process of designing and implementing tar-codec, we found many parser differential primitives that cause various tar implementations to behave very differently on identical inputs. Some of these are currently being disclosed as security vulnerabilities. However, some of the general classes we saw:

pax/GNU state machine confusion, as mentioned above. In particular, some tar implementations can be fooled into applying a pax extension's states to a GNU extension (or vice versa) instead of to the subsequent member, often resulting in full desynchronization and different extraction results.

Mis-handling of pax extension precedence, in particular both global (g) extended headers and record "tombstoning." For example, some implementations will accept a g header with size=1234 but ignore it, despite pax saying clearly that such a size record overrides any UStar size fields. This also typically results in a full desynchronization primitive.

Mis-handling of pax record parsing. For example, some implementations will ignore the prefix and instead assume that pax records can be safely split on \n. This is not true, since the of a pax record can contain a verbatim \n. As you may have guessed, this also typically results in a full desynchronization primitive.

More generally, we found a great deal of variety in how lax various tar implementations are when it comes to parsing integer fields: many use their language's native "integer from string" API, which can behave surprisingly in the presence of different (non-Western Arabic) numeral forms, leading and trailing whitespace, and so forth. These small variations can percolate into two different implementations parsing the same size field as different integers, once again resulting in desynchronization.

Decode and encode performance was not the hard part: we found a lot of low-hanging fruit while optimizing both, including optimizing tar's checksum generation and calculating pax record length prefixes.

We also picked up significant performance gains by ensuring that tar-codec reuses allocations and batches/buffers its work wherever possible (and where doing so does not weaken the API's internal consistency or cancellation correctness in an async setting).

Conversely, performant secure extraction is somewhat hard: directory capability tracking (of the kind that cap-std does) adds measurable overhead to many of our filesystem operations, particularly when extracting on hosts that lack openat2(2) or an equivalent system call20. This negatively impacts extraction on macOS in particular.

All in all, tar-codec's design rests on well-worn principles for secure parser design: we are conservative rather than liberal in what we accept, and we enforce correctness by construction through increasingly constrictive layers (from member framing to streaming to extraction). These design choices have made us confident that tar-codec is more resistant to differentials (by correctly rejecting them) than other tar implementations.

The fractured and under-specified nature of tar makes it impossible for us to say that we are entirely devoid of differentials, or that new differentials won't be discovered in the future. However, we believe that tar-codec's primitives are the correct ones if and when those contingencies occur.

This is not the end of our work, on several levels:

While tar-codec is essentially feature-complete for our own purposes (within uv), we're interested in making sure that it also solves problems that the larger Open Source community has around secure and performant tar parsing.

To that end, we'll be looking at supporting other, more niche aspects of the various tar families (like sparse files, and more control over vendor-specific pax records) as the need arises.

Separately, we'll be working with others in the community on improving the correctness of other tar implementations. In particular, we see Harmen Stoppels's nascent tar-minefield project as a promising way to test and re-align other implementations with stricter, more differential-resistant interpretations of the tar family formats.

Separately, as you may have guessed from our decision to build a generic archive-trait library: tar is not the only format we're thinking about here!

We're also actively looking into replacing uv's ZIP parsing, for similar reasons. We hope to share our lessons from that work soon, along with material benefits to uv itself.

Members of the same people who settled Easter Island also discovered Hawaii, a new study finds.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

Native Hawaiians and Easter Islanders genetically descend from the same "ultravoyager" Polynesian group that crossed vast distances of ocean to reach these remote islands, a new study finds.

Easter Island (also known as Rapa Nui) and Hawaii are part of Polynesia, a group of more than 1,000 islands spread over an area of the Pacific Ocean roughly equal to the areas of North and South America combined. Hawaii and Easter Island are the farthest of these isles from each other, at about 4,500 miles (7,200 kilometers) apart — around the distance from San Francisco to Lima, Peru.

The researchers discovered the connection by analyzing the genomes of 1,050 modern individuals across Polynesia, including 317 Native Hawaiians and 12 Easter Islanders. "Each person's genome contains genetic code from both parents, and from all four grandparents, and their parents, and so on," study co-author Alexander Ioannidis, a computational geneticist at Stanford University, told Live Science. "Each genome encodes an enormous number of ancestors. So with even a few individuals, we can get information about an enormous number of people from each island."

Specifically, the scientists compared the length of chromosomal segments shared among people from different islands. The longer these shared segments are between two people, the more recently they shared a common ancestor.

Even though Hawaii and Easter Island are far-flung, the researchers discovered that the Indigenous people of Hawaii and Rapa Nui are the most genetically related to one another. The team reported its findings Oct. 8 in the journal Science.

"This was really shocking at first — these islands are so distant from one another," Ioannidis said.

The scientists found that Hawaii and Easter Island were likely both settled by members of a single "ultravoyager" culture from the Mangareva islands, about 3,440 miles (5,530 km) southeast of Hawaii and 1,600 miles (2,600 km) west of Easter Island. (In 2020, the researchers found that members of this ultravoyaging group had mixed with Indigenous people in what is now Colombia, although it's unclear if this partnering occurred on an island or on the continent.)

Get the world’s most fascinating discoveries delivered straight to your inbox.

"Once you know there was this extremely capable long-distance ultravoyaging culture capable of reaching one of these islands, having it reach the other makes more sense," Ioannidis said.

The area near Mangareva, including the Austral Islands, the Society Islands, the Marquesas Islands, and the Tuamotu Archipelago, "spans an area the size of Europe," Ioannidis said. "Sailing in-between these islands would have required skilled navigation. So it makes sense that developing such long-distance navigation skills would have ultimately helped people settle far-off Easter Island and Hawaii."

All in all, these genetic findings suggest the ancestors of Native Hawaiians and Easter Islanders shared some time together as a common group after splitting away from the population of ancient Mangareva. Some members of this group then went on to settle Hawaii, and others from this Mangareva group voyaged to Easter Island.

This new genetic data linking Hawaii, Easter Island and Mangareva "agrees very well with what we see archaeologically," Patrick Kirch, an archaeologist at the University of Hawai'i at Manoa who did not take part in this research, told Live Science. "It gives scientists a lot of confidence when independent lines of evidence from different disciplines give similar results."

The researchers also investigated the effects of genetic bottlenecks that resulted when these islands were settled by small numbers of people — phenomena known as "founder effects."

"If you have a rare genetic trait that happens to occur in this small number of individuals, if this small population grows rapidly, this trait can go on to show up in a high percentage of the resulting population," Ioannidis said. "So if a rare genetic variant once only affected a few people, it can start affecting a decent number of individuals."

The scientists found that Native Polynesian populations examined in the study often experienced a compounding set of founder effects over time. This is because islands settled by a few people then went on to send out small groups to settle other islands, and so on.

Previous research has looked at the founder effects in groups of European descent, such as the Amish, Finns and Ashkenazi Jews, resulting in standard screenings for certain genetic conditions in these groups.

The new study revealed that an even stronger founder effect has been overlooked among Native Hawaiians, with founder effects more than 10 times larger than in those European groups.

"The founder effect doesn't just reflect genetic diversity, but also cultural and linguistic diversity," Kirch said. "It probably helps explain why the cultures of eastern Polynesia all share a lot in common — they all trace to one central group."

These findings not only shed light on Polynesians' past, but also their current health. The researchers found that three genetic variants that are exceedingly rare among the global population — linked with dislocated lenses in the eyes, progressive vision loss, and kidney failure — are carried by 2 to 3% of the eastern Polynesians they studied.

"There should be a lot more resources going into learning more about gene variants of potential clinical significance among this population of Native Hawaiians, which is twice as large as the U.S. Amish population," Ioannidis said. "There are really opportunities for precision health here."

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

With the right plan and the right discipline, you can get seriously shredded in just 28 days.

At age 62, "Big Bill" shares his wisdom to dominate one of the ultimate strength marks.

Model and actor Chase Mattson has become one of vertical media’s most sought after actors, so when the star was asked to chop it up with the superstars of WWE for ReelShort’s Agent of Shadows, he went all-in, gaining respect for these larger-than-life talents in the process, and telling M&F that he prepares himself for any challenge through serious training and a dedicated attitude to nutrition.

In today’s world, vertical media makes sense. As younger people shun the traditional television and scroll away on social media, the premise of shooting shows vertically allows for seamless viewing on phones. Still, with vertical micro-dramas offering free episodes before a pay wall hides the ending, the pressure to perform to the best of his abilities, for every second, is not lost on Chase Mattson, who was recently named People magazine’s Sexiest ReelShort Star. He’s also become one of the genre’s most able actors.

Mattson’s latest project is titled Agent of Shadows, and is a collaboration between ReelShort and WWE, featuring grapplers such as Drew McIntyre, Booker T, Jacob Fatu, and Nikkita Lyons. And, while Mattson, himself was a highly active athlete in his youth, the 6’3” star was in awe of the likes of McIntyre, who is 6’5.” “I was told that Drew was a big guy and I was like, ‘okay cool, I’m a big guy,’ and then he came on set and this dude is a giant, man!” Fortunately, the star says that he and McIntyre became fast friends.”

Among Mattson’s preferred workouts is the “Cindy” workout, a CrossFit circuit that  Tom Holland has employed to stay in Spider-Man shape. To try it, you’ll perform 5 pullups, 10 pushups, and 15 air squats, then repeat for as many rounds as you can. “I was like, that seems too easy but the first time, I got to round eight, and I was gassed,” shares Mattson. Now, the actor has reached a peak of 16 rounds and is even pushing himself further by squatting with 40-pound dumbbells.

With four kids (and two dogs), Mattson balances family life by embracing the chaos, but with the actor eating five meals per day, you better not touch his bison! Mattson explains that as a model, he simply wasn’t eating enough, but after seeking advice on nutrition, he now tracks his macros, and that means carbs are back in. The star explains that at home, he has his own cupboard for food, meaning that he can stick to his plan in order to stay screen ready.

“We have cabinets in our house, but I have one that us dedicated just for me,” explains Mattson of how he is able to stay focused and avoid the sweet temptations that may be lurking in other areas of the kitchen. The star makes smart swaps, like almonds instead of candy, and eats a large number of blueberries for the effects on muscle recovery. His commitment has certainly paid off, and the actor now weighs in at a lean and muscular 215 pounds as opposed to the 185-pound frame from his modeling days.

Still, when it comes to working out, Mattson often makes it a family affair and shares the fun side of fitness with outdoor jogs and calisthenics, which are great for young bodies.

Not only does he have to stay ripped for the next project, and that could mean just 24-hours’ notice for a shirtless scene, but during the shoot for Agent of Shadows, things got seriously physical. One scene even called for Mattson to mix it up with WWE Hall of Famer Booker T, while filming in the blazing heat of the desert.

“Physically, I was doing all the WWE stunts on the ground,” he explains, noting that his whole body was sore “for days” afterwards.

Mattson went above and beyond with the promotion of this vertical micro-drama too, enrolling at WWE’s Performance Center and stepping inside the ring for real.

“They hit me with a chair, a real chair,” says the actor, reflecting on the surreal nature of his job. “It’s harder than it looks.”

With all of the major streaming platforms now developing, or already offering vertical media, Mattson finds himself as one of the most experienced performers in this area, but as we conclude our conversation, the much sought after star is readying for a flight that will see him working on a regular, horizontal project. Still, in true secret agent style, he can’t tell us anything about it.

“I always stay ready, so I don’t have to get ready,” he signs off.
