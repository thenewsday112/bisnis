#1402. This Colorful 400-Square-Foot Apartment Makes Room for Everything Its Owner Needs to Live a Vibrant Life

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“At first glance, it looks composed and serene, but the more time you spend in the apartment, the more unexpected details you discover,” says interior designer Mila Gench, founder of Gench Design, of the 400-square-foot apartment in Moscow that she recently designed for a young professional in tech.

Gench’s description of the apartment could also be applied to the person who lives there: He’s a reserved and thoughtful man who, in his free time, creates objects with a 3D printer and crafts small lamps and decorative objects out of wood. “He wasn’t looking to create a simply contemporary and functional interior, but instead a space that would truly interest him and reflect his personality,” she says. And within this 400-square-foot apartment, every inch must have a purpose. The result is a small and very functional interior that is also a journey through different colors and shapes.

Artwork and textiles define the character of the living room; the pillow is by Izzi Decor, the rug is by Dovlet House, and the sofa is by Gench.

Before the renovation, the apartment, which is located in a modern residential development in Moscow, had a small, separate kitchen next to a larger living room. “For someone living alone, this layout didn’t seem logical to me,” explains Gench. “So we combined the kitchen and living room into a single open space, while separating the bedroom with a small wall to create a sense of privacy.”

A custom desk, shelves, and room divider integrate the workspace to the home. Artpole gypsum panels add texture to the surfaces, while items from Woon Stores decorate the shelves.

The bathroom was expanded, and given the owner’s creative interests, a workspace was a must. All of this was done, however, without taking away space from other parts of the home: “Even in the living room, there’s still enough space to exercise,” Gench says. In fact, every choice serves a specific purpose: “Everything the owner needed had to be easily accessible, without making him feel constrained by the apartment’s size.”

Designed by Mila Gench for this apartment, the mustard-colored sofa is the centerpiece of the living room.

Central to Gench’s design, however, are her bold choices when it comes to color. She explains that the color palette was inspired by the client’s preferences: “It was clear that he was drawn to a cool color scheme, but not a monochromatic one. He also wanted warm, saturated accents.” Royal blue details run through the entryway and hallway, berry red dominates the kitchen, and the living room is lighter in tone. “The mustard sofa has become the heart of the living room,” says Gench. “Its color echoes that of the yellow pendant lamp in the kitchen and reappears in small accents throughout the rest of the apartment.”

In the bedroom, the VitSet bed is complemented by textiles from LanoHome and Izzi Decor, along with accessories from Woon Stores on the nightstands. Factura wallpaper, moldings, and a Europlast frame define the space.

The dark, cozy bedroom, on the other hand, creates an atmosphere that is “slightly more mysterious, like something from a fairy tale.” The color scheme helps to tie all the spaces together: “It was important to me that the different colors appear not as isolated patches, but that they flow throughout the apartment.” This continuity is also functional, as the color palette defines the different areas of the living space without having to rely on walls.

In the living room, visual touches like a vase from Woon Stores, DzenDiz curtains designed by Mila Gench, and handmade ceramics from the Plakart Ceramics website accent the space.

“I think of this apartment as a little jewelry box,” says Gench. A checkered arch frames the kitchen, while frescoes, relief plaster panels, and brass details catch the eye. It can feel like being immersed in a Pop art piece. “I wanted the apartment to reveal itself little by little, with new details to discover in every corner: a metal object, a relief, a pattern, or an unusual surface.”

The custom-built kitchen combines a layout planned down to fractions of inches and with a bold color scheme. The backsplash is covered with Equipe tiles; the decorative items are from Woon Stores.

One choice the homeowner was particularly keen on was the perforated metal doors on the upper cabinets in the kitchen and bathroom. “At one point, I’d considered not using them. They were complicated to install and drove up the cost,” says Gench. “But the client insisted.” Their graphic precision contrasts with the painted surfaces and soft shapes in the apartment, reflecting two different sides of the home’s owner.

The cabinets reflect Gench’s broader approach to the project: “I chose to highlight them and make them part of the apartment’s decorative language, almost like architectural elements.” She points out, “Art isn’t a final touch added at the end of the project, it should be part of the concept from the very beginning.”

The design of the desk, shelves, and room divider were calculated to create a workspace that fits the size of the apartment.

“The workspace had to be as professional as possible, with a large desk to accommodate the latest technology and multiple monitors,” says Gench. The area’s height and storage solutions were carefully planned, along with the proportions of the furniture. “At the same time, I was concerned with not turning the living room into an office.”

The desk echoes the room’s colors and materials, blending seamlessly into the space. And once work is done, it takes on a new function: “A screen on the desk can be seen from the sofa, so the same space can easily transition from working to relaxing and watching a movie.”

A view of the custom wardrobe in the bedroom.

“The more compact the space, the less it tolerates rough approximations: Sometimes a single centimeter is enough to determine whether a corner will be comfortable or whether a cabinet door can open fully without hitting the wall,” the designer explains. Custom designs took into account the precise width of the sofa, the amount of room necessary to move around the bed, the size of storage units, and the dimensions of the kitchen—which is equipped with a refrigerator, dishwasher, oven, microwave, and wine cooler. Most of the furnishings come from contemporary Russian manufacturers.

In the dining area, a painting by Yaroslav Shein, from the Sample Gallery, hangs above a Delo table and custom chairs. The pendant light is by Elena Mikheeva, with furnishings from Woon Stores and moldings and baseboards by Europlast.

This attention to detail gives the owner the freedom to relax and live in his home without ever feeling limited. “I wanted all these constraints to go unnoticed in everyday life,” Gench concludes. She sums up the essence of the project and her approach: “Don’t pretend that 400 square feet is more than it is, but instead find a way to make it truly sufficient for daily life.”

The entryway features wall sconces by Freya.

The Wave Rider 30 is a comfortable, versatile daily trainer that works especially well for newer runners who want cushioning and support without a rigid or overly aggressive ride.

Running shoes have gotten wild. We have enormous stacks of foam, carbon plates, increasingly elaborate rocker geometries, and enough proprietary materials to make a chemistry professor scratch their heads. (Serves them right—a B?!) And while all of that technology has produced some truly epic trainers, sometimes you just want to put on a pair of running shoes and get your butt out the dang door.

That’s where the Mizuno Wave Rider 30 comes in clutch. It’s a decidedly normal daily running shoe, and I love that about it. There’s no break-in period, you aren’t bouncing on a plate trying to beat a land speed course record, and it’s not forcing your feet with rails. It’s happily cozy and comfy.

I think this shoe is best suited for newer runners, especially those who want to get a feel for the road and for running as a whole. It’s supportive and cushioned without being overly stabilizing or too propulsive. A lot of beginners get steered toward shoes that either lock the foot into a rigid path or push them forward with a snappy plate, and that can get in the way of finding their own rhythm. The Wave Rider 30 lets you feel the road and settle into your own stride, while still giving you enough cushioning that you aren’t beat up afterward.

The shoe is built around a full-length Wave plate, which has long been Mizuno’s signature. It adds structure and smooths out each footstrike without making the ride feel stiff or forced. On top of that, the midsole is dual-layered, with nitrogen-infused Mizuno Enerzy NXT foam sitting over an EVA base. The result is a soft but stable ride that feels protective underfoot without being mushy or making you feel “sinky.”

This is an easy-run shoe through and through, and it moves between different parts of your week without complaint.

If you want one pair that covers most of your routine, this is a boss choice. You don’t have to swap shoes depending on the day’s schedule.

The one knock I have is that it runs a little warm. It could be a tiny bit more breathable, and I noticed that on hot summer runs. But we’re heading into colder months, and in cooler weather that extra warmth is going to feel really, really good. I’m looking forward to wearing it as the temperature drops alongside my cozy Smartwool gloves and weather-inappropriate top—it’s all about balance.

I’m not the only one who feels this way. Runner’s World wear tester Jeff Deschler logged miles on roads and a rail trail and came away with a similar impression. He said the shoe is exactly what he expects from a daily trainer: good at what it does, and not trying to be something it’s not. In an era of ever-taller stacks, exotic materials, and plates of every construction, he found it refreshing to run in a “normal” shoe.

What stood out most to Deschler was how balanced it is. The cushioning was adequate, the fit was good, and the upper kept his foot secure. He didn’t have to think about the shoe while running, which he called one of the highest compliments he can give a daily trainer. Compared to the Neo Vista 3, the last shoe he tested, he said the Wave Rider 30 takes a “jack of all trades” approach rather than focusing on just one thing. There are better shoes out there, he said, but as a daily trainer at $150, good luck finding it.

The Wave Rider 30 isn’t flashy, and it doesn't try to be. It’s comfortable, supportive without being restrictive, and dependable across a wide range of uses. For newer runners or anyone who wants a no-fuss shoe for everyday miles, it’s an easy one to recommend. It runs a touch warm, but as the weather cools, that becomes a bonus.

#2460. No Liability for Scientific Publisher (Elsevier) in Defamation Lawsuit by Air Purifier Company

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From Judge Kenneth Bell (W.D.N.C.) today in Global Plasma Solutions, Inc. v. Elsevier Ltd Inc.:

In this action, now spanning over four and a half years and nearly 400 docket entries, Plaintiff Global Plasma Solutions, Inc. ("GPS") contends that its air purifier business was greatly harmed by a defamatory article published in Building and Environment ("BAE"), a scientific journal owned by Defendant Elsevier …. Specifically, GPS alleges that several critiques of its "bipolar ionization" technology were false and Elsevier's advertisement of BAE's articles as "peer reviewed" violated the Lanham Act….

The law provides strong protection for scientific inquiry and debate on matters of public concern. This includes the allegedly defamatory article, which was published in March 2021 at the height of the COVID-19 pandemic when air purification technologies were under intense scrutiny.

Because the Court finds that GPS was a limited purpose public figure with respect to the safety and efficacy of its bipolar ionization technology and cannot establish by clear and convincing evidence that Elsevier published the article with "actual malice," Elsevier is entitled to summary judgment on GPS's defamation claim. Similarly, a reasonable jury could not find that Elsevier is liable for false advertising under the Lanham Act, where there is insufficient evidence that a substantial portion of the intended audience would be misled by the truthful statements that the article was "peer reviewed" or that an Elsevier advertisement was a proximate cause of GPS's claimed diminution in its corporate value….

[V]iewing the evidence in the light most favorable to GPS, the conduct alleged here—while in several respects falling well short of being fabricated or fraudulent data—might possibly justify limiting the full, dispositive First Amendment protection for scientific opinion urged by Elsevier. However, the Court need not (and will not) reach a definitive conclusion on the scope of the Article's "scientific opinion" protection because, as discussed below, GPS has not shown evidence of actual malice sufficient to support its defamation claim, which independently resolves GPS's defamation claim in Elsevier's favor….

#1153. New Balance’s Latest 990 Is Cooler Than Ever

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The New Balance 990 isn’t like the other shoes. The silhouette is the Boston brand’s franchise player, the leadoff hitter, the North Star for everything New Balance does. While other sneaker brands routinely pump out new iterations of their flagship products (inevitably dulling their allure in the process), New Balance keeps the 990 sacred. New versions of the silhouette—prior to this week, there had only been six—only come around every so often. The V2 was released 16 years after the original dropped in 1982, while the V4 and V5 arrived merely three years apart. This week, for the first time since 2022’s V6, the brand is debuting the latest iteration of the sneaker that started it all. And this time, it truly might be better than ever.

“There’s a real responsibility that comes with redesigning it,” says Brian Lynn, New Balance’s global VP of lifestyle product. “The challenge is in finding the balance between preservation and evolution.” He notes that there are people for whom the 990, in whatever version is on shelves at the moment, has been their daily sneaker for decades. At the same time, each version is also always someone’s first; each has to offer something that can draw that new fan in. “People still appreciate heritage, but they’re increasingly looking for something new,” says Lynn.

For the shoe’s previous revamp, the 990v6, the brand overdelivered on the “something new.” The chunky rocker sole didn’t feel too far removed from those commonly seen on Hokas these days, and the mesh and suede paneling had a distinctly modern feel. Of all the 990s it’s the one that felt the most of its moment—which can be a good thing but, a few years down the line, risks that moment passing.

The 990v7 feels like a re-embracing of tradition, with the design improving on what the V6 set out to accomplish. It doesn’t shrink the monster sole, but it does reshape it into something a bit steadier and more old-school than the rocker build of its predecessor. It’s kitted out with a softer version of the brand’s FuelCell tech, plus a combination of ENCAP segment with a modified TPU midfoot shank, which is a very fancy way of saying it’s comfier and more orthopedically sturdy than it’s ever been. They’ve also leaned into the “embrace tradition” of it all by decking the debut colorway’s sole out in a vintage-y cream tone; the usual rich grey suede and mesh paneling we’ve come to expect from the line is there too, on a pleasantly weird and wavy new upper. The pebbled leather tongue and heel tab feel a bit luxe. It’s a real contender for the best a 990 has looked right out the gate.

According to Lin, the 990 is, as always, a labor of love for the brand. “I want people to love wearing it, to appreciate the comfort and the materials and the care that went into making it,” he says. You can see the results of New Balance’s latest edition of their flagship silhouette on November 6, when the shoe goes on sale for $200 from New Balance and select retailers.

The tree of heaven (Ailanthus altissima) may be one of the most ironically named plants in the entire plant kingdom. Far from being a heavenly entity, its presence on your property could signal pests, ecological distress, and even damage to pipes, if it’s close enough to a building.

As with many other invasive plants, you’ll want to be careful with how you get rid of it—cutting this tree down will only make things worse. For that reason, we tapped two experts to explain exactly how to deal with a tree of heaven in your backyard and (hopefully) prevent it from returning.

The tree of heaven is native to China and is considered to be the most invasive tree in America. In fact, as Logan Foltz, certified arborist at Urban Arborists, says, the tree “outcompetes” many of our North American trees in their natural habitats and is “notoriously challenging” to control. It’s also extremely fast-growing (up to 15 feet in the first year) and very resilient.

“The tree of heaven’s ability to thrive in many different climates and locations is what led it to rapidly spread from the East Coast gardens of Philadelphia to the California coast,” explains horticulturist and organic farmer Nathan Heinrich.

The plant can be identified by its slim but sturdy trunk and branches, and its uniform, fern-like fronds that are distinguished by having “glandular teeth” at the base of each leaf. Female varieties of the plant will have red, yellow, or green seed clusters. Note that native, non-threatening black walnut trees and some varieties of sumac also possess similar physical characteristics to trees of heaven, so always be sure you know which plant you’re dealing with.

The tree of heaven has been labeled as invasive because of its ability to produce up to thousands of seeds every year. These seeds can germinate in almost any condition, from sidewalk cracks to gutters to your very own garden. The tree’s root system, which can spread out as much as 50 feet from the trunk, can even infiltrate and crack hardscaping and pipes as it seeks out sources of water. And it’ll choke out neighboring plants.

“If the root system is damaged or the trunk is cut, the tree will respond by sending up suckers from the roots that will grow into new full-size trees,” Foltz says.

They’re considered very nearly “unkillable” because, as Heinrich explains, the tree of heaven has “virtually no natural enemies, drought tolerance, rapid reproduction, and the ability to resurrect by resprouting after being cut down.”

If that weren’t bad enough, trees of heaven are also a host plant for the equally invasive and destructive spotted lanternfly, according to the U.S. Department of Agriculture. Although they’re virtually indestructible, there are some ways experts approach these tough-to-kill trees. Calling this an “old-school approach,” Foltz believes that the best way to get rid of a tree of heaven is to cut the tree down to its base, drill the remaining stump full of holes, then fill those holes with rock salt.

“This method slowly desiccates the stump and connected root system, stopping the tree of heaven from resprouting,” Foltz says. “The drawback here is that the rock salt will make the soil inhospitable for new plantings.” So, this method may be better used for areas of your property where you’re not trying to grow anything.

Heinrich agrees with this method, saying that it’s best to do this in July and August. As an alternative to using rock salt, he recommends injecting multiple rounds of herbicide into the drilled holes and slits in the stump.

He adds that another way to potentially eradicate a tree of heaven is to exhaust its energy reserves by repeatedly cutting it back until its roots can no longer sustain growth.

Trees of heaven are a menacing presence, but is it necessary to get rid of them? Foltz says that it depends.

“These trees are not inherently evil—they are simply incredibly successful at what they do, which is to make more trees,” he notes. “While I do typically recommend removing the new trees as you see them sprout up, some mature specimens may be worth retaining. #968. We’ve Been Pairing Salt and Pepper Together for Centuries—But Why?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This centuries-old partnership may have started with a French king.

Salt and pepper have been together for so long that the pairing barely registers as a culinary decision. They’re printed side by side in recipes, passed together at the table, and sold in matching sets ranging from handsome little mills to comically flamboyant ceramic chickens. At some point, the two became a package deal, and the arrangement has hardly been questioned since.

But from a culinary standpoint, it’s a curious partnership. Salt is essential, both to our bodies and to the way we experience flavor. Pepper, on the other hand, is a spice with a distinct flavor of its own. I salt nearly everything I cook, but I only use pepper when I actually want something to taste like pepper. The idea that the two should automatically accompany each other has never made much culinary sense to me.

And yet, of all the spices that have passed through European kitchens over the centuries, black pepper is the one that secured a permanent place beside the salt. So how did pepper beat cinnamon, saffron and every other spice to the table? The answer involves changing culinary trends and a king who was very accustomed to getting his way.

Like a surprising number of things involving elaborate meals and strong opinions, this story leads to 17th-century France.

Before then, European cooking—particularly the food of the wealthy—could be extraordinarily heavy on spices. Medieval French recipes regularly called for combinations of ginger, cinnamon, saffron and other imported spices. Pepper was around, but it wasn’t yet salt’s designated companion. Spices generally were added in the kitchen rather than at the table.

By the middle of the 17th century, French cooking was changing. Chefs began backing away from heavily spiced medieval dishes in favor of a lighter style meant to preserve the flavor of the main ingredient. François Pierre de La Varenne helped codify that change with his enormously influential 1651 cookbook, Le Cuisinier François. Imported spices largely gave way to fresh herbs, butter became increasingly important, and seasoning was used with considerably more restraint. Pepper was one of the notable spices that remained.

This is where King Louis XIV enters the story. The oft-repeated explanation is that the famously powerful French king preferred his food lightly seasoned, favoring salt and pepper over stronger combinations of spices. His court was incredibly influential, and French cuisine set the fashion elsewhere in Europe. In that telling, the Sun King’s preferences eventually became everyone else’s.

Of course, food history is rarely quite that tidy. Louis XIV may have helped give salt and pepper their place at the table, but the broader shift happening in French cooking made the pairing particularly well-suited to stay there.

And French culinary influence extended well beyond the royal court. Le Cuisinier François was translated into English within just a few years and went through dozens of editions, helping popularize the newer style of cooking beyond France. French culinary ideas would continue to shape cooking throughout Europe and, eventually, North America. A preference became a convention, and conventions are remarkably difficult to evict from the dinner table.

There was also a practical reason for the pairing to endure. Salt and pepper do two very different jobs. Salt isn’t simply another seasoning; it can suppress bitterness and change the way we perceive other flavors. Black pepper brings its own flavor—warm, aromatic and mildly pungent—without necessarily taking over the dish. Together, they fit neatly into the newer French philosophy of seasoning food without burying its original flavor.

The presentation changed, too. Salt had historically been served from open containers called salt cellars, in part because moisture made it prone to clumping. In the early 20th century, anti-caking agents made free-flowing salt practical, and salt shakers soon became commonplace. By then, salt and pepper had already spent centuries together. The arrival of the salt shaker simply gave an old partnership its now-familiar uniform: two little shakers, side by side. By the time those matching shakers appeared, pepper’s place beside salt was already secure.

Perhaps Louis XIV really did have something to do with it—stranger things have happened at Versailles. Either way, pepper survived a major change in French taste, then rode the enormous influence of French cooking straight onto tables across the Western world.

Nuclear fusion occurs when two or more atoms merge into a single atom, a reaction that releases a colossal amount of energy. When you bask in the sun in the daytime or gaze at distant stars at night, you are experiencing the awesome power of fusion reactions. For more than a century, scientists have grappled with the tall technological order of reconstructing this process in reactors.

Tokamaks spark fusion reactions inside hot, charged plasma that is generated by powerful magnetic fields in donut-shaped torus chambers. This form of reaction, known as magnetic confinement fusion, has been demonstrated in tokamaks on Earth. While significant gains have been made, no reactor has ever come close to achieving “fusion breakeven,” which is a fusion reaction that produces more energy than was required to run the machinery that ignited it.

Bae’s paper suggests that space-based tokamaks might offer some advantages to ground-based versions. The microgravity environment could reduce the vast structural loads of tokamaks, and the lack of atmospheric pressure could significantly reduce the size and mass of vacuum chambers.

The study also speculates that the immense heat generated by a space tokamak could radiate directly into space with special panels operating at extremely high temperatures of roughly 1,340°F, which “could potentially dissipate heat more effectively on a mass basis than comparable terrestrial reactors,” Bae notes in an email.

And though the recent work focuses on magnetic confinement fusion in space, Bae is also optimistic that the other main type of fusion—known as inertial confinement fusion—could also be staged in space using high-powered lasers.

Bae has been pondering the potential of fusion power since he began his PhD at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1979. As part of his studies, he joined the fusion team at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and became intrigued by this exotic power source. As his career progressed, Bae also developed a research interest in space exploration, which neatly dovetailed with his interest in fusion research. If space-based fusion power were ever realized—a big “if”—it could generate far more energy than solar or fission, potentially supercharging space exploration by providing off-Earth power plants that could fuel up spacecraft in orbit or support extraterrestrial bases on the moon or beyond. In addition, many previous studies have explored the possibility of fusion reactors as a form of ultra-futuristic spacecraft propulsion for huge passenger ships or even interstellar travel.

Researchers think they might have pinpointed the exact night sky that we see depicted in Vincent van Gogh's iconic painting "The Starry Night."

Perhaps one of the most famous paintings in modern history, Van Gogh's "The Starry Night" carries a legacy that continues to inspire and mystify generation after generation. But while you might see in its swirls of color and bright spots of starlight a night sky derived purely from imagination, some scientists think it represents an actual view — one that includes a waning crescent moon, Venus and the Hyades cluster. And they may know exactly when this sky appeared..

In a new study, researchers suggest that "The Starry Night" depicts the pre-dawn sky seen around July 22, 1889 from Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, where Van Gogh painted it. But while this sky happens to perfectly match the one depicted in the artwork, art historians have long thought that Van Gogh painted this piece in June. Could these findings shift the date of this legendary artwork?

This investigation started decades ago with art historian Albert Boime and astronomer George Abell at UCLA in 1984 and continued in the following years with astronomer Charles Whitney at Harvard University. However, these earlier analyses did not agree with one another, and there was no true consensus about exactly when the sky matched with the painting.

So the question remained, lingering in the minds of those with a shared love for both art and space. And a team led by Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim, the CEO of South Korean robotic telescope company SLLAB and a Ph.D. candidate at Kyung Hee University's School of Space Research, picked up the search once more.

"When they see a fragment of a starry sky, observational astronomers like me and amateur astronomers alike will want to solve the puzzle. I am simply someone who pursued that process a little more actively," Kim told Space.com.

But how did Kim's team come to their conclusion, after others had failed to nail down a date over the decades? In short, "we analyzed the painting astronomically, analyzed documents about his situation in July 1889, and then carried out ecological and meteorological investigations," Kim said.

To start with, the team analyzed the sky from the exact location in Saint-Rémy where Van Gogh is said to have painted "The Starry Night." They then used observational geometry to map the historical night sky.

"Modern telescopes are equipped with a function called astrometry, which compares an image of the night sky with celestial coordinates stored in a computer and automatically extracts the coordinates of the image," Kim said. "We used Procrustes analysis, a method that compares only the 'shape' of two sets of points. After matching their position, size and rotation, it measures how similar the two shapes are from the differences that remain (the residuals)."

"Because a star cluster on the canvas may be larger or smaller than in reality and slightly tilted, this method was the right fit. It allowed us to explain, in quantitative terms, what earlier studies had described only qualitatively as 'resembling a certain constellation,'" Kim added.

But this quantitative analysis wasn't enough to say for sure that this was the right-fitting sky, as different constellations could fit to a similar degree, Kim explained. To get closer to their answer, the team combined their analysis with a deep dive through history.

"We also checked whether the stars were actually in that part of the pre-dawn sky on that date, whether they fall within the painter’s field of view, and whether the appearance of the moon and Venus matches the painting. It was the Hyades that satisfied all of these conditions," Kim said.

As mentioned, the date of this matching sky comes in late July, a month after art historians think Van Gogh painted "The Starry Night." But the researchers suggest that their findings could provide evidence to support the idea that the work was actually painted in July.

Letters written by Van Gogh as early as 1888 describe his desire to paint a star-filled sky. A letter dated April 12, 1888 reads: "A starry sky, for example, well — it’s a thing that I’d like to try to do, just as in the daytime I’ll try to paint a green meadow studded with dandelions."

Kim followed these letters through the months to try and find more information that could potentially alleviate the discrepancy between the matching skies and the actual month the painting was created. Kim pointed to a letter from between late May and early June 1889 that reads: "This morning I saw the countryside from my window a long time before sunrise with nothing but the morning star, which looked very big."

Kim drew on "personal imagination" to find ties between Van Gogh's sky observations in these months and what they might mean for the painting's timeline, Kim shared, so this doesn't definitively change the view of this painting's history. However, Kim suggested that these records could indicate that Van Gogh was repeatedly looking up and watching the pre-dawn sky, which could have led to him seeing this exact sky on July 22.

The painting itself even ignites uncertainty among art historians. As the researchers cite in their new paper, leading art historians see "The Starry Night" as stylistically different from Van Gogh's other works during the month of June, drawing further questions over the date painted.

"What our study shows is that the sky in the painting matches, in both observation and shape, the real pre-dawn sky around July 22. So, what we propose is the possibility that Vincent conceived the idea in June and completed the painting after July 22," Kim said.

However, "I think further research in art history and conservation science could shed more light on this question," Kim added.

The fraction "¼" contains the digit "4," but the fraction's value is much closer to zero, he noted. "The activity patterns reflected that numerical relationship, going beyond the digits themselves," Derbie, who was not involved in the research, told Live Science in an email.

The idea that the brain represents numerical magnitude in the parietal lobe is not novel. However, scientists mostly understood how the brain dealt with whole numbers, while its strategy for fractions was not well understood. The new work builds on that existing research by exploring how both numerical magnitude and number type are processed together.

"To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the geometry of neural representations in a task involving both integers and fractions," the researchers wrote in their report. "In the future, it might be interesting to expand the present work to decimal numbers."

From previous work, "scientists already knew that fractions and whole numbers engage overlapping parts of the brain's number-processing network," Derbie said. "This study gives us a clearer picture of how their values are organized."

In the future, the team could examine how this neural representation varies across people of different ages, educational levels and mathematical backgrounds, Derbie suggested. The study also included only native French speakers, so may not reflect differences across language or culture.

"I would want to see how this organization develops as people learn," Derbie said. For instance, the team could follow children before and after they learn about fractions, to see whether changes in brain activity accompany better understanding.

In the current study, the participants found it more difficult to compare fractions than to compare whole numbers, the researchers noted; they were slower and made more errors while working with fractions, which was expected. But the study's results don't offer hints as to why.

"Our study was not designed to explain why fractions are harder to learn or understand," Valério and Dehaene told Live Science. "What is new in our study is that we show that these different types of numbers are organized within a shared map in the brain."
