You may not be able to control where your digital data ends up. But you know exactly how you can securely dispose of your paper records. The trick is deciding which shredder will best satisfy your serious or casual shredding needs, which usually comes down to a handful of features.

Once you start your shredding chore, you’d rather not be interrupted by problems with your shredder. Runtime tells you how long you can keep feeding paper into a shredder before its motor overheats and shuts down, forcing you to wait 30 to 45 minutes, depending on the model, for the machine to catch its mechanical breath.

A small personal shredder may run for only five or 10 minutes, which is plenty if you dispatch only a few documents at a time. If you’re regularly confronted by larger piles to pulverize, look for a runtime of 30 minutes or more.

Once you choose a shredder with longer runtimes, don’t obsess over the advertised cooldown time. You may fill the bin before the shredder overheats and, by the time you’ve disposed of all that confetti, the motor already has had a chance to cool before you return with an empty bin.

A shredder is still largely a mechanical device, a motor driving a set of cutters, augmented in many models by sensors and electronic controls. And as with any mechanical object, failure is often less a question of if than when. Long-term owner shredder reviews are especially useful for spotting recurring problems with motors, sensors, switches, and other parts that may not misbehave during short-term testing.

Unfortunately, getting help directly from some shredder brands can be harder than clearing a paper jam. During our ongoing shredder research, we’ve encountered multiple manufacturers failing to respond to repeated emails and phone calls. Some brands don’t even provide a U.S. customer-service number.

That doesn’t automatically make shredders from these contact-resistant brands a bad buy. Purchasing from an established store or online retailer with a clearly stated return or replacement policy gives you a more reliable resource to turn to if your new shredder turns out to be a lemon. Before buying—particularly from an unfamiliar brand—check both the manufacturer’s warranty and especially the retailer’s return/replace policy.

Unlike the shredders you may remember from old movies, most modern machines no longer turn documents into long linguini-like strips. Cross-cut shredders slice paper both lengthwise and crosswise into small rectangular scraps. Micro-cut models slice and dice those scraps into even tinier bits, leaving hundreds or even thousands of petite particles.

For most home and home-office use, either cutting method provides plenty of protection. Most shredders these days offer micro-cut machines with either P-4 security, which makes reconstructing a document the most puzzling jigsaw puzzle ever, or P-5 security, whose even more diminutive document destruction methods are favored by MDs, CPAs, JDs, or by other professionals with initials in their titles.

The larger and more powerful your shredder, the more sheets it can generally chew through at one time. Just don’t treat the number printed on the box as a dare.

Manufacturers’ rated sheet capacities are best-case numbers, usually based on neatly aligned standard-weight bond paper. Thicker stock, folds, staples, uneven piles, and stuffed envelopes can quickly turn a “12-sheet” machine into something more comfortable with eight or nine. Feeding a few sheets below the rated maximum is one of the easiest ways to avoid jams and unnecessary strain.

Manual shredders require you to stand there feeding paper in batches. Auto-feed machines let you load a stack, walk away, and get something else done while the shredder eats.

Here again, don’t take the rated auto-feed sheet capacity too literally. An “80-sheet” or “200-sheet” auto feeder may accommodate that many pristine sheets under ideal conditions, but real paperwork is rarely pristine. Wrinkles, folds, different paper weights, staples, and uneven stacks can reduce how much you can reliably leave it to swallow unattended. Plus, the bin on a high-capacity auto-feed model may fill before the maximum number of sheets are fed into it.

Auto-feed mechanisms also add more rollers, sensors, and jam-clearing compartments. And more moving mechanical parts create more places for something to eventually go wrong.

However, if you routinely accumulate large piles, the time a highly rated auto-feed shredder saves can easily outweigh the added complexity and potential mechanical issues.

A bigger bin means fewer trips to empty it, but gallon capacity tells only part of the story. Pull-out drawers are lighter and easier to clean and empty. Bins that require lifting the often heavy shredder mechanism off the top of a wastepaper basket can potentially scatter loose particles still in the cutter mechanism.

Also pay attention to what happens when you pull that drawer out. Paper confetti has an impressive ability to cling to plastic, pile up behind a bin, and otherwise escape the container designed to hold it. An easy-to-remove bin that keeps most of its shredded paper actually in the bin will save you emptying and cleanup aggravation, especially if you don’t wait until the bin is filled to its brim.

Shredders may look sturdy and impervious, but most are actually quite finicky eaters. Nearly every shredder will eventually jam if you feed it too much at one time or feed it the wrong type, shape, or mix of paper. More advanced models with more advanced anti-jam systems sense an overload before one occurs, or either automatically reverse or allow you to manually reverse to relieve the jam before you need to perform paper-removal surgery from the cutters with tweezers or needle-nose pliers.

The cheapest jam protection, however, is restraint. If a machine claims 12 sheets, regularly feeding it eight or nine instead will lower your odds of spending quality time violently tugging mangled paper back out of its teeth.

Most current shredders can swallow staples and paper clips, and many can also chew up plastic credit cards. CDs and other thicker media such as metal cards are less universally welcome.

Check the manufacturer’s instructions before you buy for what non-paper materials a shredder can successfully chew and before feeding anything other than paper into its cutters. A shredder capable of mincing a stack of tax records can object like a microwave forced to cook metal—something it wasn’t designed to eat.

Probably less than you think. For most people who dispatch a few bills, receipts, financial statements, or junk-mail offers every few days, well under $100 can buy a perfectly capable shredder.

Spending more generally buys stamina, convenience, and durability: longer runtimes, larger bins, higher sheet capacities, auto-feed, better jam protection, sometimes finer-cut security, and years if not decades of service. Those features become worth paying for as the amount and frequency of your shredding increase.

As with nearly all things, don’t be penny wise and pound foolish. You will find you will shred more than you anticipated—it’s just human nature to use a new device more than you though you would. Overall, shredders costing several hundreds of dollars make the most sense for people who regularly devour large stacks or have a specialized need. Don’t decide what to spend simply by asking how private your paperwork is; ask how much of it you need to turn into confetti and how often both now and in the future.

The most important shredder “spec” may be how little time it forces you to spend with it. Sheet capacity, runtime, security level, and bin capacity are the headline numbers I’d look at first. Runtime is particularly useful because it tells you something about how much work the machine can comfortably handle before demanding a rest.

But some of the things that most determine whether you’ll love or hate a shredder aren’t specs at all. Nearly every decent modern shredder can reduce ordinary documents beyond casual recognition. The bigger differences are how often it jams, how easily those jams clear, how cleanly the bin empties, and how much confetti winds up everywhere except inside it.

Check owner reviews for recurring complaints about jams, overheating, finicky sensors, messy bins, and other annoyances. AI agents on online sites can summarize customer reviews to answer many of these before-you-buy questions.

With shredders, old doesn’t necessarily mean obsolete. Sometimes it means proven.Unlike most consumer electronics, shredders don’t become outdated because they lack this year’s faster processor, new operating system, wireless standard, or other bleeding-edge technology. An older model that’s still attracting buyers and good reviews may actually offer something a brand-new machine can’t: years of accumulated evidence about how well it holds up.

Before buying an older model, check whether the manufacturer has introduced a newer or revised version. When shopping online, search the manufacturer’s current lineup or, if the retailer provides an AI or shopping assistant, ask whether there’s a newer version of the model you’re considering. Then compare old to new rather than automatically assuming new means better.

The best shredder maintenance is self-restraint. Don’t continually stuff the cutters to their rated maximum, allow the bin overflow, or feed the machine materials it wasn’t designed to digest.

Lubrication requirements vary. Some manufacturers recommend shredder oil applied at regular intervals, others suggest pre-lubricated oil sheets, and some models claim to require little or no lubrication at all. Follow the instructions for your particular machine rather than assuming every shredder requires the same type of mechanical TLC.

Beyond that, maintenance should be pretty painless: empty the bin before the paper snow reaches the cutters, occasionally clean dusty sensors if the manufacturer recommends it, and clear jams rather than repeatedly hammering the reverse button and hoping for divine intervention.

Despite the name, most modern “shredders” no longer shred paper into long strips. While cross cut and micro cut are clues for how small the ultimate shred pieces will be, P-ratings, part of the DIN 66399 security standard, put an actual number on “small.”

P-ratings run from P-1 through P-7; the higher the number, the smaller the pieces produced. For ordinary household paperwork, P-3 is already plenty secure. A cross-cut P-3 machine reduces a document to hundreds of pieces, which is more than enough discouragement for anyone contemplating reconstructing your bills, junk mail, receipts, or other everyday paperwork. After all, a document pieced back together like a taped mosaic isn’t likely to fool many people.

P-4 is the sweet spot for buyers who want their paperwork diced considerably finer without spending much more, and P-4 is common among consumer micro-cut shredders. P-5 turns documents into even tinier bits and makes sense for especially sensitive financial, legal, medical, or professional paperwork. P-6 and P-7 go further still, into security levels intended for documents whose contents matter considerably more than last month’s Visa bill.

For most of us, then, P-3 is plenty, P-4 buys extra peace of mind, and P-5 is for paperwork you’d really rather nobody put back together.

🫀 Heart rate accuracy. Wrist-based optical sensors do a good job on steady efforts and a mediocre job on intervals, where your heart rate jumps faster than the sensor can keep up. If you care about training zones, look for a tracker that pairs with a chest strap.

🏃🏽‍♀️ Real workout modes. Running, walking, cycling, swimming, and strength should each be their own mode, not one generic “workout” button. Better modes mean better calorie and effort estimates.

📊 Structured workouts. If you want intervals, tempo runs, or a plan, check that the tracker can load and guide you through them, instead of only logging what you did after the fact.

🔋 Battery life in GPS mode. Advertised battery life is almost always for everyday wear. Turn GPS on and it drops—by a lot. Assume that GPS will shorten your battery by a few days, and make sure it covers your longest run with room to spare.

💧 Water resistance. Water-resistant and swim-ready are not the same. If you’ll swim, check the rating and look for a swim mode.

#1618. How to Get Rid of a Cough ASAP: 15 Tips From Doctors That Can Help Soothe Your Throat Fast

📅 Apr 08, 2022  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

You probably already have these remedies at home.

When it comes to cold or COVID symptoms, a cough can be especially frustrating. A cough is uncomfortable, but it can also be disruptive when it won't quit. If you’ve had a lingering cough, you might be looking for ways to get rid of it, and I get it.

But just so you know, a cough isn’t always bad; it’s a reflex that protects our airways and lungs against irritants like dust, germs, and mucus. And occasional coughing is completely normal, according to the American Lung Association.

That said, you shouldn't just shrug off a cough. “[A] cough has many causes, ranging from a common viral illness to asthma or seasonal allergies to more significant health problems, such as chronic lung conditions, congestive heart failure, or even cancer,” says Michael Hanak, MD, an assistant professor of family medicine at Rush University Medical Center in Chicago.

Some people may develop a cough after becoming infected with COVID-19. A COVID cough is dry and persistent, according to Sioux Falls Specialty Hospital. It is typically accompanied by other symptoms, including chest pain, fatigue, headaches, and shortness of breath.

Meet the experts: Michael Hanak, MD, is an assistant professor of family medicine at Rush University Medical Center in Chicago. Keri Peterson, MD, is an internal medicine doctor and WH advisor.

However, less serious causes of coughing also exist. Certain medications could be to blame, Dr. Hanak says. Environmental triggers like dust, mold, or other pollutants could also contribute, either through lung irritation or postnasal drip. Acid reflux is another potential trigger.

If you’ve been coughing for more than two weeks, Dr. Hanak suggests seeing your doctor for additional testing or recommendations. But if you’ve just started hacking, you can try to soothe your throat with these remedies.

A simple way to ease a cough is with salt water. "Salt can kill bacteria," says Keri Peterson, MD, an internal medicine doctor and WH advisor. "It's also soothing." However, salt doesn't kill viruses, so it may not help you heal your cold or cough faster, Dr. Peterson explains.

To try this at home, mix half a teaspoon of table salt with eight ounces of warm water.

Ginger is well known for its power in calming an upset stomach. Turns out, it can also work wonders for a cough.

“Ginger has been found to be helpful in reducing cough and alleviating sore throat, and ginger lozenges are widely used for this purpose,” Dr. Hanak explains.

The root also has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties that are beneficial in treating a variety of conditions, he adds. Brew some ginger tea by using a teabag or pouring hot water over some fresh slices.

Pepcid, Prilosec, or other antacids can relieve acid reflux and nix any associated coughs, notes Dr. Hanak.

If you find it to be a chronic issue, making certain dietary changes may help, such as avoiding citrus, chocolate, high-fat foods, onions, and tomato-based food products, and incorporating high-fiber foods into your diet, like brown rice, celery, lettuce, melon, oatmeal, and root vegetables (think: potatoes).

You may also want to drink lots of water and keep your portions small at meals if your coughs are due to reflux, says Dr. Hanak.

Coughing, but also craving something sweet? You're in luck! “Honey has been found to be helpful in alleviating cough, either on its own or as part of a cup of tea,” says Dr. Hanak.

It's soothing to the upper airways, taking that throat tickle away, Dr. Peterson adds. "It's also anti-inflammatory so it helps with inflammation—honey's great."

Besides helping you de-stress after a long day, soaking in a hot bath can ease common cold symptoms like congestion, coughing, and sinus pressure, says Dr. Peterson. (A shower works just as well, FYI.) "The steam opens up the airways really well and it soothes," she says.

Alternatively, you could try a humidifier, which adds moisture to the air around you and can work in the same way.

Hydration is important on any given day, but it can be especially crucial when you have a cough. “Staying well-hydrated can help thin mucus in the back of the throat, which can lead to mild improvement in a cough,” explains Dr. Hanak.

Another quick and easy at-home remedy to stop a cough in its tracks? A lozenge, like Ricola lemon mint drops, for example.

If your cough is a result of allergies, try Flonase or Nasonex paired with a daily allergy pill such as Allegra, Claritin, or Zyrtec, per Dr. Hanak.

“Treating allergy symptoms is a common method of resolving postnasal drip and related cough," he says. "This is especially true when a cough is most apparent laying flat or first thing in the morning."

A cough can be seasonal or year-round, so paying attention to the timing will allow you to predict and prevent it, he adds.

To tame a cough caused by a postnasal drip, Dr. Hanak and Dr. Peterson suggest using a neti pot, which can wash out any debris or mucus from your nasal passages. “Clearing mucus from the nasal passages can be helpful in reducing post nasal drainage, thereby improving cough,” Dr. Hanak explains.

This irrigation device can provide relief for people with allergies and sinus infections, in particular, and Dr. Peterson uses one herself. "They flush out germs, the salt kills the bacteria," she says.

But remember to use a neti pot with sterile water to make sure there's no bacteria or other contaminants in it. To ensure it's safe, boil water and refrigerate it or buy distilled water, Dr. Peterson says.

You also may want to give OTC medications a shot, especially if you've already tried everything else. Antitussives are cough suppressants and can block the cough reflex, according to the American Academy of Family Physicians. They "inhibit the cough center in the brain," Dr. Peterson adds. Then there are expectorants, which thin mucus to help get it out, she says.

When in doubt, ask your pharmacist or primary care physician, and always remember to check the expiration dates. Dr. Peterson recommends Delsym and Mucinex DM.

When in doubt, talk to your doc and see if you might need something stronger, like a prescription medication, for your cough. Benzonatate medications like Tessalon Perles, are specifically recommended by Dr. Peterson, but of course your own doctor will prescribe what’s right for you. Benzonatate "anesthetizes the stretch receptors of the nerves in the airways and works very well to suppress coughs," she explains.

Probiotics can promote good gut health, but they have also been shown to have a marginal impact on staving off the common cold and reducing coughing, Dr. Hanak says. Great sources of probiotics include yogurt and fermented foods like miso and kimchi.

By minimizing exposure to chemicals and irritants, which are harmful to the lungs, quitting smoking can cut down on inflammation, Dr. Hanak says. A cough caused by smoking can be progressive, depending on how much someone smokes and how long they have been smoking, he adds.

Chloraseptic throat spray can provide temporary relief for pain, irritation, and soreness in the throat, per Dr. Peterson. "It's instant," she says. "You spray it on, it works immediately, and so it takes away the tickle in the back of the throat."

Throat coat tea has licorice and slippery elm, which is licorice-based, and it almost, well, coats the throat, creating a barrier to soothe inflammation, making it a great option for a nagging cough, says Dr. Peterson who sips on the tea daily herself.

Again, if you’ve had a persistent cough for more than two weeks, Dr. Hanak says to contact your healthcare provider. Per Dr. Peterson, other signs to call your doctor about include shortness of breath, wheezing, high fever, chest pain, and hemoptysis (coughing up blood).

Painter’s tape is used to create crisp lines, protect ceilings and trim, and make a painting job look like it was done by a professional. Using painter’s tape, however, can be trickier than it looks.

While experienced painters sometimes cut in without tape, beginners usually use painter’s tape to mask off areas that they don’t want to paint and then peel the paint off to reveal perfectly painted lines.

This guide will teach you about the different types of painter’s tape, and how to use this kind of tape around doors, windows, and other areas you want to protect from paint spatter.

These are the most common types of painter’s tape you’re likely to see at your local home improvement store. The best painter’s tape for you depends on the task at hand.

Multipurpose painter’s tape is suitable for most common household projects. It has a medium level of adhesion and sticks to most painted walls without causing any damage. It can also be used on floors, trim, baseboards, tile, and glass. Some tape brands use colors to differentiate their painter’s tape varieties, and most blue tape is considered multipurpose tape.

When painting walls, painter’s tape can be used to protect ceilings, baseboards, and trim. The following steps will help ensure that your paint lines are crisp and that no paint splatters onto unwanted surfaces.

Before starting any painting project, it’s important to ensure that all of the surfaces are clean and free of dust. This is especially important when using painter’s tape since it won’t adhere properly to dirty surfaces.

After cleaning, make sure everything has dried completely before moving on to the next step.

The next step is identifying the areas you need to protect. In most rooms, this means the ceiling, the trim around windows and doors, and baseboards.

Rip 1-foot-long pieces of tape, which are easier to handle. Apply them to the intended area in a straight line. Start in the middle of each piece, smoothing it down with a finger or putty knife as you go. If any pieces bubble or lay unevenly, it’s best to pull them up and try again.

While painter’s tape can usually remain in place for hours, or even days, without damaging the surface underneath, it’s best to remove the tape as soon as possible.

Slowly peel the tape off the trim at a 45-degree angle. If any sections don’t come off easily, a putty knife usually helps.

When it’s time to paint your trim, you’ll need to protect your walls, floors, and ceiling from paint splatters.

When painting trim, painter’s tape can be used to protect hard flooring like wood and tile as well as carpeting. It’s especially important to protect wall-to-wall carpeting from paint because it’s really hard to get paint out of carpet.

Ensure that the floor is clean, and then apply a wide piece of painter’s tape along the edge where the baseboards meet the ground, working one foot at a time. For carpets, use a putty knife to tack the tape down so that no carpeting is exposed to paint while you work.

The next step is applying painter’s tape to the walls where they meet the trim. Ensure that walls are clean and free of dust before applying the tape along all of the trim.

If you painted your walls first, it’s important to wait 24 hours—or until the paint has dried completely—before using painter’s tape to ensure the paint doesn’t peel off when it’s removed.

“Choosing the correct painters tape for the surface makes a big difference for me. It ensures the tape adheres and functions as it should. I find it easier to tape long surfaces with a partner. Together, we pull out the correct length of tape and line it up before setting over the surface. While not mandatory, it does make for even lines with less fuss.

At the end of the day, taping is tedious and takes time to get everything perfect. Don’t rush and pay attention to the details. It will make for a cleaner and better looking paint job when everything is finished.”

If your trim includes crown molding or any other style of molding that abuts the ceiling, the ceiling will need to be protected. It would be easy to assume that white trim paint won’t be noticeable on a ceiling, but trims are typically painted with semi-gloss paint, whereas it’s generally recommended that ceilings are painted with flat paint.

Apply paint along the upper edge of the trim, smoothing it down as you work.

When painting a ceiling, protect your walls—especially if they’re freshly painted. Similarly, if the space has crown molding, tape can be used to maintain its crisp, white color.

If the room in question doesn’t have an upper molding, apply painter’s tape directly onto the walls where they meet the ceiling. If the walls have recently been painted, make sure they have dried completely.

Ensure the tape lies perfectly flush with the edge of the ceiling and doesn’t have any bubbles or ripples. Take your time—it’s important to ensure the tape is straight.

If the room has a molding that meets the ceiling, use the same technique detailed above, applying the tape to the molding instead.

Press down the edges of the tape using your fingers or a putty knife, ensuring it adheres to the molding. When working with a particularly detailed molding, this may require a bit more time.

Once you’ve finished painting the ceiling, remove the tape from the walls or moldings as soon as possible. If the paint has already dried, you may need to use the straight edge of a putty knife to score the tape in order to cleanly peel it away without causing the ceiling paint to chip.

Gas snowblowers are the best tools for heavy-duty snow removal jobs at home. They’re effective against snowbanks piled up to two feet high, and can clear out larger areas, including wide driveways and walking paths. If you live somewhere that gets hit by many large snowstorms each season, and you’ve got a considerable-sized area to clear, then this is what you need.

Not everyone needs a gas snowblower, though. For homes with smaller needs, they may feel like more trouble than they’re worth. Gas-powered machines require more maintenance. Plus, they’re usually quite heavy, which makes them hard to use on tiered walkways with steps. If heavy snow is a rare occurrence, we’d recommend going with an electric snowblower instead, which will be more nimble and won’t produce any fumes.

Most gas snowblowers feature either a single-stage or two-stage mechanism, depending on how the snowblower collects, processes, and expels snow.

Also known as “snow throwers,” they use an auger to pull in snow, break it up, and throw the snow out through the chute in a single action.

These also use an auger to pull in snow, but then mash it up with a spinning impeller before spitting it out through the chute. Separating the snow-pulling and snow-crushing/throwing mechanisms allows the two-stage snowblowers to move snow more quickly and throw it a longer distance, which can be helpful when clearing larger driveways. These robust machines also work better on gravel, since their augers are slightly elevated and won’t draw rocks into the tines.

With an extra component that requires a more powerful engine to operate properly, two-stage snowblowers are generally larger, making them more difficult to maneuver around tight spaces. If you have a small walkway or gardens with uneven ground to avoid, operating one may feel unwieldy. As you might expect, they also cost more than their single-stage counterparts.

As with deciding between electric and gas snowblowers, consider the size of the area you normally clear before picking a single- or two-stage model. Single-stage snowblowers work well for clearing walking paths and the average-sized two- to four-car paved driveway. Dual-stage models are ideal for clearing deep snow from long paved or gravel driveways.

A snowblower’s clearing width and intake height determine how much snow it can move with each pass. The larger the snowblower’s intake, the more quickly you can get the job done.

Most single-stage gas-powered snowblowers have intakes that range from 22 to 24 inches wide, while two-stage snowblowers can have intakes that are up to 32 inches wide. The height of the snowblower’s intake, which is sometimes referred to as “clearing depth,” indicates the maximum snow depth it can handle. The intake height for a gas snowblower ranges from 18 to 21 inches.

Purchasing the snowblower with the widest, tallest intake may seem like a no-brainer, but keep in mind that there’s a correlation between intake dimensions and the overall size of the snowblower. The largest gas snowblowers will have a harder time maneuvering through tight turns. Ultimately, you should choose the smallest option that can effectively clear the paths you need and the amount of snow you expect to see.

A snowblower's job is to take snow in front of you and move it somewhere out of the way. With a more powerful snowblower, you can send the snow farther away. That’s crucial when you’re clearing out a large space, and you need to pile it up dozens of feet from you. Single-stage gas snowblowers can launch snow up to 40 feet, while two-stage snowblowers have a snow-throwing range of 50 feet or more.

Some gas snowblowers come equipped with a throttle on the dashboard that allows you to adjust the throwing distance by changing the speed of the impeller, allowing you to target a specific snow-piling plot.

Beyond the basics, many gas snowblowers have additional features that make them easier to use. Since they weigh hundreds of pounds, many are self-propelled, so you don’t have to push them forward. Most also have electric starters that eliminate the need to pull a starter cord. Some even have built-in power steering that automatically slows one wheel for easy turning.

Other quality-of-life features abound: You may find dashboard controls that allow you to change the chute direction and snowblower speed with one hand, LED headlights for late-night (or early morning) jobs, and handlebars that heat up to keep your hands nice and toasty as you work.

We selected the best gas snowblowers based on recommendations from the Popular Mechanics test team, as well as research and personal testing experience. Some of our picks were recommended by PM test editor Brad Ford, who has logged hours and hours testing snowblowers over the years. The final lineup includes a mix of single-stage gas-powered models for small to moderate driveways that can house four to six cars, and larger two-stage models that can clear snow from the longer and larger driveways you usually find on rural properties.

There are a few things you need to do to make sure your gas snowblower runs smoothly through the season:

First, check the oil level between each use and top off as needed. You should change the oil once a year, even if you didn’t use your snowblower all winter.

Change the spark plug at the beginning of each season to ensure it starts smoothly. A dirty spark plug can make it difficult to get your snowblower going, especially in extreme cold.

Lastly, small gas engines require plenty of airflow to run smoothly, so check the air filter once a season and replace it if it's dirty.

Snowblowers use unleaded gasoline—the same thing you put in your car—which begins to degrade after about 60 days. Running a snowblower with old fuel can clog the carburetor and make the engine hard to start, so you should never store your snowblower with fuel left in the tank.

Before putting your snowblower to bed for the season, either run the fuel tank dry or add fuel stabilizer, which keeps gasoline fresh for up to 24 months, to the tank. If you use fuel stabilizer, run the snowblower for a minute or two after adding it to the tank to circulate it through the engine.

A snowblower should be able to handle the lion’s share of your snow removal duties, but you should definitely keep a snow shovel around to clean up whatever the snowblower leaves behind. You may also want to save the gas and dig out with a shovel after a light, soft snowfall.

#3485. 13 Black Women Share How They Found the Strength to Survive—and Thrive—With Breast Cancer

📅 Oct 18, 2021  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When it comes to Black women’s breast health, the statistics rarely tell a hopeful story. Black women are diagnosed with breast cancer at a younger median age (60, compared with 62 for white women) and are 38% more likely to die from the disease. They have the lowest survival rate for every known stage of breast cancer. And about one in five Black women with breast cancer are diagnosed with hard-to-treat triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), more than any other racial or ethnic group.

But here’s what the numbers will never capture: the phone calls at 3 a.m., the head-shaving parties, the therapy sessions that cracked something open, the moment a woman looks at her daughter or granddaughter and decides they are reason enough to fight. Black women have always been more than a data point, and perhaps nowhere is that truer than in the way they face, survive, and remake their lives after a breast cancer diagnosis.

The stories below belong to 13 Black women who refused to be reduced to a statistic. They are individuals with careers, children, humor, faith, rage, and joy whose experiences with breast cancer changed them in ways both devastating and profound. In finding their own paths through treatment and beyond, they’ve built something the data can’t quantify: community, purpose, and a hard-won blueprint for survival they’re passing on to every woman who comes after them.

Monique Bass, 53, had always been proactive about her health. A single mother of twin sons and grandmother of four, she started getting annual mammograms at 40. But it was a self-exam at 47 while lying in bed one night that changed everything. She found a lump that didn’t feel right.

What followed were three tissue biopsies of the lump over three years, each coming back benign. Doctors wanted to keep monitoring. Bass pushed back. The lump was growing, and she knew her body well enough to realize something was wrong. She insisted they remove the lump (a procedure called a lumpectomy) and test it. The diagnosis: stage 1 invasive ductal carcinoma that was aggressive enough to require immediate chemotherapy.

Bass got the call while sitting in a car wash. “I felt like I was in The Twilight Zone,” she says. “Not in a million years did I think I would ever hear the words, ‘You have cancer.’” The timing made everything harder. It was 2020, the height of the pandemic, so she navigated much of her journey alone. Adding “another emotional layer,” she was also going through a divorce. Treatment included four rounds of doxorubicin, four rounds of paclitaxel, and 21 radiation treatments. Losing her hair hit the hardest. “But the silver lining is that it grew back even more beautifully after treatment,” Bass says.

Throughout it all her faith anchored her. “I’m not a deeply religious person, but I believe in God, and I knew he had me in the palm of his hand,” Bass says. On the other side of treatment, she founded What’s Behind the Bra?, a New Jersey nonprofit that distributes comfort care packages to breast cancer patients year-round and hosts a weekly podcast of the same name every Sunday at 7:30 p.m. ET.

Jaqueline Beale, 64, comes from a family with a long history of cancer, including her mother, who had breast cancer. That’s why it wasn’t a huge surprise when, at 40, she found a lump during a breast self-exam. Even though a sonogram and mammogram came back clear, it was obvious that something wasn’t right, so Beale got a biopsy.

When the radiologist called with her result—stage 1 breast cancer, which means the cancer hadn’t spread beyond where the abnormal cells had developed—she could hear a lot of noise in the background. He said, “I’m in New York trying to hail a cab, but you have breast cancer. You need to find a breast surgeon.” Despite the bluntness, Beale says she couldn’t help but find the humor in the situation, which is something she carried with her through treatment.

“I made sure that I surrounded myself with laughter and love,” she says, “and I told my family that I knew they loved me, but I also needed a lot of laughter to get through it.”

She recalls one incident in an elevator after her sister had taken her to a chemo treatment. “I had been sick as a dog after, and a woman got in and asked if I was okay. My sister said, ‘Oh, she’s fine. She’s just got a little bit of cancer.’ The woman looked horrified, but I laughed. My family knew that their sense of humor is what I needed.”

Annita White, 51, was five months late for her mammogram. As a single mother of two boys, a higher education administrator, and a first-year PhD student, she always had something more pressing. When she finally went, she expected the usual letter in the mail saying everything was fine. Instead, she got a call. There was a mass that hadn’t been there before.

By the time the biopsy came back, she already knew. A technician had commented on how hard the mass was, and White felt the diagnosis in her spirit before the words were ever spoken. The confirmation came the next day. “When I got the call, I fell to the floor and cried tears of desperation,” she says. “My first thought was, ‘Lord, I can’t do this.’”

What followed was hard in every way: chemotherapy, losing her breasts, losing her hair, which had always been full. “Being bald left me feeling vulnerable and insecure,” White says. “Physically, losing my breasts and going through chemotherapy made me feel like my body was no longer my own.” Through it all the fear that drove her was running out of time—time to see her sons become men and to meet her grandchildren someday.

Her faith, which she’d been privately wrestling with for years, met her exactly where she was. She’d been asking God to truly show up in her life. “He did,” she says. “In the middle of one of the hardest seasons of my life, he carried me through.”

Three years later White is cancer-free, her hair is back, and she recently finished her PhD. She now walks in her purpose as Dr. Nita, a member of the African American Breast Cancer Alliance survivor network, sharing her story and those of others facing the disease. “What once felt like the end became the beginning of something greater,” she says.

Marylande Regis, 38, had been in her new Georgia home for four months when everything changed. A registered nurse, realtor, wife, and mother of three, she had just stopped breastfeeding her nine-month-old when she found the lump. Her baby had never latched well on that side. When she messaged her provider, the on-call response was that it was probably a clogged milk duct and that she was too young to worry about it being something more serious. Regis didn’t accept that. She called back and left a message, insisting that something was wrong. “Another ob-gyn saw the message and encouraged me to make an office appointment immediately,” she says. Within two weeks she had a diagnosis: aggressive stage 2B, BRCA1-positive breast cancer. Within two more weeks what had been one tumor and one impacted lymph node had become three tumors and four impacted lymph nodes. Regis was 36.

“I was relieved that I advocated for myself,” she says. “I was proud that fear didn’t factor into my decision.”

What followed was relentless. Regis went through six months of chemotherapy, a bilateral mastectomy, six weeks of radiation, a total hysterectomy (to eliminate her body’s main source of estrogen), and breast reconstruction, all over a span of roughly 18 months. She lost friends to cancer along the way, as well as a cousin diagnosed around the same time. Her faith was, as Regis describes it, “shaken and stirred and flipped every way possible.” She wrote the important letters. She wrote her own obituary. She was practical about it. “I wanted to be positive, but I am also very realistic,” Regis says.

The hardest part wasn’t any single treatment—it was losing control. As her family’s go-to planner for everything from doctor’s appointments to vacations, she found it foreign to ask for help. But when she told her parents, her mother showed up and never left. “We joke, but my mom never went home,” Regis says. “This is her house now.” Her husband, Kory, came to every appointment and held steady even through both of them losing their jobs. “He deserves a trophy,” she says.

As the daughter of Haitian immigrants, raised to be everything her parents couldn’t be, Regis says the fight was never just medical. Today strength looks different than it did before cancer. It looks like making it to the bus stop with her son, who has autism. It looks like seeing her oldest finish nursing school and work toward becoming an ob-gyn. It looks like watching her youngest hit milestones like learning to talk. “I would have missed that if I didn’t fight,” she says. “The clouds are moving. The sun has decided to show itself. I can’t wait to soak it all in.”

Illness had always been an afterthought for 75-year-old Judy Fambrough-Billingsley. Why would it cross her mind? She walked three miles every day, swam laps all summer, took Zumba classes, and played soccer for 30 years. Judy Fambrough-Billingsley had never smoked or drank, and she didn’t even need to take medication. Being healthy wasn’t something she worked at; it was just who she was. A naturalized American citizen born in Germany, she traveled back to her home country regularly to visit family. Her life was full and active.

So when she felt a lump—and then another six months later—she ignored them. After all, she’d had a clean mammogram earlier that year. But when she noticed them growing and multiplying, she went back to her doctor. The results were not what she expected.

“I was shocked,” Fambrough-Billingsley says. She suddenly found herself navigating an entirely new set of experiences: prescription bottles, hospital procedures, lab processes—the whole medical system. “Being a part of a world where sickness exists had never been my world until now,” she says.

What carried her through was far older than her diagnosis. She grew up during the Civil Rights Era, the daughter of a successful Black entrepreneur who built his business during tumultuous years in US history. Both her parents were lifetime members of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP). She was president of the NAACP Youth Council and an active member of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee. Fambrough-Billingsley knows something about fighting for your life in systems that weren’t built for you.

“Growing up during the Civil Rights Era contributed to this era of fighting,” she says. “Never giving up.” That same spirit—the one her parents modeled, the one she fell into during her work as a community organizer and across decades of a life fully lived—is the one she’s bringing to this too. She may still be on her breast cancer journey, but this is not her first fight.

Taylor Johnson was nearly 30 when she found the lump on her left breast. A mom and content creator who loves trying new things and staying active, she went to her primary care doctor, who referred her for an ultrasound and a mammogram. Johnson was denied a mammogram—she was told she was too young. She pushed anyway, got a biopsy (in lieu of the mammogram), and days later, had a diagnosis: stage 2 triple-negative breast cancer.

“My reaction was a very deep pause, trying to process what my doctor had told me,” Johnson says. “It then hit me, and all I could do was cry.”

She’s in the middle of her treatment plan, which includes six months of chemotherapy, followed by surgery and three weeks of radiation. After finishing chemo Johnson felt confident that would be the hardest part of her journey, both physically and emotionally. But her own health wasn’t her only concern: She knew her five-year-old was watching her every move, and she wanted to stay stable for her daughter. “I was so used to always being the strong one,” Johnson says. “This definitely tested that.” She held it together not because it came easily, but because she understood what her daughter was absorbing.

She is grateful for the community that rallied around her while she waited. Weekly prayer calls, rotating visits from family and friends, and a medical team at Mayo Clinic in Arizona that went above and beyond carried her through.

Though Johnson hasn’t finished treatment yet, completing chemo clarified something for her. “It has reassured me how strong I truly am,” she says. Strength, she’s learned, isn’t just physical. The emotional and mental muscle it takes to keep going counts just as much.

Jessica Ncube, 41, built her life around caring for others. A licensed clinical social worker, the co-owner of a mental health practice, and a mom, she was always in motion. Her life revolved around showing up for others and helping them navigate their hardest moments. “Looking back I do not know that I was always receiving,” she says.

Her diagnosis came quietly. A routine mammogram caught it; nothing in her body had felt amiss. That silence, Ncube says, is exactly why early detection matters. When she got the news she went into survival mode. “You hear the words, but it takes time for them to land,” she says.

Ncube’s journey started with a lumpectomy. When her margins came back unclear, meaning the pathologist could not determine whether all the cancerous cells were removed, she made the decision to move forward with a double mastectomy with reconstructive surgery. “Losing a part of your body, even when it is lifesaving, is still a loss,” she says. “The hardest part was learning how to sit in both gratitude and grief at the same time.”

As someone professionally wired to be strong for others, letting that tendency go was its own kind of work. “Strength became less about holding everything in and more about being honest about what I needed,” Ncube says. Her faith grounded her, and her community showed up, but she also had to unlearn something deeply familiar. “As a Black woman there is a resilience that is almost ingrained in us,” she says. “I had to learn how to balance that resilience with softness.”

After completing treatment Ncube has become more present and deliberate. Her definition of strength has changed entirely. “Strength today is softness. It is honesty. It is allowing myself to receive, to rest, and to not have all the answers,” she says.

Ameiko Newman, 40, didn’t learn of her diagnosis through a routine screening. She was rushed to the hospital for abdominal pain, and that’s when she learned she had breast cancer. Life before her diagnosis had been stressful, uncertain, a season of not quite knowing where she was headed. In a strange way, cancer gave her direction.

“For me, cancer saved me,” she says. “It made me a better person. It made me fight harder, push through the hard times and know what I want in life.”

Newman is still in treatment, receiving chemotherapy and immunotherapy every week, and she is honest about what that looks like: There are good days and bad days. She goes to church faithfully, prays every day, and tries to stay positive through it all.

What has surprised her most is that in the middle of fighting for her own life, she found her purpose: helping others fight for theirs. Newman rebranded her business entirely around that mission and started designing scarves with chemo patients in mind. “I started making them mainly for my Black women because you know we love having style,” she says. But a wide variety of women from all over have since bought them.

She wakes up every day, gets her kids ready, works out, and moves through life. Faith, she says, is what makes that possible. Her message to any Black woman who’s just gotten diagnosed is both simple and hard-won: “Cancer doesn’t define you. You define you. Even though you’re fighting for your life, help someone else fight for theirs.”

Charlotte Connor, 38, had just undergone a breast augmentation when she felt a lump in her breast. “When I saw my doctor, they weren’t concerned, in part because of my young age,” says Connor, who was 30 years old at the time. She had a mammogram and ultrasound to check for signs of cancer, but no MRI. “Looking back now I wish I would have asked for that MRI,” she says.

At the first checkup six months later, they told her she was fine. At the second—one year after the original lump was found—the lump had tripled in size. Her doctor ordered a biopsy, and her diagnosis was confirmed: stage 2A ductal carcinoma, which means the cancer was growing but contained to the breast and surrounding lymph nodes.

“When they told me I had cancer, I was calm,” she says. Her mindset immediately shifted from a sinking feeling to being optimistic about the future. “I focused on getting healthy again,” says Connor, who started fantasizing about what she would do after treatment.

She remembers thinking, I may be feeling sick and throwing up from chemotherapy, but what restaurants am I going to go to after all this is over? It’s an outlook that didn’t necessarily come naturally to her, either. “I’m not even overly optimistic in life—I think I’m a realist—but in this situation, I shifted to being overly optimistic.”

Part of that mindset shift was for her young daughter. “I couldn’t be pessimistic; I had to be strong for her,” she says. “And I knew that how I acted in this situation was how she was going to mirror things and react to stressful situations when she got older.”

Connor’s other pillar of support came from a tight-knit circle of friends and family, who threw her a head-shaving party before she lost her hair due to chemo. “Making things fun and lighthearted like that got me through my darkest, saddest moments,” she says.

It was 1999, and Donna Culmer had just received a clean mammogram. A few months later she felt a stone-size bump on her right side, so she went back to her ob-gyn to get it checked out. They redid the mammogram on both breasts, and while what she’d felt was actually a fibrocystic lump, the imaging found a small, not palpable spot on her left side that turned out to be a stage 1 cancer. “I was stunned,” Culmer says. “I didn’t even know what questions to ask.” She drove back to work in a daze and told her boss what had happened.

At 46 she was serving in the Navy as an instructor and trainer to personnel who were working alongside the Marines. She was married to a Marine and raising three children. There was no good moment for this. There never is.

Four months of chemotherapy followed, with nausea and exhaustion that never quite let up. Her doctors were concerned that the chemo might not work, so she had a mastectomy. Through it all her community refused to let her go through it alone. Her father drove her to every session and sat with her. Her church came to her, literally; services were held at her house.

Twenty-seven years later, Culmer, now 73, has had time to reflect. “As Black women, we have been through challenges our whole lives,” she says. “This is just one more.” Her advice is practical and clear. “Don’t panic and ask questions, even if you have to write them down,” Culmer says. “The treatments available now are so much better than what I had.”

One morning, Patricia Fox, 38, woke up with a lump in her right breast that wasn’t there the night before—she was just 26 years old at the time. Fox scheduled an appointment with her ob-gyn that day. “They told me that I was a young Black woman and had dense breasts, and it was likely a cyst,” she says. “They were ready to turn me away.”

The lump, which was a little smaller than a golf ball, seemed strange to Fox. Thankfully, she listened to her intuition. “There was something telling me to get it checked out,” she says, so her doctor did a biopsy just to be sure.

Her results came back as estrogen receptor-positive stage 2A breast cancer, meaning her cancer had estrogen receptors that would respond to hormone therapy drugs. Fox says she’ll never forget her doctor’s words: “Patricia, you’ll be a breast cancer survivor.” That sentiment set the tone for her mindset as a fighter. “It made me think that I could face this and survive this,” she says.

Still, cancer treatments quickly overwhelmed Fox, and soon she faced her lowest point: crying on the floor for hours, feeling like having cancer at such a young age was a punishment for being a “bad person.” But she pulled herself out of it with hard work in therapy.

“I found that talking to a therapist helped me process my diagnosis and examine the areas of my life—certain people and my own behavior patterns—that were cancerous for me that I needed to do something about,” she says. “While therapy didn’t make the pain go away, it did make how I coped with it much better.”

In the Black community, therapy is often taboo, says Fox, but it’s become her mission to dispel that belief. “Therapy fortified me to get through cancer,” she says, “and I tell everyone that they don’t have to suffer in silence.”

Annette Colden, 63, was sitting at her sister’s bedside during the last days of her final battle with breast cancer when the conversation turned to screenings. Her sister had been through cervical cancer and two bouts of breast cancer before passing away from the disease. “She made me promise I’d get my mammograms,” Colden says.

Colden’s first two yearly mammograms were clear. On the third there was a spot, but she was told by her doctor that things looked okay and it wasn’t cancer—she just had lumpy breasts.

“There’s this inner feeling you have,” she says. “Something was inside me saying, That’s not right.” Her sister’s doctor had told her the exact same thing, but Colden was so happy to hear she didn’t have cancer that she didn’t follow up.

A year later she went back for a mammogram, and that’s when her doctors found it: stage 0 breast cancer, meaning only abnormal cells had been found. She was 42. “Now, looking back, I always tell women that if something doesn’t feel right, get a second opinion.”

Despite the diagnosis Colden remained calm. “I’m a religious person,” she says. “In my prayer I said, ‘Lord, if you will be my pilot on this journey, I’ll be your copilot.’” She immediately felt a sense of peace.

After her recovery she decided to share that calming presence with other women going through breast cancer at a support group through Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center, where she had treatment. “I say, ‘It’s going to be a long road. If you don’t want to travel it alone, I’m right there with you.’”

And she means it. Sometimes that involves talking with someone at 3 a.m. when the pain from chemotherapy hits. Other times it means sending food delivery boxes to the homes of people with breast cancer who can’t muster the strength to leave. “My sister did a lot of support work before she got really sick,” she says. “This is her vision.”

Ricki Fairley, 69, was in the security line at the airport for a work trip when she got a call from her doctor. They’d found a peanut-like lump under her nipple that turned out to be cancer.

Fairley, who was 55 at the time, said, “I don’t have time for this right now. I’ll call you when I get to my destination.” A couple days later she found out she had stage 3A TNBC, and it was really aggressive. Compared with other breast cancers, TNBC has fewer targeted-therapy treatment options, often spreads beyond the breasts, and is more likely to recur. Black women have nearly three times the risk of developing triple-negative breast cancer.

Her diagnosis woke her up. “It made me realize that I needed to get all of the ‘cancers’ out of my life,” Fairley says. During treatment that first year which involved a double mastectomy, aggressive chemotherapy, and radiation, she quit her job and started her own company. Then she filed for divorce and later sold her house. “I quit my life and started a new one, and changed everything,” she says. “I had to learn that my peace is nonnegotiable. I really think that stress caused my breast cancer.”

A year later her life changed again. Fairley was diagnosed with metastatic breast cancer and told she had two years to live. After finding out her current doctor had only treated two previous cases of TNBC and both women had died in eight months, she found a new doctor, who was well-versed in current research and treatments for the disease. She beat cancer a second time. “I remember sitting at my daughter’s graduation thinking, Okay, I made it. What’s next for me?” Fairley says. The answer: helping others. In the years that followed, Fairley cofounded The Black Breast Cancer Alliance, which works to reduce the mortality rate for Black women.

An espresso refers to how you prepare a drink rather than the type of coffee bean you use or its roast. Essentially, espresso is concentrated coffee “generally prepared quickly under pressure,” Welch says. The difference between manual and automatic espresso machines also comes down to technique. Keep in mind that manual espresso makers can have automated features.

All automatic machines use an “electrical component to activate the pump to push the water through the coffee bed,” Welch says. Automating the extraction process allows you to achieve a more consistent beverage. Although you give up a degree of control and customization, you gain a consistently dependent beverage. Stovetop espresso makers and pod-style capsule machines also make concentrated coffee drinks, but aren’t technically espresso machines.
