Sugar maples and burning bushes may be spectacular sources of fall color, but they are far from being the only examples of shifting seasonal hues. In many cases, the most popular fall plants offer only a brief burst of red or copper before dropping their leaves almost overnight. If you’re looking for something a little less predictable or fleeting, a few lesser-known options offer unique elements like berries and bark.

October presents a golden opportunity to show some love to these tough perennials and shrubs for fall color. Thanks to a combination of warm soil temperatures and crisp, cool air, they will hit their stride that much quicker. Shrubs and hardy perennials planted now can settle in with minimal stress, developing deep, healthy root networks long before the ground freezes.

Make some room for golden, swaying grasses, deep burgundy bark and stems, and bright berries to feed local wildlife. By expanding your palette, you’ll create a mult-layered and multiseasonal array of tone and texture. Hopefully, these shrubs and perennials will inspire you with diverse visuals that often sustain well into winter. Make more of these dazzling hidden gems for effortless fall color that can keep your garden glowing for months.

Getting plants into the ground in October is one of the smartest moves you can make. Unlike spring planting, where young roots must cope with rising heat and drought, fall soil holds abundant summer warmth while rains keep moisture levels consistent. This allows young roots and crowns to focus their energy underground.

Just make sure you select plants that will grow well in your USDA hardiness zone. In northern zones (zones 3-6), aim to complete planting by the third week of the month, so roots can anchor before soil temperatures drop sharply. However, in the south (zones 7-10), you can plant comfortably well through November.

Prepare your planting holes by top-dressing with rich organic compost. This gives young roots a gentle nutrient boost without forcing tender growth that could be damaged by early frosts. A little mushroom compost makes an excellent soil amendment for these fall plantings. Either add a little aged natural mushroom compost, or a handful of Espoma Organic Natural Mushroom Blend, available from Walmart. This is ideal for conditioning planting holes and encouraging deep roots.

Once planted, water your tough perennials and fall shrubs deeply. Apply a 2-3-inch (5-8cm) blanket of organic wood mulch or shredded leaves, as this will help to insulate the roots against winter freeze-thaw cycles. Then sit back and enjoy the lovely spectacle of color and texture these hidden gems bring all season long.

If you were to choose just one underused native shrub for fall color, fothergilla (Fothergilla spp.) deserves the top spot. While famous for its honey-scented, brush-like spring flowers, autumn is when this compact shrub can really steal the show. Beginning in October, its leathery, witch-hazel-like leaves undergo a mesmerizing transformation, mixing vibrant shades of electric yellow, fiery orange, deep crimson, and rich violet on a single branch.

Hardy in zones 4-8, fothergilla grows 3-5 feet (90-150cm) tall with a neat, mounded habit. It flourishes in full sun to partial shade, though full sun produces the most intense colors. Fothergilla prefers moist, acidic, well-draining soil. Add mulch annually with pine needles or acidic leaf compost to maintain a low soil pH. Alkaline, chalky soils can cause leaf yellowing. Pair this shrub with blue-toned dwarf conifers or Japanese forest grass for gorgeous contrast. To enjoy these dazzling tones in miniature, you can buy Mount Airy Dwarf Fothergilla from Nature Hills.

Virginia sweetspire (Itea virginica) is a native deciduous shrub that can even put traditional burning bushes to shame. Forming arching mounds 3-5 feet (90-150cm) high, Henry's Garnet produces fragrant, cascading flower spires in early summer. As autumn arrives, its glossy leaves shift to a saturated garnet-red and port-wine purple that survive further into late autumn than almost any other shrub. You can buy Henry’s Garnet from Plant Addicts for long-lasting shocks of crimson and purple.

Hardy in zones 5-9, it is highly adaptable. It thrives in full sun, partial shade, and even heavy shade (though sun yields the brightest red tones). It is tolerant of wet, clay soils, making it perfect for rain gardens, low-lying garden spots, or erosion control on slopes. Just water consistently during its first year. Don't worry about heavy pruning, as its natural arching habit is part of its charm. Plant it alongside yellow-foliaged shrubs or golden grasses for a rich border display.

For structural elegance that combines dazzling fall foliage with year-round visual interest, the paperbark maple (Acer griseum) is well worth a spot. Growing slowly into a small, well-behaved specimen tree or large shrub, reaching 15-20 feet (4.5-6m) tall, its trifoliate leaves turn copper, ruby-red and scarlet in late October. After the leaves drop, its cinnamon-brown bark peels back to reveal polished mahogany wood that glows in winter sun. It may take a few years to develop those distinctive colors, but you can buy Paperbark Maple Container Trees from Amazon.

Hardy in zones 4-8, paperbark maple grows best in full sun to partial shade in well-drained, average-to-rich soil. It is virtually pest- and disease-free, and requires no complicated pruning. Just don't allow newly planted specimens to bake in severely dry, compacted clay without moisture. Underplant with evergreen groundcovers like vinca or dark hellebores to accentuate its cinnamon trunk.

While most fall plants deliver on leaf color, Coralberry Magic Berry (Symphoricarpos) adds a pop of vibrant fruit. Growing into a rounded shrub 3-4 feet (90-120cm) tall and wide, in October its stems are filled with vivid clusters of pink-purple berries. These jewel-like berry clusters remain on the bare branches well into winter, providing valuable food for songbirds. For a softer bubblegum berry tone, buy Proven Winners’ Proud Berry Pink Coralberry from Walmart for exquisite late-season color.

Hardy in zones 4-9, coralberry handles full sun to partial shade and adapts to a wide variety of soil types, including lean or clay soils. Cut back stems lightly in early spring to encourage fresh, fruit-bearing new growth. Avoid high-nitrogen feeds now, which encourage leaf growth at the expense of berries. Interplant with dark purple ninebark (physocarpus) or golden panic grasses for a lush fall tonal contrast.

Don’t forget flowers for autumn joy! For late-season color just as frost approaches, pineapple sage (Salvia elegans) is an unexpected treat. True to its name, rubbing its soft leaves releases an irresistible pineapple fragrance. Rising 3-5 feet (90-150cm) tall, this fast-growing plant erupts in October with tubular scarlet flower spikes. These act as a magnet for hummingbirds and pollinators. You can buy Greenwood Nursery Store’s Pineapple Sage from Amazon as double starter pots.

Hardy as a perennial in zones 8-11 (and grown as a late-season annual or overwintered indoor pot in zones 3-7), pineapple sage loves full sun and well-draining, fertile soil. Plant in a warm, sheltered spot near patios – don't let it sit in waterlogged soil. In colder zones, take softwood cuttings in October or bring potted specimens indoors before freezes. Pair its scarlet blooms with purple salvias or dark sweet potato vine for a fall finale.

This unique perennial is native to Arkansas and Oklahoma, but easy to grow anywhere in zones 4-9. Arkansas blue star (Amsonia hubrichtii) is regarded as one of the finest native perennials, thanks to its fall transformation. Through summer, it forms a dense, feathery 3-foot (90cm) mound of needle-like foliage, topped with soft blue spring blooms. But in October, it turns a brilliant, glowing butter-gold and amber-orange, holding its color for over a month. Buy Perennial Farm Marketplace’s Arkansas Blue Star from Amazon as live plants for fine-textured, golden foliage.

Hardy in zones 4-9, Arkansas blue star is long-lived, drought-tolerant, and deer-resistant. It performs best in full sun and well-drained soil, but otherwise this plant is not picky and is easy to grow. Plant in full sun to prevent flopping and don't disturb established clumps unnecessarily. Shear foliage back by one-third after spring flowering to encourage a dense shape. Combine its glowing plumes with purple asters or blue sedums for a classic, high-contrast fall border.

You may consider ornamental grass for texture and movement, but certain native grasses are also excellent for dynamic fall tone. Little bluestem is a gorgeous fall prairie grass option. Growing in neat tufts 2-4 feet (60-120cm) tall, its blue-green summer blades transform in October into rich shades of mahogany, coppery-orange, and warm purple, topped with fluffy, silvery seed heads that catch the light. For playful streaks of purple, Plants That Work’s Twilight Zone Little Bluestem Grass from Nature Hills is a gorgeous drought-tolerant native grass.

Hardy in zones 3-9, little bluestem (Schizachyrium scoparium) thrives in full sun and dry, lean soils where other plants struggle. Don't overwater or plant in heavy shade, which causes stems to flop. Otherwise, you can leave the coppery foliage standing in winter to provide food and shelter for songbirds and winter interest. Interplant with goldenrod, coneflowers and amsonia for luxe designer accents.

#2571. ‘Elder Millennial’ comedian Iliza Shlesinger convinced her kids ‘from the window to the wall’ is about a nap party

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The comedian on raising two little kids, the help she refuses to hide, and what being an elder Millennial mom really means.

By Sara Goldstein Updated Oct. 13, 2026

Iliza Shlesinger’s kids are allowed to watch cartoons, but nothing too grown-up. At the same time, they occasionally hear whatever’s thumping through the wall of her home office, where she’s been teaching herself to DJ. “It’s a lot of songs about female anatomy, and a lot of words we don’t use in real life,” she says.

She tries to turn it down, but doesn’t turn it off. The playlist is the exact stuff elder millennials have been getting down to since high school and college, and like many of us, she fully embraces the anthems. “These are the songs of my people,” she says. “This is called ‘Salt Shaker.’ We’re gonna go from the window to the wall, and I will tell you it’s about a nap party.”

As a fellow elder millennial myself, this jarring contrast is quite familiar. We dropped it low in the club once. Just because we’re hauling kids from school to soccer practice now, are we supposed to forget how to back that ass up?

Shlesinger, 43, has spent close to two decades turning this self-awareness into stand-up. Her 2018 Netflix hour “Elder Millennial” gave us the phrase to define a very specific subset of a generation. She’s still at it, with her latest special, “A Different Animal,” and the ongoing ILIZA! LIVE tour. On her podcast “Ask Iliza Anything,” she hands out blunt advice to strangers like the no-filter friend you trust with your most-unhinged questions. There’s a new Pampers campaign too. And there’s her home, shared with her husband, chef Noah Galuten, 4-year-old daughter and a 2-year-old son.

We got the exclusive chance to ask Shlesinger about what it means to parent as an elder millennial and how it feels to learn at 43 that Pampers wipes were never supposed to come out six at a time.

It’s no secret that motherhood requires a deep, personal shift. That doesn’t make it less difficult to navigate. You’re still the same person under there, the one who knows every word to “Salt Shaker,” and at the same time you’re turning into someone new you’re still getting to know. And though her kids aren’t newborns, the evolution continues to catch her off guard.

“I’m shocked at how often I’m re-realizing what a mom I am,” she says. Everything from the way she puts her jeans on to the fact that somehow, her phone flashlight is always on. “This didn’t happen like, five years ago. I was cool,” she offered with a declarative tone any elder millennial can relate to. Similarly, she recently snapped a naked selfie for her husband and clocked the kids’ toys in the shot. “Yes, they’re his kids, and it’s our bathroom. But that’s not hot.”

Part of parenting as a millennial is the urge to give your kids the childhood you had, or the best of it anyway, and the slow realization that you really can’t. The world they’re growing up in doesn’t match the one that made you. Shlesinger ran into that gap with a thought about her daughter. “She’s not going to go to my school and have my memories,” she says. “Once I internalized that, I got goosebumps. It’s up to me to make it just as nice as possible.” She can’t transfer the memories, so she’s salvaging the ingredients, keeping what worked and leaving the rest. “Picking all the good stuff from growing up. A scary story, a fun tradition. Letting go if it doesn’t work.”

In the name of creating new traditions, she’s gone all out with fall decor in the form of an elaborate front-yard autumn tableau, lifted straight from her Texas childhood. “I always saw the houses that decorated for fall as the ones that felt comfortable and safe, and the world feels so unsafe now,” she says. What that produced is an epic scene of controlled insanity. “So I’ve become that Texas mom. I paid cash for men to deliver bales of hay and oversized pumpkins that are rotting in the heat right now. No one on my block did it. None of my friends did it. My husband did not care.”

The display is firmly autumnal, not a drop of fake blood or oversize cobweb in sight, which is precisely why her 4-year-old objects. Her daughter wants spiders and gore, but she’s getting hay bales and tasteful gourds. “I’m like, we’re not doing that. It’s autumn.“

When asked how she manages parenting two kids alongside a bustling career, Shlesinger gives the answer few are willing to admit. “Do you want the honest answer that no one’s allowed to give, because people will say you’re unrelatable? I have a nanny. I’ve always had a nanny. We have help.”

Shlesinger believes that women are meant to raise kids in the company of other women. “It’s about letting other women help you parent. Letting other personality types in. Learning from other women.”

In fact, she didn’t crack a single manual. “I didn’t read one parenting book. I work, I have a career. I figured I’d wing it.” And she defers to her hired caregiver without ego. “Anything she said, I just did. I did not hire her to argue with her.”

She’s also clear that other women should have the same support. “If we lived in a country that valued women and parenting, and put money toward giving mothers resources so they can afford childcare, I wouldn’t have to tell you I pay for a nanny,” she says. “Other countries, the village raises them. We decided to make it very hard, for deeply misogynistic reasons, to let women be fully realized artistically and career-wise, and be mothers [at the same time].”

Like most of us, Iliza admits she’s learning as she goes. Take, for example, baby wipes. Every parent knows how hard it is to pull out just one wipe while one hand remains committed to a squirming, half-cleaned baby. Instead, you grab six. Shlesinger had always figured that was just how wipes worked.

“I thought, this seems really difficult, but all things are made difficult for moms, because the world hates moms,” she says. Then Pampers came to her with a campaign built on fixing that decades-long annoyance with a redesigned pack that releases one wipe at a time. The novelty of a company solving something is what stuck with her. “We live in a world where nothing gets done. And a corporation said, enough women talked to us about a problem, so we fixed it. I wish politics worked that way.”

For now she’s building the childhood she wants her kids to have, decorating for fall like there’s a neighborhood award at stake (there’s not), and processing all of it out loud for a living. Like so many of us, she still finds being a mom bewildering, but she’s doing her best nonetheless. “I’m constantly in awe of how little I know and how much I thought I knew,” she says. “Being in charge of everything, and then feeling so stupid most of the time. I think that’s peak parenting.”

Most cyclists, at some point, want to see how fast and hard they can go, even for just a few minutes. Riding at that nearly all-out intensity, when you’re breathing heavy and can barely talk, is likely your VO₂ max (or close to it). Scientifically speaking, VO2 max is the maximum amount of oxygen your body can take in and use and it’s a marker of your cardiovascular fitness level.

For a variety of reasons, VO₂ max naturally decreases as you get older. Fortunately, athletes are more likely to minimize this decline through consistent and purposeful training.

Read on for more information about VO₂ max by age, including how it’s determined, and how to make sure your rides help maintain this important metric as you get older.

Of all the available health and fitness metrics, VO₂ max is one of the most important. “VO₂ max is the amount of oxygen that you can breathe in, transport to the muscle, and utilize during maximal exertion and aerobic exercise,” Todd Buckingham, Ph.D., exercise physiologist at PTSportsPRO in Grand Rapids, Michigan tells Bicycling.

He explains that, besides being an indicator of overall fitness, it’s also a “gold standard” measurement of an athlete’s ability to perform well in an endurance event; essentially, the higher your VO₂ max, the greater your potential. “Once your VO₂ max gets to a certain level, other things like lactate threshold and exercise efficiency come into play. But VO₂ max is the first step in determining performance,” Buckingham says.

VO₂ max, which is measured as milliliters of oxygen per kilogram of bodyweight per minute (ml/kg/min), is most accurately assessed in a laboratory environment. During the test, you wear a specialized mask that measures inhaled oxygen and exhaled carbon dioxide while you pedal an exercise bike, run on a treadmill, or swim in a pool. Over the course of 12 to 15 minutes, you increase the exercise intensity so that, by the end of the test, you pushed your aerobic limits.

Getting access to a lab can obviously be time consuming and expensive, so you can also turn to your fitness tracker to help establish a baseline and keep tabs on how your fitness is improving. The caveat: You have to take those numbers with a grain of salt. Research shows that many wearables over- or underestimate VO₂ max by up to 10 percent.

Like many markers of health and fitness, VO₂ max naturally declines as you age. “VO₂ max tends to peak for men in their mid-to-late 20s. For women, it’s a little later, like early 30s. But after it reaches the peak, it tends to decrease,” Buckingham says.

Some research indicates that the average person can expect around a 10 percent decline in VO₂ max after age of 30. (This is particularly true for males, the study suggests.) However, the drop may be even more drastic among athletes. According to a study published in a 2024 issue of Experimental Gerontology, aging had a greater affect on VO₂ max in female runners than in sedentary women. The scientists hypothesized that the younger athletes started with a higher VO₂ max than their sedentary peers and, therefore, had further to fall. Despite this more drastic decline, active women still had better VO2 max than their non-active peers.

Generally speaking, though, age-related reductions in VO₂ max are likely due to a combination of factors, including a reduction in cardiac output. Cardiac ouput is the product of the number of times your heart beats per minute (heart rate) and the volume of blood ejected with each contraction of the heart (stroke volume). As you age, both the amount of blood your heart pumps out to the body and your maximal heart rate go down. Older individuals may also experience a reduction in blood circulation.

Age-related muscle loss, a.k.a. sarcopenia, may also be to blame for a decline in VO₂ max. “We tend to lose muscle mass and increase body fat percentage [as we get older],” Buckingham says. “Because fat does not have mitochondria and capillaries to turn oxygen into energy, we have metabolically inactive tissue.” (Mitochondria are the parts of cells that use oxygen to manufacture adenosine triphosphate (ATP), our primary energy currency.)

In other words, with lower muscle mass comes the loss of capillaries and mitochondria, making your body less energy efficient.

While everyone gets older, not everyone loses VO₂ max at the same rate. “There’s the saying, ‘We don’t stop exercising because we get old. We get old because we stop exercising,’” Buckingham says. “Just by being active, you can prolong that level of VO₂ max. Obviously, at some point, the atrophy process is going to occur. It just happens as we get older. But you can maintain it for a lot longer when you’re active than if you were just sedentary.”

If you’re already following a training program that includes a mix of easy, steady-state rides, more intense workouts, and strength training, stick with that diversified approach.

Lower intensity, or “zone 2” workouts are especially important. “When you are exercising at lower intensities, you produce more mitochondria and capillaries. Mitochondria is where the oxygen is transformed into energy, and the capillaries are the vessels that actually take the oxygen from the blood to get it into the muscle to then get it into the mitochondria to produce energy,” Buckingham explains.

Higher-intensity exercise is also essential, as it increases the size of each mitochondrion (“so we can get more oxygen into it,” Buckingham says). High-intensity exercise, Buckingham explains, necessitates a longer recovery period.

To maximize the benefits of your workouts while avoiding burnout, Buckingham recommends the 80/20 training methodology: 80 percent of your workouts are low-intensity and 20 percent are performed at higher intensities.

Strength training is also critical, as it can combat sarcopenia and maintain muscle mass. As previously noted, muscle mass is critical to VO₂ max, as it contains mitochondria and capillaries.

A pretty valid case for making these workouts a part of your regular routine: Research suggests that higher VO₂ max levels may be associated with a longer lifespan. One study published by the Journal of the American College of Cardiology followed a cohort of more than 5,000 men for 46 years. Researchers found that higher rates of cardiorespiratory fitness, as determined by VO₂ max, were associated with longer lives.

#1880. AI Landing Pages: From Project Plan to Published Page

Wondering how to use AI to build landing pages that convert without hiring a developer or designer? Want a step-by-step workflow for creating landing pages with ChatGPT and Codex, from the first project plan to a live, published page?

​In this article, you’ll discover how to plan, design, write, host, and connect AI-built landing pages using ChatGPT, Codex, GitHub, and Vercel.

The biggest misconception Dan Sanchez sees among marketers using AI is that productivity means progress. Most marketers now use AI, and many use several AI tools, so output has gone through the roof. But more output doesn’t always produce more revenue or move the right projects forward. Dan says it’s easy to chase shiny ideas. He can spend a long walk dictating new concepts to ChatGPT, yet none of them touch the work that’s actually slowing down growth.

​Every company is in one of three places: growing and struggling to keep up with that growth, plateauing and trying to break through, or declining and trying to reverse the slide. In all three cases, the job is to find the idea that fixes the problem. That might be new packaging, a new offer, a new message, a new front-end and back-end offer combination, or restructured pricing.

​No one knows the answers to those questions in advance. The only option is to test them, and nearly all of that testing now happens digitally on a landing page. A poll on social media can hint at interest, but people really vote by subscribing with their email address or making a small purchase, and both require a landing page.

​Dan found that he could only test new offers as fast as he could set up landing pages. Writing the promotional email with Claude was quick. But even with a background in graphic design, HTML, CSS, drag-and-drop builders, and WordPress, a single landing page and everything that goes with it took him four to eight hours and sometimes two days. With AI, he now builds landing pages faster, and he says the results are better than anything he could design before. That speed shortens the feedback loop on every idea.

An AI-built landing page is rarely just one page. Before building anything, Dan recommends roughly mapping the entire campaign around it.

​Define the Offer and the Goal: The starting point is what’s being tested and what visitors should do on the page. The idea might be a new package, a different price, or a new lead magnet that feeds the sales team or nurtures leads before they see an offer. The plan needs the main problem the page solves and the offer that solves it.

​Choose the Copy Approach: The next decision is which copywriting frameworks will shape the page. Marketers who don’t know the common frameworks can ask ChatGPT to recommend them. Some copy can be drafted ahead of time.

​Plan the Form: The form’s requirements come next. It could be a multi-stage form, a single form, an email-only field, or a purchase.

​Plan the Thank-You Page: Dan says this is where many marketers leave money on the table. Visitors are at their most excited right after they complete the action, so the thank-you page is the place for another offer, such as an upsell or a downsell, or a survey that captures more information as they enter the email list. If the funnel asks for more, there may be additional pages.

​Plan the Follow-Up: The plan should also cover whether an automated email sequence follows the signup. Even a simple three-part email sequence needs planning, along with what happens when someone doesn’t take the next step. A multi-stage funnel may need several email sequences, each triggered by where a person drops off.

​Mapped this way, a landing page becomes a full campaign. Dan points out that assigning a three-page funnel with two automated follow-up sequences to a team used to take a long time. That’s the scope AI can now handle.

Before moving into a coding tool such as Codex or Claude Code, Dan brainstorms and plans the entire project on the chat side of ChatGPT or Claude.
