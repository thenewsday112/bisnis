The scariest thing on a physician’s desk this October is the fourth-quarter estimated tax worksheet, propped against a 403(b) statement that hasn’t moved since spring.

Most retirement articles are written for someone worried about running out of money. You should worry about that too. On top of it, you carry a problem the average saver never meets: a tax code that takes 37 cents of every married dollar above $768,700, trims your itemized deductions once you get there, shrinks your SALT break somewhere past $505,000, and reaches two years backward to set your Medicare premiums.

Every mistake on this list costs a little more in that bracket.

This list is built for physicians with six figures before the comma, including those who collect some or all of it on a 1099.

Physicians are one of the few groups positioned to collect the maximum Social Security benefit, because most of you clear the taxable wage base ($184,500 in 2026) months before December. That makes claiming age a bigger lever for you than for almost anyone.

According to AARP, a worker at the 2026 maximum who files at 62 collects about $2,969 a month; the same worker gets $4,152 at full retirement age and roughly $5,181 by holding out until 70. Claiming early trims the benefit permanently (5/9 of 1% per month for the first 36 months, 5/12 of 1% per month beyond that), and every year you wait past full retirement age adds 8%.

Dr. Addams claimed at 62 because he “wanted the money while the government still had it.” Six months later he picked up locums shifts at $200 an hour. Before full retirement age, Social Security withholds $1 for every $2 you earn above $24,480, so his own paycheck ate his benefit. (Social Security credits some of that back at full retirement age, but the cash-flow plan he built was toast.) Semi-retired physicians are exactly the people who keep earning after they file.

Two other quirks hit doctors harder. The formula averages your 35 highest-earning years, and residency plus a late start can leave early retirees with zeros in that average; the bend points in the formula soften the blow for high earners, but pull your record at ssa.gov before assuming anything.

If you’re married, the higher earner’s claiming age also sets the survivor benefit, so delaying to 70 works like inflation-adjusted life insurance for your spouse at a price no insurer will match.

A serious diagnosis or a family tree with short branches can justify claiming early. For a healthy 62-year-old internist with a seven-figure portfolio, it’s usually the wrong call.

The textbook move as retirement approaches is to dial down risk. Physicians who take that advice too far end up with a portfolio that can’t outrun their withdrawals.

Run the timeline. Retire at 57 and your money may need to last 35 years or longer. A bond-heavy portfolio feels safe in year one and starts losing ground to inflation and withdrawals by year ten, which is why stocks still have to do some of the lifting deep into retirement.

High earners face a sneakier version of this mistake, and it lives in asset location. Taxable bond interest gets hit at ordinary rates. At 37% plus the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (which applies above $250,000 of modified AGI for married couples and has never been indexed for inflation), you hand close to 41 cents of every interest dollar to Washington before your state takes its cut.

Park bonds inside the 401(k) or 403(b), hold broad stock index funds in taxable, and look at municipal bonds if you need fixed income outside retirement accounts.

How much stock is enough? That depends on your spending, your other income, and how well you sleep through a 25% drawdown. “As little as possible” is not an allocation.

Landmark Wealth Management puts the S&P 500’s annualized return from 1926 through 2024 at about 10.3%. It’s a lovely number, and nobody in history has received it in a straight line.

Sequence-of-returns risk turns a reasonable plan into a slasher film.

Two physicians retire with the same $4 million and the same 10-year average return. Dr. Smart retires into three good years and coasts. Dr. Lucky (who, it turns out, was not) retires into a bear market, sells shares near the bottom to fund two years of travel, and never gets those shares back when prices recover. Same average, very different endings.

The 4% rule tries to account for that. William Bengen published it in 1994: withdraw 4% in year one, then adjust that dollar amount for inflation every year after. Bengen has since revised his own number upward to about 4.7%.

The real mistake is treating any single figure as permanent. Retirees who trim spending after bad years (guardrail strategies) or who keep two to three years of withdrawals in cash and short-term bonds (the bucket approach) give their stocks room to recover instead of selling them on the way down.

Pick a method, stress-test it against a crash in year one, and expect to adjust it.

Maxing your 401(k), 403(b), and 457(b) during peak earning years at 37% is a good move. The trouble starts when it’s the only move for 25 years.

Dr. Smart retires at 60 with $5 million, nearly all of it pre-tax. Required minimum distributions begin at 73 (75 if you were born in 1960 or later) and stack on top of Social Security, up to 85% of which is taxable.

Suddenly, a retired doctor is back in a high bracket, paying tax on money she deferred at a high bracket. Then Medicare notices.

Medicare’s IRMAA surcharges for 2026 begin at a modified AGI of $218,000 for couples ($109,000 single).

At the top tier, Part B jumps from the standard $202.90 a month to $689.90, plus a $91 Part D surcharge, which adds up to roughly $6,900 a year per person in surcharges alone. Medicare also uses a two-year look-back, so the income you report at 63 sets your premiums at 65.

The fix lives in the gap years. Retire at 58, delay Social Security, and you may get a decade where taxable income is the lowest it’s been since residency. Converting chunks of pre-tax money to Roth in that window, filling the lower brackets on purpose, shrinks future RMDs before they become a problem.

During your working years, fund backdoor Roth IRAs ($7,500 limit in 2026) and send part of your savings to the Roth side of your employer plan.

If you’re 50 or older and earned more than $150,000 in FICA wages last year, the IRS already made that choice for you: your 2026 catch-up contributions have to go in as Roth.

Rental income, a part-time medical director role, or expert witness work can also give you an income stream that doesn’t depend on draining one enormous tax-deferred account.

Physicians tend to underestimate retirement spending for a simple reason: they’ve never tracked it. The paycheck covered everything, so nobody looked.

Pull 12 months of actual spending from your cards and accounts, then adjust for what changes after medicine. Disability insurance, licensing fees, CME, and the commute fall away. Travel tends to swell and front-load into the first decade.

Healthcare before Medicare can run five figures a year for a couple buying their own coverage. Long-term care waits at the back of the line: Genworth figures cited in Fisher Investments’ healthcare guide put a semi-private nursing home room at roughly $111,000 a year in 2024, and federal estimates say almost 70% of people will need some form of long-term care, for about three years on average.

Errors cut in two directions. Underestimate spending, and you drain the portfolio early. Overestimate it, and you work three extra years you didn’t need, a loss that never shows up on any statement.

Anyone who practiced through 2022 watched CPI peak at 9.1% that June. Inflation cooled afterward, but prices never went back down.

The damage compounds quietly. A $200,000 annual retirement budget becomes about $361,000 in 20 years at 3% inflation. Any income stream that doesn’t adjust (a fixed annuity, a pension without a cost-of-living rider, a practice buyout paid in level installments) loses purchasing power every year you hold it.

You already know this feeling from the reimbursement side. The AMA estimates Medicare physician payment has fallen by roughly a third since 2001 once you adjust for inflation in practice costs. Give your retirement plan the same respect for erosion you’ve watched happen to your fee schedule, and build in an inflation assumption a bit above whatever the current forecast says.

The urge to leave is real, and it’s showing up earlier. In Doximity’s physician polling, the share of doctors aged 45 to 49 considering early retirement rose between 2023 and 2026, from 30% to 46%. Among emergency physicians, the figure climbed from 30% to 52%.

Burnout is a legitimate reason to change your life and a poor reason to skip the math. Retiring at 52 instead of 60 means eight fewer years of contributions, eight extra years of withdrawals, and a longer stretch buying your own health insurance before Medicare.

Several costs belong to medicine alone. If your malpractice policy is claims-made, leaving can trigger a tail coverage bill, often priced at a multiple of your annual premium, unless your employer covers it or your carrier offers a free tail at retirement age.

A few years away can complicate credentialing if you want back in, and some boards will ask for a formal re-entry program.

On the brighter side, physicians have unusual access to early withdrawals: governmental 457(b) plans carry no 10% penalty once you separate from service, at any age, and the rule of 55 lets you tap the 401(k) or 403(b) of the employer you leave in or after the year you turn 55.

A middle path often beats a hard stop. Dropping to 0.6 FTE, picking up locums, or moving into a nonclinical role keeps income flowing and lets the portfolio grow a few more years untouched.

Medicine breeds a specific condition. You hit your number, decide you need a bigger number, and a market dip convinces you to stay another year, until you’re 68 with $9 million and a partner gently asking whether you’ve thought about slowing down.

Dr. Addams (yes, him again) planned to retire at 62, then 64, then “once the kids finish school.” His youngest graduated in 2019. Money saved after you’ve crossed the finish line buys very little extra safety, and the years spent earning it come out of your healthiest stretch of life.

Hospitals are changing the math as well. Some now require cognitive and physical screening for late-career physicians past a set age, so the decision to keep practicing may not stay entirely yours.

Pick a number and a date, and write down what you’ll do the Monday after. In my experience, physicians who retire toward something stick the landing far more often than those who are simply running from the pager.

Long-term rentals generate depreciation, but for a full-time physician those losses usually land in the passive bucket, where they can’t touch clinical income.

Short-term rentals play by different rules. If the average guest stay is seven days or less, the property isn’t treated as a rental activity under the regulations. Materially participate (most commonly by logging more than 100 hours a year and more hours than any other individual) and the losses become non-passive, free to offset your W-2 or 1099 income.

Pair that with a cost segregation study and 100% bonus depreciation, permanently restored by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act for property acquired after January 19, 2025, and the first-year numbers get large.

A $500,000 short-term rental might produce $150,000 to $200,000 of year-one depreciation, depending on the property and the study. In the 37% bracket, that’s roughly $55,000 to $74,000 of federal tax that stays in your pocket.

The fine print, which the Instagram gurus skip, matters as much as the pitch:

If any of your income arrives on a 1099 (locums, telehealth, medical director stipends, expert witness work, consulting), you have access to retirement accounts your W-2 colleagues can only envy.

Plenty of contractors open a SEP-IRA and stop there, which is an expensive default.

A Solo 401(k) usually beats it. For 2026, you can defer up to $24,500 as the “employee” (a limit shared with any W-2 401(k) or 403(b) you also contribute to), plus an employer contribution of roughly 20% of net self-employment earnings as a sole proprietor, or 25% of W-2 salary through an S corporation, up to a combined $72,000.

Add $8,000 in catch-up contributions at 50, or $11,250 between ages 60 and 63. The employer side stands apart from your hospital plan, so an employed doc with meaningful side income can layer it on top of what they already contribute at work. The SEP has one more flaw for high earners: its balance counts under the pro-rata rule and sabotages the backdoor Roth.

Older 1099 physicians with steady income should price out a cash balance plan. It’s a defined benefit plan layered on top of the Solo 401(k), and allowable contributions rise with age, often into six figures a year for physicians in their 50s.

You’ll need an actuary and a funding commitment you can’t skip in a lean year, so it suits consistent earners.

Those deductions do double duty because of the QBI cliff. Physicians run a “specified service trade or business,” so the 20% qualified business income deduction phases out as taxable income rises.

For 2026, married couples get the full deduction at or below $403,500 of taxable income, and it vanishes entirely at $553,500 (the single thresholds are $201,750 and $276,750).

Dr. Lucky, married with roughly $500,000 of taxable 1099 income, sits deep in the phase-out. Fund the Solo 401(k) and a cash balance plan, drop taxable income below $403,500, and the QBI deduction rises from the dead on top of the retirement deduction itself.

Self-employment tax deserves a sentence of its own. Contractors pay the employee and employer shares of Social Security (12.4% combined) up to the $184,500 wage base and 2.9% Medicare on everything, plus another 0.9% above $250,000 for married couples.

An S corporation paying you a reasonable salary can trim part of that, though it adds payroll, a separate return, and a salary figure the IRS will scrutinize.

Physicians start saving late. Residency ends somewhere around 30 or 32, often with six-figure student debt, so the compounding years most savers get in their 20s went to call rooms and loan servicers. A year-end cram session can’t make that up.

Psychological flexibility refers to the ability to adjust your thoughts, emotions, and actions as circumstances change. Rather than becoming trapped in negative thinking or overwhelmed by frustration, people with greater psychological flexibility can recognize their emotions, consider their options, and respond in a more productive way.

"You might know someone who stays cool under pressure," said Lina Begdache, an associate professor of health and wellness studies at Binghamton University and lead author of the research. "The kind of person who misses a flight and, instead of panicking, calmly adapts to the situation. This person may still feel stressed, but they're better able to manage it through psychological flexibility."

"People may say that these are resilient people, but they also have what's called psychological flexibility. They're able to change the way they think about the situation and then use brain resources to handle the stress."

How Breakfast, Sleep, and Exercise Relate to Mental Resilience

To explore the relationship between lifestyle choices and emotional resilience, Begdache worked with Jason Cherry, a former Binghamton assistant professor of physical therapy, and former student Alexander J. Talkachov.

The researchers collected anonymous survey responses from approximately 400 college students, gathering information about their diets, sleep patterns, exercise habits, and other lifestyle factors.

Their findings revealed an important connection between healthy routines and the ability to cope with stress. Students who reported healthier habits, including regular physical activity and consistent breakfast consumption, also tended to demonstrate greater psychological flexibility and resilience.

The opposite pattern also emerged. Students who reported less healthy behaviors, including frequent fast food consumption and insufficient sleep, tended to show greater psychological rigidity, meaning they had more difficulty adjusting their thoughts and behaviors when circumstances changed.

According to Begdache, psychological flexibility gives people the mental space to recognize what they are experiencing instead of becoming completely absorbed by their stress. This ability to reassess a situation can make it easier to understand difficult emotions and determine how to respond.

She explained that improvements in diet and lifestyle may help support this kind of mental adaptability.

"When we're under stress, we feel like we fuse with the stress. We live the stress. But psychological flexibility is like stepping back and thinking, 'I feel this because of that. What can I do?' Identifying your emotions sometimes helps you find the solution for these emotions," said Begdache.

"The new finding here is that diet and lifestyle don't just make you resilient by themselves. They help you build the psychological flexibility, which, in turn, makes you a resilient person."

#4050. Scientists uncover a hidden map of the brain with 539 distinct regions

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Scientists have spent decades studying the brain's cells, connections, genes, and proteins. Yet another essential part of brain biology has received far less attention: lipids. These fatty molecules make up the membranes surrounding neurons, contribute to the myelin insulation that protects nerve fibers, and play important roles in communication between brain cells.

Although lipids are fundamental to brain function, scientists have struggled to map where different types are located. Conventional imaging techniques cannot easily distinguish individual lipid molecules, leaving a major gap in our understanding of the brain's chemical organization.

"So scientists have had only a blurry picture of how lipids differ from one part of the brain to another," says Giovanni D'Angelo, professor at The Kristian Gerhard Jebsen Foundation Chair in Nutrition and Metabolism at EPFL. "That gap matters, because changes in brain lipids are increasingly linked to conditions from depression to Alzheimer's disease."

Researchers at EPFL have now taken a major step toward filling that gap. A team led by D'Angelo, Luca Fusar Bassini, and Gioele La Manno has created a detailed map showing how lipids are distributed throughout the mouse brain.

Their Lipid Brain Atlas reveals that these molecules are arranged in highly organized patterns. Some match familiar anatomical structures, while others expose chemical boundaries that previous approaches had overlooked. The patterns are so distinctive that researchers could identify where a brain tissue sample originated simply by examining its lipid composition.

Scientists Identify 539 Distinct Chemical Zones in the Brain

To construct the atlas, researchers used mass spectrometry imaging, a technique that allows them to identify lipid molecules across thin sections of brain tissue. A laser scans each slice one location at a time, producing detailed information about which lipids are present and where they are found.

The team examined 172 different lipids in 109 brain slices collected from 11 mice. This generated approximately 7 million measurements, which scientists combined into a comprehensive 3D reconstruction using machine learning tools developed specifically for the project.

The resulting atlas revealed 539 chemically distinct territories, which the researchers named "lipizones." Each has a characteristic combination of lipids, creating a recognizable chemical signature.

Many of these regions correspond to established brain structures and cell types. Others reveal unexpected relationships between groups of nerve cell bodies and distant regions connected by their fibers. Such connections have often been difficult to detect using maps based on gene activity.

"In effect, lipids work like postal codes, giving distant but related parts of the brain the same chemical address," says D'Angelo.

Hidden Chemical Patterns Challenge Previous Brain Maps

The new atlas also uncovered surprising differences within parts of the brain that scientists previously considered relatively uniform.

One striking example is white matter, the tissue containing nerve fibers that connect different brain regions. Rather than having a largely consistent chemical composition, white matter contains numerous distinct lipid zones.

"This shows that the cells which make myelin are far more varied in their chemistry than their genes alone would suggest," says Luca Fusar Bassini.

The researchers also detected previously unrecognized chemical boundaries in the choroid plexus, the structure responsible for producing cerebrospinal fluid, as well as in the tissues lining the brain's internal cavities.

Together, these findings show that lipid mapping can reveal important features of brain organization that are not apparent from anatomy or genetic information alone.

Pregnancy Triggers Widespread Changes in Brain Lipids

Beyond identifying the brain's chemical structure, the atlas offered new insight into how that structure changes during pregnancy.

When researchers examined pregnant mice, they found substantial shifts in lipid composition across numerous brain regions. Overall, these changes were more pronounced than the typical lipid differences observed between male and female mice.

The cortex, which forms the brain's outer layer, showed particularly extensive changes. Meanwhile, levels of galactosyl ceramide, an important component of myelin, increased sharply throughout much of the brain's white matter.

These findings suggest that pregnancy involves significant changes in the brain's chemical makeup, potentially helping its neural connections adapt to the body's changing demands.

"Together, these shifts suggest the brain retunes its own wiring to meet the demands of pregnancy," says Gioele La Manno. "The Atlas reveals that the brain constantly adjusts its lipids to meet the body's changing needs."

A New Tool for Understanding Brain Aging and Disease

The Lipid Brain Atlas is freely available online, allowing scientists worldwide to explore this previously difficult-to-study aspect of brain biology.

By combining lipid information with existing genetic and cellular maps, researchers can gain a more complete understanding of how the brain is organized and how its chemistry changes over time.

The atlas also establishes a detailed reference for healthy mouse brain tissue. Scientists can use it to investigate how disruptions in lipid distribution might contribute to brain development, aging, and disorders such as Alzheimer's disease and depression.

Although the specific mouse brain map cannot be directly applied to humans, the approach used to create it could help researchers uncover comparable patterns in the human brain.

"What transfers to humans isn't the map, it's the method, and the fact that we now know that a healthy brain has a very organized lipid map at all," says Luca Fusar Bassini.

#4082. After 30 years, scientists finally have a drug for a deadly brain disease

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

The FDA has approved the first drug designed to directly treat Alexander disease, an extremely rare neurological disorder that progressively damages the nervous system and can be fatal. The treatment, called Zanvastro, represents a major advance for patients who previously had no options beyond managing their symptoms.

Alexander disease can interfere with movement, thinking, and automatic bodily functions such as breathing and heart rate. Zanvastro is the first and only approved disease-modifying treatment for the condition, meaning it targets an underlying biological process responsible for the disease rather than simply relieving its effects.

The drug emerged from more than three decades of scientific research at the University of Wisconsin-Madison's School of Veterinary Medicine and Waisman Center. Much of that work took place in the laboratory of Albee Messing, professor emeritus of comparative biosciences and former director of the Waisman Center, whose discoveries helped make the treatment possible. Ionis Pharmaceuticals collaborated in developing the drug.

Clinical trials offered encouraging results. Patients receiving Zanvastro generally experienced stabilization of their motor function, and some showed improvements in their ability to move.

"This is a wonderful and long-awaited day for the Alexander disease community," Messing says.

Alexander Disease: A Rare and Devastating Brain Disorder

Alexander disease affects fewer than one in every million people worldwide. Although exceptionally uncommon, its consequences can be severe, with symptoms that gradually interfere with everyday activities and essential bodily functions.

People with the disorder may develop seizures, lose previously acquired developmental abilities, experience increasing difficulty walking or moving, and struggle with speaking or swallowing. Some also develop dangerously elevated pressure inside the brain.

The disease does not follow a single predictable course. Symptoms may first emerge during infancy, childhood, or adulthood, and their severity and progression can differ depending on when the condition begins.

For decades, doctors could offer supportive care and treatments to ease individual symptoms, but they had no approved medication capable of targeting the disease itself.

An Unexpected Discovery Revealed the Genetic Cause

The breakthrough can be traced to research Messing conducted with Michael Brenner, professor emeritus of neurobiology at the University of Alabama at Birmingham. In the late 1990s, their work identified the genetic changes responsible for Alexander disease, providing scientists with a specific biological target for developing treatments.

The discovery began with an unexpected result in laboratory mice.

Messing and Brenner had genetically engineered mice to produce unusually high amounts of a protein known as glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). This protein is normally found in astrocytes, specialized cells that help support and maintain healthy function in the brain and spinal cord.

To the researchers' surprise, the modified mice developed abnormal protein deposits throughout their brains. These deposits appeared inside astrocytes and closely resembled the distinctive abnormalities seen in people with Alexander disease.

"We had, really without intending to, created a mouse that produced these classic lesions of Alexander disease," Messing says.

The abnormal deposits, called Rosenthal fibers, are a defining characteristic of the disorder. Additional genetic studies involving people diagnosed with Alexander disease confirmed that mutations in the gene responsible for producing GFAP were the underlying cause.

"This provided an obvious and specific strategy for therapy -- the GFAP protein," Messing explains.

Identifying the genetic cause also made diagnosing the condition much simpler. Instead of relying on invasive brain biopsies to look for characteristic tissue abnormalities, doctors could use genetic testing from a blood sample to identify the mutations associated with the disease.

Scientists Develop a Drug That Targets Harmful Protein Buildup

Once researchers understood the role of GFAP, their next challenge was finding a way to reduce its production and prevent damaging levels of the protein from accumulating in brain cells.

Messing joined forces with Tracy Hagemann, an associate research professor at the Waisman Center, to create improved rodent models of Alexander disease. These models more closely reproduced important features of the disorder, giving scientists a way to investigate potential treatments before testing them in people.

The team focused on a straightforward idea: If excessive GFAP contributes to the disease, lowering the amount produced might help protect the nervous system.

In collaboration with Ionis Pharmaceuticals, the researchers pursued a technology known as antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs).

These treatments use short, specially designed pieces of genetic material to interfere with instructions that cells use to manufacture particular proteins. Rather than targeting every protein in a cell, an ASO can be designed to reduce production of a specific one.

For Alexander disease, that target was GFAP.

The experimental treatment produced promising results in rodents, supporting the decision to move forward with human testing. A clinical trial began in the summer of 2021.

Zanvastro uses this same targeted strategy. By limiting GFAP production, the drug aims to reduce the buildup of the protein before it can cause additional damage.

Because the treatment needs to reach the central nervous system, it is administered through an injection into the spinal canal. A trained healthcare professional gives the injection once every three months.

Clinical Trials Show Improvements in Walking and Movement

The human trial provided evidence that lowering GFAP could offer meaningful benefits to people living with Alexander disease.

Ionis Pharmaceuticals conducted the international study across 13 clinical sites, enrolling 54 patients to evaluate the treatment's safety and effectiveness.

After receiving Zanvastro for a little more than a year, treated patients demonstrated significantly better walking speeds than patients who had not received the drug.

The results also indicated that treatment could stabilize motor function, an important outcome for a progressive disorder that can steadily undermine a person's ability to move independently. Some participants experienced improvements in motor performance.

These findings helped establish Zanvastro as the first treatment approved to modify the underlying disease process in Alexander disease, rather than focusing exclusively on symptom relief.

The FDA approval covers people with Alexander disease across the full age spectrum, from infancy through adulthood.

Zanvastro is expected to become available in the United States in the weeks following the approval. Outside the United States, access to the drug will be handled through a licensing agreement between Ionis Pharmaceuticals and Recordati, a major pharmaceutical company headquartered in Italy.

A 30-Year Scientific Journey Offers New Hope

For Messing and his colleagues, the approval represents the culmination of more than 30 years of research, beginning with basic investigations into brain cell biology and eventually leading to a treatment for patients.

What started as an unexpected finding in genetically modified mice helped reveal the cause of an extremely rare disease, transformed how doctors diagnose it, and provided the foundation for developing a drug.

The achievement is also an example of translational research, the process of turning discoveries made in laboratories into treatments that can directly benefit patients.

Throughout that journey, individuals living with Alexander disease and their families played an essential role by supporting the research and participating in studies.

"The whole program of research would not have been possible without the support and participation from the patient and family community, to whom we will always be grateful," Messing says.

#2844. What Trail Runners Need to Know Before They Go (Number 2)

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Sometimes you can’t stop nature from calling, but don’t let the infamous Hyrox incident stop you from getting to the start line. The perfect poop starts with a plan, and believe it or not, it’s far easier on trails than in a convention center. Sarah Wassner Flynn fleshes, or should we say flushes, out the rules for a courteous crap below.

Last month, Australian athlete Joanna Wietrzyk was competing at Hyrox Beijing, an indoor fitness competition, when she suffered a sudden bout of fecal incontinence roughly halfway through her race. Rather than step off the shared course, she kept going, through burpees on a carpeted floor, a stint on the seated rowing machine, and running segments, ultimately winning the race in just over an hour.

The story, to no one’s surprise, went mega-viral. Sure, there was the shock value of an athlete losing control of her bodily functions on camera. But that’s an unfortunate side effect many endurance athletes have experienced or witnessed on a race course at some point. Marathoner Paula Radcliffe popped a squat on the side of the road in front of live TV cameras while leading the 2005 London Marathon. 2026 Ironman World Champion and four-time Ironman 70.3 World champion Taylor Knibb famously quipped to a broadcast cameraman, “I just s*&% myself, so can you not get my ass?” en route to winning the T100 final in 2024. What made Wietrzyk’s case different from those other bowel movements etched in sports history was the indoor setting on shared equipment, with other athletes racing around (or behind) her the entire time.

The backlash that followed was less about the accident itself and more about how Wietrzyk was allowed to continue racing while putting other competitors at risk. Hyrox responded to the incident within days, updating its rulebook to allow race officials to pull an athlete whose “blood, vomit, urine, or feces” pose a health or contamination risk to themselves or others—an addition that, notably, hadn’t existed until now.

Of course, trail running doesn’t have Hyrox’s shared-equipment problem—there’s no carpet to contaminate and everything takes place outside. But in a sport where pooping mid-race is more or less a given over the course of enough miles, the underlying questions are the same. Are race organizers doing enough to provide proper bathroom access on remote courses? And what obligations, written or unwritten, do runners have to protect the trails and each other?

Ask any longtime ultrarunner and they’ll tell you: bathroom stops are a long-standing, half-joking, half-serious topic of conversation in the sport. Hours of running and bouncing shake up your gastrointestinal system. Throw in early-morning coffee, gels, and nerves, and it’s inevitable you’ll need to go at some point. Many races provide porta-potties at aid stations, but in longer races, when there’s no bathroom for miles, stepping off-course to relieve yourself is a near-given.

“Whenever you have a race of more than eight hours, if you don’t have some kind of bathroom urge, you’re likely severely underfueling,” says Kristen Kelman, an ultrarunner in Deerfield, Illinois and the 2021 Road Runners Club of America 50-Mile National Champion. “It’s often not logistically possible to have any sort of bathroom nearby. It’s a reality of the sport.”

For ultrarunners, that reality comes with a responsibility to take care of the land they’re using, which means being just as deliberate about how they go as where they go.

“It’s an issue of trail stewardship,” Kelman says. “We have a responsibility to take care of the trails and the land we are using so that we can continue to explore and challenge ourselves in these wild and beautiful places. That includes being responsible about your poop.”

While expectations and guidelines vary from race to race, most events follow the seven principles of Leave No Trace, which aim to protect natural spaces and ensure a sustainable future for the planet. The principles apply to everything from disposing of waste to where to wander in the woods, with poop being a paramount point of concern.

“Human waste is often cited as one of the most concerning impacts from recreation that land managers are facing,” Erin Collier, director of education for the Leave No Trace organization in Boulder, Colorado, says. “Given the popularity of [trail running], the scale of the events, and the often-remote nature of the courses, it’s safe to assume the potential for human waste impact is high. When human waste isn’t disposed of properly, the potential for environmental and public health impacts is significant.”

Collier adds that more than 100 protozoans, bacteria, and viruses have been identified in human waste, including Giardia lamblia, Cryptosporidium parvum, various coliform bacteria, and viruses such as Hepatitis A.

“These can contaminate water sources and spread disease,” she says. “This is why proper burial techniques or packing it out is so important. A common misconception is that if a bear can poop in the woods, so can you. ”

In other words, simply ducking a few feet off-trail to go behind a tree isn’t enough. Standard Leave No Trace guidance is straightforward: If you need to go, get at least 200 feet from the trail and water sources, dig a 6-to-8-inch cathole, and bury your waste thoroughly. Runners should carry their own supplies—like toilet paper, wipes, and a trowel—and pack out their used materials.

In some environments, however, experts recommend taking things a step further and suggest pooping in a plastic bag filled with waste-alleviating gel, also known as a WAG bag.

“WAG bags are recommended or required when you are unable to dig an adequate cathole, get the appropriate distance from water, or are in specialized or high-use environments like the desert terrain of the [American] Southwest, deep snow, or high alpine where soil is too rocky to dig,” Collier says. “It’s important to understand the waste disposal requirements for the area you’re running in.”

Who’s responsible for what when a runner has to go mid-race?

“Both runners and race organizers have important roles to play, and one shouldn’t replace the other,” Collier says.  While the onus should fall largely on runners to prepare for their own needs and pack out what they bring in, race organizers set the tone for what’s expected, though in many cases official race policy on the subject is quite thin.

The race regulations for the Ultra-Trail du Mont-Blanc (UTMB), one of the most prestigious trail running festivals on the planet which hosts some 10,000 runners each August across its various events, don’t specifically address bathroom protocol on the trails. Participants receive plastic bags at packet pickup along with their race bibs to be used for “soiled paper used during any urgent need along the route” and runners are reminded to “keep their rubbish and wrappers with them until they can throw them in the bins at an aid station.” A separate section of the UTMB website dedicated to “good practices on the trails,” is more direct: “If you’re caught short in the wild, move away from trails and water points, don’t stop behind a shelter, hut or refuge, and take your paper with you.”

The race guide for Cocodona 250, a point-to-point ultramarathon in Arizona, is more prescriptive than UTMB, though different from Leave No Trace’s standard policy of going 200 feet off-trail. Under “Restroom use on Course,” the rules say: “It happens. Step 30 feet off the trail and dig a 6 inch (or deeper) hole. Used wet wipes / TP must be packed out in a zip-lock. DO NOT LEAVE TP ON THE TRAILS!”

And the rules for the Leadville 100, the 100-mile endurance race held in the Rocky Mountains near Leadville, Colorado, include a line prohibiting “public urination,” but don’t specifically address pooping (that likely falls under the race’s Leave No Trace policy: “No garbage, clothing, or race-related items may be left on course”).

Some race directors don’t feel the need to go out of their way to spell out bathroom expectations. Andy Bacon, owner of Ex2 Adventures, an events management company that hosts dozens of trail-running and adventure races throughout the Washington, D.C. metro area each year, says while pointing out porta-potties is standard, telling people where to poop elsewhere is never on his to-do list.

“People are always asking about the bathroom situation, and I make sure they know about the porta-potties we provide at the start and sometimes along the course, but I’m not providing information about where to go when you can’t make it to a porta-potty,” he says. “My role as a race director is to inform people of what their options are, but I don’t think I have to tell people, ‘hey, you should carry toilet paper in case you need to go to the bathroom in the woods.’”

In the culture of ultra-distance and trail running, Bacon says the hows and wheres of relieving yourself are “sort of just an unspoken rule.” “Most people know that if you’re going to be spending most of your day out in the woods, you’re going to have a pack with a plastic bag with toilet paper in it,” he says. “And if you have to go, you’re prepared for it, you do what you have to do, and you move on. It’s not a big deal.”

Many ultrarunners we spoke to were equally nonchalant. Asked about his own methods, uber-ultrarunner Michael Wardian, who had just finished running all 2,650 miles of the Pacific Crest Trail, said simply, “I try to get well off trail and then bury anything.”

Awkward encounters happen, especially in the late stages of an ultra, when sleep deprivation and fatigue can lead to poor decision-making.

“I’ve definitely seen a lot more than I signed up for,” Kelman says. “For me, seeing another runner go is one thing. But if it’s a family hiking with small kids out there and they [witness] someone pooping, that’s definitely over the line.”

While individual runners may be reluctant to share unsavory stories about pooping mid-race, the bravery provided by an anonymous avatar leaves Reddit boards full of them. One runner described passing three people who had stopped less than 10 feet from the trail during a race without porta-potties. Another recounted a friend who had to stop in the middle of a flat, open desert: “I swear everyone within five miles knew he was popping a squat.”

The, uh, bottom line? Shit happens, as Joanna Wietrzyk’s viral race in Beijing made clear. But ultimately, Collier says, doing your business the most environmentally and ethically responsible way on the trails comes down to having a plan.

“It’s safe to assume that poop will be a part of anyone’s race experience and [runners] should not assume that a lack of restroom facilities gives them permission to leave waste or toilet paper wherever they choose,” Collier says. “There should be a plan in place at all levels for how to deal with poop in the least impactful way.”

The paintings, Portrait of Madame Colonna Romano and Young Woman at the Well, belong to the Musée d’Orsay in Paris and were on loan to the museum. Their combined value was reported at €9 million, or approximately $10.4 million, at the time of the theft.

Two intruders entered the museum before dawn after cutting through its perimeter fence and climbing through a window. They used an electric saw to remove the paintings’ frames from their mountings, taking four works but abandoning two in the garden as they escaped. The burglary lasted less than three minutes, according to the regional prosecutor’s office.

On October 10, a 29-year-old man identified by investigators as the principal suspect was placed under formal investigation for organized theft and criminal conspiracy, his attorney said. Authorities have not publicly detailed how the paintings were recovered.

The museum occupies Les Collettes, the estate where Renoir lived from 1908 until his death in 1919. Set among olive and citrus trees, the property preserves his studio, furniture, and personal belongings alongside paintings and sculptures. Mayor Bryan Masson welcomed the recovery, describing the works as part of the city’s history and cultural heritage.

The paintings will undergo examination before returning to the museum. France’s Culture Ministry said on October 12 that specialists in Marseille would assess their condition and carry out any necessary restoration under the Musée d’Orsay’s scientific supervision. No date has been announced for their return.

#1581. On the Couch with David Netto

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Fresh off a Hamptons triumph, the master of historic references unveils a collection of lamps with Soane Britain.

If you hire David Netto for an interiors project, not only do you get a nimble design mind and one of the foremost connoisseurs of “American chic,” you also get a quick wit who doesn’t mince words. Ask the ELLE Decor A-List designer and author to describe his studio, and he’ll answer: “Intimate, intelligent, interested in your ideas as much as ours.” As for his favorite projects of late: “The Hedges Inn. A new development in the Bahamas called Palm Grove. A penthouse we never photographed code-named Love Nest.” While we can’t speak to Operation Love Nest, we can confirm that the Hedges was a see-and-be-seen hotspot in the Hamptons this summer.

Netto’s interiors are often brimming with books, including a few he has written himself. His next, due out in 2027, is about a property he restored with architect Gil Schafer and landscape designer Miranda Brooks.

But first he’s celebrating the release this fall of a new lighting collection he designed for Soane Britain. His previous collaboration with the company, in 2021, was a group of wicker lamps that borrowed inspiration from Provençal and Californian influences. For this new four-piece line, Netto looked to everything from Austrian modernism to Jacques Garcia’s Normandy château to billiard rooms in grand English country houses.

Each of the designs—three hanging lamps and a sconce—will be available in rattan, vellum, and gathered shade fabric. The Neue wall lamp, a half drum attached to an industrial back plate, also comes in polished copper.

Netto admits to geeking out over certain details, like the leather-wrapped brass straps and origami-like edges of the rattan Snooker light, created in conjunction with Soane’s artisans in the UK. “If it’s for Soane, it can be exotic, but it also has to be the prettiest thing in the room,” Netto says. “That’s what I hope people will say about these lights.”
