The Supreme Court is asked to review several thousand cases each year. It agrees to review fewer than 1% of them. It takes four votes of the nine to decide to review a case. (The grant of review is generally called a "grant of certiorari" or just a "cert. grant.")

Sometimes Justices write opinions dissenting from a denial of cert., arguing that the Court should have taken the case. And sometimes they write opinions respecting the denial of cert., explaining why it made sense to deny review in this case but noting that the Court ought to consider the question in future cases.

Such opinions are often solo opinions, and are sometimes signed by two or three Justices from the liberal or conservative wings of the Court. But it turns out that Justices Sotomayor and Gorsuch have also jointly filed nine such opinions, eight just for themselves and one for themselves plus Justice Kagan. All of them, it seems to me, at least suggest that the Court ought to move the law in a somewhat more government-restrictive direction (loosely speaking); most of them specifically point to somewhat broader constitutional rights protections. #3303. Post-Race Weight Gain: Why You’re Heavier After Finishing a Race (And Why It’s a Good Thing)

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

You wake up feeling sore and puffy the day after a race, step on the scale, and confirm what you intuitively feel: you’re a few pounds above your pre-race weight.

But why? Logically, a massive calorie burn (up to 10,000+ calories for an Ironman!) should tip the scale the other way, so something must be wrong.

Your first impulse may be to cut calories or jump right back into training. Please don’t! What you’re seeing is your body’s normal physiological response as it goes into full recovery mode.

Post-race weight gain isn’t the same as the off-season weight drift many athletes experience. That weight tends to build over months and is a separate conversation about energy balance. The jump you see on the scale in the first 24 to 72 hours after the race is mostly water. Specifically, your body is hard at work replenishing glycogen, rebuilding plasma volume, and retaining water and sodium as part of its hormonal stress-and-repair response. It’s most noticeable after a half or full iron-distance triathlon, where fueling, fluid intake, and stress load are highest.

Let’s dig into what’s going on, and why this temporary bump is actually a good sign.

Long-course races drain your muscle glycogen. As long as you eat plenty of carbs, your body starts refilling those stores within the first few hours after you finish. But the longer the race, the more muscle damage, which slows the process.

After a long-course race, full restoration can take several days, rather than the roughly 24 hours we see after shorter events. Here’s the kicker: each gram of newly synthesized glycogen is stored with approximately three grams of water. So, replenishing 400–500 grams of muscle glycogen adds about 3.5–4.5 pounds, mostly from water, packed in your muscles. In other words, much of the bump you see on the scale in the first few days is restored fuel.

Glycogen isn’t the only reason the scale climbs. It’s normal to finish a long race dehydrated, since even a solid race hydration strategy can’t fully offset hours of sweat losses. In the 24 to 72 hours after the race, fluid-conserving hormones (aldosterone and vasopressin) remain elevated, signaling your body to hold onto fluid to restore plasma volume and electrolyte balance. The extra water weight won’t stick around long, so don’t cut back on fluids or salt to bring the number down. Your body needs both right now.

Racing 70.3 or 140.6 miles puts your body under more stress than a typical training day. During and after the race, cortisol, your primary stress hormone, spikes, signaling your body to retain water. Prolonged physical exertion causes muscle microdamage and inflammation.

The longer, hotter, and tougher the race, the longer recovery may take. As your body begins repairs, fluid moves into damaged tissues, which can explain your tight, swollen legs. You’ll notice improvement when your kidneys start clearing the excess fluid, leading to more frequent bathroom visits. A little puffiness for a couple of days post-race means the repair process is underway.

As training volume comes down after a race, you might expect your appetite to follow. And, for the first several hours, it often does, since hard effort can temporarily suppress hunger. But over the next day or two, it returns with a vengeance. That’s because a slower-moving hormonal system (primarily ghrelin and leptin) signals your brain to respond to the energy deficit you built up on race day.

You may feel hungrier for several days, often craving energy-dense, salty, carb-rich foods (think fries, burgers, and pizza). And all that yummy food, along with the water and sodium that come with it, adds to the scale number too. It’s not a lack of willpower or a total loss of control; it’s your body refueling.

Following an endurance race, some swelling is expected, but I’d be remiss not to flag a few scenarios that, although rare, require medical attention.

If one leg swells, especially with calf pain, warmth, or redness, it may indicate a blood clot. Swelling with shortness of breath or chest pain is an emergency; seek immediate attention.

Dark cola-colored urine with severe muscle pain or weakness could signal rhabdomyolysis: a condition involving severe muscle breakdown that can lead to kidney damage.

And if you finish the race heavier than you started and experience confusion, headache, or nausea, it could be hyponatremia (low blood sodium); seek medical help right away.

For most athletes, a few extra pounds in the days after a long-distance race signals a healthy recovery. Still, it can be tempting, especially if you keep close tabs on your weight, to see the number go up and immediately want to cut back or restrict.

Resist the urge. Your body needs carbohydrates and protein to rebuild glycogen and repair tissue; your fluid and electrolyte balance needs restoring, and your immune system needs adequate energy while under duress. Restricting food and fluids now won’t get your weight back on track. It will delay glycogen restoration, prolong fatigue and soreness, and potentially increase your risk of illness.

As your fluid levels normalize and inflammation subsides, most of the extra weight comes off naturally, typically within three to seven days. You’ve demanded a lot from your body in training and on race day, so give it what it needs: plenty of nutrient-rich food, adequate hydration, rest, and quality sleep. Then step back and trust your body and the process.

#1783. Flat Traffic, No Budget: What Ziff Davis’ Jonathan Berthold Tests First

Where search leaders are putting 2027 budget, plus the AI visibility scorecard that replaces session reporting.

Calls from ChatGPT and Claude often get filed as direct. This checklist shows how to track AI search as its own call source.

This rundown shows how to test briefs, audits, or rank reporting with a four-layer system built on your own data.

Ask the SEJ experts you already read what AI, agents, and algorithm changes mean for your traffic. Private, human to human. $97/mo.

Your traffic is flat, and you don’t have a budget to throw at the problem. What should you test first? And how do you get your boss to approve an experiment when you can’t promise it will work?

I put those questions to Jonathan Berthold, VP of Revenue at Ziff Davis, on The Search Engine Journal podcast. Jonathan is a longtime marketing strategist who works in both SEO and paid media.

We covered where to find bottom-of-funnel opportunities and how to keep a test small enough that it can’t take down a site. We also discussed how long to wait before judging a result, along with a gap he sees in both SEO and paid media: Marketers focus on generating traffic and overlook what happens after a user lands on the site.

When traffic is flat and there’s no budget, Jonathan starts with a full site analysis to find out why traffic stalled. He has inherited sites in that condition before, ranging from B2C SaaS companies to informational lead-generation sites. What he finds in the analysis determines the fix. Sometimes that means improving existing pages, and sometimes it means building new pages to fill a topic gap.

Either way, his experiment would target commercial intent. Top-of-funnel traffic is being “siphoned by LLMs, AI Overviews, AI Mode,” he said, and that traffic “is going away and/or doesn’t really convert or add to the bottom line.” So he would test one of two approaches: optimizing existing pages for commercial and transactional keywords, or creating new content to fill topic gaps in the market.

Without a budget, Jonathan relies on AI to do the research. He uses it to learn what type of content ranks for bottom-of-funnel terms and where the opportunities are to scale traffic in areas competitors aren’t covering. He also uses it to figure out what added perspective would make the new pages “unique and inviting to search engines like Google.”

I asked what test most marketers should be running but aren’t. Jonathan said there is “a massive market for bottom-of-funnel traffic that a lot of SEOs sleep on.”

It’s easier to build a strategy around keywords with more than a thousand monthly searches, but many of those keywords are informational. Giants like Reddit, LinkedIn and Wikipedia already rank for them, and LLMs, AI Overviews and AI Mode have absorbed much of that traffic. He called bottom-of-funnel targeting “100% the easiest low lift opportunity” available.

Jonathan sees approval as a communication problem. He never tells a leader that the team will test something and hope for a result. Every experiment is an opportunity to learn. He starts with a hypothesis, and the pitch explains what the test will reveal. It might show whether a tactic or process will help in the future, or whether a messaging or creative idea holds up.

He recommends framing the ask around the long-term payoff by explaining what the team will learn and how that knowledge will make it better at its craft. When the pitch is positioned as “this experiment will allow us to learn X, Y, and Z so that we can earn this much into the future,” he said, it is “easier understood by a leadership team that largely works within P&L.”

I raised a concern about the quick fixes circulating now that newcomers are using to enter SEO through AEO and GEO. Those tactics work until the algorithm catches up. I’d rather keep experiments contained than put an entire website at risk.

Jonathan agrees. When a team runs an SEO test across a full website and the test goes in the wrong direction, “you tank the whole organic,” he said. His rule for both SEO and paid media is to start with a very small section, then iterate and expand slowly as the results come in.

Paid media taught him the same lesson. Tripling a budget in one day “ruins all the optimization,” he said, so budgets need to rise gradually each day while the system learns. In SEO, the equivalent mistake is rolling out a schema or content-writing experiment sitewide and tanking pages that already perform well.

Results also need to be replicated. A result should repeat “so that it’s not just a random result,” he said, until a pattern emerges that you can apply to the rest of your work.

When a test isn’t going the way you hoped, when should you stop? Years ago, the standard advice was to give an SEO test six to nine months, especially for a new site, but iterations move much faster now. I asked Jonathan how he decides whether to give a struggling experiment more time or end it.

For SEO, he gives an experiment about a month. He relies on Search Console and treats its data as directional. Google needs time to crawl the updated pages, process the changes and adjust rankings before impressions and clicks move, so “definitely within a week, you won’t see results.”

After 30 days, there is usually enough data to see whether impressions are moving, and he would wait 60 days at most. He said starting small helps here, too, because it keeps expectations in check and lets the rest of the team’s work continue while the experiment runs.

Paid media follows a different rule because the feedback is immediate. He either reviews tests every two weeks or uses his preferred method, which is a spend threshold of two to three times the target cost per acquisition. Once a test has spent that much, it has run its course, and the team can either iterate on it or kill it.

Jonathan sees the same gap in SEO and paid media. Marketers put nearly all their attention on generating traffic and overlook “what happens when a user actually hits your website.”

Any page that already draws a lot of organic traffic, or that a team spends heavily to send paid traffic to, is a good candidate for split testing and funnel optimization. Landing page and funnel experiments should always be “an on thing,” he said, because you can keep raising conversion rates at each stage of the funnel, “even by a few basis percentage points.”

Jonathan is also running his own experiment that connects bottom-of-funnel targeting with link building. He uses AI citation data “to unearth link building opportunities.” He sees it as a way to spot where LLMs are heading and to build brand presence by earning the citations competitors have.

Many companies stop short of that step. A lot of them track AI visibility and watch which sites cite their competitors, he said, “but not a lot are taking the next step, which is reaching out to these publications.”

The payoff compounds. Pages that cite competitors for bottom-of-funnel keywords likely rank for those keywords themselves and get cited often in LLMs. Earning a mention on those pages can send traffic to your site and get your brand cited alongside your competitors, which he called “a more holistic approach.”

Jonathan no longer puts much stock in search volume. Search habits change every day. If the context and topic are right, a long-tail keyword can still be worth targeting even when Google, Moz or another platform reports it at one, 10 or zero searches a month.

Volume is useful as a directional signal, especially in paid media, where it helps set expectations for the total addressable market. In organic search, fragmentation changes the math. People search in more ways than they used to, including inside LLMs, and LLMs run their own query fan-outs. That makes targeting “how your target ICP searches” more important than the number in a keyword tool.

His example comes from his agency days. A client in the composites industry supplied fiberglass conduits for commercial projects. Its total addressable market was very small, and its keywords had very low search volume. But if 10 people searched a month and two of them landed on the site, “very likely they’re in that market. That’s a half-a-million-dollar contract.”

That’s where conversion rate comes in. Someone searching for the best mortgage rates will likely end up on informational content and probably won’t convert on that site. A search like “I need a plumber to fix my toilet” may show little search volume, but the person typing it has an urgent need and high intent, so they are much more likely to convert. As LLMs become more widely used, he expects search volume to be “not fully representative” of the phrases people use or the many fan-out queries a single keyword can generate.

As AI answers absorb informational searches, Jonathan says focus on high-intent searches, even when the volume is low. He also recommends running tests on the pages where visitors decide whether to convert. A small test in those places can show a measurable business result within a month or two.

That result also makes the next experiment easier to get approved. A test tied to conversions produces a number that a leadership team can use to make sound decisions.

Pedro Pascal shares how he became a cello prodigy in Tony Gilroy’s drama about sex, orchestras, and being the ultimate player.

“You can’t hide in this room,” Tony Gilroy says.

The Andor creator and Michael Clayton filmmaker is standing amid rows of orchestra musicians in the center of the historic Newman Scoring Stage on the 20th Century Fox lot in Los Angeles, California. It’s where the music for Jaws, The Sound of Music, and countless other classic films was recorded. But on this day, the recording session is part of the actual movie. Pedro Pascal straddles a cello among the dozens of co-stars—who are actual violinists, cellists, French horn players—as they all shoot the opening scene of the musical drama Behemoth!

Gilroy points at the ornate web of microphones dangling from above, and then to the glassed-in control room where a team of engineers are attuned to every whisper. “When that red light goes on, there’s 23 people listening in that room,” he says. “You can hide in a philharmonic. If you’re off. If you’re sick. A concert keeps going and doesn’t stop.”

But here, in this historic space, the precision equipment doesn’t just expose every false note—it records each one for posterity. This is where Alex Serian, Pascal’s virtuoso cellist, discovers he can’t hide anymore. His past, going way back to childhood, is littered with broken promises and false hopes. His love life is a freeway pile-up. His family has disintegrated. All he has is the cello, and now it’s literally time to face more than the music.

“The music is pulling him right from the very beginning of it, pulling him forward to this destiny, pulling him to do everything,” Gilroy tells Esquire during our exclusive set visit. By focusing on the one thing he is good at, the only thing he can execute flawlessly, Alex finds a way to revisit all these broken memories and figure out a new way forward. He’s already a world-class musician, but he’s trying hard to become a better man.

Pascal says Gilroy explained it like Alex was being swept along in a flood. The actor gets choked up as he describes this lost person, grasping for something to hold onto and pull himself out. “He's on a river,” Pascal says. “I think that the musical notes on the page can be the rope.”

The Newman Scoring Stage is usually off-limits to the public. It’s not an exquisitely styled concert hall. It’s an open space big enough to be a basketball court, but not big enough for spectators. The wood panels affixed to the walls look like they’re in someone’s unfinished basement, ready for plaster and a paint job. It’s named after nine-time Oscar-winning composer Alfred Newman, who won awards for 1939’s Wuthering Heights, 1950’s All About Eve, and 1970’s Airport, released the year he died. It was renamed for him in 1997, but this is the first time the Hollywood workspace has been one of the stars of a movie itself.

In the film, out on December 4, Pascal’s character has abandoned the world of symphonies and concerts and has returned to his hometown of Los Angeles, where he finds work as a session musician working on movie scores.

Behemoth! doesn’t have one single composer—it has nine. Each of them contributed themes for the various fictional films that hire Alex as a performer. Henry Jackman, best known for Kick-Ass and Captain America: Civil War, penned the tense tones of the made-up Spies Don’t Run, which you can hear a sample of below.

Among the other contributors are Back to the Future composer Alan Silvestri, Marvel Studios and Pixar mainstay Michael Giacchino, Moana 2 co-writer and Beyoncé pianist Emily Bear, Get Out and Nope maestro Michael Abels, and The Hunger Games and Batman Begins composer James Newton Howard. The film also features composers Lukas Frank (singer-songwriter with the band Storefront Church), Nami Melumad (Star Trek: Strange New Worlds) and Brandon Roberts (an Emmy-winner for Free Solo). Each contributes a different flavor to the story, underscoring the fact that Behemoth! is sometimes a romance, sometimes a family drama, sometimes a farce, and occasionally even a cartoon.

As Alex plays the music for these various movies, he unlocks more memories from harrowing times: His late composer father (Hank Azaria) pushed him to extremes before exiting the stage and leaving it all unresolved. Now Alex must face the gradual decline of his harp-playing mother (JoBeth Williams), who is battling Alzheimer’s disease. He has a loving but fraught relationship with his brother (Will Arnett), a rockstar turned podcaster, who—if it’s not already clear from that description—has fallen on hard times.

Olivia Wilde plays a ferociously jilted ex who also happened to be a major symphony benefactor—and can’t abide her favorite player moving on. “The one language he understood best was music. Gilroy says it was vital to cast someone like Pascal, who could bring a sense of wounded innocence to Alex. “He's obviously damaged by this toxic family that he came from,” Gilroy says. “He's not a predator in any way, but he's a very sensual person and a lot of women have been attracted to him.” He adds with a laugh: “And he's really easy.”

But none of this has given Alex the comfort and stability he seeks. “I get a little emotional because it's such a subtle thing that Tony's done,” Pascal says. “It’s healing in a way that Alex isn't completely conscious of, but he’s led by the language of music and starts to apply that language to his life."

On the day Esquire visited the set last January, it was also the first time Alex has this awakening. Behemoth! pokes fun at Hollywood tropes while paying tribute to the unseen men and women whose music often make films stronger than they might be otherwise. “I've been obsessed with studio musicians since I was 19,” Gilroy says. “I came out to L.A. to try to be a studio musician, and I realized how deficient I was within three days of playing guitar.”

Around the recording stage, there are posters for the made-up Spies movie, but the face of the dark-haired, suave secret agent—billed as Dominic Diaz above the title—is obscured. Behemoth! was put together in a backwards way. The usual method is to shoot it and edit it first. Composers might be working on various themes in advance, but after they have a working print, they study its cuts while penning the final score to make sure the music cues hit at just the right moments. Gilroy and his team turned that approach around.

There's some aspect of that that must personally appeal to me, that makes me satisfied.”

With Behemoth!, Gilroy explores what happens when being brilliant at one thing makes everything else fall short.

Expertise can help you see the world more clearly, but it can also help you hide from it. That’s what Pascal’s character in the film comes to see in his return to the movie music scene. Behemoth! isn’t necessarily autobiographical, but Gilroy says he feels a connection to that. “If you took everything away, I have the feeling I would still be able to go into a room and write my way out of it, and I think there is some satisfaction in that. But I don't think it protects you from everybody else.”

It’s the “everybody else” that Pascal’s Alex finally has to confront. The rest of his life may be out of tune, but this is the story of a man seeking balance. You might even call it harmony.

#1148. How to Spot the Signs of Chronic Stress and Help Your Body Recover

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Experts explain what it is and its effects on sleep, skin, hair, mood, and long-term health.

These days, a growing list of things can lead to stress. Chronic stress in particular, though, can be very damaging mentally and emotionally, and can even show up physically through major hair and skin changes. Briefly worrying about something, waking up anxious in the middle of the night, or having a lingering distracting thought can all be signs of stress. But persistence separates chronic stress from regular stress. Experts explain how chronic stress differs from other forms, how it can show up in different areas of your life and body, and how to get a grip on it before the repercussions get out of hand.

While there’s no clinical definition or diagnosis for chronic stress, Karinn A. Glover, MD, MPH, attending psychiatrist at Montefiore Einstein Psychiatry Associates, describes it as life events or dynamics where we feel (consciously or unconsciously) anxious, on edge, irritated, or fearful. “These conditions can be constant or intermittent, but they persist over an extended period. In practice, that can be four to six weeks or even decades,” she tells ELLE.

Stress has an innate negative connotation, but Dr. Michael J. Hall, MD, MSc, founder and medical director of Hall Longevity Clinic and author of Infinite Youth: Solving the Aging Equation, argues that stress itself isn’t necessarily the enemy. “It’s an ancient survival system designed simply to switch on, solve a problem, and then switch off. Chronic stress is what happens when it never switches off,” he tells ELLE. “In our modern society, the hyper-stimulus of interconnectivity that is surrounding us at all times is unnatural and causes the body to stay in a low-grade emergency for weeks, months, even years,” says Hall.

As a psychiatrist, Glover says she looks at the mind and body together when addressing stress. “That means insomnia can be an issue, as well as the other responses to chronic stress: low mood, cynicism, fatigue, chronic pain, headaches, repetitive dietary choices that result in weight gain, high cholesterol, as well as high blood pressure and even prediabetes,” says Glover.

What happens internally when the stress response stays on can be a combination of things. Hall explains that cortisol and adrenaline can remain elevated, fear-based living sets in, certain diseases can be triggered, and almost every system in the body pays a price. “Blood pressure and heart rate run higher. Blood sugar stays up, which over time pushes toward insulin resistance and fat storage around the midsection. Inflammation rises quietly. Immune function weakens, so you get sick more often and heal more slowly. Sleep becomes lighter and less restorative, digestion suffers, and hormones like testosterone, estrogen, and thyroid get chaotic,” Hall describes. He adds that research has even linked long-term psychological stress to shorter telomeres, the DNA end tips, a marker of cellular aging. “In plain terms, chronic stress makes you biologically older than your birthday,” says Hall.

Hall points out that the obvious culprits are ongoing pressures like life, work, finances, caregiving, relationship strain, and health worries. “But I see just as much hidden stress,” he adds. “The body doesn’t distinguish much between an angry email and a skipped meal. It all goes into the same bucket. In my Aging Funnel framework, I call this Load. Most people only count the emotional part and miss everything else filling the bucket,” says Hall.

Glover notes a few additional factors that can contribute to chronic stress: “Life hands us any number of situations and challenges that can be overwhelming: witnessing the suffering of a loved one or a community member, living in an unsafe neighborhood or an area that is heavily policed, facing health challenges, or employment in a very tense workplace. These are all examples of situations where we may feel intermittently powerless, vigilant about our safety, or threatened in our well-being,” says Glover. Additionally, she notes that the constant exposure to stress hormones and neurotransmitters is linked to premature aging and chronic disease.

Glover describes “regular stress” as the everyday challenges we face: commuting, caring for an infant, doing a presentation at work, or taking examinations at school. “Now, what if all those circumstances were happening in one day, every day for a month? That would be chronic stress. Anything that taps into our fight/flight/freeze reactions is usually a source of chronic stress and can affect our well-being if we are repeatedly exposed to such conditions or dynamics,” Glover explains.

Highlighting the differences, Hall argues that regular, or acute, stress is not only real, but it’s also healthy. “A tough workout, a deadline, a cold plunge, a public talk: you rise to the challenge, then you recover, and you come back stronger. That’s adaptation,” he challenges. “Chronic stress is when the recovery phase never arrives. The difference isn’t the size of the stressor. It's whether you return to baseline. Think of it as a sprint versus never being allowed to stop running, and you never can recover,” says Hall.

Both Glover and Hall have established that chronic stress has links to premature aging. Signs show up both on the body through weight gain or loss and hair loss, and in lifestyle changes like insomnia, appetite changes (increased snacking, more sweets, more salty foods, or the total opposite, a big drop in appetite), and nervous tics like absently pulling hair, twirling it to the point that it becomes damaged, and fidgeting more than usual.

Chronic stress can also show up in our posture. “We hunch our shoulders, our neck muscles start to contract unconsciously, and we may even find ourselves clenching our jaw and squeezing our pelvic floor muscles unconsciously,” Glover explains. Another sign can be the onset of digestive conditions like IBS (irritable bowel syndrome) and acid reflux, which Glover notes exemplify how interconnected mental well-being, stress responses, and physical well-being are. “The link between chronic stress and cardiovascular health is perhaps the clearest and most well-known because we see it depicted on television and because ignoring it can often be a matter of life and death,” Glover adds.

Hall backs many of the signs Glover describes, with a few additions to keep an eye out for: fatigue that sleep doesn’t fix, waking at 3 a.m., tension headaches, a tight jaw or teeth grinding, digestive issues, belly weight that won’t budge, frequent colds, low libido, irritability, and brain fog.

From a beauty perspective, Hall says the skin is often the first to tell the story. In his book Infinite Youth, he describes aging as an equation, suggesting chronic stress is one of the most powerful accelerants in it. “It's essentially load without recovery, and that wears the system down faster than time alone ever would,” says Hall. Cortisol breaks down collagen and weakens the skin barrier, so you see dullness, dryness, fine lines, and slower healing. Stress can trigger breakouts and commonly flares acne, eczema, psoriasis, and rosacea. Poor sleep shows up as dark circles and puffiness. “Many women notice increased hair shedding two to three months after a stressful period, and there’s emerging research tying stress to premature graying. Nails can become brittle. You can’t out-serum chronic stress. The glow has to come from the inside,” Hall says.

Glover says that recognizing chronic stress is the best place to begin, and working toward a solution can be rewarding. “Think about the most stressful days you’ve had lately and consider the people, conversations, or activities that were the most stressful,” she suggests. “If scrolling on your phone isn’t on your list, it should be. Yes, it’s important to be aware of current events, but how aware and how frequently?” she asks. If you’re routinely exposed to chronic stress, Glover recommends changing your settings. “Get yourself outside for some exposure to nature, or to spend time with loved ones, or to have spontaneous laughter, nutritious food, and some exercise so you can put all those stress hormones to good use,” says Glover. Go for a bike ride, walk the beach or through a park if there’s a safe place outdoors to do so.

The “history” in question is the Kehlsteinhaus’ original owner. Bormann’s boss, after all, was Adolf Hitler.

The massive project took 13 months to complete and cost 30 million Reichsmarks (Roughly $120 million in 1937, or $2.81 billion today), but it was ultimately a folly. Hitler only visited the Eagle’s Nest 14 times, disliking heights, distrusting the building’s ornate elevator, and frowning upon Bormann’s excess in its construction.

The book Inside the Third Reich: Memoirs by Albert Speer recounts that some around Hitler would remark that “Bormann has created a gold-rush town atmosphere [in Obersalzberg]. Only he doesn’t find any, he spends it.” In response, Hitler reportedly “regretted the hubbub” and remarked that “It’s Bormann’s doing; I don’t want to interfere.”

While Eva Braun and her family spent ample time in the mountaintop retreat, including hosting the reception for Gretl Braun’s wedding in 1944, Hitler himself preferred his summer home, Berghof, located just four miles away.

The headlines surrounding the fire focus on the Hitler connection, understandably so, as it’s perhaps one of the only Hitler-related sites that was left standing (Berghof was demolished by the Bavarian government in 1952, for example). But a new report in Stars and Stripes calls attention to some other famous occupants of the Eagle’s Nest, and what remnants of them we lost in the fires which consumed the historic site.

By May 1945, U.S. Army units reached Berchtesgaden and Obersalzberg, ultimately occupying Kehlsteinhaus, which had been abandoned but remained largely undamaged by Allied bombs. There is some debate around which unit reached the Eagle’s Nest first, but what is certain is that elements of both the 3rd Infantry Division and the 101st Airborne Division entered and occupied the mountain retreat. Amidst the latter was the company known as “Easy Company,” latter immortalized in the book and HBO miniseries Band of Brothers. The occupation of the Eagle’s Nest is depicted in the Emmy-winning series’ finale, “Points.”

“American soldiers occupied the site,” Stars and Stripes notes, “used it briefly as a command post and recreation area, and took souvenirs from the interiors.”

They also left some souvenirs behind. Kehlsteinhaus boasted a lavish red marble fireplace, a personal gift from Benito Mussolini to Hitler. That fireplace provided a fine canvas for the men of the U.S. Army, many of whom emblazoned it with their names, a little G.I. graffiti to mark their territory.

For a time, the Eagle’s Nest served as rest-and-relaxation facilities for American soldiers, before it was turned over to the State of Bavaria in 1960 and converted into a tourist attraction with a restaurant and beer garden.

It’s easy to bristle at the idea of one of Hitler’s homes being a “tourist attraction.” It’s reasonable, at first blush, to think it gauche that a restaurant operated for decades in a building built by the Nazis. One might even question if anything was truly lost when a monument of such infamy goes up in flames.

Yet, a deeper understanding of the building’s full history shows why its continued existence held such significance, and why the Bavarian government intends to rebuild once the dust has settled on the rubble. As the Army newspaper noted: “Generations after the end of the war, American veterans often returned to the Eagle’s Nest, walking the same terraces they had first seen in 1945.”

In 1927, Norwegian civil engineer and amateur radio operator Jørgen Hals made an unusual discovery: while monitoring transmissions from a shortwave radio station in Eindhoven, The Netherlands, he began hearing odd, "delayed" radio echoes, arriving approximately three seconds after the original transmissions, that sometimes accompanied the station's signals.

"From where this echo comes I cannot say for the present," Hals wrote of his peculiar discovery, adding that he could "only confirm that I really heard it."

Almost a century after Hals' discovery, the exact cause of this unusual phenomenon — known today as long delayed echoes, or LDEs — remains unknown. Their baffling nature has prompted a range of potential explanations, most of which focus on a variety of unexplained natural phenomena occurring in Earth's atmosphere. However, a few more far-out possibilities, including potential evidence of extraterrestrial technology, have also been considered.

The enigma of long-delayed echoes has perplexed scientists since their initial detection by Hals, who at the time reported his odd findings to Norwegian physicist Carl Størmer. This prompted an investigation by Størmer with physicist Balthasar van der Pol, during which the duo was able to reproduce the phenomenon but were unsuccessful in determining its cause.

Since those early observations, ongoing reports of long-delayed echoes have continued to be recorded. Under normal conditions, radio echoes occur whenever a radio signal reflects or "bounces" off an object, although other conditions such as signals traveling through a delayed path can also cause echoes to occur.

Long delayed echoes, by contrast, are generally characterized by radio signals that return after more than 2.7 seconds following their original transmission, although the terminology has sometimes been applied more broadly.

Complicating investigations into this unusual brand of radio phenomena over the years has been their unpredictable nature, along with significant reported variances in the timing and nature of the delays, which has made it difficult for researchers to narrow down a single cause.

Despite the challenges presented by this unique class of space mysteries, there are several theories that have been put forward over the years about what could cause long-delayed echoes.

In the mid 1980s, researchers R. J. Vidmar and F. W. Crawford conducted experiments in Alaska that aimed to detect long-delayed echoes, which built on earlier research they published involving possible detections in 1970. According to their findings at the time, Vidmar and Crawford hypothesized that radio signals could be captured between ionized layers in the atmosphere and "guided" over long distances.

More recently, University of Oslo researcher Sverre Holm has advanced a similar concept that he calls magnetospherically ducted echo (MDE), in which radio waves may become trapped within electrically charged regions of Earth's upper atmosphere and guided along magnetic field lines extending far into the magnetosphere. Under certain conditions, these signals may reach as far away as the opposite hemisphere before they are "bounced" back toward Earth, resulting in the delayed echo effect.

According to Holm, MDE "is the best understood mechanism for long delayed echoes" out of five promising explanations he outlines, adding that based on the MDE model, such long delayed echoes "can be predicted to what seems like about 5 ms accuracy using a simple model for the magnetic field lines."

Still, one problem that arises involves the relatively short delays associated with this mechanism, which may not necessarily account for the much longer, multi-second echoes reported in some historical observations. Since radio waves travel at close to the speed of light, a signal could theoretically cover distances equivalent to the planet's circumference several times in a single second. While this doesn't prove the signals actually followed such paths, it does imply that some of the reported instances of long-delayed echoes might correspond to dozens of journeys around the planet.

Speaking with Space about historical cases involving much lengthier long-delayed echoes, Holm was clear in expressing that the MDE theory also has its limitations.

"In my view," Holm told Space in an email, "Magnetospheric Duct Echoes cannot [account] for those historical LDEs at all."

To illustrate this, Holm points to two primary differences. First, the historical echoes were detected at approximately 9.55 MHz, well above the 1–4 MHz frequency range typically associated with MDEs. Additionally, historical LDEs reportedly exhibited delays ranging from 3 to 30 seconds, whereas MDEs generally produce delays of only a few hundred milliseconds.

By contrast, "MDES are only a few hundred milliseconds," Holm explained, meaning that they cannot account for all the characteristics of the earlier historic observations of long-delayed echoes.

Other theories that might be capable of filling some of the gaps in the long-delayed echo mystery include the idea that electromagnetic radio energy could be coupled into slower plasma-wave "modes" while traveling through Earth’s upper atmosphere, which, according to Holm’s review of the earlier work by Vidmar and Crawford, could potentially create much longer delays than ordinary radio propagation would allow.

After this proposed coupling into a plasma-wave mode, the signals may be converted back to ordinary electromagnetic signals and bounced back down to lower levels of Earth's atmosphere. Finally, a similar line of investigation has looked at whether clouds of plasma originating from the sun might also be able to produce the well-characterized echo effect.

Although scientific research has identified several promising explanations for long-delayed echoes, the mysterious nature of these events has also inspired more speculative theories over the decades, including some involving the search for extraterrestrial intelligence.

This unique dimension to the LDE mystery has its roots in the theories of Australian physicist Ronald Bracewell, who was the first to propose the idea that autonomous interstellar probes might be used by an advanced civilization as a means of establishing contact.

According to Bracewell, the initiators of such a hypothetical contact scenario might choose to reveal their presence by intercepting and re-transmitting radio signals rather than engaging in direct communication. Carl Sagan explored a similar concept in his novel "Contact", in which transmissions from the 1936 Olympics — the earliest broadcast signals to leave Earth's ionosphere — are intercepted and rebroadcast back to Earth as a data-encoded message originating from the Vega system.

However, more than a decade before the publication of Sagan's famous novel, Scottish writer Duncan Lunan attracted international attention for his 1973 article in the journal "Spaceflight" outlining his belief that he had successfully decoded a radio message dispatched from an extraterrestrial probe. According to Lunan, the initial detection of this "hidden" alien signal had taken place decades earlier in 1927, with the famous discovery of long-delayed echoes by Jørgen Hals.

Lunan's theory essentially went like this: an extraterrestrial civilization in the Epsilon Boötis system had deployed a hypothetical autonomous communications probe (known as a Bracewell probe) to Earth in the distant past, which had been comfortably orbiting in one of Earth's gravitationally stable Lagrange points for thousands of years.

Using the original long-delayed echo detections from Hals and Størmer's research decades earlier, Lunan placed the transmitted sequence on a vertical axis, while placing the recorded echo delay time on a horizontal axis, revealing what he believed to be a view of the constellation Boötes as it might have looked 15,000 years ago — right around the time the suspected space probe would have been dispatched to Earth.

Despite Lunan's imaginative theory, an alternative hypothesis proposing a natural cause behind long-delayed echoes was subsequently advanced by Anthony Lawton, which ultimately garnered an award from the British Interplanetary Society. By 1976, Lunan had withdrawn his ideas in an article published in the Journal of the Society of Electronic and Radio Technicians, although he would eventually revisit a version of the theory in the 1990s.

While few modern researchers seriously consider extraterrestrial hypotheses to explain long-delayed echoes, these obscure phenomena remain intriguing, even as ongoing research sheds light on their possible causes. Explanations such as Sverre Holm's magnetospherically ducted echo model offer promising mechanisms that may account for at least some of these unusual signals, although important questions remain unanswered.

For now, long-delayed echoes serve as a reminder of the challenges of studying electromagnetic phenomena, and how even seemingly familiar aspects of our physical world can harbor mysteries that resist straightforward explanations, even after nearly a century of investigation.

#965. The George Lucas Museum Celebrates All Things Kitsch. Will the Art World Get on Board?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The permanent gallery at the new Lucas Museum of Narrative Art, the part you are meant to visit first, is not a bright, four-walled gallery, but a dark cave. Its first images are vivid backlit reproductions of some of the earliest ones made by humans: horses, deer, the Paleolithic bison, and human hand prints of the Altamira cavern complex in northern Spain.

I knew immediately what George Lucas was up to. He is alluding to the iconic scene from Star Wars Episode V: The Empire Strikes Back where Luke Skywalker, urged by Yoda, enters a cavern (“In you must go!”), encounters a dream-like version of Darth Vader, and, with a whoosh of his light saber, cracks open Vader’s helmet-mask to reveal… Luke himself! Lucas is manifesting—in his movie and his museum—a core archetype of mythologist Joseph Campbell. In this case, the confronting of one’s Jungian “shadow.”

Joseph Campbell, the author of A Skeleton Key to Finnegans Wake and The Hero with a Thousand Faces, is Lucas’s Yoda of ideas. The museum, which opened in September, is their shrine, holding a substantial collection of Lucas and his and partner Mellody Hobson’s 40,000 artworks.

From murals and manga to props and costumes, the museum stages its massive cache of storytelling art in a place where half the buzz is the architectural experience. In Exposition Park just south of Downtown Los Angeles—and across the street from Lucas’s USC alma mater—the 300,000-square-foot structure is a blorpy, biomorphic mound designed by Ma Yansong of MAD architects. All edges are rounded in a futurefied Deco. Its mothership shape holds portals and flight decks. It flows and floats over a 13-acre campus of green space, where even the roof is landscaped with native grasses and wildflowers. Glass pneumatic elevator tubes zoom you up to the fourth-floor galleries. The staff wear snappy uniforms, their ticket scanners emitting “blaster” sounds. Its two theaters are wide wombs. Sleek and chic, the view from the museum’s Skywalker Grill soars over the city.

All told, it’s as close to a thrill ride as a building can be. And the whole thing cost some $1 billion, all funded by Lucas and Hobson.

I share Lucas’ archetype fetish. It’s what first led me, back in the 1980s, to the Star Wars saga in all its epic hokeyness. (I’ve seen each one, including the cringe-maxed prequel trilogy, all the offshoots, plus pretty much every other movie the now-82-year-old Lucas has directed or concepted). And still, he sees deep meaning in mythic pop. For him, transcultural Campbellian archetypes and their thousand-in-one quests, trials by fire, death-and-resurrections—their gods, monsters, prophesies, saviors, heroes, mentors, virgin births, and sexual awakenings—are the story-stuff of art.

Because these stories permeate all narrative art, often subtextually, there’s a lot of the low- to middle-brow on view at the museum. He posits a supreme vernacular. There’s mythic gold even in adorkable visual pulp. Entire galleries are devoted, for example, to Norman Rockwell and Maxfield Parish (the latter’s golden, late-afternoon light and Lake Como settings inspired Lucas’ depiction of the garden-planet Naboo in Star Wars: Episode I – The Phantom Menace). There’s also a shit-ton of comics, magazine, children’s illustrations, and even advertising, the kind of material that’s mostly marginalized by the fine-art hierarchy of traditional museums.

Legitimate greatness acts as a check on all the kitsch. That first-floor cave leads to “The History of Narrative Art: Heroes + Myths + The Sacred Exhibition.” This low-light, moody, winding environment fast-forwards through thousands of years of storytelling, joining original items from the Lucas collection with high-def reproductions and projections, including the Sistine Chapel ceiling, the Aztec Sun Stone and the interior of Hagia Sophia.

The upper galleries hold the more contemporary goodies. Imagery from Westerns, samurai epics, and sci-fi—all genres that informed his movies—abound. Some are enormous and overpowering. Here also are political posters: Shepard Fairey and Ernesto Yerena Montejana’s 2010 “Immigration Reform Now,” and Richard V. Correll’s “I Want Your Social Security Dollars for US Military,” with a leering, finger-jabbing Ronald Reagan. There’s some badass lowrider art. Dominating the Manga and Anime gallery is a leggy, life-size statue by Takashi Murakami: super-cute warrior “Miss Ko².”

Will the art-industrial complex take to the Lucas Museum? It arrives at a time of slumps. “The money’s not flowing,” collector-dealer Stefan Simchowitz recently told Artnet. “If you speak to the people in the business, they’re exhausted, they’re demoralized, the energy is not there because the business isn’t there either.” Anonymous critic-practitioners, such as the Diva Corp collective in Los Angeles, carp with glee at hypocrisies in the gallery ecosystem and art fairs, which are also struggling. According to The American Alliance for Museums, many major venues face falling attendance and rising costs, with 55% of them reporting fewer visitors in 2025 than in 2019, “a setback from 2024, when 49% remained below pre-pandemic levels.”

Now comes the Lucas Museum, funded via its own philanthropic 501(c)(3), beholden to neither government grants nor donors and collectors. And with its niche focus on the vernacular, it is singular among establishments that prize fine-art prestige. Time to reignite the age-old conflict between high-brow and low-brow. There have even been major—and very popular—exhibitions exploring this schism. New York Museum of Modern Art’s 1991 “High and Low: Modern Art and Popular Culture” upheld the profound transgressions of comic-book genius R. Crumb alongside pop masters Roy Lichtenstein and Andy Warhol. (I saw one Warhol at the Lucas: the color screenprint “Howdy Doody,” from his “Myth” series.) And The Museum of Contemporary Art in Los Angeles had its “Helter Skelter: L.A. Art in the 1990s,” a trippy funhouse that helped catapult Mike Kelley, Paul McCarthy, Raymond Pettibone and others of their “bad boy” ilk, all of whom deal in besmirched pulp and pop.

But, for the most part, museums come at this genre with blinders, excluding it either to service big-money sponsors and investors or just because they scorn it. One imagines that Lucas’ monomaniacal focus on everyday to age-old mythology might aggravate them, what with its end-run around their approved canons.

Is this capital-A Art or “just” illustration? Is it a private gallery or a museum for the public? Is it an excessive monument to one man’s own taste? Perhaps speaking for many, a writer in Art Review dissed the “…dominant American ideology reflected in the country’s most commercialized artforms,” where “gendered stereotypes and sexist visual conventions abound,” as sci-fi/fantasy illustrator “…Frank Frazetta’s heroines cling to a male saviour’s leg, their preternatural proportions barely contained by bikinis.” Oh my!

That review griped that the “Western Stories” gallery “consists entirely of Wild West imagery from a beloved genre that has enabled an insidious historical revision of the forced displacement of Indigenous people. The ‘Work’ gallery largely focuses on white men engaged in the dignified labour of industrialization.”

I would not respect a pop-culture cathedral that sanctifies harmful stereotypes. But that’s not where Lucas stands—neither in his movies nor here. The fifth-floor Murals gallery, in fact, is filled with work extolling the proletariat, blasting gender and ethnic norms, and explicitly attacking capitalist tyranny.

In 2021, the museum acquired the archive for Judy Baca’s nearly one-mile-long mural “The History of California,” often called “The Great Wall of Los Angeles. It is represented in its totality via a life-sized scrolling video that, in the artists’ version of social-realist style, depicts working-class struggles, marginalized communities, and political dissidents.

Also recreated is Diego Rivera’s “Man, Controller of the Universe.” It’s a monumental amalgam of scenes showing the clash of capital and the proletariat. Transcending rote social realism, it verges on science fiction by including images of inner and outer space. Judging by the clunky but righteous anti-imperialism of the entire Star Wars franchise, Lucas likely loves this piece.

Of course, there is one special interest group he is beholden to: his fans. The halls are crammed with people of all ages and many languages sporting Star Wars gear. In the “Star Wars in Motion” installation of the Cinema gallery are full-scale versions of Luke Skywalker's Landspeeder, Anakin Skywalker's podracer and General Grievous's wheel bike. (Here I overheard my Star Wars completist comrades explain to their kids the origin of each prop.)

It’s difficult to imagine anyone else creating with the focus of Lucas on this scale. I shudder to think what such a place would be in the hands of, say, Elon Musk, or any of the techbrolocrats dominating politics and the economy. Still, Lucas and Hobson could expand their vision. What if a savvy curator presented ultra-contemporary artists? There are a number of young women collagists extolling and/or critiquing sexual archetypes in analog (María Berrío, Daisy Holden, Solá Saar, Erin C. Alexander, Ashley Pottenger) or producing digital and A.I. video (Petra Cortright, Karina Bush, Erin Robinson). R. Crumb is in the Lucas with a few comix covers. And the museum has purchased his 200-drawing opus “The Book of Genesis.” How about a full-on retrospective dedicated to Crumb and his collaborator Aline Kominsky-Crumb? Is there another artist X-raying American archetypes with such vicious satire?

I believe Lucas agrees with me. As he told The Los Angeles Times, “I’ve spent more than 50 years collecting the work of artists who don’t always get their due — illustrators, cartoonists, muralists, painters — because I believe their images are as vital as any other art form. They deserve to be in a museum.” Don’t myth it.

InfoQ Homepage News Uber Moves beyond CPU Cores with Performance-Aware Scheduling for Stateful Workloads

Oct 27, 2026 3 min read

Uber has introduced Normalized Compute Units (NCUs) to make CPU allocation more consistent across its highly heterogeneous server fleet. The approach, implemented in Uber's Odin stateful orchestration platform, accounts for differences in processor performance rather than treating every CPU core as equivalent.

Odin manages around 5 million CPU cores across on-premises data centres and public clouds, including multiple generations of x86 hardware and ARM processors. Uber found that single-threaded performance can vary by almost a factor of two between the fastest and slowest cores. Consequently, the same workload can require significantly different numbers of physical CPU cores depending on where it runs.

The problem creates two competing risks. Allocating resources according to the slowest hardware can waste capacity on faster machines, while allocating according to average usage can leave workloads under-provisioned when they move to slower servers. For stateful workloads, where moving traffic to another machine is often not possible, the latter can become a reliability problem.

Uber's solution is to separate the amount of compute performance a workload requires from the physical cores used to provide it.

Odin assigns each server type a performance factor, using an older server generation as a baseline of 100. Newer hardware can have considerably higher values; Uber reports factors approaching 190 for some servers. These factors are initially determined during hardware qualification and then refined using production workload data.

CPU usage is converted into normalized units using these factors. Odin can then determine that a workload requires a particular amount of compute capacity and translate that requirement into a different number of physical cores depending on the server hosting it.

A workload on faster hardware may therefore receive fewer cores, while the same workload on slower hardware receives more. The objective is not to make the number of cores identical, but to make the computational capacity more comparable.

Uber also found that performance factors differ between technologies. Its measurements for Kafka, for example, showed substantially different performance between ARM, older on-premises hardware, and newer x86 systems. The company therefore updates factors using production measurements rather than relying exclusively on synthetic benchmarks.

For stateless services, differences between servers can often be addressed through load balancing. Traffic can be directed towards less-loaded or faster machines.

Stateful systems do not have the same flexibility. A request may need to reach the server containing a particular shard or piece of data. Moving the request elsewhere does not solve the underlying resource problem.

In the post, Uber describes workloads where a leader consumes more than ten times the CPU of its followers. If that leader moves from a fast server to a slower one, its CPU consumption can increase substantially, potentially causing resource pressure even though the workload itself has not changed.

Uber has rolled the approach out across approximately 1.4 million CPU cores of automatically scaled Odin workloads. For Kafka, CPU utilisation initially varied by around eight percentage points between server types, with the hottest hardware running at approximately 50% higher utilisation than the coldest.

After introducing performance-aware allocation, the difference fell to around three percentage points, with the hottest hardware only about 10% higher than the slowest. Uber also reduced total Kafka CPU allocation by approximately 8%, with much of the reduction occurring on faster hardware where the savings are potentially more valuable.

The approach differs from the CPU abstraction used by Kubernetes, where CPU requests are expressed as cores or millicores. Kubernetes deliberately treats a CPU unit as an absolute quantity of CPU time, regardless of the size of the underlying machine. That abstraction works well for portable workload definitions, but it does not inherently express the relative performance of different processor generations.

Uber's NCU model addresses a somewhat different problem: making the resource itself performance-aware. Rather than primarily deciding how many workloads should fit on a machine or how much CPU a workload should receive, it asks how much computational capacity a CPU allocation actually represents.
