#174. Barry Diller shares why his deal to buy MGM fell apart

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Media mogul Barry Diller thinks businesses that operate in the real world have a strong protective moat against AI.

That was the inspiration for his company People Inc.'s June bid for MGM Resorts International's casino and hotel business, which he called "forever assets."

As some consumers turn to AI chatbots to complete tasks like buying plane tickets or managing subscriptions, digital-only businesses could get boxed out. That creates real risks for internet brands like Expedia Inc., where Diller is an investor and board member. It's less of a problem for companies whose businesses exist in the real world, he said.

Nothing is going to replace Las Vegas, Diller said — and no one's going to buy another one. Gaming is only part of the equation, he said.

"No disintermediation is coming between a person who wants to go to a place, and that place," he said, speaking to BI's Chief Correspondent Peter Kafka at Business Insider's The Long Play event in New York on Thursday.

Follow along with our coverage from Business Insider's The Long Play

People Inc. first took a 27% stake in MGM in 2020. The company, which operates magazines such as Food & Wine, People, and Southern Living, later offered to buy the Las Vegas brand outright in June but withdrew its bid in September.

Diller said the deal fell apart after he became uncomfortable with the proposed structure, which involved taking on debt and asking third parties for money.

MGM later offered to buy People Inc. instead, but told investors on Wednesday that it had shelved the plan. Diller said it was "highly unlikely" that MGM was interested in People's magazine assets, but rather it had hoped to buy back People's MGM shares.

People remains MGM's largest investor, though it has agreed not to buy more than a 29.9% stake in the company and to vote with MGM's board in connection with a change-of-control transaction.

People, which, beyond its magazine portfolio, also owns stakes in The Daily Beast and the car-rental company Turo, laid off 195 workers on Thursday, per a memo from CEO Neil Vogel, which Business Insider viewed. The vast majority of impacted staffers worked on its "print and legacy web businesses," the memo said. The company has "added 320 new employees so far in 2026," it said.

Diller told Kafka that the layoffs were a sign of a healthy company that has a profitable print business in a "steady kind of decline."

"We've been transferring out people in these legacy businesses and transferring in people to do the new work," he said.

Diller is a longtime media operator. He served as chairman and CEO of Paramount Pictures in the 1970s and 80s, before joining Fox Inc. and helping to start Fox Broadcasting with Rupert Murdoch in the mid-1980s. He then worked at QVC before serving as CEO of IAC and taking on roles at Expedia Group. He is on the board of directors at MGM.

Bank Mandiri mencatatkan impairment loss sebesar Rp 8,47 triliun secara kumulatif hingga September 2026. Khusus kuartal III-2026, beban pencadangan bank pelat merah ini mencapai Rp 3,04 triliun, naik 5,9% secara kuartalan (qoq).

Berbeda dengan Bank Mandiri, beban pencadangan BNI justru menurun. Secara kumulatif, impairment loss BNI mencapai Rp 6,94 triliun hingga September 2026.

Sementara itu, pada kuartal III-2026, nilainya sebesar Rp 1,89 triliun, turun 37,8% qoq. Penurunan ini berbalik dari kenaikan 51,2% qoq pada kuartal sebelumnya.

#184. Instagram is trying to woo YouTube creators with six-figure checks

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Instagram wants what YouTube has — and it's willing to pay up to get it.

In an effort to persuade content creators to post long-form video on Instagram, the company is paying a select number of creators hefty sums, according to people familiar with the deals.

Not every deal is equal. The Meta-owned platform has offered select creators $50,000 to post long-form video to its new "Features" product, while others have been offered six-figure deals, some of which have reached $500,000, Business Insider exclusively learned.

"We've been public about the fact that we are funding Features content directly with some creators right now to support them," a Meta spokesperson told Business Insider. "The details of those deals are confidential, but we're excited to see the content going live."

On Monday, Instagram unveiled an overhaul of its video formats. Most importantly, it's resurrecting IGTV — a long-form video product Instagram launched in 2018 and sunset in 2022 — in the form of Features. The horizontal, wide-screen videos will exist within the Instagram app, and can be as long as a feature film: 90 minutes.

Long-form video isn't exactly what people are used to watching on Instagram. Reels, the platform's answer to TikTok's meteoric rise, has redefined the Instagram experience and is driving time-spent and revenue across Meta's apps.

YouTube, however, reigns king as the internet's destination for sit-down, longer-form videos.

Instagram knows this. The deals do not require exclusivity to Instagram, multiple people familiar with the agreements said; one person familiar with the offers said it's asking creators to post to Instagram any of the long-form content they publish to YouTube that same day.

Reza Izad, partner at Underscore Talent, which reps clients like "Subway Takes'" Kareem Rahma and Noah Beck, called the deals an "exciting opportunity" for creators.

"Every time there's a new feature in these apps that the company's promoting, people will have more opportunity to be seen because there's going to be algorithmic preference to this kind of content," he said. "And unlike short-form, there's not a lot of abundance."

Vendrov began the post by saying his account should not be taken as gospel. He said he recalled the events "from memory," wrote the "gist of the dialogue rather than the exact words used," and that the post is "merely the experience of one guy in a junior role during a short window of time."

Still, it's a remarkable look into the early days of one of the world's preeminent AI companies, with a named employee explaining how the company's formative period created a highly particular workplace.

It also comes days after Anthropic filed to go public. Anthropic declined to comment on this story.

Vendrov, whose LinkedIn says he now works at Midjourney, wrote that he was weighing contract offers from OpenAI and Anthropic in 2022.

He says he decided to join Anthropic after lunch with one of the AI company's cofounders, who said they were already chatting with regulators in Washington.

A recruiter asked whether he wanted to pledge two-thirds of his equity to charity.

#45. Why Expertise Alone Isn’t Enough to Earn You Media Coverage

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

Many experts and thought leaders want media coverage but lead with their title and credentials. Editors are looking for something more.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

I hear it all the time. “I’m a doctor and want PR. But I want PR only about doing surgery, because I am a surgeon.”

Alas, that’s not exactly how media relations works.

I know that can sting, and I get it. After all, most experts I talk to have so many opportunities to get featured in the media. Reporters, podcast hosts and editors are looking for credible voices all the time, and an expert with years of hands-on experience is exactly who they want.

So why do so many experts send pitch after pitch and get little or nothing back?

As a longtime publicist and the host of the Grow Your Wellth podcast, I see this from both sides. I receive guest pitches regularly, and they often open with the expert’s credentials: where they trained, what they are certified in, how long they have been in practice. What they less often pitch me is what the episode would be about or what problem my listeners would walk away solving. And I’m left trying to figure out where to place them — or worse — just unable to respond.

Why? Because even when it’s impressive, a resume alone does not make for good programming.

Many experts lead with the job they do every day, because that’s where their credibility lies. But if you want to see more recognition as an expert, that comes from building your visibility around a problem you solve or a unique perspective you bring, not just around your title. I want to show you what that looks like and why it works.

Think about how an editor decides what to cover. They are not asking what you do. They are asking what their reader is struggling with or wants and who can help explain it.

Your daily work is a solution. The problem behind it is the story. Editors write about problems and how to solve them, and they do it more now than ever because readers arrive through search. People type what they are feeling or worried about, and the coverage that earns their attention is the coverage that meets them there.

The same is true when someone asks an AI tool for help. According to OpenAI, more than 300 million questions are asked in ChatGPT every week about health and wellness. Users describe a problem, and the experts who have built their presence around that problem are the ones that surface.

So, if you are getting into PR just to talk about yourself, you are missing a huge visibility opportunity.

Believe it or not, there are nearly a dozen types of media coverage. They include dedicated features, expert commentary, trend-driven coverage and podcasts, among others. Almost everyone wants the dedicated feature, which is the story built around you. But there are far fewer of those opportunities to begin with.

The other types are not consolation prizes. They are how you build the authority that makes a feature possible in the first place.

L&P Aesthetics in Palo Alto is led by two dual board-certified facial plastic surgeons. Like most physicians we work with at FACTEUR PR, they are most excited about discussing what they do each day, which is facial plastic surgery. It is technical, it is fascinating, and they are very good at it.

However, if you ask them or their patients, you’ll find that the problem they solve is bigger than any one procedure; it’s about helping renew their patients’ sense of identity.

That journey happens well before the patient is on the operating table. It may begin with the moment when they look in the mirror and do not quite recognize the person looking back. Maybe it starts with their skin, their eyes, their smile or their hair. In other words, their patients don’t just seek a snatched jawline — they want to feel like themselves again (maybe even better!).

That is the problem these doctors solve for.

To keep that focus, we mapped their authority across five tiers, moving from the broadest conversation to the most personal one. Most media stories in health, wellness and aesthetics happen in the first three layers because that is most searchable and what readers want.

The first layer starts with problem awareness — it’s the universal conversation about why skin, hair and the face age, and what science actually supports. From there, the layers get deeper, going from problem aware to solution aware, and finally landing at the company as the go-to solution.

This approach has earned L&P Aesthetics expert commentary published in Vogue and ELLE, and yes, dedicated features in People, The Daily Beast, USA Today … and counting. The mix matters. Authority is not built with one medium. It is generated across them.

You do not need a new brand to start. Try finishing this sentence: I help [this type of person] who are struggling with [this type of problem] so they can [achieve, feel, or do something they couldn’t before]. If you fill in the blanks with your title or your service, go back and name the feeling or the problem instead.

Then ask yourself three questions. What conversation is already happening around this problem? Where do I have a perspective most people covering it do not? And what are the five topics an editor in my field could cover this month where I could truly help their reader?

Remember, your credentials still matter. They are the proof behind your point of view! But they are not the headline. Build around the problem, join the conversation first, and the coverage you expected from the beginning becomes far more likely to show up and be sought after.
