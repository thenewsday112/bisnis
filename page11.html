India voted in favour of a UN General Assembly resolution calling for an end to the economic, commercial and financial embargo imposed by the U.S. against Cuba, reiterating that the restrictions should be lifted to help the Caribbean nation pursue its development aspirations.

The 193-member General Assembly adopted the resolution on Wednesday (October 28, 2026) with 130 countries voting in favour, 17 against and 25 abstaining.

Delivering India's statement, Counsellor at India's Permanent Mission to the UN Soumya Nair said the General Assembly has repeatedly called for an end to the embargo and rejected laws and regulations with extraterritorial effects that adversely affect the sovereignty of other states and the progress and prosperity of their peoples.

"India reiterates the Assembly's call to all states to refrain from promulgating and applying, and to repeal and invalidate, such laws and measures in conformity with their obligations under the Charter of the United Nations and international law," she said.

Ms. Nair said India's commitment to multilateralism is rooted in the belief that international challenges are best addressed through dialogue and diplomacy, in accordance with the UN Charter.

"It is in this spirit that India hopes that the embargo will be lifted at the earliest to help create an environment that would enable the people of Cuba to participate more fully in international cooperation and pursue their legitimate development aspirations,” she said.

Citing a report by the UN Secretary-General, Ms. Nair highlighted the impact of the restrictions on Cuba's economy and essential services.

Cuba's health system faces shortages of over 60% of essential medicines, while food production has declined by more than 50% in the past five years, she said.

“The restrictions on fuel exports to Cuba have also aggravated the country's ongoing energy crisis. The shortages have disrupted essential services, including healthcare, education, water supplies, food distribution, and transportation,” Ms. Nair said.

The conflict between America and Cuba stems from a decades-old political, economic, and ideological divide that began when Fidel Castro overthrew a U.S.-backed government in 1959 and aligned the island with the Soviet Union.

Washington has maintained a strict trade embargo and sanctions against Cuba for over six decades to pressure its communist regime. Cuba blames these restrictions for crippling its economy, causing severe fuel shortages, blackouts, and deep poverty.

The resolution, titled "Necessity of ending the economic, commercial and financial embargo imposed by the United States of America against Cuba", reflects the General Assembly's longstanding opposition to the U.S. restrictions. However, General Assembly resolutions of this nature are not legally binding.

On Tuesday (October 27, 2026), unprecedented scenes unfolded in the UN General Assembly hall as tensions escalated between U.S. Ambassador to the UN Mike Waltz and Cuban Foreign Minister Bruno Rodriguez Parrilla.

While Mr. Waltz was addressing the UNGA on Cuba, Mr. Parrilla and other Cuban diplomats rose from their assigned seats and walked up towards the podium to confront the American envoy, triggering a verbal duel between the two sides.

As Mr. Parrilla walked up to the podium, Mr. Waltz stepped aside from the lectern and gestured the Cuban Minister “to back away from this podium”.

“Mr. President, this individual needs to back away from this podium. He is not going to intimidate me in the United States of America like he tries to intimidate political prisoners,” Mr. Waltz said, as Parrilla and other Cuban diplomats continued to confront him.

UNGA President Khalilur Rahman, who was presiding over the meeting, subsequently had to suspend the session.

Later, Mr. Waltz said he had questioned why Cuba's “rulers” came to the UN to talk about the suffering of the Cuban people while, at the “height of hypocrisy, enjoying luxury watches, Rolexes, yachts, mansions, and even private islands, "while the Cuban people starve and sit in the dark”.

“In response, the regime's so-called diplomats responded by pounding their desks, screaming across this chamber, storming the podium, not respecting the sanctity of this institution, and even assaulting an American diplomat," he said.

Mr. Waltz alleged that the conduct violated the Host Country Agreement and said the diplomat concerned would be put on a plane back to Havana.

"It is unacceptable. If you lay a hand on an American diplomat in this body, there will be consequences," he said.

A train passenger’s barking dog saved the day for him by apparently causing a “marauding” knifeman to run away, as another traveller said the attacker who stabbed 10 people on the service had the “greatest delight on his face”, a court heard.

Raza Aslam, who was travelling on the London-bound LNER service, heard people say a man was saying “the devil won’t win”, before he spotted 33-year-old Anthony Williams with a knife, Cambridge Crown Court was told on Thursday.

Prosecutor Christopher Paxton KC said car delivery driver Mr Aslam grabbed a frying pan but was stabbed in the hand by Williams on 1 November last year and felt an “immediate loss of energy from his arm”.

Mr Paxton said Samir Zitouni, who was working on the train that was stopped at Huntingdon, Cambridgeshire, responded by throwing things at the defendant and “saved” Mr Aslam’s life.

The prosecutor said Mr Aslam told police Williams “had the greatest delight on his face”.

Mr Aslam said: “I saw his eyes expand with pleasure. I saw the contempt on his face, like a person who’s high.

“He was just loving every single moment. He loved seeing us in desperation.”

Another passenger, Scott Green, was travelling with his dog Poppy when he heard something he thought was a “scrap”, Mr Paxton said.

He said Mr Green “felt a jab to his left side, then he saw a knife, then he saw the defendant”.

Mr Green told police he thought “I have to get the knife” and “I’ve gone to grab the knife, and I remember the pain”.

He said he “tried to yank it, but I couldn’t remove it” and remembered the “sharpness”.

He said his dog started barking and Williams had “done a runner and the last I saw was him running down the aisle”. Mr Paxton said it seemed that Poppy “saved the day” for Mr Green.

The prosecutor said the defendant had previously been “bitten by a dog” in an “unrelated incident”.

He said Mr Green took his dog and ran the other way along the train, and a woman took them to safety in a toilet.

The service had left Peterborough at around 7.29pm and was stopped at Huntingdon train station at 7.44pm by the “quick thinking” train driver.

Williams denies the attempted murders of 10 people on the service.

A recording of a call made by passenger Stephen Crean was played to the jury, in which he said “I need help” and “I’m scared” from a toilet he locked himself inside until police knocked on the door at Huntingdon station.

The court was shown footage of Mr Crean confronting Williams, after telling others to shut the door and stay hidden in a cafe bar area of the train, when he was stabbed to his back, hip, fingers and head.

Mr Paxton told the court: “Mr Crean had no knife, no weapon, and looked, he will tell you, the defendant in the eyes, and Mr Crean says the defendant said something he will never forget.

“It was these words, ‘Do you want to die?’”

The court heard Mr Crean, who was a Nottingham Forest fan travelling home from a game, described Williams as being on a “mission” during the attack and their interaction “was like Come Dancing, it was like we were all over the place”.

Mr Paxton said another passenger, 73-year-old David Presland, fell while trying to flee from Williams and was stabbed while on the floor.

He said Mr Presland told police: “I thought I was going to die. I’m very lucky that I’ve survived.”

The barrister said the pensioner told police he was “on his hands and knees” and “that’s when he stabbed me in the back, and that’s when I turned around in front of him, and I just looked at his face and he stabbed me under the arm”.

Mr Paxton said Mr Presland said he was helped by someone he knew as Kevin, who “we believe to be Kevin Deely, who himself was stabbed by the defendant”.

The prosecutor said: “In this storm of violence, and even when gripped by fear, many there on the train helped others and some stood tall, quite literally, against the defendant’s violent onslaught.”

The court heard another passenger, Scott Bletcher, recalled saying “I don’t think I’m going to make it” after being stabbed to his head, chest and forearm.

Mr Paxton said Mr Bletcher, who was in a window seat, was a “sitting duck for the armed defendant”.

The prosecutor said Mr Bletcher called 999 and hid in a bike rack until the train stopped.

“At that stage, Mr Bletcher will explain he felt incredibly tired, was struggling to keep his eyes open and recalls saying, and I quote, ‘I don’t think I’m going to make it’,” Mr Paxton said.

Williams also denies the attempted murders of three people in Peterborough on the evening of 31 October, and of a 17-year-old boy at Pontoon Dock DLR station in London in the early hours of 1 November.

He has admitted intending to cause harm, but denies 14 counts of attempted murder.

#223. Trump administration attempts to take down leak of ICE personnel data

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An Icelandic web company, FlokiNET, has defended the data’s publication as satisfying a ‘legitimate’ public interest.

The United States Department of Homeland Security (DHS) has asked an Icelandic web hosting company to take down a site with information about tens of thousands of its employees.

On Thursday, the Icelandic company FlokiNET posted a letter it received from the cybersecurity firm ZeroFox, on behalf of DHS.

The letter accused FlokiNET of violating its terms of service by publishing a “stolen” employee dataset and asked it to “remove or disable” the information.

“The content is live and places more than 40,000 identifiable individuals at risk of harassment, stalking, and physical harm,” the letter said.

FlokiNET said the letter concerned one domain, icepi.cx, which has compiled information about employees from a range of DHS agencies, including Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) and Customs and Border Protection (CBP).

Those agencies have come under intense scrutiny since the start of President Donald Trump’s mass deportation campaign in 2025.

But the Trump administration has often refused to identify DHS agents involved in specific operations, citing safety risks.

Though FlokiNET said it refused to comply with DHS’s request, it noted that the website’s registrar ultimately suspended the domain. The employee information has since moved to a different domain.

In a statement posted online, FlokiNET defended its decision not to remove the site.

“Federal officers carrying out an enforcement programme are not private individuals while they are carrying it out,” the company said. “The public has a legitimate interest in knowing who is doing that work, under whose authority, and in which office.”

The database was first posted online on Wednesday by Distributed Denial of Secrets (DDoSecrets), a group that showcases leaked government data.

In the past, DDoSecrets has released emails from convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein, as well as information from the Israeli Ministry of Justice and police departments in the US.

A hacker group calling itself the “Department of Peace” has claimed responsibility for obtaining and sharing the DHS data.

Since the database was first published, other groups have spread the information elsewhere, including on sites like icepi.cx. As a result, the information is unlikely to be entirely scrubbed from the internet.

The Trump administration, however, has taken an aggressive approach to online platforms purporting to contain information about ICE employees and other agents involved in immigration operations.

Last year, for instance, the US Department of Justice pressured tech giants like Apple to remove apps that track ICE agents, including one called ICEBlock.

The move sparked backlash from Democrats and free-speech advocates, who argued that the public has a right to report on ICE’s movements.

“Blocking access to these apps at the request of the federal government raises the distinct possibility that Apple is working with government to violate its customers’ First Amendment right to record and report on the activities of their government,” Democratic Representative Jamie Raskin wrote in a letter last year, asking Apple to review its decision.

Immigration agents, meanwhile, have taken several steps to conceal their identities during operations, including wearing face masks or street clothes.

That lack of transparency has spurred criticism from human rights groups and immigration advocates, who argue that they have little recourse to pursue allegations of excessive force or impropriety during ICE raids.

Since Trump was sworn in for a second term, at least 25 people have faced gunfire from immigration agents. Six of the shootings have resulted in deaths.

Other cases have involved alleged violations of civil liberties – including unlawful arrests and racial profiling – as well as controversial tactics like car ramming and tackling.

#706. Australia sitting on unused goldmine of old gigantic tyres

Recyclers say mining companies are burying $200m worth of natural rubber every year. (Supplied: Tyrecycle)

Every year, Australia's major miners choose to bury more than 100,000 tonnes of end-of-life tyres.

The Australian Tyre Recyclers Association says the natural rubber alone could be worth up to $200 million.

Advocates are calling on state and federal governments to more tightly regulate mining tyre disposal.

Among the largest tyres ever manufactured, they help extract and haul billions of dollars of Australian resources.

But recyclers say the huge amount of rubber discarded by mining companies is its own kind of buried treasure.

For decades, the mining industry has been legally allowed to bury, stockpile, or send to landfill more than 100,000 tonnes of end-of-life tyres every year.

It is in sharp contrast to the rest of the tyre market, which collects about 96 per cent of passenger and commercial tyres for recycling, according to the Australian Tyre Recyclers Association (ATRA).

A haul truck operating in Kalgoorlie-Boulder's Super Pit gold mine. (ABC Goldfields: Jarrod Lucas)

ATRA executive officer Robert Kelman said just two per cent of off-the-road (OTR) mining tyres were recovered.

"These are very big mining tyres. They might be four, five, six tonnes," Mr Kelman said.

Long argued on environmental grounds, a new report is mounting the economic case for OTC tyre recycling.

ATRA found up to $200 million of raw, natural rubber was being buried annually on mine sites.

That dollar figure is expected to climb as global rubber production lags behind demand.

"There are risks to virgin rubber production, which is essential for these mining tyres," Mr Kelman said.

"The mining industry should be a part of shoring up the production of new tyres, getting that material off site, [and] getting it into the recovery markets."

Global production of natural rubber is expected to fall short of demand. (Reuters: Nguyen Huy Kham)

The Association of Natural Rubber Producing Countries forecasts a natural rubber shortfall of 317,000 tonnes in 2026, citing erratic weather across tropical forest plantations in south-east Asia.

Miles Kenney-Lazar, a rubber expert at the University of Melbourne, said the commodity often cycled through highs and lows, but now other factors were affecting production.

"More recently, there's a lot of environmental changes that are impacting rubber plantations, like flooding and climate change," he said.

Meanwhile, the need for the material has only grown alongside electric vehicles, which tend to be heavier and wear through rubber more quickly.

"If better systems can be put in place to ensure those tyres can efficiently be recycled and put on the market, then I don't see why … mining companies or others wouldn't want to take advantage," Dr Kenney-Lazar said.

The West Australian and Commonwealth governments have collectively invested $20.7 million into six tyre recycling infrastructure projects under the Recycling Modernisation Fund, while a further $62 million has been kicked in by industry.

One of those facilities was built by Tyrecycle in WA's Pilbara region — home to 49 per cent of the nation's mining tyres.

But 18 months after opening for business in Port Hedland, the plant is processing a fraction of its 30,000 tonne capacity.

Tyrecycle's Port Hedland facility is operating at 10 per cent of its capacity. (Supplied: Tyrecycle)

"It has been slow going, to be honest," company spokesperson Ashley Battilana said.

"We can do 30,000 tonnes easily and, with some more infrastructure put in there, we could easily go a bit more, but currently we're only doing 2,000 to 3,000 tonnes per annum."

Mr Battilana called on lawmakers to tighten regulations and force companies to face the up-front costs of recycling.

"The government's undermining the tyre recycling industry by allowing the burial of tyres," he said.

"The infrastructure is there to process these tyres. The offtake is there as well, both domestically and internationally.

Western Australia's Chamber of Minerals and Energy said the sector aimed to minimise waste "wherever feasible."

Policy and advocacy director Anita Logiudice pointed to existing partnerships with tyre recyclers to produce crumb for road bitumen.

"However, some barriers remain," she said.

"These include logistical challenges associated with transporting OTR tyres from remote mines to the nearest recycling plant, as well as the relatively small market for crumb rubber in WA."

The Chamber of Minerals and Energy says logisitics remain the biggest barrier to tyre recycling. (ABC News: Keana Naughton)

Earlier this year, a parliamentary inquiry made 12 recommendations to the federal government, including the staged, legislated reduction of onsite burial for OTR tyres over the next five years.

A spokesperson for Industry and Innovation Minister Tim Ayres said the government was still considering its response.
