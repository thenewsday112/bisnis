When the most recent meeting of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), held in Addis Ababa, ended earlier this month, its conclusion drew negative media headlines. The United Nations body — the world’s forum for climate scientists — had failed, not for the first time, to agree a timetable for its next comprehensive review of the science of global climate change.

Many countries would like the review, known as the Seventh Assessment Report (AR7), to be published in 2028. This would be in time for a UN climate meeting called the Global Stocktake, at which countries assess their progress towards meeting climate pledges. A number of nations, including some European countries and small island states, want the report to be published ahead of the stocktake. This would allow decisions such as adjustments to pledges to be informed by the latest science.

A second, smaller group, including India, Iran, Kenya, Russia and Saudi Arabia, is arguing that 2028 is too soon. They say that some of the poorest countries lack the capacity to participate in the process of reviewing the report before it is published. They also object to the fact that the studies under review are dominated by science from institutions in high-income countries. However, for AR7, the IPCC says 51% of authors and review editors are from developing countries and nations with economies in transition.

Reviewing IPCC assessment reports is a big undertaking. When AR6 was published in 2023, it ran to more than 7,500 pages and received some 200,000 comments from reviewers around the world.

But the IPCC faces an even more serious problem: it is running out of money. For the first time in its history, it might not have the funds to finish an assessment report. The body relies on voluntary donations from governments and other UN agencies, and these have been falling since 2023 (see ‘Lack of trust’). The funds are also used to support scientists from the poorest countries, allowing them to attend meetings. Many of the countries that want to delay the next report have contributed nothing to the IPCC in years, if ever, although Russia is a notable exception. If contributions continue at current levels, by the end of 2028 the IPCC will be left with just 5 million Swiss francs (US$6 million) — the amount needed for an orderly wind-down of the body.

But there is a way forwards. The IPCC needs more than 20 million Swiss francs to stay operational until the end of 2029, and to complete AR7. Nations must consider increasing their contributions immediately. Other donors, including philanthropists and non-governmental organizations, should also approach the IPCC to see how they can help.

There are those who question whether the world still needs the IPCC. They say that there are faster ways to synthesize climate research, and that much is already known about climate change, its impacts and the measures needed to prevent dangerous consequences. Last week, a separate UN report confirmed that countries are not on track to limit global warming to within 1.5 °C of pre-industrial levels.

But these arguments overlook the true value of the IPCC, which includes its processes. IPCC reports are produced in a highly consultative way — scientists from the richest to the poorest countries collaborate, ensuring that voices from around the world are heard. This global, scientist-led process also ensures that the quality of the evidence under review takes precedence over political considerations. Once the scientists agree on the main text, governments are involved in writing the summary report, and can choose whether and how to act on the findings. But they cannot change the main text of the assessment report.

The IPCC has survived crises before. One of the most serious was in 1995, when AR2 concluded that “the balance of the evidence” suggested humans are responsible for global warming. This was challenged by representatives of some fossil-fuel-producing countries and oil and gas companies. But the organization’s scientists stood firm, often at great personal cost. Most IPCC-sponsoring governments backed the scientists, and the crisis eventually passed.

The current crisis is more serious because it concerns the IPCC’s very survival. Ideally, governments would come together again and ensure that the organization is properly funded. One way to resolve the matter would be to replace the current process of ad hoc voluntary contributions with multi-year budgetary commitments, similarly to those used to fund many other UN agencies. Support from non-governmental sources should also be considered.

The timing of the next assessment report must be resolved. But the dispute must not be allowed to distract from the most important matter of all: ensuring the continued survival of the IPCC itself.

#928. Nobel Prize in chemistry goes to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai won the Nobel Prize in chemistry for their groundbreaking discoveries related to the origin of chirality and life's building blocks.

The Nobel Prize in chemistry on Wednesday was awarded to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai "for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis."

Kagan, who is 95 years old and was born in France, is affiliated with France’s Université Paris-Sud, and Soai, who is 76 years old and was born in Japan, is based at the Tokyo University of Science.

The scientists share the Nobel Prize for answering longstanding questions in chemistry about life's origins.

Specifically, the duo solved an age-old mystery that has long puzzled chemists: some molecules, like amino acids, exist as two variants that are mirror images of one another, but just one of the variants out of the two is found in living organisms.

The two answered questions about why life chooses one variant over another.

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced the winner in Stockholm.

In chemistry, life's building blocks are what scientists call homochiral, after the Greek words for "same" and "hand."

The Nobel Committee said the chemistry prize recognizes discoveries that have enabled chemists to drive chemical reactions that lead to homochirality.

"Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai have provided a solution to a chemical mystery that is over a century old: how homochirality can emerge spontaneously," Heiner Linke, chair of the Nobel Committee for Chemistry, said in a news release. "The chemical reactions they have developed are spectacular."

The duo's discoveries, the news release said, "have been decisive for chemists who design reactions for the manufacture of pharmaceuticals."

#920. '90s art scene in the UK: An international, edgy trendsetter

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The 1990s are remembered for Brit-pop and provocative fashion, but beyond the nostalgia, it was a decade of cultural experimentation and social change. Tate Britain's new exhibition looks past the familiar icons.

The 1990s in Britain are often remembered through a haze of nostalgia consisting of rave culture, Britpop and provocative art and fashion by the likes of Tracey Emin and Alexander McQueen.

Undeniably, the decade was a time of major change in British society — the long reign of Margaret Thatcher had come to an end and the West entered a period of relative post-Cold War stability.

Meanwhile, British cultural icons like Oasis, the Spice Girls and Vivienne Westwood garnered international fame.

But Tate Britain's new exhibition "The 90s: Art and Fashion," which opens on October 8 and runs through February 14, 2027, urges viewers to look beyond the familiar names and images.

Even though now prominent artists such as Damien Hirst and Juergen Teller are among the show's 70 contributors, the exhibition also aims to highlight the creative input of those in communities which were left out of the mainstream discourse.

After all, says chief curator Edward Enninful in the exhibition's program, "the energy of the 90s was not concentrated in a single movement, but distributed across communities, disciplines and geographies that have not always been equally represented."

For Enninful, the exhibition is personal: The Ghanaian-born British fashion editor, stylist and creative director became the youngest-ever fashion director of i-D Magazine in the 90s at age 18, helping shape the diverse and experimental London fashion scene of the 1990s. He later became editor-in-chief of British Vogue in 2017. Enninful writes that he and his co-curators aimed to present a body of work which "reflects a broader landscape of practices and voices that shaped the period, many of which operated beyond the structures of visibility at the time." The exhibition pays special attention to artists of color and women whose work was not necessarily showcased as prominently at the time.

Black masculinity became "a site of cultural negotiation" in Britain in the 90s, writes co-curator Helen Little in the program text: "Figures such as Chris Ofili, Steve McQueen, Keith Piper, Koto Bolofo and Ozwald Boateng reimagined the Black masculine, challenging the racist specter of Black men, who had been defined as part of a masculinity crisis linked to post-industrial decline and excluded from the narrative of 'Cool Britannia.'"

Just like "Swinging London" in the 1960s, "Cool Britannia" became a widespread phrase to refer to the 1990s as an era during which British youth culture, pop music, art and political optimism boomed.

The 90s also offered artists plenty of chances to create without waiting for support from traditional gatekeepers. Britain entered the decade in recession, but that economic uncertainty also left artists free to do what they wished.

Empty buildings and industrial spaces were turned into informal venues. A 23-year-old Damien Hirst orchestrated "Freeze," a self-organized student exhibition in 1988 in a disused London warehouse. It became a landmark early art exhibition for the generation of artists known as the "Young British Artists" or YBA.

Artists Sarah Lucas and Tracey Emin opened The Shop, a temporary art gallery and social space in 1992, as a place where exhibitions could develop outside established galleries. Lucas and Emin also paved the way for women artists thanks to their deeply personal works and their edgy expressions of female liberation. While the Spice Girls helped make feminism accessible to a younger generation of Brits in the 90s by popularizing the "girl power" slogan and shaping "identity through agency power and resistance," as Tate curator Helen Little argues, Emin and Lucas meanwhile "presented a new visual language of clothing as art to tackle societal taboos around female sexuality, vulnerability and desire" — from t-shirts with provocative slogans to a grunge look of scuffed Dr. Martens boots with razor blades. Their work "held a mirror up to gender, behavior and identity," Little writes.

At the same time, the British fashion industry came into the international spotlight.

In a feminine sensuality counter to the gender stereotypes dismissed by Emin and Lucas, waif-like Kate Moss came to represent the feminine ideal of the 1990s. Dubbed "heroin-chic," the style was an antithesis to the glamorous, athletic physiques of the supermodels who preceded her. Corinne Day's photographs of the model led to the raw aesthetic to be labelled "grunge" after the anguished style of rock music hailing from Seattle and led by Nirvana.

The trend has undergone significant cultural reexamination since then: "Along with the look also came criticism that the strung-out look of drug or alcohol addiction was glamorized while encouraging serious eating disorders in a generation of young women," points out Alex Farquharson, director of the Tate Gallery in his foreword to the exhibition.

The 1990s were also an exciting era for Britain in haute couture, as legendary designers John Galliano led Dior, while Alexander McQueen started directing Givenchy at the age of 27.

McQueen's runway presentations were more akin to performance art than commercial fashion shows — and they often included historical critiques. Among them was the famous finale of his Fall/Winter 1998 collection at a London warehouse, during which the model stood in a circle of flames. Titled "Joan," the show was a reference to the killing of Joan of Arc. McQueen designed several other runway shows that reflected on British history, with topics of conquest and punishment repeatedly coming to the forefront. His 1995 presentation "Highland Rape" referenced the history of Scotland's subjugation, with the designer describing it as "England's rape of Scotland."

As co-curator Jessica Vaughan points out in her exhibition text: "Art, fashion and photography were not simply transgressive reactions … but reflected a genuine public unease with Britain's global identity and its past." In a world that can now feel increasingly mediated by screens, algorithms and established platforms, the artwork of the 90s offers a reminder of the value of physical spaces and fresh DIY creativity of the last smartphone-free decade.

The reaction to neuroscientist Doris Tsao’s latest preprint, she says, has been “surreal”. Tsao, at the University of California, Berkeley, described the study on social media on 17 October. Ten days later, her summary has drawn more than two million views and 11,000 likes.

Posted on bioRxiv, the study1 probed the brains of macaques (Macaca mulatta) during a visual-perception experiment. Using only the monkeys’ neural activity, Tsao’s team managed to reconstruct with high fidelity not only images that the animals were consciously seeing, but also images that were in front of their eyes but escaped their awareness.

Previous studies in monkeys have detected neural activity in response to content they are not aware of, but only as weak signals. “It’s super surprising to find this strong signal that is not consciously perceived,” Tsao says. Even more unexpected to Tsao, the study showed that brain activity switched between patterns associated with conscious processing and those associated with unconscious processing several times per second.

The authors say that the findings, which have not yet been peer reviewed, could be a small step towards solving one of neuroscience’s biggest mysteries: how brains give rise to consciousness, or the subjective experience of being aware.

Scientists studying consciousness were intrigued by the research and praised its rigorous methods. But some pointed out that the social-media buzz might have been overblown. “It’s an interesting little firestorm of a paper,” says John Sakon, a cognitive neuroscientist at the University of California, Los Angeles. “But some of the findings were not totally unexpected.”

Imagine someone looking through binoculars that show a different image to each eye. The person would perceive only one image at a time, rather than a mix of the two. This phenomenon, known as binocular rivalry, is important for the study of consciousness, because it enables researchers to study what happens in the brain when an image enters someone’s consciousness.

Exploring this paradigm with monkeys, Tsao and her team presented one image of a face to one eye of a monkey and, after a couple of seconds, introduced an image of a different face to the other eye. When this experiment is done in humans, people who are shown both images report seeing only the second. Researchers assume that the same thing happens with monkeys.

The scientists recorded activity in two brain regions that are known to be involved in facial recognition: the inferotemporal cortex and the prefrontal cortex.

Researchers first showed both faces to the monkey, one at a time, so that they could identify the distinct patterns of neural activity elicited by each image. Then they began the experiment by introducing the first image to one eye. When they introduced the second image to the monkey’s other eye, its brain activity quickly flickered between two neural patterns 15 times per second.

#911. Nobel Prize in literature goes to Canada's Anne Carson

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Carson won for her "bold and inventive oeuvre." The decorated poet and essayist is known for her reverence for Latin and Greek classics.

The Swedish Academy awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature to Anne Carson on Thursday, praising her "bold and inventive oeuvre."

Her work has "in playful dialogue with the classical tradition...created new forms for contemporary literature," said Ingrid Carlberg, permanent secretary of the academy.

The 76-year-old writer is a longtime professor of classics, creative writing, and comparative literature at prestigious institutions like McGill and Princeton universities. She is only the 19th woman of 123 laureates to win the award.

Born in Toronto in 1950, Carson moved with her family among several small towns in Canada during her youth. She has credited a beloved Latin teacher for sparking her interest in language and classics.

Her first book of criticism, 1986’s Eros the Bittersweet, was published to great acclaim and has had significant influence on other classicists, poets, and even film and television. Carson has also published her own poetry such as the 2001 collection The Beauty of the Husband as well as highly regarded translation of ancient Greek poets such as Sappho and Sophocles.

Carson is also heavily influenced by the King James Bible and more modern writers including Emily Dickinson and Virginia Woolf. She has received numerous awards such as the T.S. Eliot Prize for Poetry, a MacArthur fellowship, and the PEN/Nabokov Award.

Deeply private, Carson often declines to speak about her personal life in interviews and discourages autobiographical interpretations of her work. However, she did reveal in 2024 that she has Parkinson's disease.

The winner of the Nobel Prize in literature receives prize money of 12 million Swedish kronor, equivalent to about $1.2 million. The winner also receives a medal and a diploma at an official ceremony in December, and is expected to deliver a speech known as a Nobel lecture.

On Friday, the winner of the Nobel Peace Prize will be announced, followed by the Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel on Monday.

#901. How a multibillion-dollar oilfield has ignited the UK-Argentina feud over the Falklands

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Argentina and the UK once fought a war over the Falkland Islands. Now, the multibillion-dollar Sea Lion oil project has reignited old tensions, with Buenos Aires seeking to put a stop to the plans.

Home to fewer than 4,000 people and located in an isolated corner of the South Atlantic Ocean, the Falkland Islands don't often feature in global business headlines.

However, a UK-Israeli oil exploration project in the North Falkland Basin is stoking long-running tensions between Argentina and the people of the archipelago.

Argentina has laid claim to the islands, which it calls Las Malvinas, for two centuries. In 1982, the dispute over the islands' sovereignty led to a war between the UK and Argentina, though British control was quickly restored after Argentine forces invaded the islands.

A 2013 sovereignty referendum on the islands returned a 99.8% vote in favor of remaining tied to the UK as a self-governing British Overseas Territory.

The Sea Lion oilfield project has brought the issue back to the surface. Last month, Argentine President Javier Milei threatened to launch an international legal challenge against the UK unless the project was stopped within two weeks.

The oil project is being run by Israeli company Navitas Petroleum and the UK's Rockhopper Exploration and has the full support of the Falkand Islands Legislative Assembly, which has pursued the project since 2010. The companies plan to begin extracting oil from the Sea Lion oilfield by early 2028.

Milei says the project is "the illegal plundering of our resources" and that he will take the matter to the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea, an independent judicial body responsible for the application of the United Nations' Convention on the Law of the Sea.

Stacy Bragger, a member of the Falklands Islands' 12-member assembly, told DW that the dispute was "nothing new" and that Argentina is using the oil project as "a vehicle to pursue their sovereignty claim."

He thinks Milei may be stepping up rhetoric ahead of the 2027 elections in Argentina.

"When there's elections coming up, the Falklands is a very convenient tool to use in order to rally people together and distract them from domestic problems," he said.

Henry Ziemer, an analyst with the Americas program at the Center for Strategic and International Studies in Washington, says the Falklands is "a perennial sore spot in Argentine politics" and that since the 1980s, various governments have raised the issue in an attempt to rally support and to "stoke the fires of nationalism."

Howerver, he thinks Milei's intervention is noteworthy given that he had previously been open to better relations with the UK on the issue. He agrees that it may be a strategy to "boost approval ratings at a time when the government is under a lot of strong domestic pressure."

Even if Milei sees political gain in the issue, the possibility of the Sea Lion project being successful would significantly raise the economic stakes for all concerned.

The project has a budget of $1.8 billion (€1.6 billion) and could generate revenues of more than double that for the Falkland Islands. The companies hope that at its peak, the field will produce up to 50,000 barrels per day and could last for up to 35 years.

That would substantially increase the Falklands' current annual GDP, which is almost entirely dependent on the volatile fishing sector and tourism. The administration is even looking into the possibility of creating a sovereign wealth fund if the oil project is successful.

"It's a very significant project for us with a lot of potential for our future," said Bragger. "It's been a big effort to get to this point and assuming all goes to plan, we want to retain as much value as possible."

However, beyond playing to domestic politics, Milei may also spy an opportunity for Argentina to position itself as an energy player amid the war in Iran and the troubles in the Strait of Hormuz.

Argentina's own domestic oil sector is growing, with production surging at Patagonia's Vaca Muerta, one of the world's largest shale developments. Were Milei's legal efforts to prove successful, there may be further gains.

"When natural resources overlay existing territorial disputes and bad blood, that tends to galvanize a lot of conflict," said Ziemer.

However, Bragger counters that Milei and Argentina have no legitimate claim to either the Falklands or whatever oil wealth may be extracted over the coming years.

"They don't respect our right to self-determination," he added. "They don't respect our right to develop our own resources. They just want to colonize us."

Milei plans to put pressure on the Falklands by stepping up existing economic sanctions. Andrea Clausen, chief executive of the Falklands since 2025, recently described this campaign as "economic warfare."

Many international suppliers and shipping companies already refuse to supply the Falklands for fear of angering Argentina. Now, Milei's government has initiated sanctions against 60 people involved in the Sea Lion project.

In a televised address last month, the Argentine president also vowed to sanction the companies involved in the project. Such a move could cast doubt on the project, which previously collapsed in 2014 and 2020, due to financing troubles, oil-price weakness and delays caused by sanctions.

Navitas this week confirmed that two contractors had pulled out of the project due to sanctions concerns, and that it was replacing them. There are also concerns that Milei could try to garner international support in favor of his position, with US President Donald Trump recently suggesting that US support for the status quo on the islands was "under review."

Bragger played this down, pointing to recent comments from US Secretary of State Marco Rubio confirming support for the Falklands. Ziemer said it would be "very shocking" if the US came down on the side of Argentina.

While uncertainty reigns over whether the oil project ultimately gets going or not, there is little expectation that the dispute could lead to a return of armed conflict like in 1982.

"That's a step too far, and the situation would have to dramatically deteriorate before we would see the potential for military conflict," said Ziemer.

#891. Early-onset cancer rising worldwide: Causes, risk, prevention

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Across Latin America, early-onset cancer rates have risen, especially of bowel, breast and cervical cancers. But it is a worldwide trend.

The numbers are striking. Some cancer rates have doubled in the past 20 years. It's just not yet clear whether we're seeing a rise in actual cases or whether we're seeing more early-stage cases because of better awareness, diagnosis and treatment in hospitals.

Take, for example, colorectal (or bowel) cancer: In 2001, about one in every 26,000 people under 50 in England were diagnosed with it each year. By 2023, the rate of diagnoses was about one in 12,000. Twice what it was at the turn of the century.

That's according to the World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF), an international network of charities, which analyzed registry data from England, the United States and the Netherlands. It found the number of new cancer cases had risen for seven cancers in England alone.

So, rates have doubled, but the numbers overall are still small, said Amy Berrington de Gonzalez, a professor of clinical epidemiology at the Institute of Cancer Research in London.

"Cancer is still very uncommon among young people," Berrington de Gonzalez told DW. "Three times a small number is still a small number."

But she and other scientists still want to know why this is happening, because it is a worldwide trend. Latin America is a case in point.

A 2025 study analyzed registry data from 50 countries and territories up to 2017. It found early-onset colorectal cancer rising in 27 of those regions.

In Latin America, rates rose in Chile, Argentina, Ecuador, Costa Rica and Puerto Rico. Chile's rise — at almost 4% per year — was among the highest.

In Brazil, the evidence comes directly from hospitals. At the A.C. Camargo Cancer Center in Sao Paulo, the number of colorectal cancer patients under 50 grew by 7.6% per year between 2000 and 2023. That's a five-fold increase.

The number of patients over 50 grew even faster — at 8.1% — or about six-fold.

But since these figures came from a single hospital, they do not show whether cases are rising across Brazil. They may only reflect that this one hospital is treating more patients.

"We need better cancer registration systems," said Berrington de Gonzalez, "especially in countries like Brazil and Mexico, where obesity rates have also been increasing."

Marc Gunter, professor of cancer epidemiology at Imperial College London, said he expected Latin America to follow richer countries as diets there become more Westernized and lifestyles more sedentary. "I think it's already happening," he said.

In another study in 2025, involving 42 countries, Berrington de Gonzalez compared data from two age groups — 20 to 49 years and 50 years and older — between 2003 and 2017.

Six cancers — thyroid, breast, colorectal, kidney, uterine and leukemia — had risen among the younger groups in most countries. All but colorectal cancer were seen to rise in older adults, too.

In fact, colorectal cancer stood out. In about a third of the countries, it rose faster in younger adults than in older ones. "It is the one cancer that seems to be increasing in younger people whilst decreasing in older people," said Berrington de Gonzalez.

This has consequences for research. If cancers are rising in all age groups, the causes are likely to be widespread, so studies looking only at young people risk chasing complicated explanations with too little data to test them, she said.

A US study of pancreatic cancer in adults aged 15 to 39 years found the rise was almost entirely made up of early-stage tumors.

The authors said this suggested that instead of the number of cancer cases going up, it was the number of early diagnoses that had gone up. And that can be explained by better awareness in the community, with people getting checked more often.

But the data for colorectal cancer looks different: Some US studies show it rising in advanced stages of the disease as well, which suggests an increase in the number of cases, not just the number of diagnoses.

The immune system is central to human health. When it fails, people can become more susceptible to infection and cancer. When misdirected, it can attack the body’s own tissues. And as it ages, it can contribute to chronic disease.

People of the same age can have strikingly distinct immune systems, shaped by genetics and by environmental and lifestyle factors, including air pollution, smoking, diet and past infections1,2. Growing evidence suggests that variation in the immune system’s ‘biological age’ — the rate at which it has aged, which is distinct from chronological age — matters for health. This variation has been linked to neurodegeneration, cancer and mortality risk3.

Immune cells have a surprising role in exercise endurance

Yet, clinicians have no routine way of measuring how healthy someone’s immune system is. That’s because the immune system is a dynamic network that can change over time. It involves specialized organs that support immune-cell development and function, as well as immune cells that can circulate throughout the body and reside in tissues4. Signalling molecules, such as cytokines and chemokines, allow these immune cells and tissues to communicate.

Clinical tests provide only a fragmented picture of the immune system. A physician might examine an individual’s white blood cell count to establish whether circulating immune cells are at unusually high or low levels. They might also measure blood biomarkers such as C-reactive protein, which can indicate inflammation. But neither measurement is specific to a particular health condition, and they do not indicate whether the immune system as a whole is resilient or in decline, or if it is capable of mounting an effective response to infections and other challenges.

But advances in immunology, molecular profiling, medical imaging and artificial intelligence are opening up fresh possibilities for measuring the state of people’s immune systems. The stage is set for researchers to develop the immunome: a comprehensive characterization of immune health across cells, tissues, organs and molecular interactions (see ‘Defining the immunome’).

A raft of assays can provide information about the state of a person’s immune system.

Large-scale analysis of circulating proteins and antibodies

Patterns of immune signalling and antibody recognition beyond individual clinical markers

Types, abundance and molecular states of immune cells

Diversity and composition of immune-cell populations

Detection of quantifiable features from medical images of immune-related organs

Structural and compositional features of the thymus, spleen and bone marrow

Locations of immune cells and how they are organized relative to other cells

Measurements of immune-system activity before and after immune challenges

Strength, timing and persistence of responses to challenges such as vaccination

Long-term modelling of immune health using artificial intelligence

Research initiatives such as the Human Immunome Project, which one of us (J.S.T.) co-leads, are beginning to provide the data needed. What remains unclear is whether this information could be used to improve patient care, and how that could be achieved. Which measurements best capture clinically important aspects of a person’s immune health? How stable are these variables over time? And, most importantly, can assessing them improve clinical decision-making and, ultimately, disease outcomes?

Measuring every aspect of the immune system will not be feasible for every person. Instead, researchers should try to identify key features that can be readily assessed in the clinic to flag aspects of the immune system that are weakened, or declining quickly when they shouldn’t be. Such assessments could open avenues for disease prevention, treatment guidance and methods to monitor interventions.

For instance, these features might enable physicians to recognize cases of accelerated immune ageing and spot individuals who need close disease monitoring. For cancer treatment, measures of immune health could help to identify people who are most likely to benefit from therapies that rely on the immune system5,6, as well as those with a high risk of adverse reactions.

Such measurements could also help researchers to understand why people of similar ages and apparently similar health sometimes have substantially different responses to the same infection — a phenomenon that was apparent during the COVID-19 pandemic. Antibody responses to the same vaccine likewise vary widely between individuals2,7. If this variability could be measured, clinicians could use it to tailor preventive measures, treatments and monitoring to an individual’s immune system.

Researchers, health-care systems and funders must work to find the smallest, most scalable set of measurements that can capture meaningful differences in immune function and guide clinical decisions. Here, we outline a road map for moving the immunome from the laboratory to the clinic.

First, scientists should generate longitudinal data sets using large research populations and biobanks. Gaining a full understanding of immune health will require measuring several components of the immune system alongside broader indicators of health and disease in the same people over time.

Analysis of genes and gene expression in single cells from large research populations and biobanks could help researchers to understand which immune-cell groups are present in distinct populations. This could reveal which genes and signalling pathways are active in particular populations of cells, and what combination of markers best distinguishes between healthy, declining and dysfunctional immune states2,3,8. Analysing blood proteins can indicate whether a person is experiencing inflammation and whether their immune system has been activated. And studying the receptors on immune cells called lymphocytes can reveal the diversity of cells available to recognize threats. These receptors determine which pathogenic antigens the cells will identify; greater diversity generally allows the immune system to detect a broader range of threats.

Researchers must also study how the immune system changes over time to respond to challenges. Studying people before and after they are vaccinated is one standardized way to do this. It’s already clear that a person’s immune state before vaccination can predict the strength and characteristics of the immune response that follows2,9. The task now is to find out whether a person’s response to routine vaccination could provide a practical read-out of their immune competence10, and whether it would be possible to modify the immune health of an individual before interventions such as vaccination or cancer immunotherapy to improve their response2,5.

Data sets used to probe the immunome must represent the diversity of human immunity, because genetics and environmental factors alter immune states1. For example, malaria vaccine efficacy can differ markedly between populations across continents7,11. Yet, many existing immune-profiling data sets are concentrated in populations from high-income nations. When studying diverse populations, researchers around the world should use the same protocols so that results from various regions can be compared.

A big challenge will be making sense of complex data sets. AI tools can identify patterns across thousands of immune measurements, but those patterns might reflect differences between populations, laboratories or technologies rather than underlying immune health. Models will therefore need to be tested across independent populations and over time to establish which signals are robust and clinically meaningful.

Scientists working on the Human Immunome Project are developing and applying AI approaches to immunome data collected from various populations. An important goal for the project is to create reference maps and foundation models that scientists can use for tasks such as identifying small, manageable sets of measurements that would be useful in clinical settings.

With large-scale data in hand, researchers should work towards simplification. The success of the field should be judged not by how many immune variables can be measured, but by whether it is possible to improve clinical decision-making with just a small, manageable set of them. Tests used in the clinic would ideally be cheap and should not put extra demands on patients. Before any AI tools can be used routinely, they will need to be tested across a range of hospitals and populations. Researchers must establish whether a tool’s outputs truly improve treatment decisions.

One example of a tool that might be useful in the clinic is an AI model capable of extracting information about immune health from routine blood tests and clinical information such as age, sex, body weight and medical history. In a study published last year6, such an approach was able to predict health outcomes after immunotherapy treatment for people with cancer.

#882. 2026 Nobel Prize winner Anne Carson: Where to start reading

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The works of this year's Nobel Prize-winning poet and essayist Anne Carson can feel slightly daunting. Here are some keys to understanding her universe.

This quote is from the long, lyrical poem "The Glass Essay" published in Anne Carson's 1995 collection "Glass, Irony and God" — an entry point into the works of the 2026 Nobel Prize in literature laureate.

In the poem, the narrator explores the burden of memory and obsessive desire after a failed love affair, all while regularly reflecting on Emily Brontë — an author renowned for her intense literary depiction of destructive and passionate love in "Wuthering Heights."

The poem brings several of Carson's recurring obsessions together into one work. She has repeatedly written about desire, abandonment and jealousy, and has often used other literary figures as emotional doubles, as historical anchor points to her ideas.

In a way, "The Glass Essay" encapsulates Carson's method; the Canadian poet, essayist, translator and classicist has often intellectualized emotional themes by intertwining her personal reflections with analysis, etymology, translation, literary history and philosophical thought.

Another trademark of her work is the genre fluidity found in "The Glass Essay," which — as the title indicates — is partly a literary criticism essay, even though it is also a poem due to its form.

"Almost all her literary production has centered upon the intersection between the philologist's obsession with words and the poet's desire for new meaning," noted Anders Olsson, chair of the Nobel Committee, in his description of the Nobel laureate on October 8.

Born in the Canadian city of Toronto, Carson first developed her fascination for ancient Greek myths as a teenager. She told literary journal The Brick that she spotted a bilingual edition of "Sappho" by Willis Barnstone in the display of a bookstore of a shopping mall in Ontario, and that had led her to ask a high school Latin teacher to teach her ancient Greek.

She went on to publish her doctoral thesis in Classics, entitled "Odi et Amo Ergo Sum" (I hate and I love, therefore I am).

Carson's first book of criticism, "Eros the Bittersweet," was published in 1986. It lays the foundation of her approach, as she draws on ancient Greek writers such as Sappho and Plato to examine how desire — and the obstacles between the lover and the beloved — creates emotional tension.

The book suggests that the ache of wanting is not separate from love; it is one of the things that makes desire so powerful.

Because "Eros the Bittersweet" moves between Greek poetry, philosophy and literary criticism and personal interpretations, it is not considered a conventionally easy read. A more immediately engaging work and good starting point to discover Carson is the verse novel "Autobiography of Red," published in 1998.

"Autobiography of Red" was inspired by the ancient Greek myth of Herakles (or Hercules) and the red-winged monster Geryon, which the hero kills as his 10th task in his series of 12 monumental labors.

An otherwise minor figure from mythology, Geryon is turned into Herakles' lover in Carson's book. She reimagines the myth as a modern, queer coming-of-age story, with the monster experiencing love, jealousy, sexuality and heartbreak.

"We all feel we're monsters most of the time," explained Carson.

The work is accessible, funny and moving, and no deep knowledge of Greek mythology is required to enjoy it.

The key to reading Carson is not to expect every connection to be immediately clear. Her books reward curiosity rather than certainty, and her sprawling, non-linear web of knowledge can be compared to going down a hyperlinked rabbit hole of narrative ideas.

"Men in the Off Hours" (2000) is another intellectually playful collection of poems. Here Carson collides the modern with the ancient, with references to figures as diverse as Beethoven, Virginia Woolf, Emily Dickinson, Jean-Luc Godard, Sigmund Freud, Homer and Hokusai.

The poems bring together fragments of history, mythology and ordinary life, allowing unexpected emotional and intellectual connections to emerge.

In the spoken and sung performance piece "Norma Jeane Baker of Troy" (2019), Carson similarly brings together classical and pop culture, reimagining the story of Helen of Troy alongside the life and cultural image of Marilyn Monroe.

How do people become the victims of desires they cannot control? This is a core theme Carson explores in "The Beauty of the Husband: A Fictional Essay in 29 Tangos," published in 2001.

It features the Canadian author's wry humor in a powerful and moving way, with erotic, painful and heartbreaking scenes from a doomed marriage: a 30-year obsession with a pathological liar whose singular redeeming quality is his sheer physical radiance.

The collection of poetry, which won her the T.S. Eliot Prize, is dedicated to the English Romantic poet John Keats, challenging his famous aphorism, "Beauty is truth."

#839. Author or artificial intelligence? Literature in crisis

AI is a hot topic at this year's Frankfurt Book Fair. An international literary scandal has raised questions about machine-generated texts.

It's the hot-button topic at the Frankfurt Book Fair: How profoundly will artificial intelligence (AI) transform the book industry, and how can this transformation be regulated?

Given the warnings about the technology's long-term risks to humanity as a whole, such debates might appear rather narrow in scope. In reality, however, they concern very concrete issues — copyright protection, the power of tech giants and the future of creativity. Many agree that the political measures taken don't go far enough.

This year, however, another aspect has entered the debate: Can we actually recognize when a text has been written with the help of artificial intelligence, and if so, can this be proven?

These are some of the questions raised by the current scandal surrounding Haitian-Canadian author Thelyson Orelien and his debut novel, "C'etait ca ou mourir," (This title means "It Was Either That or Die" in English but an English translation has not yet been published), which was published in Canada in the spring.

The book about global migration was universally acclaimed, with translation rights sold in more than 20 languages. Soon after the book was published in France, it was long-listed for the prestigious Prix Goncourt.

But then, all of a sudden, allegations emerged that the book had been written almost entirely using AI. Orelien, a rising literary star, found himself branded a fraud despite denying all the allegations. His nomination for the Goncourt was withdrawn.

So, who should we believe? An author or an AI that claims to have identified AI-generated writing?

"We will have to learn to live with doubt," said Hannes Bajohr, a professor of German literature at the University of California, Berkeley.

Bajohr has spent years examining how AI is changing both writing and reading. His prediction: If the origins of texts can no longer be reliably established, we will have to evaluate them differently. What matters will be their quality alone, rather than whether AI was used in the writing process.

Nina George, a bestselling author and the honorary president of the European Writers' Council, is unwilling to accept this.

At the Frankfurt Book Fair, she was awarded the Friedrich Perthes Prize in recognition of her commitment to issues such as AI and copyright reform.

George has addressed the European Parliament and the lower house of the German parliament, speaking and writing tirelessly about the dangers of AI models. It is perverse, she said, that an author should suddenly have to prove that their writing is the creation of a human. The focus of her campaigns are the tech corporations' self-serving mentality and copyright protection.

But what if an author sees AI not as an enemy but as a tool that makes writing easier?

US author and artist Luke Stoffel deliberately used AI in his memoir, "How To Win One Million Dollars and Shit Glitter," and was open about doing so.

"I'm dyslexic," he said in a video interview. "I've always written and made up stories, but I never dared to take them any further."

It was only with the help of AI, he said, that he was able to fulfill his dream. The technology drew his attention to weaknesses in his writing and suggested a marketing strategy that actually worked.

In May 2026, he received an award from the Independent Book Publishers Association for the book.

For South African author Coral Hart, AI is actually the foundation of her business model. Publishing under various pseudonyms, each year she releases more than 200 different novels, spanning the genres of romance, thriller and mystery.

Hart sees herself as an ideas person, as well as an editor and publisher. Although none of her books has yet become a bestseller, the business is profitable, she said, adding that each book reaches more than 5,000 people.

Stoffel considers the entire debate hypocritical and points out that many publishers have been working with AI tools for some time. He's certain that "right now, a million-copy bestseller is being printed that was created with the help of AI. The author won't admit it, and then there'll be a scandal."

It is entirely possible that the scandal surrounding Thelyson Orelien is precisely that story. But we may never know for sure.

For now, the battle against the tech giants continues, some of which are themselves increasingly concerned about losing control.

At the very start of the Frankfurt fair, the German Publishers and Booksellers Association issued an appeal to policymakers in Berlin and Brussels, calling for stricter regulation of artificial intelligence, which they said is more urgent than ever. More than 140 publishers have signed it.

But for now, it remains just an appeal. And the question of what authors themselves should be permitted to do with AI will undoubtedly continue to fuel debates in the future.

Results from the first human trial of an experimental gene-editing therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), in which a young Chinese boy died, have been published1. Although the trial provides evidence that the treatment developed by HuidaGene, based in Shanghai, China, edited some target genes, the therapeutic benefit was limited and led to a catastrophic immune reaction in one participant. Researchers say there is an urgent need to establish safe dosages for these kinds of experimental treatment.

The trial gained attention when HuidaGene announced on 5 August that one participant, a boy, had died roughly a year earlier. Researchers criticized the company for waiting so long to publicly report the death — and the event put a spotlight on China’s fast-track clinical trials. On 27 August, HuidaGene told Nature that the firm wanted to “establish the clinical facts and undertake a comprehensive investigation” before communicating with the broader public. They said a manuscript detailing the boy’s death and post-mortem findings had been submitted to a journal in January, which has now been published in Cell.

The boy who died was one of four children to receive the experimental therapy for DMD, a progressive and ultimately fatal genetic disorder that mostly affects boys, causing difficulty in walking and gradual muscle weakening. Children with DMD have mutations in the DMD gene that affect their ability to produce dystrophin, a protein that strengthens muscle fibres. The therapy used an adeno-associated virus (AAV) to deliver a base editor — a form of CRISPR gene-editing technology — to muscles to correct the mutation. The boy who died and another boy received a high dose of the therapy in AAVs; two other boys were given a low dose.

Within 24 hours of receiving the therapy, the authors say that the boy who died experienced sudden, severe abdominal pain, followed by a fever that responded only partially to treatment. The team says his condition declined rapidly, and he developed discoloured skin due to a lack of oxygen in his blood, as well as a slow, irregular heartbeat. His heart eventually stopped, and doctors could not revive him. The authors report that an autopsy revealed that he died of acute respiratory distress syndrome, which was related to the treatment.

Ian Woodcock, a paediatric neurologist and researcher at Murdoch Children’s Research Institute in Melbourne, Australia, says the boy’s fast decline suggests it was a reaction to the dose he received. In general, a large dose of AAVs are required so the gene therapy reaches muscle cells across the body. But this increases the risk of severe immune reactions, including death, that have been reported in this and other gene therapy trials for DMD2,3. “Even though the AAV doesn't cause disease in humans, this amount of foreign invasion really triggers the immune system,” he says.

The authors report that they performed safety testing in cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis) before the trial in people. No negative side effects were observed with either a high or low dose, but the monkeys did have a weak, short-lived immune reaction following treatment. The authors acknowledge that the monkey experiments could not detect rare side effects, such as the severe immune reaction experienced by the boy who died, because they only tested the treatment in four animals.

When asked whether they should have run tests in more animals, co-author Hui Yang, co-founder of HuidaGene, said that increasing animal numbers alone could not guarantee prediction of an uncommon reaction in people, particularly because different species have different immune responses.

The boy who died was the second to receive the high dose, and only after safety data for the other high-dose recipient and the two low-dose recipients were reviewed by the trial’s safety review committee, the authors said. The exact dosage the boys received was based on their weight.

The team said that the boy who died received a higher dose than the other boy in the high-dose group because he had a higher body mass index (BMI). But that does not necessarily mean that he had more lean muscle, which is the target of the therapy. Yang says that the boy’s muscles might have been exposed to a higher dose than in the other boys, but it is still unclear whether that could have triggered the severe immune reaction. The authors suggest that dosing on the basis of body weight might not be appropriate.

Yang says the team tried to reduced the risk of severe immune responses by giving the boys immunosuppressants, testing both doses in animals and staggering each boy’s treatment following a safety review. But given the boy’s death, those measures were insufficient, he says. “We therefore need to re-examine the assumptions underlying dose selection, the role of body composition,” and the predictive value of preclinical studies, he adds. Stronger independent safety oversight and ways of reducing the effective dose also need to be developed, he adds. “The most important lesson is that severe systemic immune toxicity following AAV administration remains difficult to predict.”

Next-generation AAVs that are better at penetrating muscles could mean that lower doses can achieve the same editing effect, says Woodcock. Or the team might consider an alternative to AAVs, he adds.

"Ragebait" was on a shortlist of three words for the 2026 Youth Word of the Year, alongside "Peak" and "Crashout."

It is often used with a touch of humor, to emphasize that the reaction might be somewhat exaggerated.

#820. German 'lawfare': When lawsuits become a political weapon

The Left Party is suing two government ministers for accusing them of antisemitism, while Chancellor Friedrich Merz has been sued several times by various parties. Is German politics descending into "lawfare?"

A verbal slip, an attempted quip, a lawsuit: Things can move fast in the rancorous atmosphere that currently rules German politics.

The brief exchange happened on a political TV talk show shortly after the Berlin election on September 20, won by the socialist Left Party. The show's host, Maybrit Illner, quickly corrected herself after accidentally saying of the Left Party's Berlin mayoral candidate, "Elif Eralp says she is an antisemite…. She is not an antisemite."

One of her guests, Federal Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt, of the conservative Christian Social Union (CSU), responded immediately: "Na doch" (or, "well, yes she is"). In the angry fallout, the Left Party's lawyers jumped on these two words, and have filed a lawsuit, arguing that this amounted to defamation of Eralp, as it falsely suggested that the Left Party politician did indeed consider herself antisemitic.

Not only that, the suit stated, Dobrindt's remark would "undermine her integrity as a political officeholder" and hinder her attempts to form a government in Berlin.

It was not the first time the Left Party has got its lawyers involved following its election victory, as it gets bombarded by accusations of antisemitism from the center right, especially over an incident at an election campaign event in Berlin when a rapper chanted "Death to the IDF" — the Israel Defense Forces, or the Israeli army.

Economy Minister Katherina Reiche also angered the socialist party by giving a speech in which she said that the party was now "openly embracing antisemitism." A video of the speech was posted on the Economy Ministry's Instagram account.

The lawsuit has become an increasingly popular weapon in German politics in recent years, usually by deploying sections 185, 186 and 187 of the German Criminal Code, which cover offenses including insult, defamation and "intentional defamation," which means knowingly asserting or disseminating untrue facts about others. Theoretically, the punishment can be a fine or up to a year in prison.

In September 2025, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) pressed defamation charges against Chancellor Friedrich Merz after he said that the far-right party's policy of remigration amounted to "ethnic cleansing."

Similarly, the Green Party in Berlin brought hate speech charges against Merz last year, when he said "we still have this problem in the cityscape" when describing the presence of immigrants.

In another case, the Green Party's own Robert Habeck was sued in 2024 by the Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) for claiming that the new upstart party was "buying votes" on the internet by building up "troll armies." Prosecutors dropped the case after Habeck agreed to pay €12,000 ($13,500) to three charities without admitting any wrongdoing, on the grounds that there was only limited public interest in a criminal procedure.

But charges like these, especially when politicians claim defamation, have little chance of success. "Prosecutors usually dismiss such cases very quickly due to a lack of sufficient grounds for suspicion," media lawyer Christian Solmecke, of the firm WBS Legal, told DW in an email.

"The Federal Constitutional Court has consistently held that, in the political arena, the boundaries of what is permissible are significantly wider than in the private sphere," Solmecke added. "Anyone who voluntarily enters the political arena must also be prepared to endure sharp, polemical, exaggerated and hurtful criticism."

Felix Zimmermann, editor-in-chief of the legal affairs outlet Legal Tribune Online (LTO), agrees, because German law makes a careful distinction between an opinion and a false statement. "'Antisemitic' or 'not antisemitic' is an opinion, especially at the moment, when people have very different definitions of it," Zimmermann told DW.

That hasn't deterred politicians from trying it more and more often. "We are undoubtedly observing a new level of intensity and scale of this practice," said Solmecke. "Political culture has become harsher and more polarized. The threshold for no longer merely engaging political opponents through debate, but instead outright criminalizing them, has dropped dramatically."

The danger, Solmecke argues, is that this often amounts to the "misuse" of law enforcement agencies as political instruments.

But lawfare appears to be effective as a political tactic, not least because it's a guaranteed headline. "From the point of view of the parties filing the complaints, they are highly effective as a PR strategy," said Solmecke. "The political benefit arises entirely independently of the legal outcome."

In the Left Party's case, Zimmermann thinks it certainly pays to show you won't stand to be called antisemitic — not least because Eralp has repeatedly underlined her commitment to the Jewish community in Berlin.

"I think it's mainly about the fact that the Left Party wants to form a coalition in Berlin and Ms. Eralp wants to become mayor," Zimmermann said. "So of course it's good if you're insulted to challenge it, as a signal."

The motion against Katherina Reiche, on the other hand, may have much better chances — because she chose to post her inflammatory speech on the Economy Ministry's official social media channels. This could, Zimmermann said, well be seen as a violation of the "principle of neutrality," a constitutional rule that forbids the state from vilifying any particular political party.

"That is of course difficult for a politician, because if they say it on their own account, they're allowed, but if they say it on the ministry's account, they're not allowed," said Zimmermann. "It's a little absurd, because you're effectively splitting one person into two."

But even though the Left Party filed the complaint against Reiche as an urgent motion, the Constitutional Court may take several weeks, if not months, to actually decide the case — by which time the coalition negotiations in Berlin may have long since finished and Reiche's speech has had its effect.

The consequences for Reiche, in other words, will be limited — even if her ministry is forced to take down the post and the Left Party can eventually claim a victory.

When Greg Bannard started her master’s degree in chemistry at the University of Calgary, Canada, she didn’t have an arsenal of tools available to her, so it became incredibly frustrating when equipment went missing. And, being the sort of scientist who’s picky about the kind of spatula that she uses, it could be extremely annoying when her favourite ones disappeared. After several instances of vanishing kit, she could no longer endure running around trying to find tools that had been misplaced.

This inspired her to create a portable pouch that fitted into her pocket of her laboratory coat. “It’s mostly things that I ended up finding myself reaching for,” says Bannard, who now works as an industrial designer, but still keeps the pouch as a keepsake of her time in research.

At the time, Bannard worked in a dye lab, studying the functionality of perylene diimide dyes for the active layers of solar cells. She packed a whole lot into the pouch: a diamond glass cutter, a carbide glass cutter, an ear curette used for working with tiny amounts of powdered dye, a test-tube holder, a pencil, a spatula, little scissors, a felt marker and an “army” of tweezers. “It was a busy lab and people needed to get their own work done,” she says. “The best way that I could get my work done was knowing where everything was.”

Beyond Bannard’s frustration, missing equipment can disrupt crucial scientific research. Scientists often need particular tools to keep delicate experiments on track. Momoko Takahashi, a neurobiologist at Northwestern University in Evanston, Illinois, knows this too well. She recalls panicking when she could not find a syringe during a time-sensitive procedure involving mouse neurons. “There’s an entire domino effect that starts if one particular moment fails,” she says. “If [the experiment] is not successful, I just wasted a bunch of animals’ lives and that makes me feel really guilty.”

Everyday acts of borrowing, accidental theft, mislabelling and sample moving can turn shared laboratories into crime scenes. The social-media platform Reddit is awash with scientists asking how to guard against ‘disappearing’ items and seeking solutions to track down their missing solutions. Nature spoke directly to researchers about how they protect their work, equipment and sanity without becoming the lab secret police. After all, much ‘stealing’ is done by accident or at least not with malicious intent.

Put systems in place. Scientists who spoke to Nature recounted that all kinds of equipment can disappear from the lab, from small items such as pens and pipettes, to larger and more expensive equipment such as centrifuges and computers. Many interviewees emphasized the importance of having systems in place, such as inventory lists, sign-out sheets and shared equipment guidelines, so that it’s much harder for things to vanish without a trace.

Takahashi highlights the importance of centralizing organization by having official, or unofficial, lab managers and having documentation to keep track of equipment. “A lab is a small business and it should be run like a small business,” she says, noting that she has fallen into a “pseudo lab-manager” position in every lab she’s been in. “Having organizational management implemented right off the bat is crucial to making sure that everything runs smoothly.”

Sandra Petrus-Reurer, a cell-therapy researcher at the University of Cambridge, UK, also stresses the importance of having a really good inventory from the start so people can track who has used specific samples. This way, if something goes missing, it’s easier to work out when and how it could have happened.

Have clear procedures and usage rules. Procedure is especially important when several researchers share equipment or space. According to a PhD student in Australia, who wishes to remain anonymous, shared fridge and freezer space, reagents and fume hoods cause the most conflict in her facilities. To reduce issues in these communal lab spaces, the university has a booking system for benches and hoods. Everyone has allocated trays in each fridge and freezer, each with its own managed inventory to keep track of items and sample movements.

Some expensive machines or equipment might be funded by several labs, which can also create confusion and conflict, says George Chen, a neurologist at the Children’s Hospital Los Angeles, California. Displaying who can use the equipment can make a world of difference: something as simple as a poster that explains which materials are co-owned and can be shared between labs could be enough. “Be transparent and find systems in which it’s just immediately obvious to everyone what's going on,” he says.

Mark your territory. Making ownership unmistakable can help to mitigate issues. One of the first things Takahashi did when joining her current lab was to label everything. “I always tell the undergrads to initial it or write your name on it,” she says. In Bannard’s lab, each member had electrical tape of a specific colour, which they would wrap around their designated tools. Bannard’s, for instance, was green.

The Australian PhD student says that having clearly labelled reagents and pipettes helps to provide clarity about what some people can and cannot use. After being accused of nabbing another lab’s antibodies, her lab started putting custom stickers on anything that was their property. Indeed, labelling something with a name only the lab group can understand can also ward off sticky fingers. “My lab group once made up code names for our cell lines, as previously our cell cultures were going missing,” she said. The thinking was that potential ‘thieves’ would not know what the code names corresponded to. She says that her group’s cells stopped disappearing after they started adding code names.

You might also consider using hidden labels. A former neuroscientist at the University of Oxford, UK, who preferred not to be named so as not to reveal his lab’s method, had his own hack: “We would put some marker hidden on the inside of the hardware,” he said. “Labels can easily be removed or rubbed off if they’re visible, so by using hidden labels, we could prove that misplaced equipment was ours.”

Hidden and kept under lock and key. Takahashi recommends keeping equipment in cabinets or drawers rather than leaving it out in the open where it can easily be taken or picked up by mistake. People are less likely to rifle through drawers than they are to take things from the bench, she says, noting that she used to hide her prized forceps in her drawer before she went home.

And if you need an extra level of protection, the PhD student recommends getting a lockable box or container that only you, or your team, can access. Petrus-Reurer once bought a lockable fridge, both for extra space and for the appearance of limited access: “I never removed the key, but adding a bit of a physical barrier helped.”

Store separately. When equipment does go missing, there are ways to lessen the impact. Petrus-Reurer and Chen recommend keeping a second stock of everything, because commonly used items tend to run out faster than scientists can replenish them and if they get stolen or misplaced, it can further this nuisance. Chen personally keeps a secondary stash of tissue-culture plates and pipette tips in a hidden drawer. “They’re not expensive, but they just seem to always disappear from the cabinets before someone can reorder them.”

Bring your own tools. Despite best efforts, tools kept communally in the lab can still go missing. “In a well-stocked lab, stuff still happens,” says Bannard. You can plan accordingly, however. Like Bannard, Chen keeps some of his own personal instruments separate, making it clear that they belong to him so that others do not accidentally — or purposefully — nab them.

#810. UK students call for school uniforms to be banned in pornography

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Short skirts, knee-high socks, white blouses: Girls' school uniform items are widely used as costumes in pornographic videos. As students experience harassment, a group wants to change that.

Aylin and Mae, both age 15, are members of their school's Little Feminist Book Club in Bicester, Oxfordshire, England.

During a group discussion, the club learned that teenagers at other schools were trying to have the sale of school uniforms banned in sex shops.

Schoolgirl costumes are also widely used in pornographic videos.

"All of us unanimously agreed. We wanted to take it a step further; we wanted to ban school uniform from all pornography," Aylin told DW. As a result, they started a petition to raise awareness of the issue.

Many of their fellow students, as the petition states, have already had unpleasant experiences while wearing their uniforms. Some reported being harassed as early as the age of 11. Many of these experiences had left them feeling frightened, ashamed and insecure. "I don't wear my school uniform outside of school," said Mae. "I don't like wearing it out."

In their petition, the girls also refer to a 2018 report by the charity Plan International. According to a survey in the report, 35% of the 1,004 respondents aged 14 to 21 had experienced unwanted sexual attention while wearing a school uniform. Eight percent said they had been filmed or photographed by a stranger without their permission, or that someone had taken a photo under their school skirt, known as "upskirting."

"Something that should actually be innocent, something that is supposed to symbolize a community, has now become discredited in society," said Mae. "And our perception of what a school uniform represents has been distorted by this disgusting media."

School uniforms are common in many countries around the world. They are meant to make class and financial divides less visible, which is also supposed to prevent bullying while strengthening a community feeling.

In the United Kingdom, uniforms are mandatory at almost all schools and preschools, which means essentially all children between the ages of 4 to 16 wear them.

Outside school, children have no way to escape unwanted attention.

"We just want to prevent people from thinking about children and school uniforms in that way," said Mae. "Hopefully, we'll be able to actually turn the ban into legislation."

Until that happens, they still have a long way to go, but the girls are optimistic. As of October 5, 2026, the petition has garnered more than 24,000 signatures. Thanks to an article in the English newspaper The Times, it's also gotten plenty of attention.

Now, Mae and Aylin hope to get parliament to debate the issue. Two Members of Parliament have already voiced their support.

But the girls also know some people believe longer skirts are the solution. "I believe that's wrong," explains Aylin. "We shouldn't blame the victims. We should blame those who think it is acceptable to sexualize them."
