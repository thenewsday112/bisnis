#591. 1.9-million-year-old discovery rewrites the story of human evolution

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

An archaeological site in the Jordan Valley is rewriting the timeline of early human migration. New research shows that 'Ubeidiya is at least 1.9 million years old, placing humans in the region hundreds of thousands of years earlier than previously believed. The revised dating makes 'Ubeidiya, alongside Dmanisi in Georgia, one of the oldest known sites of early human activity outside Africa. The findings suggest that ancient human populations, using different types of stone tools, had already spread into the Levant during a crucial early period of human expansion beyond Africa.

The research was led by Prof. Ari Matmon of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Prof. Omry Barzilai of the University of Haifa, and Prof. Miriam Belmaker of the University of Tulsa. By combining three advanced dating methods, the team established a more reliable timeline for 'Ubeidiya, a site that has played an important role in research on human evolution.

Their findings indicate that the site is at least 1.9 million years old, making it one of the earliest known locations where ancient humans lived outside their African homeland.

Ancient Stone Tools Reveal Early Human Activity

The 'Ubeidiya Formation has attracted scientific attention for decades because of its early evidence of the Acheulean stone tool tradition. This technology is known for large tools shaped on both sides, including hand axes. The site also contains a rich collection of animal fossils, representing species originally associated with Africa and Asia, including several that are now extinct.

Despite its importance, researchers have struggled to establish exactly when early humans occupied the area. Previous estimates generally placed 'Ubeidiya between 1.2 million and 1.6 million years old, but those dates relied on comparisons with other archaeological and geological evidence rather than direct measurements.

To resolve the uncertainty, the researchers collected new samples from the site and analyzed them using three complementary dating techniques. Each provided an independent source of evidence about the age of the ancient deposits.

The first technique, called cosmogenic isotope burial dating, uses rare isotopes that form when cosmic rays interact with rocks exposed at Earth's surface. After the rocks become buried, these isotopes decay at known rates. Measuring what remains allows scientists to estimate how long the rocks have been underground.

The researchers also investigated evidence of Earth's ancient magnetic field recorded in sediments deposited at the bottom of a lake. As these sediments accumulated, they preserved the orientation of the magnetic field at the time. Comparing these records with known magnetic reversals showed that the deposits formed during the Matuyama Chron, a period that began more than two million years ago.

A third technique involved fossilized shells from Melanopsis, a genus of freshwater snails found within the sediment. Using uranium-lead dating, the team was able to establish a minimum age for the geological layers containing the ancient stone tools.

Together, the three approaches pointed to a much older age than earlier estimates.

A 1.9-Million-Year-Old Discovery Changes the Human Migration Timeline

The combined evidence indicates that 'Ubeidiya dates back at least 1.9 million years, significantly changing the timeline of early human settlement beyond Africa.

This places the Jordan Valley site at approximately the same age as Dmanisi in Georgia, another major location for understanding early human migration. The similarity in age suggests that ancient human populations were expanding into different regions around the same time.

The findings also raise intriguing questions about how these early migrations unfolded. Evidence from the two sites suggests that different groups of ancient humans may have carried distinct stone tool technologies out of Africa during overlapping periods.

These included the Oldowan tradition, associated with relatively simple stone tools, and the more sophisticated Acheulean tradition, known for carefully shaped tools such as hand axes. Rather than spreading in a single technological sequence, these different approaches to toolmaking may have traveled beyond Africa at roughly the same time as separate groups of early humans entered new environments.

Solving a Three-Million-Year-Old Dating Puzzle

The researchers also had to explain an unexpected complication in their results. Initial measurements of cosmogenic isotopes suggested that some rocks were approximately three million years old, considerably older than indicated by the site's magnetic, fossil, geological, and archaeological evidence.

Further investigation revealed why the measurements appeared to conflict.

The sediments had undergone a complicated history of repeated deposition, burial, exposure, and movement within the Dead Sea rift and the surrounding landscape. Some of the material had been buried long before it was eventually transported and deposited again near the ancient lake at 'Ubeidiya.

This recycling meant that the geological history recorded by certain rocks extended much farther into the past than the formation of the archaeological layers themselves.

"The exposure-burial history that emerges from the model implies recycling of sediments previously deposited and buried in the rift valley... and then redeposited along the 'Ubeidiya paleo lake shoreline."

By accounting for this complex sediment history, the team reconciled the conflicting measurements and strengthened the evidence for the site's revised age.

The results establish 'Ubeidiya as one of the oldest known centers of early human activity outside Africa. They also suggest that the movement of our ancient relatives into new territories was already underway 1.9 million years ago, potentially involving multiple populations with different toolmaking traditions.

#3767. Franki Branding: Inside Koto's New Identity

Koto's Franki branding turns restaurant discovery into a shared joke, with a yellow, rounded system built around the line Make Time Dance.

The first thing you notice is the yellow. It is not a highlight or an accent, it is the whole field, and the charcoal type sits on it at a size where the rounded terminals read as soft, almost inflatable shapes. Koto calls Franki an insider's guide, a shortcut to knowing where to go and when, and the work behaves that way: a character symbol that stands for being in the know, candid photos of people mid-meal, and lines that sound like a friend texting you the plan. One poster reads, Welcome to the Not-So-Secret Club, Katie, and another answers with IWYK, short for If we know, you know.

Diatype Rounded carries the headlines, and Koto lets it run huge. On the yellow poster, a single line about after-work drinks stretches across the top in gold-brown letters while a small candid photo and the line Franki knows a place sit low on the right, so the hierarchy is blunt: joke first, proof second, sign-off third. The same grid repeats in the IWYK poster, where the four letters fill the top of the frame and the small print, the symbol and the FRANKI lockup gather in the lower right corner. Even the wordmark keeps the same soft geometry, with round bowls and terminals that match the symbol.

The Franki branding system is built for a video-first app, so it has to work as motion as well as print. Koto says motion drives the system across typography, UI and content, giving it rhythm, momentum and a playful confidence, and the stills already suggest that movement: type set at poster scale, photos cropped into rounded cards, and a palette of Bright Yellow and Gold against off-white and charcoal that stays readable at phone size. Franki Social Club carries the same language off the app and into the world, rewarding creators and diners for recommendations, and the website frame in the set shows it plainly: Experiences Are Best Shared, over a grid of food, drinks and people.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

CEO admits service used for foreign exchange trading had holes company was slow to fix

Japanese ISP GMO Internet has warned customers the cloudy desktop service it offers as an ideal foreign exchange trading environment was compromised by an attacker, and that it had been slow to improve the service's security.

GMO runs a service called Onamae.com Desktop Cloud and last week admitted “a third party had installed malicious programs in the server environments of some customers.” That’s never welcome news but is an especially poor outcome because the servers host cloudy Windows desktops that GMO recommends customers use to host foreign exchange trading applications.

In later updates the company revealed that some customers reported “unauthorized operations were performed on their trading accounts” and advised that third parties had probably seen users’ passwords for trading services.

The next day, the company advised users to immediately change passwords for their trading accounts, and to do so using a device that they don’t use for trading.

The company has since admitted that its Onamae.com Desktop Cloud service’s “systems had security vulnerabilities, that its environment was accessed without authorization, and that customer information was taken outside the Company.”

“Some customers have also reported transactions in their trading accounts that they do not recognize.”

Those transactions have GMO sufficiently worried that it contacted major trading services, asked them to forcibly terminate all active sessions, monitor for unauthorized transactions, and to block trade requests coming from the Onamae.com Desktop Cloud.

GMO said it has “received a demand for money from a person believed to be the attacker. We have also been told that, if we refuse, the attacker will take actions such as publishing information about the Company and our customers.”

The company won’t pay because it feels “complying would not guarantee that the obtained information is deleted or that its misuse is prevented.”

Investigations into the incident continue, with GMO warning that 26,739 VDI servers may have been hit by the attack, and that users’ personal devices may be at risk.

The company is offering all users a ¥5,000 ($32) condolence payment and promised to make good on any trading losses caused by the attack.

This incident may have quite a blast radius, as GMO has warned that some trading apps may keep sessions alive even after passwords change. “Check your securities or FX company's guidance or your account page, and either disconnect all active sessions or ask the company's support desk to disconnect them,” GMO advised.

That ¥5,000 isn’t going to get anywhere near paying for the work customers will do to sort this out.

GMO CEO Masatoshi Kumagai acknowledged that in a blog post in which he described the payment as “a token of our initial sincerity towards our customers who have experienced inconvenience and worry.”

“We will dedicate the full resources of our group to providing a free alternative environment for six months, fundamentally overhauling our system infrastructure, and conducting a thorough investigation into the cause and preventing recurrence with the help of external experts,” he added.

But he also admitted that GMO knew the service’s security could be improved.

“Our in-house white-hat hackers had previously pointed out areas for improvement to the team,” he wrote. “The team took these points to heart and was working on countermeasures.”

“However, while we proceeded cautiously, considering the impact on service availability and prioritizing other issues, our response lacked sufficient depth and speed, allowing the attackers to exploit the vulnerability. I deeply regret that, despite having professionals on staff, we failed to fully translate their expertise into effective defenses as an organization.”

The CEO also said GMO can’t secure itself without government help.

“I've been involved in this world for 31 years, since the dawn of the internet industry,” he wrote. “The cyberspace that I built with my colleagues, believing it would bring smiles to people's faces, is now on the verge of being completely destroyed by malicious actors.”

“The ease with which open weight AI models can be combined and spread into countless attack tools has transformed cyberattacks, which once required advanced skills, into an era where anyone can easily carry them out,” he added, before claiming that GMO Group’s cybersecurity operation “has confirmed that the source of an unauthorized access attack targeting approximately 20 Japanese companies was the misuse of ‘ARTEX,’ an autonomous AI penetration testing tool originally intended for defense.”

Kumagai thinks public/private partnerships are needed to improve infosec across Japan.

“Yesterday, we submitted a proposal to the government to ‘apply proactive cyber defense,’” he wrote.

“We are by no means trying to shift the blame for our company's actions onto the government and beg for a bailout,” he wrote, but argued that unless Japan implements national-level defenses, more such incidents are unavoidable. ®

#584. The surprisingly simple exercise that could lower your blood pressure

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

New research suggests that exercising just once or twice a week may be enough to maintain some of the blood pressure benefits gained from regular physical activity, at least over a short period. However, people who continue exercising three times weekly may experience larger and more sustained improvements.

The findings, published in BMJ Open Sport & Exercise Medicine, suggest that even a single weekly session of isometric exercise could help people preserve improvements in blood pressure when they cannot commit to a more frequent workout schedule.

Isometric exercise involves tightening muscles while holding the body in a fixed position for several minutes. Common examples include wall squats, planks, and handgrip exercises.

Earlier research has found that isometric training can lower blood pressure more effectively than other types of exercise, sometimes producing reductions comparable to or greater than those achieved with certain medications. However, researchers have not fully understood how often these exercises need to be performed to maintain their cardiovascular benefits.

To investigate, researchers enrolled 100 adults whose average age was 37.4 years. Participants had systolic blood pressure ranging from normal to high-normal levels (120-140 mmHg) and did not regularly participate in structured exercise programs.

Under expert guidelines, systolic blood pressure between 130 and 139 mmHg falls into the "high-normal" category, which warrants specific lifestyle recommendations from healthcare professionals. None of the participants had been diagnosed with hypertension.

To qualify, volunteers had to be free of injuries and illnesses, have no current or previous use of blood pressure medication, and be nonsmokers. They also had to consume no more than 14 units of alcohol per week.

Participants were randomly assigned to five groups. One served as a control group, while the other four began the same four-week exercise program at home, focusing on isometric movements involving the lower body.

The training consisted of four wall squat holds per session, with two minutes of seated recovery between each hold. Participants completed this routine three times weekly during the first four weeks.

After this initial training period, the four exercise groups followed different schedules for another four weeks. One continued with three weekly sessions, another reduced its frequency to twice weekly, and a third performed the exercises only once a week. The fourth group stopped training altogether.

Researchers evaluated blood pressure, heart rate, and additional measures of cardiovascular function at the beginning of the experiment and again after four and eight weeks.

Even One Weekly Workout Helped Preserve Blood Pressure Benefits

After the first four weeks, all four exercise groups showed significant improvements compared with the control group. These included reductions in systolic blood pressure, which measures pressure when the heart contracts, and diastolic blood pressure, which measures pressure when the heart relaxes. Mean arterial pressure, a measure of average blood pressure throughout the cardiac cycle, also decreased significantly.

These initial results were consistent with earlier studies demonstrating the blood pressure benefits of isometric exercise. However, after participants switched to their assigned maintenance schedules, the groups no longer showed statistically significant differences in diastolic blood pressure.

By the end of the eight-week study, participants exercising just once or twice weekly had maintained significant reductions in systolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure compared with the control group. Neither schedule maintained a statistically significant improvement in diastolic blood pressure.

The greatest benefits appeared among participants who continued performing wall squats three times per week. Their systolic blood pressure fell by an average of 13.73 mmHg (-13.73 mmHg) from the beginning of the study.

By comparison, the twice-weekly group experienced an average reduction of 6.95 mmHg, while participants exercising once a week recorded a decrease of 6.56 mmHg.

Those who maintained the three-session schedule also preserved improvements in other cardiovascular measurements, including total peripheral resistance (an indication that blood vessels have narrowed) and heart rate variability.

Stopping Exercise Caused Blood Pressure Benefits to Fade

The results also revealed what happened when participants stopped exercising completely.

Those who discontinued wall squats after the initial four weeks experienced a reversal of their earlier blood pressure improvements. By the end of the study, their readings were no longer statistically different from those of the control group.

This finding suggests that maintaining some level of regular isometric exercise may be important for preserving the cardiovascular improvements gained during training.

However, the researchers emphasized that the maintenance phase lasted only four weeks. Additional studies following participants for longer periods will be needed to determine whether less frequent training can preserve these benefits over months or years.

"The study demonstrates that while lower frequency isometric exercise training may be sufficient to maintain blood pressure reductions over the short term, higher frequencies are required to preserve these benefits and the associated vascular and cardiac autonomic adaptations," the researchers write.

"The study offers evidence that lower frequency isometric exercise training can help maintain short-term blood pressure reductions, providing a pragmatic option for individuals with limited time or adherence challenges."

Last month, the Australian government began preliminary investigations into potential new laws that would force social media platforms to provide users with an option to switch off algorithmic recommendations, and set nonalgorithmic feeds as the default. The end result would be that users who choose to opt out of algorithmic feeds would only see updates from people and profiles they’ve explicitly chosen to follow.

Australia’s Communications Minister Annika Wells said in a recent address to the National Press Club that algorithmic incentives don’t necessarily align with what’s good or healthy for users, meaning that engagement-focused algorithms will often amplify harmful content to keep people scrolling.

“Platforms aren't passive,” Wells said. “They decide what is amplified, what captures us, what behaviors get rewarded,” Wells said. “They have been optimized for engagement and for attachment, not for well-being. Because the longer we stay, the more valuable we are.”

Requiring platforms to provide an algorithm opt-out could limit over-exposure to potentially harmful content, and it could also prevent users from being inundated with material that doesn’t benefit them or their communities.

Clearly, there are established links between social media algorithms and harmful impacts on users. As such, Australia’s proposal to enable an algorithm opt-out makes sense, and could have a significant impact on mental health while also reducing social media usage time overall.

Though the platforms themselves are keen to avoid that outcome.

Instagram Chief Adam Mosseri responded to a question last month about the potential of removing engagement algorithms, and said that if engagement-aligned algorithms were removed it would lead to a worse result for creators.

As per Mosseri: “What happens [without algorithms] is people become a bit less interested in the experience and they use [Instagram] a lot less, which then means that there’s less reach to go around and people start to get more and more mad about the fact that their reach is going down because the overall pie gets smaller.”

Although his response was not specifically linked to Australia’s proposal, Mosseri reiterated this perspective over the weekend. In a post on Threads, he said: “Every major platform has had to lean into recommendations — posts from accounts you don't happen to follow — to stay relevant. Going back to a following-only feed would quickly mean a smaller Instagram, with less reach to go around for everyone.”

Mosseri’s responses seem to indicate that he believes highlighting reduced usage of Instagram and reduced feed value is something that the public will see as a negative. Perhaps Mosseri believes this will help to drive opposition against the proposals to ban algorithmic amplification.

However, based on the research, banning algorithmic feeds seems like a minor price to pay for a range of potential benefits.

Mosseri’s secondary note is that creators will get less reach, and thus less exposure potential. That is a legitimate concern, considering the impact that Instagram now has on exposure for emerging artists.

But compared to the potential harms outlined in the above-noted studies, this doesn’t seem like a strong enough counter against at least a trial of removing algorithms.

What is clear is that Meta will lose money if Australia’s proposal goes into effect. That reason alone will likely lead to Meta and others unleashing an army of lobbyists to oppose any such push.

One particular angle that’s currently being presented in opposition to the bill is that the true motivation for Australia’s proposed change is actually speech control. The opposition alleges that the government wants to limit speech through its own determination of what’s defined as harmful content.

Earlier in the month, American conservative commentator Michael Shellenberger gave a speech at a Conservative Political Action Network conference in Brisbane, in which he claimed the proposed bill could give Australia’s eSafety Commissioner “sweeping powers over online speech, including the ability to operate fake social media accounts.”

Though both of those claims are misleading.

On the first claim, a provision in an initial draft of the bill would have given the communications minister the power to determine new elements of harm to be covered by the bill via “legislative instrument.” That means the minister would be able to make this determination without passing it through Parliament. However, such acts would still need to be tabled in Parliament, where they could be questioned and disallowed by MPs.

After concerns were raised about this, Wells confirmed in her Press Club speech that the government removed this element entirely. That will ensure that any determination of what qualifies as harm under the bill will be determined by Parliamentary review.

Regarding Shellenberger’s second point, the original draft of the bill included a proposal to allow approved researchers to use fake accounts for research purposes within pre-approved and strictly controlled means.

This provision relates primarily to testing whether platforms are adhering to the laws. It was designed to stop the platforms from blocking approved research accounts, which could enable them to avoid scrutiny.

As reported by the Conversation, the proposed provisions don’t give regulators unrestricted power to “covertly manipulate public debate.”

“Instead they allow the regulator to test platforms and their claims, rather than simply take their word for it,” the Conversation said.

The provision’s specific wording will likely be revised to reflect this since the bill is still in the consultation stage.

Which is the key note. None of this has been agreed to or even proposed as a bill yet, and the government is seeking community input on the language and substance of the law.

Yet, the opposition has already begun. As social media platforms try to ensure that their business interests are not impacted, it’s likely these angles will see more discussion online.

#615. Scientists Trap a Rare-Earth Metal in a Molecular Cage, Unlocking a Strange New State

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Molecular cages helped chemists remove one electron from terbium, making an unusual form easier to study for possible uses in quantum computing and advanced sensors.

Rare-earth metals are essential to modern technologies, from smartphones and high-performance magnets to advanced medical devices. Yet separating these valuable elements remains a major challenge because many share remarkably similar chemical properties. Terbium is particularly difficult to manipulate, as it strongly favors a stable electronic state that resists change.

Now, researchers led by the University of Iowa have found a way to overcome this barrier, trapping terbium inside specially designed molecular cages and stabilizing an unusual form of the metal under ordinary environmental conditions. The breakthrough could open new possibilities for rare-earth separation and technologies ranging from quantum computing to advanced sensors.

Terbium usually favors the plus-3 oxidation state, as do the other lanthanides. Oxidation states track how many electrons an atom has formally lost or gained, and moving terbium from plus-3 to plus-4 requires removing one electron. The plus-3 state is terbium’s most stable and has been well documented, while its behavior in other states remains much less explored.

“You can compel it to give away or accept an electron,” explains Korey Carter, an assistant professor in the University of Iowa’s Department of Chemistry, “but it’s generally energetically unfavorable.”

Carter led the study with Pere Miro, an associate professor in the same department. Their team built a molecular assembly of tungsten and oxygen called a polyoxometalate and added binding agents known as ligands. The resulting cage-like structures held terbium in place while the researchers changed its chemistry.

With a terbium atom sandwiched in these cages, the chemists added potassium persulfate, a chemical oxidant. The reaction removed an electron from terbium, changing its charge and shifting it into the plus-4 oxidation state. The team isolated this unusual form and described it in detail.

“One of the challenges with lanthanides is they prefer to be in the same oxidation state, and it’s tricky to change that state and thus separate one from another without it being performed in a tightly controlled environment, such as a lab,” says Carter, the study’s co-corresponding author. “But we devised a method to isolate, or separate terbium, by changing its oxidation state in an environment that doesn’t require specialized lab controls.”

Previous researchers had produced terbium in the plus-4 state in rigorous air-free environments, according to Carter. The Iowa team worked under ambient conditions, without special controls for temperature, humidity, or pressure.

“If you were in your house and you were setting up a chemistry experiment, it would be analogous to what we were doing in the lab,” he says.

The silvery-gray metal is mined primarily in China, though it is also found in the United States and a few other countries. It already supports a broad range of technologies. Terbium is central to the green phosphors that produce light in televisions, fluorescent lamps, and LED displays, and it plays a key role in solid-state devices and fuel cells. It has also improved the safety of medical X-rays by allowing the same image quality to be achieved with much shorter exposure times.

The new method provides a proof of concept for potentially easier, less expensive separation of terbium, with possible lessons for separating other lanthanides. Making these less familiar chemical states more accessible could also help researchers investigate properties relevant to quantum technologies, which use the unusual behavior of atoms and smaller constituents. Potential applications include computing and advanced sensors for navigation, medical imaging, and other fields.

Carter’s group now wants to apply what it learned from terbium to actinides, a group of highly radioactive elements. Members of this group are used in cancer-fighting treatments, to power cardiac pacemakers implanted in the body, and in smoke detectors.

“Our findings highlight the ability to do transformative research at the University of Iowa, and they provide a platform to extend the chemistry to the actinide series in UI Radiochemistry laboratories,” Carter says.

#413. A-81 Reaches South Georgia

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The Antarctic iceberg made a relatively quick exit from the Weddell Sea, only to slow at the edge of a remote island’s underwater shelf.

Some Antarctic icebergs linger close to home for decades, running aground near the ice shelves that spawned them or circling partway around the continent on coastal currents. Others, like Iceberg A-81, make a faster exit, heading north out of the Weddell Sea along a route well-traveled by the region’s bergs.

Fast is relative, though. After breaking from the Brunt Ice Shelf in January 2023, the roughly 1,550-square-kilometer (600-square-mile) berg spent more than three years in the Weddell Sea, carried along the coast by the clockwise-flowing Weddell Gyre. By May 2026, it reached the tip of the Antarctic Peninsula. It then moved north into the Scotia Sea, and by late October 2026, it approached the remote island of South Georgia in the South Atlantic Ocean, 2,300 kilometers (1,400 miles) north-northwest of the Brunt Ice Shelf.

This image, acquired by the MODIS (Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer) on NASA’s Terra satellite, shows the berg on October 20. In the days before, its progress slowed noticeably, and it appeared to be bumping along the edge of the island’s underwater shelf, according to Jan Lieser of Australia’s Bureau of Meteorology and Christopher Shuman of the University of Maryland (retired). South Georgia has slowed and even stopped icebergs before. In 2025, for instance, Iceberg A-23A ran aground on the shelf and later broke apart.

Though Iceberg A-81 was a steadfast traveler once it broke free, it took its time in becoming an iceberg in the first place. For about 35 years, one of the Brunt Ice Shelf’s rifts known as Chasm 1 was relatively stable. Then, around 2012, it began growing again, and by 2019, it was advancing quickly enough that a break seemed imminent. But the floating glacial ice held on for several more years until finally, in January 2023, the berg let go.

Calving is a normal part of the life cycle of ice shelves, but scientists keep a close eye on Brunt, which has recently shed several large icebergs in quick succession and is home to the British Antarctic Survey’s Halley Research Station, a major base for Earth, atmospheric, and space science research since the 1950s. Besides A-81, the ice shelf calved Iceberg A-74 from the North Rift in 2021 and Iceberg A-83 from the Halloween Crack in 2024. A-74 has since broken up, while A-83 remains intact and is trailing A-81 northward, along with a handful of other icebergs that appear to be clearing out of the Weddell Sea.
