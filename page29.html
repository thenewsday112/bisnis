#887. I Tried the First BEMT Sunscreens to Launch in the US—Here’s How They Wear

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

When people ask me what my favorite sunscreens are, I almost exclusively tell them about the French and Korean formulas that I hoard back from my travels (special shout-outs to the Caudalie Vinosun High Protection Glow Oil and Fromier EGF Cica Water Sun Ampoule). The textures work better for my acne-prone skin and just feel so good when I slather them on. But the more impressive feat, of course, is the advanced formulations and ingredients that haven’t made their way to the US—until now. Earlier this year marked the first time since the 1990s that the US Food & Drug Administration has approved a new sunscreen filter to be used stateside: meet BEMT.

More formally known as bemotrizinol, BEMT is a chemical sunscreen filter that protects against both UVA and UVB rays. Compared to other sunscreens in the States that combine different ingredients and filters to provide sun protection, BEMT works to protect skin not only from the UVB rays that cause skin cancer, but also the UVA rays that cause skin aging and hyperpigmentation in a single filter.

Charlene Valledor, cosmetic chemist and president of SOS Beauty, adds that it is a more photostable ingredient and provides longer-lasting protection. Corinne Erickson, MD, board-certified dermatologist and owner of Georgia Skin Specialists, agrees and adds that it’s a large molecule that won’t absorb through the skin and into our bloodstreams, which is a main concern for many when it comes to chemical formulas. Sunscreens with BEMT also blend in more seamlessly and won’t leave behind a white cast, which makes it a great option for those with darker skin tones.

But BEMT is not new and it’s been used by many parts of the world for decades. While this is an exciting development in the US sunscreen space, we’re still playing catch up ingredient-wise to places like Korea, Australia, and Europe. “It’s a step in the right direction, but we aren’t running to a better place yet,” says Charlotte Palermino, co-founder and chief brand officer of Dieux. “What I hope is that this opens the door to approving all the new and more innovative filters that would make America truly competitive globally.”

“Brands and formulators have been trying to mimic the lightweight nature of the sunscreens from the EU and Korea using the approved filters in the US for years, but we’ve never been able to match them,” adds Valledor. “The new SPFs formulated with BEMT that are finally launching will be much closer to their international variants in texture.”

And just because a sunscreen doesn’t have BEMT, it doesn’t mean they aren’t effective. Dr. Erickson says that it’s just great to have more options available to encourage more people to use sunscreen daily. “Because ultimately, if you love your sunscreen, you’re much more likely to wear it every day,” she says.

So if this has piqued your interest and you’re curious how BEMT sunscreens actually perform, we’ve done the hard part and tested them for you. Read on to see which ones are actually worth it.

The same basic sunscreen rules still apply. Dr. Erickson recommends looking for a sunscreen with broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher and be sure to use it everyday. You’ll want to use a shot glass full of sunscreen for your entire body, and reapply every two hours when outdoors and after swimming or sweating. “Even though BEMT is photostable, you still need to reapply your sunscreen,” she says.

BEMT sunscreens are generally safe. Valledor says that BEMT has been tested and ruled as generally safer and effective at concentrations up to 6%. Erickson also points to its already favorable safety profile, with it being a popular ingredient in sunscreens abroad. But as with any skin-care product, there’s always a slight risk of irritation. She recommends that those with allergy-prone or sensitive skin to read the full ingredient list to check if it contains ingredients that might cause a negative reaction. “It’s important to understand what is in the final formula in addition to BEMT to determine whether a formula is right for you and your skin type,” Valledor agrees.

#2714. The Worst Advice I Ever Heard Was “Shut Up And Die Like a Man”

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Will Gadd (left) and his friend, the late Ken Wallator. (Photo: Will Gadd)

“Shut up and die like a man!” sounds like a line from a bad action movie, but we all heard it on the radio from the paragliding competition director. The director was yelling at a terrified pilot who thought he was falling to his death. The pilot had accidentally keyed open his radio’s shoulder mic, allowing his frantic screams to be broadcast across the competition frequency.

The unfortunate pilot begging to god to not die had collapsed his wing and plummeted in front of my eyes. Twenty-one years later, the memory of this sight and soundtrack is unforgettable, and so was the director’s barking, because there was clearly no sympathy for what the pilot thought was his final, horrific ride. Miraculously, the pilot recovered his wing and flew out to land and reflect on his life choices alone.

Most pilots didn’t think the competition director’s statement was out of line for the time. Many still don’t. I see terrible parallels between flying culture and some facets of climbing’s; in alpinism especially, some see death as an attractant, or an inevitability. Over my 40 years as an alpinist and 35 as a pilot, I’ve watched climbers experience gut-wrenching trauma—then do their best to ignore it, too scared or proud to deal with the ugly, messy truth. This avoidance sends a message to younger climbers, much like the competition director’s statement so long ago: Suck it up, move on, and die with honor if things go bad.

But living with trauma doesn’t have to be that way.

When my late friend Ken Wallator was 21, he climbed Mount Belanger in the Canadian Rockies with his girlfriend Heidi. They reached the top of the difficulties, unroped, and walked along a low-angle snow ridge to the summit of the peak. But the snow ridge was actually a massive, overhanging cornice of windblown snow, and it collapsed as they stopped to take a photo. Ken somehow managed to leap to safety but Heidi fell hundreds of meters down the face.

Ken, a brave bear of a man, downclimbed the face, found Heidi somehow still alive, and started carrying her out to a distant hut. She died before they reached it.

In the aftermath of Heidi’s death, I watched Ken receive a surprising amount of blame from grieving members of the climbing community. Didn’t he know better than to walk on a cornice? Why weren’t they roped up? Ken was already wracked with guilt from the accident. When his community piled on, their condemnation lit an internal firestorm.

Thirty years later, in 2019, Ken helped me re-roof my house. He still had the strength of a bear and I watched him haul 100-pound loads of shingles up my rickety ladder. In a rare moment of emotionality, he told me how he still hadn’t gotten over Heidi’s death, or the climbing community’s blame. We talked more about trauma, and I realized only then how that day on Mount Belanger broke my friend in a way that never healed.

As two young, hungry alpinists, Ken and I didn’t know what mental “help” looked like 30 years ago, and Ken was too much a product of his culture to take it 30 years later. Despite his immense physical and mental strength, Ken died by suicide a few weeks after our talk.

More recently, Will Stanhope’s death has brought to mind this idea of “asking for help” once more. For years Stanhope had battled desperately with alcohol. He was facing various charges at the time of his death—including assault and sexual assault—while still succeeding on difficult routes as a professional climber and mountain guide.

In our last real conversation, Stanhope talked about needing to “harden up and stop letting the carnage get to me” one minute, then sobbed about the “death camp” of hard alpine climbing the next. Like Ken and me, he had lost many friends in the mountains.

“You and I [watched] our mentors and friends die, one after another, as if everyone around us was climbing their way to oblivion,” his friend Kieran Brownie wrote in an obituary. “We couldn’t return to the mountains, we couldn’t return to each other.”

Stanhope carried a heavy weight in the final months of his life, made even heavier by grief, guilt, and irreversible mistakes. After a lifetime of hard solos and even scarier roped climbs, it seemed impossible that Stanhope would ever fall off an easy 5.11. But when he did just that last April and succumbed to a head injury, to me and other friends, Stanhope’s death felt more related to the weight he carried in his head than the climb itself. His family and friends made truly profound and sometimes wrenchingly difficult efforts to help him, but, like falling, the weights of addiction and trauma develop their own momentum.

Hard drinking, hard living, and celebrating dangerous acts—usually lauded as “bold”—are hallmarks of every mountain sport I’ve ever done, from kayaking to flying to skiing to climbing. Our literature is full of stories celebrating those who climbed hard, drank hard, and died silently.

I can’t help but wonder if our literature strives to make these deaths seem somehow valiant and necessary, like dying in the mountains is a noble sacrifice rather than the tragedy it really is. The classic mountaineering texts rarely explore the trauma that comes after seeing a friend die. They barrel ever onward to the summit.

Military veterans and first responders have a trauma-treatment framework that climbers should strive to adopt. Soldiers are not considered “weak” if they are affected by bloody combat. Paramedics are not ridiculed for taking leave after responding to a grisly car wreck. These are normal human responses, and professional groups typically have protocols for helping those involved in traumatic situations. These groups have no other choice; a traumatized team simply cannot function.

Those professional protocols weren’t there for Ken, Will, that terrified pilot, or many others, including me.

When I was 19 I tried with every tool I knew to save a fellow kayaker stuck just inches from the surface in his boat. I couldn’t reach him, and he drowned in front of me as I tried to remember how to rig a better haul system. I had nightmares for years of the paddler chasing me in the dark beside that river.

Only decades later did I seek help, after a divorce and a lot of behavior I understood even less than that terrible accident. I’m lucky I survived and sought belated help. Ken and Will and many others didn’t. Many more in our community—your friends and mine—are barely standing today, carrying crippling loads. They need help putting those loads down.

The pain of trauma does not disappear with six or 600 beers. Doing dangerous climbs or flights or other high-stimulus experiences temporarily distracts from the weight, but doesn’t solve it. There is no pack heavier than one filled with trauma you can’t put down.

One of the smartest things I’ve ever heard about trauma came after we failed to resuscitate a dying man at the base of an ice climb a few years ago. A professional responder on the scene could tell I was shaken, and looked me in the eye and said, “Post traumatic stress is normal; post traumatic stress disorder is optional—if you get help. Get help.”

I see real change today, from my wife Sarah Hueniken’s Mountain Muskox foundation to the American Alpine Club’s Grief Fund, two organizations built to support mountain-trauma survivors.

But I also still see a lot of the “Shut up and die like a man” attitude, and the celebration of “bold” yet physically unimpressive climbs that are often little more than Russian Roulette. Huge, low-angle alpine routes with immense serac and avalanche hazard are not that cool, in my book. And I see many climbers who still romanticize the wrong parts of climbing, whether that’s meaningless objective hazard or the very idea of “suffering silently.”

We need to recognize that a mountain death doesn’t end with the funeral; often that’s just the start of a very long and difficult process for everyone involved. In climbing more people die on the descent after the summit than on the climb itself.

At times I’ve felt deeply angry about our outdoor tribe’s celebration of “bold” hazards, and our denial of the after-effects of engaging in high-risk sports where participants die regularly. We often celebrate the bold free solos or dangerous alpine routes where everything clicked into place. We never celebrate the dead parent who doesn’t show up for their kids’ birthdays.

But the truth is that I laughed when the paragliding competition director said his infamous line. I promised myself that if I was in that situation in the future, I’d shut up. That became my normal. Today, I hope I’d speak up on the comment, and reach out to the guy who almost died that day. Because I’ve been him, and it sucks, and I’m trying to do better.

I’m less angry today when I think about Ken, Will, or others who have left the game, and just hope that our culture continues to evolve in the same way the first responders did, and that we ultimately celebrate survival more than dying silently. We can do better, if we want to.

Finally, if, like me, you’ve been through the trauma wringer, it often takes help to sort it out. We’re not tougher than the veterans or first responders even if our culture says we “should” be—we’re just less educated about intense trauma.

The goal is to return to full function and fly in life, not get stuck in the inevitable mental dust devils of mountain death. Peace to those hurting, it can get better with help. Substances and more dangerous living may distract us short term, but long term the combo is deadly despite the romantic words we historically place on both. I write these words for the future Wills and Kens, they are me, you, our friends.

Shutting up is not a solution, because silence is death.

#2140. 1,000,000 Miles on United

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

I've completed approximately 2 round trips from the Earth to the Moon.

A few moments ago, I crossed the 1,000,000 mile threshold on United Airlines. There was nothing particularly special about this flight. (I'm en route from Denver to Birmingham for a talk tomorrow at the University of Alabama.) The flight attendant gave me a brief congratulation (shortly after I initially published the post.) It was just like any other flight. If my math is right, a million miles is roughly two round trips from the Earth to the moon.

I started accruing United miles around 2012 when I moved to Houston. United and Continental had just finished the merger. For the first eight years or so, I sought to earn as many miles, as that was the key to status. Longtime readers will recall that I took mileage runs to Singapore, Hong Kong, and Sydney to make status at the end of the year. All of these tickets were purchased at low rates. The goal was to fly as far as possible while spending the least amount of money. By 2016, I had already accrued nearly 300,000 lifetime miles. But shortly before the pandemic, United switched the format. Now, to make status, you had to spend money, not fly miles. It made more sense to take a very expensive trip to San Francisco than to take a very cheap trip to Singapore. As a result, my pace of collecting miles dropped precipitously. I barely flew in 2020 through 2022. I ramped up in 2023. In 2024, I reached about 750,000 miles. And it took another two years to complete the goal.

Hitting the one million mile mark provides me with lifetime gold status. That status, alas, is basically meaningless. There are very few benefits with gold status that are not provided by the United Club credit card. In the past, United would let you gift your status to a companion--for me that is 1K--but United will only allow you to gift gold status.

Going forward, United had substantially increased the cost of making status. For 2026, I could earn 1K status with 60 segments and about 22,000 PQP. For 2027, segments no longer matter and I will need to hit 32,000 PQP. Unless something significant changes in my spending, I won't be able to meet that threshold. I could become a free agent, and take flights on whichever airline provides the best deal, but living in Houston severely limits my options. So I will likely stay tied to United, albeit without 1K status.

At this point, even 1K status is less desirable than it once was. I rarely receive complimentary upgrades. And even when I use plus points, the upgrades are still rare on busy routes. I can't remember the last time I was upgraded on a flight to DCA or EWR. (I was upgraded on the flight to Birmingham.) United, and the other airlines, would much rather sell an upgrade than provide it to a loyal flyer. The value proposition just isn't what it used to be.

On the plus side, United now has Starlink on most of the flights, which makes the skies far more productive.

#523. Beyond Bad Cholesterol: Scientists Uncover a Hidden Driver of Heart Disease

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Protein patterns linked to TRL and Lp(a), two types of fat-carrying particles, suggest shared inflammatory pathways that could offer new targets for heart disease prevention.

Lowering “bad” cholesterol substantially reduces the risk of a heart attack, yet some people still develop coronary heart disease after treatment brings their LDL cholesterol down to a recommended level. Other particles that carry fats through the blood can contribute to the risk that remains. Understanding how they affect blood vessels could help researchers find more precise ways to prevent heart disease.

Coronary heart disease develops when cholesterol accumulates in the blood vessels supplying the heart and can lead to a heart attack if untreated. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), cardiovascular diseases kill nearly 17.9 million people worldwide each year, with heart attacks and strokes accounting for more than four out of five of those deaths.

Researchers at Chalmers University of Technology in Sweden have now identified blood protein patterns that offer clues to what happens beyond cholesterol buildup. Two types of fat-carrying particles, triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRL) and lipoprotein(a), or Lp(a), shared many protein patterns and signs of potentially harmful biological effects. The pattern associated with LDL, or low-density lipoprotein, was mainly linked to cholesterol accumulating in artery walls.

All three types of particles can deposit cholesterol in artery walls, and LDL is the most abundant. But abundance does not tell the whole story. A previous study by the same research group found that counting these harmful particles was the most effective way to predict future heart disease risk. It also showed that TRL and Lp(a) were more harmful per particle than LDL. Researchers believe these two types account for much of the remaining risk after LDL cholesterol reaches a healthy level.

“For this study, we wanted to understand what happens beyond the accumulation of cholesterol in artery walls, whether different fat-carrying particles can damage the blood vessels differently and why the remaining risk of heart disease still occurs,” says Jakub Morze, lead author and a postdoctoral fellow at Chalmers University of Technology.

The team used proteomics, the study of proteins, to examine how the three types of particles affect the body. Proteins carry out essential functions, and their levels can change with diet, stress, aging, or disease. Measuring them provides a snapshot of how the body is functioning at that moment. The researchers combined measurements of almost 3,000 blood proteins with genetic data and long-term health records to identify patterns that might help explain how the particles damage arteries.

The overlapping protein patterns associated with TRL and Lp(a) appeared to be linked to the later development of heart disease. These particles also contained proteins that attract immune cells, which could contribute to inflammation and changes in the structure of blood vessel walls. Although TRL and Lp(a) are different types of particles, the findings suggested that some of their harmful effects may involve shared biological pathways.

“The overlap for TRL and Lp(a) is one of the most interesting findings,” says Morze. “It suggests that these particles may affect some of the same inflammatory and vascular processes. This could help identify targets for more precisely directed prevention in the future.”

“Lowering LDL cholesterol remains a cornerstone of prevention,” says Clemens Wittenbecher, who led the study and is Assistant Professor of Precision Medicine and Diagnostics at Chalmers. “Our findings point to biological processes that may help explain some of the risk that remains beyond LDL. The next step is to establish whether measuring or targeting these processes can improve prevention alongside existing treatments.”

Reference: “Apolipoprotein B-containing lipoproteins: distinct roles in atherosclerosis revealed by plasma proteomic profiling” by Jakub Morze, Elias Björnson, Michael Y Mi, Martin Adiels, Giorgio E M Melloni, Gonçalo R da Silva, Andrzej Rynkiewicz, Nicholas A Marston, Marta Guasch-Ferré, Michael Y Tsai, Jerome I Rotter, Robert E Gerszten, Qi Sun, Frank B Hu, Rikard Landberg, Göran Bergström, Chris J Packard, Jan Borén and Clemens Wittenbecher, 31 August 2026, European Heart Journal. Fall is when a lot of homeowners get around to overseeding cool-season lawns. You rake out the thin spots, spread the seed and set the sprinkler going. Two weeks later, plenty of those patches still look like bare dirt, and it's tempting to blame the seed.

Chances are, the seed is fine. Anyone who has spent time seeding a lawn knows some grasses are just slow, and a couple of the usual fall picks can take weeks to show anything. Fall is still a smart time to seed, though. The ground stays warm from summer while the air cools off, and the rain usually picks back up.

Grab the bag and check what's in it. A blend might mix ryegrass, fescue and bluegrass, and those don't keep the same schedule. Ryegrass can be up in about a week, while bluegrass may take a month, so a new blend looks spotty at first. And green doesn't mean done – new grass still needs several more weeks to root in.

The rest happens underground. Cool-season seed generally won't germinate until the soil is above about 50 degrees Fahrenheit, and colder ground can leave it sitting there until spring. Push a soil thermometer (like this one from the Urban Worm Store) in to see where yours is. The seed also has to stay damp and reach dirt, not just rest on dead thatch.

Any lawn that slows down and browns a little in July, then greens back up when the nights cool off, is a cool-season lawn. Bermuda, zoysia and bahiagrass are the opposite; those warm-season grasses do better seeded in late spring or early summer, after the soil has warmed up.

Across the upper Midwest and other northern areas, the ground cools early, so the window runs from mid-August to mid-September and has usually closed by October. Farther south, in places like Tennessee, there's room to seed into mid-October. Anything sown late has less time to get established before the first hard freeze.

Perennial ryegrass is what you want when a bare spot has to look green before winter – a worn strip by the mailbox, let's say, or the patch where the dog cuts across the yard. It's often up in 5 to 10 days. Sow it early in the fall window. One option is Pennington Fairway Supreme perennial ryegrass from Lowe's, which comes in a 50-pound bag with fertilizer mixed in. It's at its best in cool, moist weather and doesn't take drought well. In the South, it mostly gets sown over dormant Bermuda for winter color, not as a permanent lawn.

If your USDA Planting Zone is somewhere between Kansas and the Carolinas, your lawn may well be tall fescue already. Bluegrass struggles in those hot summers, but fescue's deep roots carry it through dry spells. Seed usually comes up within a week or two. GreenView turf-type tall fescue seed, available on Amazon, comes in 7-pound bags. Fescue grows in clumps rather than spreading, though, so thin spots won't fill in by themselves and need reseeding in early to mid-fall.

Bluegrass asks for patience up front. It usually takes at least two weeks to sprout and can take closer to a month. Once it's up, it spreads by underground rhizomes into a thick, soft lawn that can fill worn spots back in. It does best where summers stay fairly cool. Get it down right at the start of your fall window. Home Depot stocks a 5.6-pound bag of Scotts Turf Builder Kentucky bluegrass mix.

Keen for your grass to get a move on? Look this way...

Get down and look closely at a patch that still hasn't come up. You'll often find the seed sitting on top of old thatch or dead leaves instead of on the soil. It dries out between waterings up there; indeed, its first root may never reach the soil. Before you seed, mow the grass you already have down to about 1 1/2 to 2 inches (4–5cm), rake out the loose debris, then scratch up the surface until you can see bare, crumbly soil.

On small areas, a stiff 16-tine steel bow rake like the one Walmart carries will break up a crusted surface. Once the seed is down, rake over it lightly to work it into the top 1/8 to 1/4 inch (3–6mm) of soil, or press it in with the back of the rake. Don't worry if some seed is still showing, since bluegrass actually sprouts better with a little light.

On a breezy afternoon, soil watered at breakfast can be pale and crusty by the evening. Seed that's begun to swell can dry out and fail. Short, light sprinkles once or twice a day, and one more on days like that, keep the top inch (2.5cm) damp, not muddy. Long soaks can float seed into low spots or waterlog the soil, and an overnight rain counts as a turn.

A pulsing impact head can knock seed loose, but an oscillating sprinkler lays water down in a soft sweep. With the Melnor XT turbo oscillating sprinkler from Amazon, you can narrow the spray so it stays on the seed, not the sidewalk. For days when nobody's home, the Orbit single-outlet hose faucet timer from Home Depot can run short cycles as often as once an hour. Once seedlings have roots, water less often but more deeply.

A thin layer of screened compost over the seed helps keep the surface from drying out so fast. That layer gives the seed some protection from heavy rain, too. Spread it no more than about 1/4 inch (6mm) deep. You should still be able to see a little seed and soil through it.

Thicker isn't better. Grass seed carries only a small store of energy, and seed buried too deep can use it up before the shoot reaches light —, and fine bluegrass seed may not sprout under a heavy layer at all.

If you don't have finished compost, a bag of Scotts Turf Builder lawn soil from Home Depot is made for overseeding and top dressing.

So how long should you wait? With ryegrass or tall fescue in soil that's stayed warm and damp, about three weeks is a fair wait. Before reseeding, scrape back a little soil and look. Swollen or split seed is still working, while seed that looks dry and unchanged probably dried out or never touched the soil. Old seed can be the culprit too, so check the packaging date.

Don't count on starter fertilizer to hurry any of this along. Seed carries its own food for germination, and starter fertilizer is there to feed seedlings once roots form – too much can burn tender new grass. If your fall window has closed, it's better to stop than to keep sowing into cold soil. Some people dormant seed instead, spreading it in late fall once the soil is too cold for sprouting but before the ground freezes. Otherwise, early next fall is the more reliable time to try again.

Slow grass seed usually just needs a few more weeks. Choose the right grass for your area, sow it while the soil is still warm, and keep it damp. That will do more than any quick fix. With some patience, those bare spots may be filled in by the time you mow next spring.

We spend our lives trying to find our power and our freedom. It is not easy: We live in a culture that has distorted the meaning and measure of power, confusing visibility with value, status with substance, attention with love; we live in a universe where free will may be an illusion, where chance deals the vast majority of the cards — the family and epoch we are born into, the body and biochemistry we live in, the people we cross paths with, the accidents of chemistry and happenstance we call love. The great double-edged question is how to keep power from becoming a prison and how much freedom we can find between the narrow parameters of our givens. But somewhere deep down, in the marrow of our language, we seem to know: The word power shares its Middle Latin root with possibility — its only measure is that which liberates the possible in us; our only power is our freedom.

“Nothing is more unbearable, once one has it, than freedom,” James Baldwin wrote. It is unbearable largely because it demands a shedding of the self with all of its attachments — to ways of being, to ideas about how the world ought to be, to particular interpretations of reality. Such attachments are shackles best broken by what Irish Murdoch has so splendidly termed unselfing, key to each of the five kinds of freedom she identified in literature and life. For Murdoch, freedom takes radical unsefling — only then are we truly free to both be ourselves and love each other:

Freedom is exercised in the confrontation by each other, in the context of an infinitely extensible work of imaginative understanding, of two irreducibly dissimilar individuals. Love is the imaginative recognition of, that is respect for, this otherness.

This is why, for Octavio Paz — who thought deeply about freedom — love is “a knot made of two intertwined freedoms.” And if love is our fulcrum of belonging, then belonging itself is woven of the same paradox — something Maya Angelou articulates in one of her finest interviews:

You only are free when you realize you belong no place — you belong every place — no place at all.

In this sense, freedom is the hinge between being and belonging that swings open the door to being ourselves — which, yet again paradoxically, must include the freedom to change, to confront the illusory solidity of the self, its ceaseless mutability. Psychoanalyst Karen Horney formulates this perfectly in her pioneering work on the fundaments of personal growth:

We do not need an inner strait jacket with which to shackle our spontaneity, nor the whip of inner dictates to drive us to perfection. There is no doubt that such disciplinary methods can succeed in suppressing undesirable factors, but there is also no doubt that they are injurious to our growth. We do not need them because we see a better possibility of dealing with destructive forces in ourselves: that of actually outgrowing them. The way toward this goal is an ever increasing awareness and understanding of ourselves. Self-knowledge, then, is not an aim in itself, but a means of liberating the forces of spontaneous growth… And as we lose the neurotic obsession with self, as we become free to grow ourselves, we also free ourselves to love and to feel concern for other people.

Then, and only then, can we contact the soul beneath the self, which is the only real locus of our freedom. Vassily Grossman — who spent a thousand days as a correspondent on the frontlines of the Holocaust — captures this beautifully in a passage from his almost unbearably poignant reckoning with life and death:

What constitutes the freedom, the soul of an individual life, is its uniqueness. The reflection of the universe in someone’s consciousness is the foundation of his or her power, but life only becomes happiness, is only endowed with freedom and meaning when someone exists as a whole world that has never been repeated in all eternity. Only then can they experience the joy of freedom and kindness, finding in others what they have already found in themselves.
