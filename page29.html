ATHENS - Greek Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis has called for a global maritime truce after an Oct 28 drone attack sank a Greek container ship off Bulgaria, the latest of a series of strikes on ships in the western Black Sea this month.

Speaking at a high-level debate of the Greek presidency of the UN Security Council on maritime security, Mitsotakis said commercial vessels must be able to navigate safely and freely even during times of conflict.

“Targeted civilian commercial shipping is completely unacceptable. It is yet another reminder for the urgent need for a global maritime truce” he said, adding that disruptions to freedom of navigation drive up shipping and insurance costs, and ultimately increase costs for consumers.

Commenting on a Turkey-Libya maritime accord which has drawn condemnation from Greece and Cyprus and three Arab states, Mitsotakis said that no bilateral arrangement could override international law of the sea, which provides a framework for cooperation and the peaceful settlement of maritime disputes.

“We cannot allow our seas to become a jungle,” he said. REUTERS

#844. Famed oceanographer calls for ending krill catch near Antarctica as governments debate fishing quota

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Renowned oceanographer Sylvia Earle is calling for an end to krill fishing near Antarctica as officials from the U.S., China, Russia and 24 other governments negotiate a possible deal this week to dramatically expand fishing for the crustacean that's a vital food source for whales in the international waters surrounding the ice-covered continent.

“There is no sustainable extraction of krill in Antarctica possible, period,” Earle, who in 1990 became the first woman to serve as chief scientist of the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, said in an interview in Rome, where she was promoting her conservation work. “Krill are critical to the integrity of the entire system — whales, fish, birds, seals, sea lions, you name it."

Her comments come as negotiators wrap up the annual meeting in Australia of the Commission for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources, or CCAMLR, an international organization that manages the world’s southernmost fishery. The group has been deadlocked for years over an industry-backed management plan to expand krill fishing, which environmentalists and CCAMLR's own scientific committee insist should be accompanied by the long delayed creation of a California-sized marine protected area near the environmentally sensitive Antarctic peninsula that would restrict fishing. It was first proposed a decade ago by Argentina and Chile.

Krill fishing around Antarctica shut down early in August for the second year straight after trawlers from mostly Norway and China hit a 620,000 metric ton limit before the season's scheduled end.

The tiny crustaceans are primarily harvested for feed used to raise farmed fish and for the oil in red omega-3 capsules taken to improve heart, brain and joint health. But other sources of omega-3 exist — in fish oil, algae, even some vegetables — and nutritionists say it's not necessary to take dietary supplements if you eat a balanced diet rich in the fatty acids.

Researchers increasingly are also studying the role krill plays in removing huge amounts of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. Krill aren’t just a bulwark against climate change — they’re victims, too. As oceans warm, the krill populations, which need water colder than 4 degrees Celsius (39 Fahrenheit) to survive, have started to migrate toward cooler waters closer to the South Pole, research has shown.

Earle, 91, was among those who pioneered the use of Scuba diving to conduct marine research after launching her career in the 1950s studying algae. In recent years, “Her Deepness,” as she is sometimes known, has focused on conservation through Mission Blue, an organization that champions so-called “Hope Spots” in the oceans critical to marine health. Its work supports a United Nations target of protecting at least 30% of the world's oceans by 2030.

Although krill are one of the planet's largest biomasses, Earle, a vegetarian, said there was no “safe number” that can be extracted without harming the surrounding Antarctic ecosystem. She singled out for criticism Norway's Aker BioMarine, the pioneer in harvesting and marketing products made from krill. The company was founded by billionaire Kjell Inge Rokke, who amassed a fortune in Norway's oil and gas industry.

“There’s one country, Norway, with one company really pushed essentially by one individual, that is responsible for taking about two-thirds of the krill to marketplace,” said Earle. “Some of it goes to make krill oil that has been marketed as good for our health. Maybe it is, but at what cost to the environment?"

“It’s all for choices. These are not necessities,” she added.

Aker CEO attending krill negotiations in Australia

Rokke remains the largest shareholder in the company, which recently delisted from the Oslo stock exchange, through his Aker ASA industrial conglomerate. In 2024, Aker BioMarine spun off its fishing and krill meal operation, selling a majority stake to American Industrial Partners, a New York-based private equity firm.

Rokke did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Aker QRILL's Chairman, Matts Johansen, is attending the CCAMLR talks in Australia. Johansen over the past few years has tried to bridge differences among negotiators, pushing a proposed plan that would both increase the overall fishing quota but spread out the catch to avoid excessive pressure on any single hot spot. The company also supports the creation of a marine protected area near the Antarctic peninsula.

“Decades of independent science from multiple countries, including the latest assessment of the UN's Food and Agriculture Organization, support the sustainability of the Antarctic krill fishery,” Johansen, who is also CEO of Aker BioMarine, said in a statement. "We recognize the concerns around concentrated fishing activity, which is why we support stronger science-based spatial management. With El Niño putting pressure on global marine resources and omega-3 supplies, responsible fisheries like this matter more than ever."

Motorists in Italy will soon be permitted to personalise their vehicle number plates, in a revenue-raising initiative that follows the example set by Britain, the United States and other nations.

Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni announced the measure on Wednesday as she unveiled Italy's 2027 budget law, the final package ahead of a general election due next year, describing the number plate initiative as a "colourful note".

"One will be able to indulge oneself more or less as one sees fit, obviously at a cost. Something tells me that Italians' creativity could shine here," Meloni said.

Personalised plates will cost roughly €600 ($669) for cars and €360 for motor-bikes, according to a decree.

A Treasury official declined to estimate how much revenue the government expects the policy to generate.

Italian car registration plates currently consist of a string of two letters, three numbers and two letters. The extent to which motorists will be allowed to stray from this standard formula remains unclear.

The reform has prompted a wave of social media memes and jokes, alongside concerns that custom licence plates could open the flood-gates to insults and profanities.

One meme depicted Meloni dressed up as Marie Antoinette holding a platter of licence plates and saying, "Let them eat personalised plates" - a play on the famous phrase by the 18th century French queen "Let them eat cake".

Some welcomed the measure, including taxi drivers, who said distinctive personalised plates could make it easier to identify legitimate operators and combat illegal services.

Opposition parties on the centre-left were less enthusiastic, saying the licence-plate initiative was a distraction from more pressing issues that the budget failed to address.

"Meloni should know that Italians have already chosen their personalised plate, and it reads: 'Meloni, go away'," said Francesco Silvestri, a lawmaker from the Five Star Movement.

Education Secretary Linda McMahon intends to spend $43,000 to spruce up her government office — while she seeks to shutter her department, according to a new report.

Staffers for McMahon, a former professional wrestling executive, have told Congress that her office furniture is dilapidated and “cannot be configurationally or aesthetically integrated into the agency’s new office space,” The New York Times reported, citing department records.

In total, $43,278.64 will be shelled out for chairs, a conference table, a desk and other pieces.

“Believe it or not, just like in the private sector, new federal offices need places to sit, write and meet,” Savannah Newhouse, the Education Department press secretary, told the Times.

She added that the department, which recently relocated to a smaller building amid sweeping layoffs, is “saving taxpayers millions of dollars.”

McMahon isn’t the first Trump administration Cabinet official to face scrutiny over spending on office revamps.

In 2018, then-Housing and Urban Development Secretary Ben Carson canceled an order for a $31,000 dining set after facing backlash. The same year, then-Interior Secretary Ryan Zinke raised eyebrows for shelling out $139,000 to upgrade doors in his office.

McMahon, a billionaire and major Trump donor, has made it her mission to shutter the Education Department, founded in 1979.

“The American people elected President Trump with a clear mandate: to sunset a 46-year-old, $3 trillion failed federal education bureaucracy in Washington, D.C., and return authority to where it belongs, to parents, teachers and local leaders,” she told lawmakers in May.

Since Trump’s inauguration, MacMahon has effectively hollowed out much of the department, which provides federal education funding, administers financial aid for college students and upholds education-related civil rights laws.

About 43 percent of the department’s staff has been laid off or departed, and many of its functions have been delegated to other departments.

However, neither MacMahon or Trump can officially shut down the department. Doing so would require Congressional legislation, and the GOP-controlled Congress has not voted in favor of the move.

“Ultimately, Congress has to vote if we do close the Department of Education, and if I, in fact, am able to fire myself,” MacMahon told Fox News last week. “Hopefully, they will vote [for it], because I believe the outcome will be better for students, and that is the goal. That is certainly the president's goal.”

In a YouGov survey last year, 58 percent of respondents said they opposed dismantling the Education Department, while 29 percent said they favored it.

The department’s budget for fiscal year 2026 stood at about $80 billion — less than one-tenth of what the Defense Department was allocated.

A state judge is set to consider whether social media posts by New York’s attorney general should disqualify her from leading a criminal investigation into gang rape allegations at Cornell University.

Attorney General Letitia James was appointed special prosecutor to oversee a renewed criminal probe earlier this month after a former Cornell student filed a lawsuit alleging that she was raped by several men at a fraternity party. Jane Doe said in her lawsuit that some of the men pressured her into using ketamine and marijuana and that she drank alcohol that left her too intoxicated to consent before the alleged gang rape in October 2024.

Within days of James’ appointment, lawyers for two of men named as defendants in Jane Doe‘s lawsuit filed challenges, one that Judge James R. Farrell will hear on Thursday and the other in Tompkins County, where Cornell is located.

The men argue that James is biased because she expressed support for the accuser in social media posts days before she was appointed to the case.

“What this young woman has been forced to endure is horrific and unacceptable,” James posted to X and Facebook on Sept. 28 alongside a link to a news article about the lawsuit. “I’m holding her in my heart and in my prayers. To her, and to all survivors: you’re not alone. We stand with you.”

Gov. Kathy Hochul signed an executive order on Oct. 1 that took the case away from the local prosecutor, Tompkins County District Attorney Matthew Van Houten, who declined to bring charges in 2024, and named James as special prosecutor.

James, a Democrat, is not expected to attend Thursday’s court hearing in Monticello, about 72 miles (116 kilometers) northwest of Manhattan.

Andrew Miltenberg, a lawyer for the man whose case is being heard, wrote that James' social media post “has unequivocally demonstrated a bias and presumption of guilt against the defendants” named in Jane Doe’s lawsuit.

Miltenberg has requested that Farrell order Hochul to appoint a special prosecutor “who is free from actual or perceived conflicts of interest or bias.”

James' office said in a court filing that the challenge is “procedurally defective and substantively meritless,” and argued that courts have previously ruled that the governor is free to exercise her executive power as she chooses, including in appointing a special prosecutor.

“While Petitioner relies on the AG’s remarks in a single social media post as proof that the various ills he fears will come to pass, such speculative and conclusory claims of harm — premised entirely on his own mischaracterization of the AG’s statements — are insufficient for hardship,” Assistant Attorney General Kaitlin Vigars wrote.

James declined to comment on the social media posts at a news conference announcing her appointment. Hochul reiterated her support for James, saying she’d thoroughly investigate.

“I have every confidence in our attorney general to do what she has always done: Look at the facts, examine the record, draw the proper conclusions,” Hochul said. “That’s exactly what’s going to happen in this case.”

James is known for a number of high-profile cases, including against the National Rifle Association and President Donald Trump. James labeled Trump a “con man” and “carnival barker” and vowed to shine a “bright light into every dark corner of his real estate dealings.”

She also headed up a civil investigation that led to Gov. Andrew Cuomo’s resignation over sexual harassment allegations.

Federal prosecutors accused Soliman of attacking in June 2025 because the demonstrators were Jewish. The flames badly burned Karen Diamond, 82, and injured a dozen others.

Diamond died three weeks later. Her family says she suffered “indescribable pain.”

The charges Soliman previously pleaded not guilty to in federal court in Denver include nine counts of hate crimes, two of using fire or an explosive to commit a felony and one of carrying an explosive while committing a felony.

The charges are punishable by life in prison after federal prosecutors declined to seek the death penalty. Soliman is already serving a life sentence after pleading guilty to the state charges.

Boulder residents, particularly the Jewish community, described being unnerved by the attack in their city northwest of Denver that’s home to the University of Colorado.

He was granted work authorization, but it too expired, federal officials said

He brought 18 Molotov cocktails — bottles filled with flammable liquid that are lit, thrown and burst into flames when they shatter — to the demonstration in a box, but only two were thrown.

Of the 29 attack victims identified by state prosecutors, 13 were physically injured. Prosecutors considered the rest victims because they could have been hurt.

An injured dog led to a state animal cruelty charge.

While in court to plead guilty to the state charges in May, Soliman said he has known and respected Jewish people but questioned the deaths of innocent people in Israeli attacks on Gaza.

“Yes, I am against Israel and I can’t deny that. And that is my right,” Soliman said.

#872. Airbnb commits $500 million for emergency stays amid housing crisis criticism

Airbnb is committing $500 million to its philanthropic arm over the next five years to expand temporary housing assistance for medical travelers and disaster survivors.

The short-term rental platform will fund this initiative by contributing 10 cents from every booking and covering any remaining gap, according to Airbnb CEO Brian Chesky.

Airbnb.org, an independent nonprofit whose operational costs the company already covers, aims to scale up to provide 1 million nights of free lodging annually.

The pledge, announced Wednesday, comes amid an uncertain climate for corporate philanthropy.

A recent survey by Chief Executives for Corporate Purpose showed most companies reduced charitable contributions over the past five years, driven by changes in tax law.

Airbnb.org's updated funding framework has renewed questions about the charitable efforts of a business criticized by affordable housing advocates for reducing the availability of lower-cost residential homes.

Critics argue the broader housing crisis, exacerbated by short-term rentals, leaves fewer options for people displaced by disasters.

The dynamic highlights a debate over whether for-profit companies should use philanthropy to offset negative business impacts. Major artificial intelligence firms, currently drafting plans to distribute a portion of their wealth, maintain that they should.

For Chesky, the answer is an emphatic "no." He rejected claims that Airbnb drives up city housing costs, though he admitted some hosts take a small fraction of local housing off the market.

He described the commitment as doing the "right thing" with the platform's "natural superpower."

"I don’t like this idea that somehow you create a nonprofit to make up for or apologize for your core business," Chesky told The Associated Press. "Our core business, independently, is gonna affect a lot more lives than this organization. And our core business needs to be responsible. And if we’re doing something that’s not responsible, we need to correct it."

Governments including Spain and New York City have recently introduced measures to limit short-term rentals. The second major rail accident to hit Spain in 2026 is likely to renew scrutiny of the country’s railway safety systems, with Transport Minister Óscar Puente referring to an “error” and “inexplicable circumstances” that preceded the crash. The accident involved a Renfe AVE service and an Iryo train travelling from Madrid to Seville. The impact caused the rear coaches of the Iryo train to derail and plunge down an embankment near kilometre marker 118.

Mr. Puente said preliminary assessments suggested a possible technical failure at the Atocha control centre involving the LZB safety system, though he stressed that the exact cause remained under investigation.

According to the Minister, an automatic train-control system failed over a 69-kilometre stretch of track, resulting in services being halted. However, the control centre later authorised the resumption of traffic despite the disruption.

“The control centre decided to resume traffic in circumstances that, from our point of view, are inexplicable,” Mr. Puente said, adding that the Renfe train travelled around 25 kilometres before colliding with the stationary Iryo service.

He said the Renfe train had resumed operations about 15 minutes earlier and may have been travelling at speeds of up to 200 kmph at the time of the impact.

Following the collision, several coaches derailed and tumbled down a steep embankment. Debris and twisted metal were strewn across the crash site, and some carriages appeared to have suffered severe structural damage.

Authorities said initial findings pointed to a serious communication failure.

“It is clear that, in the control centre, the decision was taken to restart, and then they had more than enough time to verify the error, and they did not fix it,” Mr. Puente said.

He told reporters that the collision occurred after services resumed despite the failure of the automatic control system that had earlier brought trains on the affected stretch to a halt.

“The decision to resume traffic, while that system had not been restored, belongs to the control centre, and the control centre took the decision to resume traffic in circumstances that, from our point of view, are inexplicable,” he said.
