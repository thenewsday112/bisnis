MOSCOW, October 29. /TASS/. The West is working hard to sully the Russia-Africa partnership, TASS Director General Andrey Kondrashov said during a plenary discussion at the Russia-Africa media forum.

"Russia-Africa cooperation has become one of the main targets of information attacks by Western ill-wishers and their henchmen. Today, when Russia and African nations are working to build an equal partnership, any distortions are especially noticeable and come at a particularly high cost to both sides," he stressed.

Modern fake news is rarely based on crude fabrications, Kondrashov pointed out. As an example, he cited a deepfake video featuring Russian Foreign Ministry Spokeswoman Maria Zakharova. "We brought together 130 experts from 55 countries. This is an effective tool that will allow you to detect fake news almost automatically from the start," Kondrashov said, adding that media outlets could join the organization free of charge and that they did not have to pay membership fees.

The Russia-Africa media forum is taking place in Moscow on October 26-29, bringing together leading Russian and African media outlets. Media leaders are discussing the role of the countries of the Global Majority in the international media landscape, exploring the prospects for the development of media resources as tools to combat disinformation, and assessing ways to "decolonize" media content.

#736. Bird flu cases are spiking again in the US. Here is what you need to know about the spread

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

The nation’s biggest outbreak in Illinois has sickened close to 1 million chickens

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

H5N1 bird flu activity is spiking at poultry farms across the U.S., with 1.32 million birds affected over the past month, according to the latest federal data.

Illinois’s Clinton County has seen the biggest outbreak of highly contagious virus, with 934,600 chickens reported sick.

There are also large outbreaks in Minnesota, Wisconsin and North Dakota. A turkey farm in Minnesota’s Roseau County has seen 57,000 birds affected, another in North Dakota has had 60,600 birds fall ill and one in Wisconsin has seen 41,400 infections, the Department of Agriculture’s tracker shows.

Past outbreaks at egg-laying farms have resulted in major surge in egg prices for Americans after sick birds were killed. Bird flu has also led to the deaths of farm workers and people exposed to sick animals.

Smaller bird flu outbreaks have been reported in California, Montana, South Dakota, Utah, Ohio, Idaho, Wyoming, Kansas, Kentucky and Washington state since October 5. These outbreaks came from both backyard flocks and commercial farms.

But bird flu is spreading in wild birds, as well. There have been reports of bird flu in Canadian geese, mallards, an American white pelican, a turkey vulture and an American black duck this month. Jackie, the famous California bald eagle, died of bird flu in August, a recent necropsy found.

Birds aren’t the only animals that have been impacted, either. There’s reportedly been concern about bird flu spreading at a mink farm in Utah and Washington just confirmed its first-ever case of bird flu in cattle at two farms a couple of weeks ago.

One unidentified resident exposed to the cattle tested positive for the flu, but has not experienced severe illness and is isolating at home, KING 5 reported.

Between September 2025 and last month, at least 5,000 people have been monitored and more than 150 were tested for bird flu. There have been two deaths tied to bird flu and 72 cases since the outbreak began in 2024, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

Bird flu typically spreads through the infected saliva, and feces of infected birds or other animals.

Humans are infected when the virus enters the eyes, nose, mouth or is inhaled. Infections range in severity, but can lead to acute disease that results in death. Symptoms include pneumonia fever, cough, sore throat, runny nose, muscle or body aches, fatigue, trouble breathing and headaches.

Bird flu has yet to spread from human to human - but experts fear it’s only a matter of time.

Spreading cases in mink could help the virus mutate enough to make that transition. Mink have similar lungs and airways to people and past research has shown they are susceptible to spreading human viruses, including bird flu.

“As long as it continues to go mink to mink, then it becomes more and more likely that it could jump to a human,” Gail Hansen, a former Kansas state public health veterinarian and epidemiologist, told ProPublica last week.

The Department of Agriculture told The Independent its Animal & Plant Health Inspection Service does not have a disease control program for mink and that Congress has not appropriate funding for such a program.

“For HPAI, APHIS’ depopulation and indemnity authorities apply only to domestic poultry,” Tanya Espinosa, acting assistant director of Public Affairs, explained in a Thursday email.

“In this case, influenza investigations in mink are led by State animal health officials, with USDA providing confirmatory testing through the National Veterinary Services Laboratories (NVSL),” she said.

Regardless, the service encourages prompt reporting of suspected disease events, Espinosa added.

Another big fear? The return of sky-high egg prices. As animals, including chickens, are slaughtered to slow the spread of bird flu, supplies can be affected. Fewer chickens mean fewer eggs are produced. That hurts supply and can drive prices up.

That is what happened in 2024 when egg prices rose to $8-$10 per dozen. The cost became a major campaign issue with President Donald Trump promising to bring down prices. It also became a symbol of the rising food costs Americans have faced in post-Covid inflation.

Prices have dropped since their high and currently sit at $1.32 per dozen, the USDA says.

#798. Albania takes anti-riot crackdown cue from France

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Albania has imposed steep fines on people who take part in violent protests, citing a recent law imposed in France, in an attempt to quell weeks of clashes between police and protesters demanding the resignation of Prime Minister Edi Rama.

Under measures adopted Wednesday, remaining at a demonstration that turns violent will be a criminal offense, even for those not directly involved in clashes. Anyone who “chooses to remain in a crowd” engaged in violence or looting, or is found “concealing their identity,” faces fines of up to 500,000 leks ($6,130), over six-times the Balkan country’s average monthly salary.

Initially sparked by student anger over education costs and deteriorating facilities, the demonstrations have evolved into nationwide protests against inequality, soaring living costs, and cuts to public services.

Citing “urban anarchy” when announcing the crackdown, Rama acknowledged France’s controversial ‘casseurs-payeurs’ (rioters pay) bill, which would hold demonstrators liable for up to €10,000 ($11,160) in damages. The bill passed the French Senate on Wednesday and is now headed to the National Assembly, following weeks of protests and riots in which over 1,300 protesters were arrested in a single day.

Italy has also followed suit. Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni’s government approved measures last week that criminalize “violent participation in a demonstration,” punishable by up to five years in prison and fines of up to €20,000.

In Albania, a “special electronic register” will track suspected offenders, while police gain powers to restrict gatherings near schools, hospitals, and government buildings over potential violence. The measures – which also provide compensation for businesses damaged in the unrest – are already in place and require parliamentary approval within 45 days to remain in force.

Anti-government protests in the country began peacefully in May over plans for a luxury resort in the Zvernec nature reserve, a project linked to US President Donald Trump’s son-in-law, Jared Kushner.

By mid-October, the protests escalated into daily clashes with police amid growing anger over corruption, economic mismanagement, unemployment, and failing public services. Demands for Rama’s resignation took center stage, but the prime minister, who has been in power since 2013 and was elected to a fourth term last year, has refused to budge.

The unrest has caused over €2 million in damage, according to official estimates, as rioters torched ministries and attacked police stations, public buildings, and businesses. Clashes flared up again over the weekend, with protesters chanting “Revolution” and “Rama in prison” and police using tear gas, rubber bullets, and water cannons in Tirana.

Despite the protests being driven by grievances over domestic matters, Rama blamed the violence on a “hybrid war” by foreign actors, specifically Russia and Iran.

Russia has dismissed the accusations. Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova suggested last week that Rama should look closer to home. “These are citizens of your own country who took to the streets to protest. Don’t shift your responsibility onto others. Deal with your own domestic problems.”
