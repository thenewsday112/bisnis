#171. 'An annoying buzz': The search for stealthier drones

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Amazon Prime Air is expanding to 500 US towns and cities

Nature lover Ashley Schmidt no longer spends much time in her garden, in the Dallas suburb of North Garland.

She's also given up on cycling to the local parks where she previously enjoyed taking close-up photos of the bugs, lizards and toads.

It's all because she can't stand the sound of the delivery drones that fly overhead, depositing her neighbours' online orders.

"They emit this very annoying buzz. It triggers my fight or flight response," says the 44-year-old administrative assistant.

"You can hear the drones on approach and you can hear them inside your home when they pass directly overhead and then you hear them as they leave," she says.

Amazon says it is working to reduce the noise of its drone fleet

Schmidt is one of a growing number of people living with the noise of drones dispatched by companies including Amazon and Walmart as they seek to speed up home deliveries.

Delivery drone services, not long ago a novelty, are becoming part of everyday life for many people in the US.

Among them, Amazon Prime Air is in the process of expanding deliveries to nearly 500 cities and towns, delivering items that fit into a space the size of a shoe box. It has also launched to customers in and around Darlington in the north of England.

Smaller drone delivery services are popping up too: in some US cities, customers can order flying treats from their local pizza joint or ice cream parlour.

Even in cities where noise is already a steady part of life, the sound of drones seems to irk in a particular way.

While the steady passing of traffic might blend into the background, the sudden whine of a drone's rotors can pierce the everyday hum.

The sound tends to be "particularly noticeable, even when overall sound levels are relatively low," says Antonio Torija Martinez, professor in acoustic engineering and psychoacoustics at the University of Salford.

Unlike planes, drones operate at low altitudes and may fly directly over residential areas.

"They can expose communities that have not previously experienced significant aircraft noise, and who may therefore be more sensitive to changes in their local soundscape," says Torija Martinez.

He points to research showing that people respond strongly to a noise when they consider the source to be "novel, intrusive or frequent". People who benefit from a drone delivery service, for example, may be less bothered by the noise.

However, complaints about the sound of drones are likely to grow, he warns: "At the moment we are seeing some of these problems localised in some areas, butâ¦ as the drone sector expands, more and more of these complaints will happen."

In the US, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) regulates commercial drones, but it has come under fire for allowing noisy drone delivery operations. The FAA says operators and local community leaders have primary responsibility for addressing any community concerns.

Greenjets encloses some of the blades on its drone inside a pod

Might better technology muffle the irritating buzz?

One startup, Greenjets, set out to tackle both "noise level and the frequency of that noise," as Darren James, future technologies lead at the company, puts it.

The London-based business has designed a propulsion system it claims can reduce drone noise by at least two thirds.

While standard drones use spinning propellers that slice through open air, creating that high-pitched buzz, Greenjets encloses spinning blades inside a pod, to muffle the sound and direct the noise away from the ground.

Its system also quietens things down through slower rotor speed and by using materials that reduce vibration noise.

Making quieter drones isn't just about keeping the noise down for neighbours who are already sick of airborne deliveries.

Greenjets' low-noise tech has been used by police and harbour security teams in Germany. It has also helped conservationists in Africa, who are seeking to track poachers from above without being detected.

Battlefield drone operators also have good reason to keep the noise down, to avoid alerting the enemy of the approach of their unmanned vehicles.

"There are some efforts to reduce the sound of drones. The US Special Forces are interested, at an experimental stage," says Robert Tollast, a research fellow at Whitehall think tank The Royal United Services Institute (RUSI).

But he stresses that drone speed, rather than stealthiness, tends to come first for larger drones used in the war between Russia and Ukraine. "Make it faster, not less quiet, seems to be the approach," says Tollast.

Sharrow Engineering developed twisted propellers that reduce noise

For Detroit-based entrepreneur, Greg Sharrow, it was the challenge of broadcasting a classical music performance back in 2012 that set him on the path to quieter drone design.

He needed to film a violinist from a tricky angle and a drone seemed the only solution â except that it would be far too noisy.

As a starting point, "I knew that most of the noise from the drone was coming from the propeller, not the motor," he says. Specifically, it was the tips of the blades that he realised were creating the noise as they swiped through the air.

"I asked myself a very simple question, what would a tipless propeller look like and how would it function?"

The result was to create a propeller with twisted loop-shaped blades with no points.

It turned out it was easier to test his propeller in water than it was to repeatedly fly a drone, which led Sharrow to initially develop a less noisy boat propeller. Now, however, his company, Sharrow Engineering, is focusing on developing "ultra-quiet" drone rotors.

"We have validated that the technology works in drone applications and that it is dramatically quieter," says Sharrow.

Companies at the forefront of drone deliveries say they are also working to reduce the noise of their fleets. A spokesperson for Amazon says it has made "measurable sound improvements" to its drones and its latest designs use custom-designed propellers "that produce almost half the perceived sound of the prior generation, with a milder tone".

Drone maker Wing, which provides its vehicles for Walmart deliveries, says its propellers are designed to minimise noise. It also varies its flight paths to prevent repeated overflights over any single home or neighbourhood.

For Schmidt, fed up with the noise of drones, the only truly satisfactory solution would be to halt drone deliveries in her area. "I would like them to completely stop until they figure out how their services stop being a nuisance," she says.

#182. The Indian workers filming their work to train AI

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mohammed Ashim is paid to film his hands at work

Mohammed Ashim has been cutting hair and trimming beards for four years in Bengaluru.

But recently the 22-year old has been strapping a camera to his head to record his work.

The camera only records his hands and fingers - his client's faces are blurred out.

"The routine is simple, but you have to be disciplined about how you move. The operations team taught me that my hands always have to stay cleanly inside the frame, the lighting has to be sharp, and I can't tilt my head too abruptly while working."

He wears the camera for five or six hours a day.

"It's good money, but honestly, the feeling of doing something this futuristic is what keeps me excited. I never imagined my razor techniques would be useful to a global tech company," says Ashim.

He is one of hundreds of thousands of workers across the world paid to record their activities.

Ashim's footage is collected by a local company, which might have hundreds of people filming their work.

That video is then sold on to technology companies and research organisations who use it to train artificial intelligence - in particular so-called physical AI, a type of artificial intelligence that can control robots in the real world.

Rashdan Technologies collects and processes such human generated data.

"Every robotics company, every frontier AI lab, has a specific kind of data they want: industrial tasks, agriculture, household chores, small business work like pottery, garment-making, embroidery, candle-making. We go wherever the task is," says co-founder and CEO Shahil Baig.

His company pays $5 to $10 (Â£3.74; Â£7.47) for eight to 10 hours of recorded work.

"For a lot of these people, that's meaningful," Baig says.

Making sure workers give their consent and are comfortable with the process is important, he says.

"We record hands, not faces, and where multi-camera rigs do capture more of the body or environment, we blur faces out. No names, no contact details, nothing that identifies them personally goes into what we hand off."

But his customers are getting more demanding.

"Now clients are asking for four to six cameras rigged around a person's head to cover the entire scene, not just the hands.

"Getting someone to agree to one small camera is one conversation. Getting them to agree to wearing what's effectively a five-camera headset is a completely different, and harder, one. We pay more for that tier of data collection because of what we're asking of people."

And the industry is becoming more crowded, Agarwal expects dozens more competitors to emerge in India this year.

Shahil Baig expects dozens more firms to start collecting video of workers

If you want robots to navigate the real world, they can't just be trained inside computer simulations, argues Manish Agarwal, the founder of Humyn Labs, which collects and processes human-generated data.

Most of that data comes from video, but sometimes collectors wear hardware on their hands and fingers to capture physical contact.

"Physical tasks require high-quality signals involving limb movements, fingers, wrists, elbows, shoulders and dynamic force application. For example: applying the correct grip force so a plastic bottle doesn't crumble versus a steel glass," he says.

And until recently, that data was not available.

"There is no existing internet archive of embodied human activity - no dataset of a kitchen being cleaned, or a car being assembled by hands that know exactly how much force to apply. That data has to be captured from scratch, in the real world, across diverse environments," says Agarwal, whose company has bases in Bangalore and San Francisco.

At the moment his firm has 96,000 data collectors across countries in Latin America and Southeast Asia.

Humyn Labs filters the thousands of hours of video supplied by those collectors, selecting the most useful content. It's then labelled so the AI knows what it is looking at and packaged in a format that can be used by clients.

"You need to source across a variety of factories, a variety of tasks, in different geographies because if you want to build deployment for US factories or US houses, you'd want environments that are a proxy for that. Residential houses in India may not be the right context for building a robotics use case for the US."

The factory workers teaching robots how to work like humans

But are workers collecting data for machines that will one day do their jobs?

At the moment it's too early to tell says Bino Paul, senior professor at the School of Management and Labour Studies at the Tata Institute of Social Sciences (TISS) in Mumbai.

For some workers AI might be a useful tool, but robots could automate other jobs, he says.

The problem is that, if machines do start to take over, there will be little workers can do about.

"By the time workers realise what they have participated in, things may have gone too far," he says.

Like many others, he wants governments to regulate AI.

"AI cannot be viewed merely as a technical system. We need to develop a socio-technical system in which ethics is upheld through human agency, ensuring that AI delivers beneficence rather than maleficence."

Despite concerns over where AI is heading, money is still being poured into its development.

Some of that trickles down to workers in India, where there is no shortage of workers prepared to wear a camera for a few dollars a day.

Mohammed Arman has been working in the textiles industry in Bangalore for 10 years.

"Garments stitching and cutting. I know this work so well I could probably do it with my eyes closed. Cloth, thread, needle, machine after 10 years, your hands just know what to do before your mind even tells them," he says.

He has recently started wearing a head-mounted camera that records his work.

"For me, it's simple. This is survival. I have a family back home in Bihar, and every rupee I send back matters. If someone offers me extra money to wear a camera while I do the exact same work I've been doing for 10 years, I'm not going to say no.

"It doesn't cost me anything. I'm already doing the stitching, already doing the cutting. Now I just get paid a little more for it," he says.

Mohammed Arman has been filming his tailoring work

While there is demand for such footage it will be collected somewhere.

"India's current role in this industry is largely as a supplier of raw material - people strap on cameras, record themselves doing ordinary tasks, and that footage gets shipped off to labs abroad," Agarwal says.

But he argues the value of that kind of work will quickly become eroded, as more companies compete for collection contracts.

"India can't afford to stay a footage supplier. It needs to move up the value chain: from recording raw video to owning the pipeline that turns that video into something usable - cleaning it, structuring it, validating it, and ultimately setting the standards for what counts as high-quality training data," he says.

Jolly Arifur has been filming domestic work for a few hours a day

For some people collecting data is attractive part time work.

Science teacher Jolly Arifur was looking for work she could do from home that would fit around her young son.

"For the past seven months, I strapped a rig-mounted camera on my head while doing my routine domestic work scrubbing, cooking, sweeping tile floors, and folding laundry."

Arifur, who lives in Guntakal, Anantapur, fits the recording work around her teaching.

"I get paid two to three dollars per hour. If I record three to five hours a day, that's a good amount in hand it's become an extra source of income for me, on top of teaching. I'm managing both honestly, fantastically."

#192. The grads ditching the UK for a fresh start abroad

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Charlie West, left, Rachel James and Owein Iveson have all moved overseas

Last year Rachel James quit her corporate job in London, packed a suitcase and booked a one-way ticket to Sydney, without a job lined up or a plan in place.

"I went for a fresh start," says the 26-year-old from Surrey. "To take a risk and try something completely different. With the worst case being there will always be a flight home if all fails."

More than one in 10 UK university students are now planning to move abroad for better opportunities after they have graduated, up from 7.8% two years ago, according to a survey, external of 15,000 final year university students.

Meanwhile, a study by the Institute of Student Employers (ISE) says that competition for graduate jobs in the UK was at a three-decade high.

UK employers now receive 140 applications per graduate vacancy on average, an increase of 14% from 2025, and up from just 30 applications per vacancy 20 years ago, according to the ISE's report, external.

For young British adults going abroad, where are they heading?

While there is no age-specific data for which countries people go to, Australia remains by far the most popular nation for British adults overall who move abroad, official figures show., external

In addition to Australia's weather and use of the English language, another reason is its visa-friendly policy. Under its Working Holiday Maker programme,, external British passport holders between the ages of 18 and 35 can live in Australia for up to three years.

Rachel James says that Sydney is a nicer place to live and work than London

Rachel, who graduated from Loughborough University in 2022 with a degree in English, had been working for accountancy giant PricewaterhouseCoopers in the UK. She now has a job in marketing in Sydney for gym chain Fitness First.

She says her annual salary has gone down from Â£42,000 to Â£37,000, while she now pays $AUS 2,000 (Â£1,000) a month to rent a two-bedroom apartment with a friend, similar to what she was paying in London.

Despite having less disposable income, she has no regrets. "The weather's beautiful, even in the winter, and the walks on my doorstep are unmatched.

"People want to leave work at 5pm so they can go and do their hobbies - whether it's surfing, running, enjoying the sunset, a picnic or sunset yoga. I didn't come with career progression as a focus - it's more about the different way of life."

Around two-thirds of British nationals who emigrate are aged between 16 and 34, according to the most recent figures, external from the UK's Office for National Statistics.

It said that 75,000 more people in that age bracket left the UK in 2025 than arrived.

After Australia the next most popular destination for British adults is the US.

Charlie West says that working in the US has given him better pay and more opportunities

Charlie West, 25, from Cheshire, moved to the States in 2024 for better pay and opportunities in academia after he completed a masters in medieval studies at Oxford University.

West is now two years in to a six-year funded PhD course at Yale's Department of Italian Studies, which includes him teaching.

"I really wanted to do a PhD but it's difficult to get funding in the UK, particularly in the humanities, and funded stipends are about Â£21,000. I'd have had to work [outside academia] at the same time to afford to live.

"In the US I'm paid $51,000 a year (Â£37,770), about to rise to $52,000, and it comes with a lot more opportunities to teach and travel to conferences."

The European Union also remains popular with young Britons looking to move abroad, although the visa requirements since Brexit have made it more difficult.

One way to get into the European jobs market is to have studied in the EU. International graduates can apply for a permanent residence permit after their degree ends.

Owein Iveson has no plans to return to the UK from Finland

This is what Owein Iveson, 24, from Sunderland, is doing as he is about to graduate from Finland's Aalto University with a masters in design.

Owein says he "fell in love with the country" after visiting for the first time in 2020.

"Finland's a beautiful country," he says. "The streets are very clean. It's a very quiet lifestyle. The nature is fantastic and the public transport works really efficiently."

Owein lives in Espoo, a city about 15 minutes on the metro from Helsinki, and doesn't expect to return to the UK anytime soon.

"To be honest the jobs market is not great in Finland, but there is high demand for IT workers, and if you can work across the EU there are many more opportunities."

Immigration lawyer John Kiely, a London-based partner at international law firm Howard Kennedy, says it is easy to see why more UK graduates are looking overseas.

"When you combine student debt, high housing costs, relatively weak wage growth and uncertainty about future employment, the willingness to roll the dice somewhere else becomes quite rational."

Back in Sydney, Rachel says the city simply offers a better quality of life than London.

"I don't see life in Sydney as any more expensive than if I was in London, but personally I have found the perks in Sydney better."

#203. The tech helping crack decades-old cold cases

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Crime scene evidence is often not enough to identify suspects

Randy Gail Sperino was last seen alive on the evening of 9 November 1993, as she was getting into a pickup truck in Granite City, Illinois.

The 34-year-old's body was found hours later in a field nearby, the victim of blunt force trauma.

The identity of her killer was a mystery. DNA evidence found at the crime scene was tested against standard law enforcement databases, but no matches were found.

For three decades, investigators from the Madison County Sheriff's Office chased leads and interviewed hundreds of people as they attempted to identify the perpetrator.

The case looked like it would remain one of more than 250,000 unsolved homicides in the US.

Then, last year, detectives tried a fresh approach. They sent genetic evidence to Othram, a Houston-based company that uses DNA technology to help identify the perpetrators in cold and current crime cases.

The Madison County detectives had been inspired by investigative genetic genealogy techniques used to identify the Golden State Killer, Joseph James DeAngelo, back in 2018. In that case, an investigative team constructed a family tree to help identify the killer.

They were able to do so thanks to DNA data that relatives had provided to commercial databases, similar to consumer ancestry platforms.

Unlike traditional forensic DNA testing, which generally relies on a direct comparison between evidence and a known person, investigative genetic genealogy can identify distant genetic relatives of an unknown individual.

Othram set about applying this technique to track down Sperino's killer. While the suspect's own DNA profile wasn't available, profiles submitted by his extended relatives allowed the company to identify a male family member.

"That lead broke the case open," said Captain Tim Lawrence of Madison County Sheriff's Office, which was responsible for investigating the crime.

It was a crucial clue that led the detectives to Albert "Buddy" Zigler, 70, who police say then confessed to the murder. He was charged in May this year.

Wes Sperino, Gail's son, said at a press conference: "I finally get closure and I finally know who did this."

Albert Zigler was charged with murder in May

The technique that led detectives to Zigler is credited with revolutionising how forensic cases are solved.

It has been highly successful in helping solve cases involving unidentified human remains and identifying suspects in longstanding investigations.

Othram claims it has helped solve thousands of cases. The company's chief executive David Mittelman likens investigative genetic genealogy to a GPS system: "The DNA profile is the GPS signal and it says 'you belong here.' But to understand where 'here' is, you need a map."

Public records, obituaries, and census data build that map so Othram's scientists can establish how different family trees connect.

However, Mittelman is quick to point out that DNA technology can only go so far before "a cascade of investigative steps" are needed.

An Othram scientist prepares a bone for DNA extraction

DNA science isn't the only technology that is helping detectives solve cold cases.

One of the challenges facing detectives on major investigations like the hunt for Sperino's attacker is the sheer amount of evidence they dig up.

Officers investigating her murder realised AI could help them wade through the mountains of evidence they had accumulated since 1993.

They used a platform called Centerline AI, from Florida software company CentralSquare, to analyse more than 2,000 pages of records and identify evidence for the DNA testing that helped to break the case.

The AI helped them reconstruct timelines and organise witness information. "It's so much easier than having a detective have to read through 2,000 pages," Lawrence says. His team could interact with the AI like a chat bot.

This also helped when it came to interviewing Zigler after they had tracked him down. Detectives were able to ask questions of the AI to help "figure out if Zigler was being truthful", says Lawrence.

But the technology is "only as good as the investigator," says the captain. There comes a point, he says, "where you leave the technology behind and you have to go track people down and talk to them."

Investigative genetic genealogy has its limitations too. Mittelman says: "It can't solve every case. If you have no DNA, you can't really use this technique."

"But this technique is a lot more powerful and a lot more likely to yield identities than having to rely on the chance that you find a match somewhere in a database," he adds.

While firms like Othram only use ancestry platforms where users have provided consent for law enforcement matching, there remain some concerns about the ethical implications of using this data.

"There are significant privacy concerns," says Denise Syndercombe Court, professor of forensic genetics at King's College London. While one person may have opted in to their DNA being used, genetic relatives may not have given consent.

A lack of funding is another hurdle. Mittelman says: "Every law enforcement agency wants to do it. There's just not a lot of funding for it."

Sometimes cases are funded through donations or crowdfunding. Othram operates a website listing cases that members of the public can help fund.

While investigative genetic genealogy has been adopted in countries such as Sweden, Denmark and Australia, it is not yet used in the UK. The Home Office is conducting a study to consider whether it should be adopted in the UK but did not respond to a request for comment.

The UK is already in a strong position, says Syndercombe Court. "We have such an efficient DNA database that if you can get any DNA out of a crime scene, you've got over a 65% chance of putting a name to that person, which is enormously powerful," she says.

#213. Living with a stink? Technology might help

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The operators of Newby Island landfill have been ordered to take action over odours

#262. Rainmakers: The drones used to seed clouds

Rainmaker's drones were used over Alaska this year

On 23 August, Cooper Freeman had no idea clouds above his head were being filled with a silver compound in order to cause rainfall.

Neither did thousands of other residents in Homer, Alaska, who later discovered a start-up called Rainmaker conducted an experiment in that region using cloud-seeding technology.

The company, based in El Segundo California, claims it produced 19 million US gallons of water in the sky in three hours.

This approach to weather modification, now more than 80-years-old, involves using planes and drones to disperse compounds such as silver iodide into clouds to form ice crystals. Those crystals eventually become large enough to fall as rain or snow, depending on the temperatures below.

Essentially, cloud seeding speeds up the natural process of water vapor condensing inside of clouds. Gravity then pulls those crystals to the ground. The technology, though, needs ideal climate environments, such as mountainous or cold regions such as Alaska.

Freeman, the Alaska director of the Center for Biological Diversity, isn't just concerned Homer residents weren't widely alerted to these experiments. He's sceptical about what was shot into the clouds, and questions how safe it was for both residents and their habitat.

"It doesn't appear that there was any downstream monitoring to verify that this silver iodide didn't impact the environment," he says.

Freeman adds, "Having a company come in to do this seeding isn't going to solve our water woes, and it feels like a distraction from solving the urgent issues related to water conservation."

Concerns are mounting about the challenges surrounding water security. United Nations Secretary-General AntÃ³nio Guterres said in July "our world is using freshwater faster than it can be replenished, external", which echoes reports noting how the past five years have led to the driest period for global rivers, external in more than three decades.

Cloud-seeding technology may be a drop in the bucket, as it doesn't create rainstorms. For example, Rainmaker's 19 million gallons equates to around 0.01 inch of rain across 100 square miles.

Nevertheless, more than 50 countries, external are developing programmes to modify the weather and a surge of start-ups are developing systems to create rain.

As well developed and promising as this innovative field has become, these businesses continue to face thorny questions on safety and efficacy, leading to novel approaches with both finding the right material to spray into clouds and how they do it.

Augustus Doricko, the founder and CEO of Rainmaker, is quick to answer any questions on the consequences of how his company uses the silver iodide compound. It's clear he's been asked about chemical safety many times.

He says Rainmaker releases this form of silver at 20 grams per flight, distributed across thousands of square kilometres. "The resultant increase in concentration on the ground is in the parts per quadrillion, way below the threshold laid out by the FDA [Food and Drug Administration] on what is allowable."

Rainmaker's flights use drones to soar into clouds because without clouds, "we can't do anything," he says, pointing to one of the key limits of cloud-seeding processes. Cloud-seeding sessions have to wait for clouds to form to try to coax them into precipitation.

Their drones are outfitted with silver iodide flares, and when lit the burning atomizes the water particles into a small enough size so they can stay suspended in the freezing cloud for hours, eventually turning into crystals and later rain.

Rainmaker operates in states such as Idaho, Utah, Colorado and areas of the Middle East such as Jordan. Doricko says, "Jordan residents often only have water for hours per week from their utility because of how scarce water is, and it's a privilege to try to help this storied region."

Cloud seeding's roots began in 1946 when researchers from the General Electric Research Laboratory used dry ice as the first seeding agent. A year later, silver iodide was found to be more effective than dry ice, which quickly turns into useless gas.

While seeding agents caused more rainfall, they didn't move the needle much. In fact, sobering statistics point to how patchy this technology can be in what it pours on the ground.

"With the right conditions, cloud seeding squeeze out about an extra five to 10% of precipitation," says Jon Meyer, an assistant state climatologist with the Utah Climate Center.

What was encouraging, though, for the cloud-seeding sector was a landmark 2017 study that found how silver iodide worked as expected, one of the first reports to observe cloud seeding in action using radar and precipitation gauges.

In the past several years, Meyer has been encouraged to see more companies such as Rainmaker bring innovation to an old industry. "Drones [such as Rainmaker's products] present this new avenue," says Meyer, "and they offer a more targeted nature to these clouds we hope will be responsive."

Olivia Li is developing an alternative to silver iodide for cloud seeding

What is sprayed into clouds may also be undergoing a major shift. Recast Systems in San Francisco is experimenting with a type of protein made from amino acids found in the soil, says CEO Olivia Li.

While still undergoing tests at Texas A&M University, this new seeding agent moves Recast away from silver iodide as the go-to compound.

"It's safe, biodegradable and nucleates ice at a higher efficiency than silver iodide," says Li, whose company currently uses silver iodide to seed clouds in several US states.

Meyer is optimistic this technology's trajectory will only continue to rise. "We're going to see more demand for these services as so many regions are experiencing water demand," he says, "and they're going to be looking for ways to help bridge that gap."

The street used to be packed with shoppers from across the north-east of Scotland.

"I come to Union Street to take a bus somewhere else... there's nothing to shop for nowâ¦ it's horrendous," complains Marie Sim, a lifelong Aberdeen resident.

It's a similar story across the country: High Streets, once bursting with shops, hit first by the rise of out-of-town retail centres and then by online commerce.

But Aberdeen is in the thick of an ambitious turnaround plan. The port city has essentially declared war on its empty shop problem, taking an unusually hands-on approach to filling vacant units and bringing long-neglected upper floors of buildings into use.

The Marks & Spencer flagship store in Aberdeen closed in August, 80 years after it opened there

On top of that, the council is pouring tens of millions into trying to make the city centre a destination again. Like many other places, it's leaning into the "experience economy", with a new indoor food and drink market and a major redevelopment of the seafront.

High Streets are integral to how we feel about where we live. The sight of vacant shops and graffiti can quickly become a vicious circle, deterring shoppers, discouraging investment and leading to anti-social behaviour.

Politicians of all stripes have been trying for years to breathe new life into town centres - and Andy Burnham, who has made a focus on "place" part of his political identity, is the latest. He argues they should become "symbols of Britain's renaissance".

But can it be achieved? And if they're to survive, do town centres need a fundamental rethink?

One in eight properties on British High Streets are currently vacant, according to Green Street, the property and retail analytics company, a number that's stayed stubbornly high for years.

"If there was a silver bullet, it would have been fired years ago," says Jackie Sadek, a former government adviser on urban regeneration, who has spent nearly four decades working in town centre renewal.

One of the biggest obstacles is fractured ownership. Shops and buildings are often owned by dozens of different landlords, sometimes through offshore companies or complex structures, each with their own interests and priorities. That can slow decision-making and make large-scale regeneration difficult.

But even with empty units that are available, you need to find someone willing to take them on.

One in eight properties on British High Streets are currently vacant

The cost of running a High Street business has also risen; employment costs are higher, and there's the burden of business rates.

#327. Aviation faces hotter, stormier skies – and passengers might have to accept more disruption

📅 Sep 05, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Aviation faces hotter, stormier skies â and passengers might have to accept more disruption

As the country sweltered through one of the many heatwaves of a baking summer in late June, towering thunderclouds were gathering over the south of England and across much of northern Europe. It was a Saturday, and with the holiday season getting under way, thousands of passengers were heading to London's Heathrow and Gatwick airports to catch flights abroad. It was always going to be a very busy day.

What followed, however, was chaos for travellers. As air traffic controllers struggled to route aircraft around the storms, airspace became heavily congested and many planes due to fly were simply forced to wait on the ground. In the end, some 900 flights to and from both airports were delayed, some by up to 11 hours. Dozens more were cancelled. At airports across Europe, passengers expecting to fly into London also faced long waits and uncertainty.

The weather in the UK may now have cooled, and passengers may soon forget their experiences. But some within the industry believe what happened in June should be seen as a warning.

The timing in this case was clearly unlucky. But there are concerns that such incidents are only going to become more common as the effects of climate change mount up around the world and weather systems are likely to become increasingly unstable, making disruption more likely and adding to costs for airlines, airports and travellers.

Tim Atkinson, an airline risk consultant and former pilot, says the very crowded skies over the UK and northern Europe mean the region is particularly exposed.

He thinks there is a real risk, for example, that all of London's airports could be affected by storms at the same time, potentially with major consequences in the air and on the ground.

"What we're seeing now is very rapid climate change," he says.

"We're going from the kind of weather activity which takes out an airport, and which the system kind of copes withâ¦ to a situation where, with very little predictability, it would be possible to lose all of the London airports from real-time operation quite swiftly and with little notice".

Such a scenario, he thinks, would make the disruption for passengers caused by the incident in June seem relatively minor â and could even put lives at risk. But how likely is it?

For controllers at National Air Traffic Services's (NATS) main control centre in Swanwick, Hampshire, thunderstorms are a major preoccupation. When I visit, it is a hot and humid day, there are ominous dark clouds in the sky, and heavy, thundery downpours are hitting the surrounding region.

In Terminal Control, the part of the building responsible for flights heading to or from airports in London and southern England, those same storm cells are being monitored continuously. Their progress is tracked on large radar screens, while at the same time weather forecasts are carefully scrutinised.

Air traffic tends to come in waves during the day. Right now, at lunchtime, it is relatively quiet and the storms have yet to affect major routes. But with activity expected to intensify in the afternoon, staff are already preparing for what could be a tricky few hours.

Stormy weather can shrink the available airspace, meaning traffic has to be restricted and rerouted. If planes are unable to land at one airport, for example due to high winds, they may need diverting to another. But that can create its own problems; if too many unscheduled aircraft land at an already busy airport, it might not have sufficient infrastructure to accommodate them all.

Heavy rain and storms can make flying harder

Planes need fuel and somewhere to park. Passengers need food and facilities. Ultimately, if there isn't space, it can simply refuse to accept all but emergency traffic.

This is where the scenario set out by Atkinson comes in. He warns of a situation where traffic is diverted from Heathrow, Gatwick, Luton and Stansted at the same time, leaving aircraft coming in across the Atlantic and a large number of European flights all looking for somewhere to land at the same time, and some with dwindling fuel reserves.

This would at the very least create serious disruption for passengers, who might end up hundreds of miles from their destinations. But it could also, he thinks, create "a very serious risk event, such as an aircraft running out of fuel in the sky".

Aircraft are legally required to carry enough fuel to take them to an alternative airport if necessary, and to allow them to fly for an extra 30 minutes on top of that.

Not everyone shares Atkinson's concern. Former airline executive Andrew Charlton, who manages the Swiss-based consultancy Aviation Advocacy, thinks the risk is low.

"You've always got to imagine the worst-case scenario", he says. "But I also think we need to keep that worst case in context.

"Yes, it's possible to imagine it. But I'm also fairly comfortable with the ability of this industry to react well and make good decisionsâ¦ of all the things that wake me up at 3amâ¦this is not the top of that list."

Thunderstorms are caused by what is known as convective activity, where moist air close to the ground warms rapidly and rises quickly. As it rises, it expands and cools, allowing the moisture it carries to condense, forming large clouds. More heat means more convective energy, potentially creating instability. In addition, for every degree the Earth warms, the atmosphere can hold about 7% more moisture, increasing the probability of heavy rainfall.

It is not clear whether this means we will actually get more thunderstorms in the UK and across Europe, as so many variables are involved beyond the simple matter of energy and moisture. Some parts of the world may even get fewer. However, studies have shown that higher latitudes, particularly in the northern hemisphere, will see the biggest increases in favourable conditions for storms to form, and those that do may be more violent. According to the UK's Met Office, "while we may not see more thunderstorms overall, the ones we do experience could be more impactful".

Where possible, pilots will avoid flying through convective weather. Even though aircraft are designed to cope with severe conditions, storms can produce intense turbulence, capable of damaging the structural frame of the plane, as well as throwing people around the cabin if they are not strapped in. Hail can also be a big problem, potentially cracking windscreens or causing engine problems. So, however much inconvenience it may cause passengers, crews will invariably put safety first.

New technology helps pilots navigate tricky flying conditions

Onboard weather radars can help aircrew to see up to 320 nautical miles ahead. They also have access to live weather forecasting resources online. But thunderstorms can move quickly and, according to Prof Guy Gratton, an expert in aeronautics at Cranfield University, these systems are essentially "tactical rather than strategic". In other words, although they enable pilots to see storm cells on their screens from a relatively short distance away, and request a diversion, they do not allow for detailed long-distance planning.

The result is that unplanned diversions often happen late in flight, which is likely to mean burning more fuel. If storm delays do become more common, says Andrew Charlton, then aircraft will have to load more fuel as a matter of course, in case there is trouble brewing ahead. "Aircraft are going to have to fly further, and they're going to have to factor in the assumption they're going to fly further," he says. For an airline, fuel is one of its major costs â and if the fuel bill goes up, he says, it is inevitable passengers will pay more.

More intense storms would be likely to make air traffic management in busy regions harder and potentially lead to more delays and cancellations. However, it is possible some of this could be mitigated using new technologies.

Both Heathrow and Gatwick, for example, have moved away from mandating minimum distances between incoming aircraft in favour of a system which continually calculates spacing times based on wind speeds and aircraft types. In practice, this is meant to allow controllers to reduce the distance between landing aircraft, particularly in high winds, and get them on the ground more quickly. According to NATS, it has successfully improved landing rates and reduced delays by a significant margin.

However, there could be other effects from more severe storms as well. Heavier rainfall, for instance, increases the likelihood of flooding. In a worst case, this can paralyse airport operations, as happened in April 2024 when torrential downpours hit Dubai International Airport. It briefly had to close its runways, while disruption lasted for two days. More than 1,200 flights were cancelled, affecting tens of thousands of passengers.

Passengers were delayed during the floods in Dubai

But even if runways and taxiways themselves are clear, and aircraft are able to fly without any difficulty, passengers may struggle to catch their flights if roads, railways or car parks are flooded.

Protecting against such risks can be expensive. The problem of clear-air turbulence is also a real one. It cannot be seen, and is becoming increasingly common as the climate warms.

This is when air moves violently at high altitudes with no clouds or other visual cues to help a pilot avoid it. For passengers, it usually only means a little bit of bumpiness, but occasionally it can be a lot more serious. On 1 March 2023, for example, a Lufthansa flight from Austin, Texas to Frankfurt encountered what the airline described as "brief but severe" turbulence.

Those aboard described how passengers and flight crew were thrown around the cabin as the plane suddenly lost altitude. After it made an emergency landing in Washington DC, seven people were taken to hospital.

While still rare, this kind of turbulence is already much more prevalent than it used to be and is likely to become even more so.

"You know the projections are pretty dire: doubling, trebling or worse in the amount of severe turbulence on many busy flight routes around the world," says Paul Williams, a professor of atmospheric science at the University of Reading. Nevertheless, he remains confident solutions can be found, including improvements to forecasting and making use of laser-based technology.

There is another climate-related threat to aviation, which may prove challenging to overcome: rising sea levels. When Hurricane Sandy arrived off the US East Coast in October 2012, it provoked a storm surge that flooded the city. The two runways at New York's LaGuardia airport were left under water for several days, preventing it from reopening even after the winds had subsided.

Significant flooding that would have affected the New York City area once in every 500 years in pre-industrial times is now experienced every 25 years or so â and could occur every five years by the middle of the century, according to one major study. It is not alone. With some 40% of the world's population living within 100km of coastlines, many airports have been built on low-lying areas near the sea. According to research by Newcastle University, 269 airports around the world are already at risk of flooding due to rising sea levels, and that total is expected to rise. Among those potentially most exposed are the likes of Amsterdam Schiphol, London City, Bahrain International and Newark Liberty International.

Dr Yasir Ali, a reader in Transport Modelling at Loughborough University, says e-scooters are "complementary" to other modes of transport, rather than replacing them. Due to factors such as range and being exposed to the elements, they are less suitable for longer trips, rural areas with less infrastructure or in bad weather. They work best as "a first- and last-mile solution" in urban areas, to reduce reliance on cars for short trips.

Shared mobility charity CoMoUK's 2025 report suggests that for more than half of the people using rental e-scooters, the most common use was travelling to work or school. Its analysis of DfT data suggests the majority of people using hired e-scooters are male and under 35.

"Sometimes it's a bit difficult to take the bus from dot to dot", says Yan Zhang, who works at Southampton university and doesn't drive, "so for shorter distances, I'll take the scooter."

Like Yan Zhang, e-scooter users are less likely to have a car, CoMo's report says - this is partly explained by official trials' concentration in urban areas

Around half of respondents to CoMoUK's online survey used it in conjunction with another mode of transport - such as riding to a railway station, like Keith.

Earlier evidence from a national evaluation of the trials published in 2022 found that although some users would have driven instead had they not had access to an e-scooter, the biggest "modal shift" - meaning switch in ways of getting around - to e-scooters was from walking. Given walking is an active mode of travel, this has raised questions over how significant the public health impact of e-scooter use really is.

And the usage of privately owned e-scooters isn't reflected in this research. The DfT has said between 760,000 and 1.2 million people in England aged 16 and over own one - despite the fact that it remains illegal to ride them in public.

In the eyes of the law, e-scooters are classed as motor vehicles. Outside of the trials, they can't be insured, and thus can only legally be ridden on private land, with the owner's permission.

Parking bays for e-scooters and e-bikes are available across Southampton

The trials themselves are bound by strict rules. The devices' top speed is limited. Businesses who operate the pilots must insure them. Users must have a full or provisional driving licence, and they must not ride on the pavement. Helmets are recommended but not mandatory.

Richard Dilks, CEO of CoMoUK says "mass-scale use of something that is inherently low-carbon, low-impact, low-cost" is a "good thing" but "we also don't have rules of engagement about how they should be ridden".

And as a result of the delays in introducing regulation, Loughborogh University's Yasir Ali says "we are increasingly in a situation where the law and reality have diverged", with "the disadvantages of e-scooter use without securing the full benefits of regulation".

Back in Southampton, I meet a lady pushing a black e-scooter, who doesn't want to give her name. She says nobody told her when she bought it that it was illegal to ride on the streets.

The Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency has a unit that carries out surveillance of retailers to check they're complying with the law. But earlier this month, an investigation by the Press Association news agency discovered some retailers were advertising them for commuting purposes.

Police tell me they regularly see children illegally riding private e-scooters. Margaret Winchcomb, deputy executive director at the Parliamentary Advisory Council for Transport Safety (PACTS), says some are being specifically marketed at kids.

Police say they will seize privately owned e-scooters that are ridden illegally

So what can police do? Forces regularly launch crackdowns where they seize illegally used devices, and often crush them. Each year, awareness campaigns run urging parents not to buy their kids an e-scooter for Christmas. But enforcement is a challenge.

Insp Matt Boiles from Avon and Somerset's road policing team says problems arise even with the legal trial devices - for example, people riding them after drinking alcohol, which counts as drink-driving. Riding with another person on the back is another common issue.

But officers see private e-scooters being illegally ridden in public places "all the time, every single day" and they can be really hard to catch - it isn't always safe for police in a car to pursue someone on an e-scooter, he says.

Insp Boiles despairs over parents buying them for children: "We do have parents attend a police station quite a lot, getting very, very upset that their child's Â£500 e-scooter has been seized." But "the legislation is really, really clear that you can't have it back".

There's a potential risk of fire if unregulated devices with lithium batteries are left to charge unattended. Most train operators have banned e-scooters on board for this reason.

But there's also the fact that riders are unprotected in the case of falling off or being hit by motorised vehicles. And - along with e-bikes - they can end up involved in collisions where pedestrians are hurt. Many trial e-scooters in the UK are restricted to 12.5mph, but privately owned scooters' speeds aren't.

Some coroners have even raised concerns. In 2022, a coroner in London called for action after the death of 14-year-old Fatima Abukah on a privately owned scooter.

According to the DfT, in 2024 - the most recent year for which figures are available - 1,390 casualties were recorded in collisions that involved e-scooter in England, Scotland and Wales, three more than in the previous 12 months. The vast majority, 1,096, were e-scooter users themselves.

Young boys and men appear to be over-represented in the stats. Of the casualties, 302 - the most of any age and sex category - were males aged 10-19.

Six deaths were recorded in 2024, unchanged from the previous year. Five of these were riders and one a pedestrian.

Although the numbers aren't conclusive, it is thought more accidents are happening on privately owned e-scooters. Winchcomb says police statistics "aren't reflective of the number of injuries".

Nonetheless, campaigners believe there is enough evidence to show that regulation is urgently needed.

Carly Calland's 14-year-old son Jacob died in March 2025 of a catastrophic head injury. He was a passenger on an e-scooter that was involved with a collision with a car.

If privately owned e-scooters are legalised for public use, Carly believes, there should be mandatory helmets, a ban on carrying passengers and penalties for parents that allow children to ride illegally.

"If Jacob was wearing a helmet that day, he would still be here," Carly, from Wythenshawe in Greater Manchester, says.

Carly is not against e-scooters. They "are really good for people to get to work, and they are eco-friendly", she tells me, but "they just need to be used in the correct way."

What happens if the current situation goes on? Her answer is emphatic: "More deaths."

Meanwhile a number of major European cities have ended their shared e-scooter schemes. Paris abolished them after a referendum in 2023. Brussels is the most recent, announcing theirs would end in 2027, following a rise in accidents.

The last evaluation of the trials in England was in 2022. New findings are expected by early next year.

In recent years, successive British transport secretaries have supported the idea of a new law to legalise and regulate the e-scooters available for sale. E-scooters were mentioned in the 2022 Queen's speech, but nothing materialised.

Winchcomb says some of the "dithering" may be because of the potential knock-on effect of any legislation for users of mobility scooters.

There is some frustration from devolved administrations around the UK about the delays in Whitehall. The Scottish Government "will continue to urge the UK government, which holds powers on registration and licensing over off-road vehicles, to take action in this area", a spokesperson says.

The Welsh Government says it will "monitor developments on legalisation plans and consider the implications for Wales".

Northern Ireland's Department for Infrastructure says it is also monitoring the trials in England, as well as the impact of recent law changes in the Republic of Ireland.

The DfT says it says the trials are "building the real-world evidence needed to get regulation right" and it will consult on longer-term rules, including registration and licensing requirements, within the next year.

"E-scooters have real potential to improve local connectivity and cut emissions, and we want to see that potential realised safely and responsibly across the UK," a spokesperson says.

But Ali says: "The current halfway house becomes harder to justify as usage grows."

Even if legislation and associated technical standards were introduced, Richard Dilks acknowledges enforcing them would remain difficult.

But, he adds: "That problem only gets bigger if you leave it."
