#1711. One year in: DMG Media’s social-only briefs account for 30% of New Media revenue

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

DMG Media’s year-old New Media division is carving out a new social revenue stream with creator-led IP aimed at younger audiences.

The commercial shift is beginning to show up in the numbers. Direct social-only briefs — campaigns that exclude DMG’s print and digital products — now account for 30% of New Media revenue, according to the division’s director Nathan Aspell. He said that revenue stream “wasn’t available” before the social-publishing division launched a year ago. This month, DMG signed a cross-platform campaign spanning social, print and online; the social component alone was worth seven figures, he added.

DMG Media doesn’t break out revenue for its New Media division yet. The wider group reported revenue was £1.92 billion ($2.56 billion) in 2025, per the company.

New Media now includes more than 100 staff and spans 11 brands including Daily Mail, Mail Sport and This Is Money (personal finance for Gen Z), and lifestyle and shopping brand Eliza, using salaried, in-house creators, editorial teams and production staff to create social-first content.

DMG’s push reflects a broader shift among publishers facing pressure on referral traffic: rather than using social mainly to send audiences to their own sites, they’re building formats people watch in-feed, and selling sponsorships around them.

The shift is also changing how advertisers value publisher-made social, aid Abi Watson, head of publishing for Enders Analysis. “The last year has really seen an acceleration in the recognition and professionalisation of the creator industry, coinciding with the decline in referral traffic,” she said. The growth in social-only briefs at DMG suggests that advertisers increasingly see value in these formats as “destinations in their own right,” rather than simply as extensions of existing publishing brands,” she added.

The social publishing strategy draws on both sides of DMG’s portfolio: established editorial ideas that can be reworked for social, and formats created specifically for the platforms, said Aspell, who was promoted to director of the division in August. It can then sell social-only campaigns while putting its brands in front of younger audiences on platforms such as TikTok, per Aspell.

Since its launch, the social content has generated a total of 3 billion monthly views and publishes an average of 2,000 pieces (a mix of video and static/carousels) per month on both Instagram and TikTok, per the company. “There might be an 18-year-old who’s never picked up the newspaper, but now is very much aware of the Daily Mail through its social content,” he said.

DMG’s New Media team includes 25 salaried creators who work alongside producers, videographers and editors. Aspell said its creators feature in 90% of branded content and integrating them into its content strategy helps lift engagement across the network by 50%. “That shows a huge appetite for individual and creator-first content,” he said.

For Aspell, the model means creators are involved in making the work, not simply appearing in front of the camera. “A creator in our world is someone who’s sitting with the videographer and talking about how they’re going to shoot it,” he said. The company owns the IP they develop.

The creator-led approach sits within a broader shift in how the team thinks about social output. Before Aspell joined, he said, a breaking story could generate around 50 posts in two hours — producing huge reach, but dividing engagement across many pieces of content. He pushed instead for a smaller number of higher-quality posts that give audiences more information. He expected views might decline, but said engagement has risen month over month in the past year, while the portfolio still reaches billions of people a month. “That is a testament to us doing quality content, not just quantity,” he added.

The team’s approach to finding creators follows a similar emphasis on potential over scale. Aspell said he looks beyond follower counts when it comes to scouting creators: someone with only 2,000 followers but a real passion for news and strong content might be a good fit for the Daily Mail’s platform to help them grow, he explained. He framed it as finding people with a genuine interest in news and potential; not necessarily chasing the biggest creators.

He also wants to build more IP around the company’s in-house creators. He is interested in formats built around a distinctive voice, pointing to Goalhanger’s work with Guardian columnist Marina Hyde as a model worth exploring: pairing an established personality with the production and social support to create something of their own. Aspell said DMG is already taking early steps with shows such as “PM for a Day” — launched in August and featuring creator-journalist Mikey Olympitis asking people what they would do if they were prime minister for a day. Meanwhile, Metro’s “Rush Hour Crush” is an example of how it has reworked the newspaper’s dating feature as a social show, with creator Erica Figueiredo trying to connect people in London. He wants to push further into that territory.

The appeal is IP with a recognizable host and a clear premise; not simply another post on a publisher’s page. He cited the popular “Subway Takes” — a show where people are asked to give their “hot takes” while on a subway train — as a standout example of that kind of distinct, personality-led show. For DMG, the next question is how to build more formats that audiences return to, while giving the company new opportunities to work with brands. Currently, it has four to five show formats per 11 of its brands. Watch time is an important metric, and rates vary across the format, but he cited sports format “Now You Know” which averages 30 seconds of watch time, with some bespoke short-form videos built around individual stories averaging more than 30 seconds.

Alongside branded-content deals, DMG earns revenue through platform monetization, including revenue-sharing arrangements with Meta, Aspel said. That creates a balancing act: posts can generate revenue and engagement on Facebook, while links can send readers to DMG’s own sites. “You get money from the posts you’re doing, but then you’re also trying to drive people to websites,” he said. The team uses a mix of video, static posts and links, aiming for the formats to complement rather than cannibalize one another.

#1705. Creators are now classified as small businesses, but what does that actually mean?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

This article is part of an ongoing series detailing the most important information readers need to know about the ad industry’s most complex topics. More from the series →

On Oct. 15, the U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) issued the nation’s first small business classification guidance for content creators. This comes just a few months after the organization, which helps Americans build and scale small businesses, proposed amendments to simplify the classification so that the millions of creator-led businesses could come into the SBA tent.

Creator-led businesses will now be classified under the Motion Picture and Video industries portion of the SBA. Though they have been eligible for support from the organization for years (when they meet the program requirements which include citizenship, business location, size, and credit availability), it’s been unclear which North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) they should use when applying for programs.

What does this classification mean about the state of the creator economy, and does it actually serve to help creators or is it just window dressing? We break it down for you.

The creator economy’s rapid growth has creators, companies, and agencies scrambling to build the infrastructure up underneath it to sustain that growth. The SBA classification helps with both sustainability and legitimization.

“For over a decade, creators have shaped culture, commerce, and consumer behavior, yet have largely been treated as marketing expenses rather than businesses,” said Daniel Abas, founder and president of the Creators Guild of America. Now, they can be seen in the same light as traditional businesses.

“When a federal agency formally recognizes millions of creator-led businesses, the people who decide where money goes start to treat creators as an asset class,” said Lauren Lyster, vp and head of social media at Go Fish Digital. “That includes banks, CFOs and procurement teams.”

Lyster said this classification could mean brands no longer pay creators like a media buy, but consider them more traditional suppliers.

“This is a first step toward understanding and recognizing the sheer size and value of the U.S. creator economy,” said Avi Gandhi, chair of government advocacy for the CGA.

Terra Fernandez, vp of content partnerships at Canvas Worldwide said the federal government’s move reinforces what the industry has long known: “This isn’t a side hustle economy anymore. It’s a real business ecosystem.”

So, is this just window dressing? Not really, but it’s also not technically a new benefit.

“Creators were already eligible for SBA support if they met program requirements,” explained Iluka Enright, associate director of influencer marketing at Movers+Shakers.

“They had no obvious industry code to put on the forms. That left loan officers guessing at how to judge a business built on YouTube ad revenue, brand deals, and affiliate income,” said Lyster.

Rather than trying to figure out which one of the thousands of NAICS codes they fall under, the SBA is now flat-out telling them: 5121, Motion Picture and Video Industries. SBA put creators under this classification because they so often film, produce, and edit their own content, and it’ll make filling out paperwork and tracking its progress much easier.

Creators now have easier access to SBA loans, which include micro loans of $50,000 or less and the 7(a) loan program which has a maximum amount of $5 million.

According to Gandhi, the SBA delivers around $45 billion in loans each year through the U.S. banking system. These loans can help creators build their businesses in a variety of ways — they can buy equipment, hire employees to help with editing or PR, and otherwise expand their operations and build structure around them.

Visa’s 2025 creator report found that 88 percent of creators who responded plan on growing their business in the next year, with 86 percent of them using personal funds to finance their work. This funding works while creators are earning money, but if they have a dip in engagement or a gap in branded partnerships, using up their own savings to build their business could become a problem. The creators who sell their catalogue or future revenue are giving up a share of their income.

Creators looking for other ways to earn money can go the creator capital markets route, or get into equity investment. The SBA loans offer a more traditional and predictable route.

With an SBA loan, “a creator could hire an editor, rent a studio or build a product line without signing away equity or IP,” said Lyster.

Creators can also get support through other SBA resources, like its Small Business Development Centers, which helps with the nitty gritty parts of running their own businesses like writing contracts and bookkeeping.

In terms of how this classification could affect brand deals, sentiment is mixed. Gandhi believes the SBA classification is “unlikely” to alter how brand deals are structured or negotiated, but does think it could change how creators build their businesses beyond typical brand deals.

“Access to financing and mentorship means more creators will be able to build more capital-intensive businesses, like consumer products, local restaurants and retail, and real-world services,” he said.

Shira Lazar, creator and creator advocate, thinks brand deal negotiations could change due to the SBA’s classification, which might convince brands to stop thinking about creators as strictly marketing channels and look at them as their own media businesses and potential strategic partners.

“Having greater access to capital means creators may be less dependent on taking the next brand deal just to keep their business running,” Lazar said. “That can give them more flexibility to negotiate fair rates, payment terms, ownership, and longer-term partnerships.”

Lyster agreed that the further professionalization that comes with the SBA classification might give creators more leeway to be pickier with brand deals. She also noted that creators who show up as “a properly set-up small business” are more likely to get through vendor onboarding and procurement faster.

“Some brands may also be able to count that spend toward small-business supplier goals,” Lyster suggested.

Crucially, the SBA is proposing an exception to the size standards rule for the NAICS Motion Picture and Video Industries qualification because creator-led businesses tend to be incredibly small. The ruling on whether creators will get that exception will come after Nov. 20, when the SBA’s public comment period closes. If the exception doesn’t go through, it could mean that less creators qualify for SBA programs.

Lyster also noted that SBA loans are taking on debt, and a creator who might have a major falloff after a platform’s algorithm change could be “stuck with real personal liability.”

There’s also the chance that more financially independent creators mean even higher creator fees, which could make the market more competitive.

Still, others warned to be aware of too much fanfare.

“It’s important to recognize that this is a relatively minor change rather than a sweeping shift in how creator businesses operate,” noted Kyle Hjelmeseth, CEO and co-founder of G&B Digital Management. “The bigger opportunity is what comes next. As these conversations continue, I hope we’ll see more meaningful resources and support become available.”

This week’s Future of TV Briefing looks at the messy state of genre labeling in CTV advertising.

Connected TV ad sellers have been reticent to share show-level information with advertisers. Passing genre labels would be the next best thing, if only they were more reliable.

Only 11% of CTV ad requests contained a usable show name in an analysis conducted by streaming tech provider Wurl. By comparison, 93% of requests contained a genre label, though the accuracy of those genre labels varied. Roughly a third of requests (35%) evaluated against Wurl’s own classification system carried a genre label that didn’t match Wurl’s classification for the corresponding program.

Wurl’s analysis was limited to a two-hour window during a single prime-time evening in August 2026 across the platform’s supply footprint, which includes Wurl-powered channels across LG Channels, Pluto TV, Samsung TV Plus and Tubi.

So what gives? Well, the root issue seems to be a lack of standardization around content labeling.

“As a guy who has a legacy TV background – I come from the cable industry – historically, the metadata around content libraries is pretty inconsistent,” said Dave Bernath, CEO of Wurl.

Not sure what he means? Well, let me ask you this: Is “The Bear” a drama or comedy? Anyone watching the show, especially the two most recent seasons, would probably say drama; I would say drama. And yet FX submits it in the comedy category for awards, right alongside ABC’s “Abbott Elementary,” which is as traditional a comedy as they come.

Which gets at another problem with how CTV ad sellers label genre: The labels they append can be pretty broad. Of the 1.09 billion ad requests that did carry a genre label, 22% had what Wurl considered a vague genre label, such as “entertainment” or “general variety.”

On the flip side, there can just be an overwhelming number of different genre labels appended to shows that can make it hard to reconcile them. During that two-hour window of Wurl’s analysis, the company tallied more than 3,600 different genre labels.

This content classification confusion is exactly the thing that could derail the CTV ad industry’s efforts to pass content signals. Over the summer, Comcast-owned (for now) FreeWheel added show-level reporting for advertisers using its platform. However, the company wasn’t taking an active role in ensuring that ad buyers and sellers were using the same show naming conventions, which could nullify any attempts to match programs and provide accurate show-level reports.

If only there were a standard taxonomy for the CTV ad industry to rally around… Oh wait, there is.

In June 2022 – four years ago – IAB Tech Lab updated its Content Taxonomy to include categories suited to CTV. And two years ago, it further refreshed the standard to add more genre categories, like “Thriller.” And even then, it features only 704 categories, one-fifth the number of unique genre labels Wurl had observed.

For its part, Wurl uses IAB Tech Lab’s Content Taxonomy to undergird its classification system.

“We’re using our machine learning, our content intelligence to analyze these streams and understand the metadata, the [electronic programming guide], to try to coalesce around the IAB standard genres specifically,” Bernath said.

Why others aren’t following suit is beyond me. Content owners are responsible for appending content labels to bid requests, Bernath said, so I asked him why they’re missing the mark so often.

“I would call it sort of historical metadata hangover,” Bernath said.

That is to say, these content owners are sitting on large, dusty libraries of shows and movies. Those programs may have been originally categorized decades ago, and no one has reviewed them to see if the labels still hold up or need to be updated. And I guess no one has thought to throw the problem at Claude or ChatGPT. Sure, the AI tools could hallucinate categories, but then would that be worse than the current state of things?

#1687. Apple’s Safari crackdown pushes ad tech toward server-side solutions, but doubts remain

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Apple’s latest crackdown on identity resolution has accelerated efforts among publishers and ad tech companies to move advertising infrastructure beyond the browser, although questions remain over whether the industry’s preferred response will provide a lasting solution.

Following weeks of uncertainty over Safari’s restrictions on domains associated with identity providers, companies including ID5 are advancing server-side integrations, while others are rallying behind IAB Tech Lab’s Trusted Server Framework.

The developments follow Apple’s WebKit explanation last week, which clarified its objections to identifiers derived from information such as email addresses being used to recognize individuals across websites without explicit consent.

However, the explanation came after the restrictions took effect, leaving publishers and technology providers scrambling to understand their implications. Those ad tech companies impacted included Audigent (an Experian company), ID5, LiveRamp (now a Publicis Groupe entity), Permutive, and The Trade Desk, according to live test results seen by Digiday during the week beginning Oct. 19.

Apple’s communication with the industry remains selective. Most sources contacted by Digiday described its public-facing ⁠WebKit portal as the principal channel for addressing technical questions, although separate sources, requesting anonymity to preserve commercial relationships, indicated that some back-channel discussions had taken place to help resolve outstanding issues.

Digiday’s inquiries into Apple’s motivations, beyond its publicly stated commitment to privacy, went unanswered by press time.

Meanwhile, sources in the privacy industry suggested Apple’s approach may reflect concerns about potential litigation arising from third-party tracking practices enabled by its technology.

Such an interpretation differs from suggestions that Apple is deliberately undermining independent advertising to strengthen its own growing advertising business. Apple does not separately disclose advertising revenue, although the business remains comparatively small against the more than $200 billion it generates annually through iPhone sales.

Nevertheless, critics argue that restricting addressability across independent publishers could ultimately redirect advertising spending toward environments that offer more predictable targeting and measurement.

“An open, ad-funded internet depends on consistent rules,” said Amy Jansen-Flynn, managing director of Audience360 & Publift, and executive tech council member at IAB Australia, in an emailed statement. “When one company can switch off addressability for independent publishers while walled gardens keep theirs, it doesn’t end tracking; it concentrates it,” she said, noting that such maneuvers are likely to concentrate ad spend further into the hands of outfits such as Apple, Google and Meta.

For publishers, the immediate commercial consequences remain hard to establish; according to Permutive, only a small number of its customers were affected by Apple’s recent changes, as consumer adoption remains low, though, predictably, it is expected to increase.

Digiday understands such publishers have since been placed on an interim solution while migrating to the company’s established first-party infrastructure, which has supported most customers since 2020.

Several sources indicated that relatively low adoption of iOS 27 has generated far more discussion than measurable disruption, a testament to Apple’s ability to generate buzz, or trepidation, across the industry.

Many publishers first learned about the restrictions through industry reporting or direct outreach from technology partners, and Apple’s later explanation provided greater clarity.

ID5, meanwhile, is preparing to migrate more of its identity infrastructure server-side, with Mathieu Roche, ID5 CEO, confirming the development to Digiday, explaining that the company has historically balanced ease of implementation against resilience to browser restrictions.

“Going server-side is more resilient because you’re not on the device anymore, so there’s nothing the device can do to block you,” he said.

Under the proposed approach, identity-related functions previously performed through calls to third-party domains would instead operate through publisher-controlled infrastructure.

Roche acknowledged that implementation would be harder for smaller publishers, particularly those outsourcing advertising operations, but he maintained that larger organizations could complete the technical work in days rather than months.

The company has begun working with selected publishers to test its revised integrations, according to a source familiar with the process, although broader deployment remains at an early stage.

The developments have renewed interest in ⁠IAB Tech Lab’s Trusted Server Framework, introduced in March 2025.

The open-source initiative proposes shifting advertising functions traditionally executed within browsers into publisher-operated infrastructure, including server-side advertising requests, Prebid Server integration, edge processing and ad stitching.

IAB Tech Lab argues that the framework can reduce publishers’ dependence on browser policies while improving control over advertising auctions, first-party data and information transmitted to technology partners.

However, the proposal drew criticism even before Apple’s latest intervention, with some questioning whether the added infrastructure would increase complexity and dependence on cloud providers. Others raised concerns about potential fingerprinting and preserving existing identity practices.

More fundamental objections concern whether moving third-party identity resolution server-side addresses Apple’s underlying privacy concerns or simply places those activities beyond its browser-level controls.

Sources familiar with publisher-side identity strategies questioned whether such an approach could perpetuate another round of cat-and-mouse between Apple and the advertising industry. Such parties worry that publishers could invest substantial resources in infrastructure built around practices Apple has already indicated it opposes, only to face further restrictions.

The distinction between publisher-controlled first-party personalization and infrastructure supporting cross-site identification is central to that argument. Trusted Server could deliver operational benefits independently of identity resolution. However, critics question whether preserving existing advertising practices should be its primary purpose, especially if it exposes publishers to further technical disruption.

Digiday raised these concerns with IAB Tech Lab leadership, including questions about privacy safeguards, implementation costs, and the possibility of future restrictions, but it declined to comment.

Supporters maintain that focusing exclusively on Safari overlooks the framework’s broader commercial implications.

Anastasia-Nikita Bansal, TeqBlaze CEO and a member of IAB Tech Lab’s Trusted Server Working Group, acknowledged that future restrictions remain possible. “There is no absolute guarantee that any single solution will permanently solve the challenges posed by Apple’s ecosystem updates,” she wrote in an emailed response to Digiday’s inquiries.

However, she argued that publisher-controlled infrastructure could also become increasingly important following Google’s ad tech antitrust proceedings, particularly behavioral remedies intended to provide publishers with greater flexibility over their ad-serving arrangements while retaining access to AdX demand.

Such developments could strengthen the commercial rationale for independently operated auction infrastructure, irrespective of Apple’s next intervention. TeqBlaze also maintains that investing in first-party decisioning and moving advertising operations server-side are complementary approaches, rather than competing alternatives.

Nevertheless, the costs of operating and maintaining additional infrastructure remain significant considerations, particularly for publishers without dedicated engineering resources.

The company warned against technology providers exploiting the disruption to impose additional costs on publishers seeking assistance.

Meanwhile, Roche questioned the industry’s tendency to turn disputes over browser restrictions into competition between identity providers and alternative advertising approaches. “I think we’re picking the wrong battle by trying to kind of fight against each other,” he told Digiday.

These competing positions illustrate a longstanding tension in digital advertising: whether publishers should invest in preserving existing identity-based monetization arrangements or focus resources on approaches less dependent on cross-site recognition.

For now, Trusted Server offers a potential way to reduce publishers’ operational dependence on browsers, while Apple’s latest intervention has increased the urgency to develop it.

Whether the framework ultimately provides lasting independence will depend on more than where advertising infrastructure is located. The underlying data practices and their compatibility with Apple’s privacy requirements remain unresolved.

Let’s level set. I live in a 1780s barn that was turned into a house in the 1950s, and as far as I can tell the windows and the siding haven’t been changed since. The fact is, many parts of our money pit farmhouse need updating. Some, like the 1950s asphalt roof built directly on top of a much older shake shingle roof, we replaced last year. Other things we’re still waiting on, like the vintage 1950s bathroom tile with vintage crumbling grout. But since we can’t close many of our windows without pushing on them from the outside, and we have an inexplicable leak behind our very ancient siding, we put off the inevitable long enough. It is time for some grand-scale renovations.

Stay tuned for some of the other things we’ll be changing, including a kitchen refresh. But first up, if we’re going to go to all the trouble—and, ahem, expense—of replacing all of our siding and windows, can we make our old home more environmentally responsible in terms of both materials and energy efficiency? Let’s find out.

My guide on this journey is Shannon Pendleton, principal of Sanderson Sustainable Design and co-host of Passive House Accelerator. In addition to being a friend and neighbor, Shannon is a leading expert on how to make homes more sustainable and energy efficient. And while those concepts might immediately make you think of solar panels and the like, Shannon explains that in energy efficiency, an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure. In other words, using green energy to power your home is great. But designing your home to need less energy in the first place is even better.

But with apologies to Shannon, when she explained the science of the “building envelope,” all I could think about is that envelope feels like the wrong word. Maybe it should be “building envelop”—as in, how enveloped is your building. Because your “building envelop” is what stops the outside environment from getting in and the inside environment from getting out. That means everything from bugs and dust to wind to heat and cold.

So I’m making two big changes to my “building envelop,” both of which create an opportunity for more energy efficiency. Let’s start with the siding. We have very old, very stained, very falling-apart cedar. I could just replace it with cedar, maybe even sustainably harvested cedar. But the fact is, our old cedar is cupping and splitting. Yes, more maintenance would help, but that’s hard to do with second-story wood. Also I’m lazy. Alternatively, I could have gone with some plastic product or chemically treated wood but, like I said, we care about the environment and also want to preserve the look and feel of our historic home. So we’re going with Thermory siding.

Thermory takes sustainably forested wood—in our case, we’re using pine—and puts it through an intense heat-treating process that makes the wood more stable and rot-resistant. It’s an updated twist on the Japanese shou sugi ban process, where wood is charred to make it water and rot resistant. Thermory is that, without the blackening (though they actually do have a product that mirrors the shou sugi ban look, but with the added benefits of Thermory’s advanced thermal modification). In other words, Thermory gives us everything we like about natural wood plus all these added benefits.

Using a more stable rot-resistant siding definitely envelops our house better (take that, flying squirrels), but the energy efficiency upgrades come from what will be behind the siding. Our contractor—the wonderful, lovable, fantastic-to-work-with John Gemmi and his company, Gemmi Construction—were already planning to weather-seal behind the siding, and of course put new insulation behind that where we find any lacking. But Shannon suggested installing “a rain screen, a continuous air barrier, and continuous exterior insulation” to give us the most enveloped envelope possible. Ultimately, we settled on Zip R sheathing, which does all three of those things in one system. So that’s our plan.

As for the windows, well…. The most energy-efficient option is triple-pane glass, but in our case, since we’re replacing a gajillion windows, we just couldn’t make it work in our budget. We have two walls of windows in our great room, most of which are currently fixed panes that we’re excited to replace with operable awning windows. We looked into doing triple-pane for just those windows, but that would have made the windows too heavy to open. So yes, we’re choosing a little less efficiency there but counting on the fact that having new windows that don’t require a screwdriver and grunts to close will nevertheless up our efficiency. Plus, thanks to Ambiance Design, we’re adding retractable Duette Honeycomb shades over those large window walls, which will give us some extra energy efficiency on super hot days.

Niece Lumber helped us pick out Lincoln Windows, a family run U.S.-manufactured brand with two advantages. First, we could get retractable screens which we’re very excited about. And second, they offer custom colors on their exteriors. You’ll have to stay tuned to figure out what color choice we’ve made. It’s gonna be…dramatic.

Work on the siding and window part of the renovation is starting soon. I’ll be posting updates on my Instagram (@sallykohn), plus more updates along the way here as we get into our kitchen renovations, including the most sustainable and cool countertop and our very locally made and custom color stove. Watch this space!

None of the killings were captured in the thousands of hours of body camera footage sent by Rio de Janeiro Military Police to the Public Defender’s Office, which Human Rights Watch reviewed after being granted access.

Human Rights Watch interviewed 41 witnesses, family members, and experts, and analyzed 4,400 hours of body camera footage, 9,000 pages of official investigation files, 122 autopsy reports, court records, and hundreds of videos and photographs. Despite the stated goal of carrying out 100 arrest warrants for alleged Red Command members, police records show they arrested only 17 of those for whom they had warrants, although it is not clear whether those are the warrants that justified the operation or other pending warrants. Police did not enter the home of the local Red Command leader, for whom they had a warrant, a Civil Police commander told prosecutors, even though body camera footage shows police moving around it. 

Despite a state law and a Supreme Court order mandating that police wear body cameras, only about 23 percent of the 2,500 civil and military police officers who participated in the raid did, the Rio de Janeiro government said.

Human Rights Watch analysis of the footage of the 527 military police officers who wore the cameras found widespread tampering and obstruction of their use. Officers left cameras running before the operation, effectively draining batteries; covered lenses; placed cameras where they would not capture officers’ actions; and left cameras in vehicles. 

Such tampering consistently occurred at key moments, during arrests, shootouts, or while officers entered homes, researchers found. Most tampering was on cameras worn by officers whose units engaged in frontline operations, such as the Battalion of Special Police Operations of the Military Police, the unit deployed to the forested area where the majority of killings took place. Researchers determined that 36 percent of gunshot clusters heard on their cameras occurred when the cameras were obstructed.  

Body camera footage showed officers assaulting and threatening to kill detainees, including a child, whom they forced to lie down next to a dead body while photographing him. Only seven suspects were taken to a hospital and subsequently arrested, compared with 117 people killed by police. That unusually high ratio of people killed to people injured (17 to 1) raises serious questions about whether police prioritized lethal force over arresting suspects.

Over 40 percent of non-police victims in the reports analyzed were shot in the head, over 60 percent were shot four or more times, and nearly 75 percent were shot at least once on the back. One man was shot and then decapitated.

Brazilian authorities should ensure a thorough and independent investigation into the planning and decisions by senior officials and police commanders that led to the deadly operation. Rio de Janeiro’s new governor, who will take office on January 6, 2027, should introduce a bill to separate forensic services from the Civil Police.

#400. A simple daily fiber supplement reduced arthritis pain and improved strength

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A simple daily fiber supplement may offer a surprising new way to relieve knee arthritis pain while improving muscle strength. In a clinical trial led by researchers at the University of Nottingham, people with knee osteoarthritis (OA) who took a prebiotic fiber called inulin experienced significant reductions in pain, improved grip strength, and decreased sensitivity to pain.

The supplement also appeared to be considerably easier for participants to stick with than a digital physical therapy program. Only 3.6% of those assigned to inulin alone dropped out of the study, compared with 21% of those assigned to physical therapy alone.

Published in the journal Nutrients, the findings suggest that improving gut health could become an additional strategy for managing arthritis. They also reveal an intriguing connection between the bacteria living in our digestive system, the way our bodies process pain, and muscle function.

A Simple Dietary Change Could Help Relieve Arthritis Pain

Knee osteoarthritis affects hundreds of millions of people worldwide and is a major cause of chronic pain and disability, especially among older adults. The condition develops as the tissues within the knee joint, including the cartilage that cushions the ends of bones, gradually deteriorate. This can lead to pain, stiffness, swelling, and difficulty with everyday activities such as walking or climbing stairs.

Treatment typically focuses on reducing symptoms and maintaining mobility. Pain medications can provide relief but may cause unwanted side effects, while physical therapy and regular exercise can help improve joint function. However, keeping up with an exercise program can be difficult, particularly for people already struggling with persistent pain.

The new research raises the possibility that a simple dietary supplement could provide another way to manage symptoms.

"This study raises the exciting possibility that a simple dietary change -- adding a fiber supplement to your breakfast or yogurt -- could meaningfully reduce pain and improve physical function," said Dr. Afroditi Kouraki, lead author of the study from the School of Medicine at the University of Nottingham.

The supplement tested in the study was inulin, a naturally occurring fiber found in foods such as chicory root, Jerusalem artichokes, and certain other vegetables. Unlike many nutrients, inulin is not broken down by digestive enzymes in the small intestine. Instead, it passes into the large intestine, where beneficial bacteria can use it as food.

How Gut Bacteria May Influence Arthritis Pain

Researchers are increasingly discovering that the gut microbiome, the vast community of microorganisms living in the digestive tract, influences much more than digestion. These microbes interact with the immune system, produce biologically active compounds, and may even affect how the nervous system responds to pain.

Inulin is classified as a prebiotic because it nourishes certain beneficial gut bacteria rather than directly supplying living microorganisms, as probiotics do.

When bacteria ferment inulin, they produce substances known as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). One of the most important is butyrate, a compound that helps support the intestinal lining and can influence inflammation and biological pathways involved in pain.

The researchers found that participants taking inulin had increased levels of butyrate, suggesting that the supplement was influencing activity within the gut microbiome.

They also observed higher levels of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), a hormone released by the gut that helps regulate blood sugar and appetite and has also been linked to pain regulation and muscle health.

GLP-1 has attracted widespread attention because it is the hormone targeted by medications such as Ozempic and Wegovy. However, the inulin supplement used in this trial was not a GLP-1 medication. Instead, researchers examined whether supporting beneficial gut bacteria could influence the body's own production of the hormone.

One particularly interesting finding was that higher GLP-1 levels were associated with better grip strength. Although this does not establish that GLP-1 caused the improvement, it suggests that changes originating in the digestive system may be connected to muscle function as well as pain.

To investigate these effects, the researchers conducted the INSPIRE trial, a six-week randomized controlled study involving 117 adults with knee osteoarthritis.

Participants were assigned to one of four treatment groups: a daily inulin supplement alone, digitally supported physical therapy exercises (PSE) alone, a combination of inulin and physical therapy, or a placebo.

This design allowed researchers to examine the effects of the supplement and exercise separately, as well as investigate whether combining them offered additional benefits.

Both inulin and physical therapy independently reduced knee pain, indicating that each approach provided meaningful symptom relief.

However, the supplement produced additional improvements that were not observed with physical therapy alone. Participants taking inulin demonstrated better grip strength and reduced pain sensitivity.

Pain sensitivity reflects how strongly the nervous system responds to potentially painful stimulation. Reducing that sensitivity could be particularly valuable for people with osteoarthritis, because persistent pain is not always explained by the amount of visible damage within a joint. Changes in how the nervous system processes pain can also contribute to discomfort.

The improved grip strength was another unexpected result, suggesting that the supplement's effects might extend beyond the knee itself.

The researchers also found a substantial difference in how many participants completed their assigned treatments. Only 3.6% of the inulin-only group dropped out, compared with 21% of the physical therapy-only group.

This difference suggests that taking a daily fiber supplement may be easier to incorporate into a regular routine than following a structured exercise program.

A Surprising Connection Between Gut Health, Muscles, and Pain

Dr. Kouraki said: "Our findings suggest that targeting gut health with a prebiotic supplement is a safe, well-tolerated, and effective way to reduce pain in people with knee osteoarthritis. The very low dropout rate compared to the exercise group is also encouraging from a public health perspective -- people were able to fit this supplement easily into their daily lives."

Beyond the reduction in arthritis pain, the researchers are particularly interested in what the findings reveal about interactions between the digestive system, muscles, and nervous system.

The association between GLP-1 and grip strength raises the possibility of a broader biological relationship connecting gut health with physical performance and resilience as people age.

Senior author Professor Ana Valdes from the School of Medicine added: "The link we observed between GLP-1 and grip strength is particularly intriguing and points to a broader gut-muscle-pain axis that warrants further investigation. This could have implications not just for osteoarthritis, but for understanding how gut health influences aging and physical resilience more broadly."

The findings are promising, but the trial was relatively small and lasted only six weeks. Larger studies with longer follow-up periods will be needed to establish whether the benefits persist, identify which patients are most likely to respond, and clarify the biological mechanisms responsible.

The results also do not suggest that fiber supplements should replace exercise or other established arthritis treatments. Instead, they point toward a potentially useful additional approach that could complement existing care.

The potential importance of this research becomes clearer when considering how many people live with persistent arthritis pain.

Professor Lucy Donaldson, Director of Research at Arthritis UK, said: "The pain of arthritis can severely impact quality of life. Our recent lived experience survey showed that six in ten people are living in pain most or all of the time due to their arthritis.

"Researchers are starting to explore the role of the gut microbiome in our experience of pain. This exciting preliminary research highlights how diet and physiotherapy can act in different ways to have benefits for people with arthritis. We know a variety and balance of healthy foods, including fiber, and regular physical activity matter, and we're glad to be supporting research that explores how they work to help people with arthritis."

For people living with knee osteoarthritis, the findings offer an encouraging possibility: a relatively simple change in daily nutrition may help reduce pain while supporting other aspects of physical health.

Perhaps most intriguingly, the research suggests that treating arthritis may involve looking beyond the joints themselves. By exploring how gut bacteria influence inflammation, pain perception, and muscle function, scientists may uncover new ways to manage a condition that affects millions of people every day.

Otter, a portable newborn warmer developed by Design that Matters and MTTS, is designed to prevent and treat neonatal hypothermia in hospitals with unreliable electricity and limited resources. Awarded the 2026 Seoul Design Award Grand Prize (find top 10 finalists here), the device rethinks newborn warming through an integrated, heat-conducting polycarbonate bassinet, a built-in battery, and an intuitive touchscreen interface. Otter addresses the practical challenges of neonatal care in low-resource settings, where power outages, limited maintenance capacity, and the risk of user error can compromise the reliability of conventional devices.

Newborn incubators and radiant warmers often depend on stable electricity, regular maintenance, and specialized equipment, requirements that can become obstacles in rural hospitals, where power outages and limited technical support complicate the delivery of neonatal care. Otter addresses these constraints through a design developed over eight years of field research, expert interviews, and product reviews across Africa and Southeast Asia. The need is particularly acute for premature and low-birthweight newborns, who are more vulnerable to losing body heat. Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC), which relies on skin-to-skin contact between a newborn and their caregiver, is an established approach to maintaining warmth, but interruptions are sometimes unavoidable. A baby may need to be examined, transferred between departments, or temporarily separated from their mother. Otter provides supplemental warming during these moments, including transfers within hospitals.

The central design decision by US-based nonprofit Design that Matters and Vietnamese medical device manufacturer MTTS is to replace the removable water or gel mattress found in many conductive warmers with an integrated, heat-conducting polycarbonate bassinet. The smooth, continuous surface has no fabric, seams, or gaps, making it easier to clean and disinfect between patients. It also prevents direct contact between the newborn and the heating element, reducing the risk of burns associated with conventional systems when a mattress is removed or incorrectly positioned. The heating element and temperature sensors sit within a water-resistant housing beneath the bassinet, protected from rough handling. This integrated construction reduces reliance on consumables and replacement parts. Warming mattresses can be vulnerable to punctures, seam failures, and deterioration, while water-filled systems may require additives that are difficult to source in some settings. Otter simplifies the structure of the product, aiming to reduce the maintenance burden throughout its working life.

Otter weighs 3.6 kg and can be carried between clinical departments without interrupting warming. Its integrated battery automatically takes over when mains electricity is disconnected, supporting continued operation during power failures and patient transfers. The battery provides more than two hours of backup at an ambient temperature of 15°C. The touchscreen displays temperature, heating status, and estimated remaining battery life, while alarms alert staff to problems with the sensor, heating element, or power supply. The interface is designed to make the status of the device easy to assess without requiring extensive technical training. Its physical form follows the same principle. Resembling a familiar newborn bassinet, the open structure allows clinical staff to access the baby while making the device less intimidating for parents. This familiar appearance also supports interaction between mothers and newborns during periods when supplemental warming is needed.

Five Otter devices have been operating at Kapiri District Hospital in Zambia since May 2025, where an ongoing implementation study evaluates performance, staff acceptance, and integration with existing care protocols. The devices have been used with low-birthweight newborns, including infants weighing as little as 840 grams, and to maintain warmth during transfers between hospital buildings. The study also takes place in conditions that expose the practical challenges the design is intended to address, including documented power interruptions lasting seven to eleven hours and limited access to biomedical support. The project summary reports successful use during transfers, but the implementation study remains ongoing, and its findings should be understood in that context.

#405. The autistic brain processes faces differently than scientists thought

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A major new study has uncovered important differences in how the brains of autistic children respond to human faces. Published in Nature Mental Health, the research is among the most extensive investigations yet into how face perception works in autism.

Some autistic people tend to look at faces less frequently or make less eye contact. They may also find it more difficult to remember faces or interpret facial expressions compared with neurotypical individuals. Scientists have spent decades investigating the brain activity behind these differences. In the early 2000s, for example, researchers at Yale reported that brain regions responsible for processing faces showed lower activity in autistic individuals than in people without autism.

James McPartland, PhD, Harris Professor in the Child Study Center and director of the Center for Brain and Mind Health, studies how autistic people process faces using electroencephalography (EEG), a technique that records the brain's electrical activity. Previous EEG studies, however, have typically examined signals from limited regions rather than analyzing activity across the entire scalp.

To gain a more complete picture, McPartland and his colleagues conducted a study involving approximately 400 autistic children, examining electrical signals recorded across the scalp. They discovered that brain activity associated with face recognition was less clearly distinguishable in autistic participants. They also identified a striking difference in development: In neurotypical children, the brain's response to faces became more specialized with age, while autistic children did not show the same pattern.

"This is the first time we've leveraged the full volume of very rich information provided by an EEG recording to understand face perception and autism," McPartland says.

How Scientists Discovered Differences in Autistic Brain Activity

McPartland compares traditional approaches to studying face recognition with attempting to understand traffic throughout an entire city by monitoring just one downtown street.

"That's the way most research on face processing so far has worked," McPartland says.

The new approach provides a much broader perspective, similar to observing traffic across an entire city using satellite imagery. To investigate these patterns, the researchers analyzed information collected through the Autism Biomarkers Consortium for Clinical Trials (ABC-CT). The dataset included autistic and neurotypical children across different age groups.

During the experiments, participants viewed pictures of faces and everyday objects while EEG equipment recorded their brain activity. Instead of concentrating on a small number of recording locations, the scientists examined electrical signals from all 128 electrodes positioned around each participant's scalp.

They then applied machine learning techniques to determine whether patterns in the recordings could reveal what each child was viewing.

The results showed that brain activity provided a clearer indication of face viewing in neurotypical children than in autistic children. In other words, the electrical signals associated with seeing faces were less distinguishable in the autistic group.

"The neural activity that we measure at the scalp is just not as distinct in autistic children," says first author Jason Griffin, PhD, assistant professor of psychology at University of Houston. Griffin previously worked as a postdoctoral researcher in McPartland's laboratory.

The researchers also found that the ability to identify what a child was viewing from brain activity improved with age among neurotypical participants. This age related improvement was not observed in autistic children, suggesting that their neural responses to faces develop along a different pattern.

"Their face-specific processing is not following the same trajectory," Griffin says.

Face Recognition Differences May Begin Earlier Than Previously Thought

Beyond identifying differences in brain activity, the study offers new insight into how early those differences emerge during face perception.

Recognizing someone involves multiple stages of neural processing. The sequence begins when visual information enters the eyes and continues as the brain interprets that information until a face is recognized.

Much of the earlier research on autism and face perception has concentrated on a brain signal called the N170. This measurable change in electrical activity typically occurs about 170 milliseconds after a person sees a face. Previous studies have shown that the N170 response tends to occur later in autistic individuals.

By analyzing brain activity over a wider period, the researchers found evidence that differences in face processing begin before this familiar signal appears. This suggests that autism related differences may arise at earlier stages of visual processing than researchers had previously established.

"But when you look at face processing through an approach that's more inclusive of the full temporal range of information, we're seeing that differences in autism occur even earlier," McPartland says.

What the Discovery Could Mean for Autism Diagnosis and Early Support

The findings could eventually influence how researchers approach autism diagnosis and the development of supportive interventions.

Historically, some clinicians attempted to address difficulties with face perception by requiring autistic individuals to maintain eye contact. Research has since shown that forcing eye contact is not an effective solution. A better understanding of how face processing develops in the brain could help identify approaches that are more useful and appropriate.

An ongoing challenge in autism research is separating neurological differences associated with autism itself from changes that may develop through the experience of living with the condition. According to the researchers, the patterns identified in this study suggest that differences in face processing are connected to underlying developmental characteristics of autism rather than simply reflecting accumulated life experiences.

"When we're seeing this expected refinement and specialization in non-autistic people, we're seeing a reduced developmental change in autistic people," McPartland explains.

Because the differences appear during development, the findings also raise the possibility that support aimed at helping autistic children interpret facial emotions could be particularly beneficial when introduced earlier in childhood.

However, the researchers emphasize that autism does not necessarily create difficulties that require intervention. Many neurodivergent people experience little or no impairment related to their autism and may neither need nor want additional support. For others, challenges with recognizing faces or understanding facial expressions can complicate everyday social interactions.

"Imagine navigating life when it's hard for you to perceive the information in another person's face, from navigating a playground to navigating a job interview," McPartland says. "This is one of the critical areas that causes difficulty for many autistic people."

New Brain Signals Could Help Improve Autism Diagnosis

Another potential application involves the search for more objective ways to assess autism. Currently, clinicians diagnose autism primarily by evaluating behavior because reliable biological tests are not yet available for routine diagnosis.

Scientists are investigating several possible biomarkers, which are measurable biological characteristics that could improve diagnostic assessments or help distinguish different groups within the autism spectrum.

The newly identified patterns of face related brain activity could contribute to that effort. By examining how these neural signals differ among individuals, researchers may eventually develop additional diagnostic tools or better identify autistic people who are likely to benefit from particular forms of support, including social skills therapy.

Although more research is necessary before these findings can be used in clinical practice, the study provides a new way to investigate how the autistic brain processes faces and how those responses change throughout childhood.

"This is a first step in introducing a new biomarker," McPartland says.
