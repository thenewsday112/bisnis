#3973. Two centuries since his death, Antonio Canova’s reputation is on the rise again as a new book of essays explores

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Perseus with the Head of Medusa (1804-06), at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. Canova was also instrumental in negotiating the return of Italian art looted by Napoleon Public Domain Open Access

“Europe—the world—has but one Canova.” So wrote Lord Byron in 1818 when the Italian sculptor’s fame was at its zenith. Antonio Canova’s (1757-1822) dexterity and bravura had ushered in a new age of art, and popes and princes vied for his attention. His Cupid and Psyche Embracing (1787-93) and The Three Graces (1812-16) were not only technical tours de force but scarcely imaginable in marble; they would become international icons like the Mona Lisa.

Canova’s fall from grace, however, came swiftly after his death in 1822. Ironically, his weakness lay in what his contemporaries like the French writer Stendhal hailed as his strength: “Canova had the courage not to copy the Greeks, and he invented a beauty as the Greeks had done.” By the mid-19th century, such an opinion would be curtly dismissed by the German scholar Gustav Waagen, who saw the Three Graces as “pretty but insipid”, with heads that “cannot gratify a taste familiar with the antique”; the limbs, he added, lacked “bony structure”.

Canova’s reputation continued to languish well into the 20th century, and even as perceptive a scholar as John Pope-Hennessy would damn him with faint praise as “fettered by the shackles of good taste”. Now, Canova’s star has risen again, thanks largely to the meticulous studies of the late Hugh Honour as well as a series of outstanding exhibitions, beginning with that at the Museo Correr in 1992 through to more recent ones in Rome, Milan and Chicago.

The sculptor’s oeuvre is ripe for a new monograph but, in its absence, Livio Pestilli’s Canova and His World is the next best thing. Beautifully illustrated and with a text steeped in scholarship, the book distils the current state of Canova studies into a series of essays addressing topics like the critical debate that raged over Canova’s work in his lifetime, the technical challenges posed by his sculptures, and his heroic role in returning looted art to the Papal States after the fall of Napoleon.

Canova certainly divided opinion in his own day, and the younger Danish sculptor Bertel Thorvaldsen (1770-1844) was viewed, chiefly by Nordic critics, as the better artist and the embodiment of a pure strain of Neo-Classicism. Nevertheless, Canova found a powerful ally in the French aesthetician Antoine Quatremère de Quincy, who moulded the sculptor’s international reputation and encouraged his growth as an artist. Yet even Quatremère shared some misgivings about Canova, perceptively observing of Cupid and Psyche Embracing that it veered into the realm of “a classical Bernini”.

In his defence, Canova was trying to reconcile technical virtuosity with fidelity to the narrative of the two lovers. In general, the clay models of Canova do share a degree of spontaneity with those of Gian Lorenzo Bernini (1598-1680), but whereas Bernini maintained that degree of spontaneity in his finished marbles, Canova refined his finished work into a more restrained idiom.

The Veneto-born Canova achieved an early success with his tomb for Pope Clement XIV, completed in 1787 (the Church of Santi Apostoli, Rome). That monument employed many of the same tropes of earlier papal tombs—the pyramidal form, attendant virtues and a statue of the deceased—but everything was subdued, echoing the “noble simplicity and quiet grandeur” that the German art historian Johann Joachim Winckelmann—who laid the foundations for Neo-Classicism—found in the best Classical sculpture.

The virtues of Temperance and Humility are presented as mourning figures with their attributes and rhetorical gestures minimised. Canova’s friend and biographer Leopoldo Cicognara spoke for many when he hailed Canova’s achievement here as a decisive break with previous papal tombs and banished the bad taste of the Baroque era.

What is fascinating about Canova’s work is its glorious inconsistency: that is to say, his unabashed embrace of elements from classical, Renaissance and Baroque art. For example, the verisimilitude of Pauline Borghese’s couch (1804-08, Galleria Borghese), the sentiment of Cupid and Psyche Standing (around 1797, Louvre) or the heroic stance of his Perseus (1804-06, Metropolitan Museum of Art) hardly appear the work of the same artist; yet Canova would probably have defended himself along the lines of Anton Raphael Mengs: “he who effectively studies and observes the productions of great men with a true desire to imitate them, makes himself capable of producing works which resemble them, because he considers the reasons with which they are done…and that makes him an imitator without being a plagiarist”.

Pestilli’s final chapter focuses on Canova as a diplomat, pleading for the return of art looted by Napoleon. Even after the Battle of Waterloo in 1815, there was resistance to returning those works of art to Italy because of a tacit assumption that France should be the natural repository of the best of human creations.

Still, Canova’s patient advocacy won over the British, and this resulted in roughly half of the stolen artefacts being recovered by the papacy. It was a great achievement and of a piece with Canova’s single-minded dedication to art. Towards the end of his career, Canova could say, like Shakespeare’s Othello, “I have done the state some service.”

#2259. The Greenland Deal Is a Huge Success

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Ignore the naysayers: President Trump’s successful conclusion of the Greenland deal with the governments of Denmark and Greenland is a huge win for the United States, and also one that will improve Danish, Greenlandic and, indeed, trans-Atlantic security.

This is a major diplomatic achievement. It expands U.S. defense areas in Greenland, prohibits a non-NATO military presence, and protects critical parts of the Greenlandic economy that adversarial governments could otherwise use for dual-use purposes.

The agreement contains language clarifying the core U.S. interests at stake and the rationale behind increasing activities in the Arctic, recognizing “the security challenges in the Arctic and the High North posed by adversaries’ increased military activity and growing strategic interest in the region” and that “the security situation in the Arctic is changing and that more efforts are needed to ensure security there in the future.”

Critics have said the deal is the same as the one we had before. Not true. The 2026 agreement augments, updates and improves the 1951 treaty, expanding U.S. military infrastructure on the island with two new bases at Narsarsuaq and Mestersvig. It also prohibits Russian or Chinese military bases in Greenland, and creates a mechanism for the governments in Copenhagen, Nuuk and Washington to veto investment in critical parts of the Greenlandic economy. Importantly, the new agreement and its provisions will continue to apply even if Greenland becomes independent.

The strategic rationale for the United States to maintain a military presence in Greenland and secure it from adversarial influence should be obvious. Greenland commands the approaches to the North Atlantic and North America, forming the northwestern end of the Greenland-Iceland-U.K. (GIUK) Gap through which Russian ships and submarines entering the North Atlantic pass. At more than 836,000 square miles, Greenland is a major geographic shaper of the Arctic region, and U.S. radars based in Greenland play an essential role in detecting and intercepting any hostile movements toward the U.S. mainland.

Indeed, the strategic rationale for American military presence in Greenland is strongest when considered in the context of great power competition. The Arctic is the shortest and most likely path for strategic bombers or intercontinental ballistic missiles heading toward the United States, and Greenland in particular lies in the shortest and most likely path for these systems when fired from Russia. Pituffik Space Base, an existing U.S. military site in Greenland, supports American missile warning, missile defense and space surveillance.

Greenland is an autonomous territory of the Kingdom of Denmark, with significant self-rule but responsibility for defense resting with the Danish government in Copenhagen. Russia and China have consistently demonstrated interest in the island and surrounding region, with Chinese investments in particular targeting Greenland’s key economic sectors and infrastructure.

To combat this, the deal also creates a mechanism for the three parties to deny Chinese or Russian investment in key Greenlandic sectors such as ports, airports and mines. Greenland is estimated to possess 1.5 million tons of critical mineral reserves. Denmark had already played a positive role in preventing malign investment in critical sectors of the Greenlandic economy, but the new deal formalizes the need and mechanism for doing this and gives the United States a say.

>>> How Norway Can Do More To Forestall the Russian Threat

Denmark has long been a very strong and reliable ally of the United States. Danish troops served in combat roles alongside Americans in Afghanistan after the 9/11 attacks and suffered one of the highest casualty rates in the war among our NATO allies. Denmark is one of the highest spenders on defense within the NATO alliance, had already reached 3.2 percent of gross domestic product as of 2025, and is projected to reach the mandated 3.5 percent spending level in the near future — among the first NATO allies to do so.

Much of Denmark’s new spending is going into capabilities relevant to defending its Arctic territory. The successful conclusion of this agreement, of course, will create new avenues for Arctic security cooperation between the American and Danish militaries.

The American, Danish and Greenlandic peoples have a shared interest in a secure Arctic where Russia is unable to threaten North America or Europe and where China (a non-Arctic state) is unable to meddle where it doesn’t belong.

This deal secures the sovereign security and economic interests of Washington, Copenhagen, and Nuuk and helps cement trans-Atlantic security cooperation in the Arctic for decades to come.

#1564. This Is Exactly What to Do With Your Peonies in October for Better Blooms Next Year

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Blowsy pink peonies in full bloom are one of the joys of late spring. But if you want to encourage a greater flush of flowers next year, October is the time to give them a little extra attention.

This doesn't mean getting overexcited and pruning before your peonies are ready. According to Julian Palphramand, head of plants at British Garden Centres, timing is everything.

“The main job for peonies in October is to let the foliage finish its work before cutting it back. The leaves are still feeding the roots and helping to form next year’s buds, so cutting them too early can reduce the following season’s flowers,” he tells us.

“Once the foliage has turned yellow and started to die back, cut it down to about five centimeters (about two inches) above ground level. Clear away the cut leaves and any other debris from the bed and dispose of it, as this helps reduce the risk of fungal diseases overwintering on the plant.”

If you're keen to grow even more plants next year, or perhaps want to move your peonies to a new spot, late October is the time to act.

“If your peony is established and you want to move or divide it, late October into early November is the ideal window,” Palphramand says. “Lift the clump carefully, keeping as much root as possible and making sure each division has at least three to five ‘eyes’ or buds, then replant with the crown no more than five centimeters below the surface. Don't plant too deep, as this can reduce flowering.”

In terms of caring for these plants, Palphramand also has a few key tips. “Add a thin layer of compost or well‑rotted manure around the base, but avoid covering the crown or letting mulch sit against the stems. If the autumn is dry, water at the base rather than overhead, as wet leaves can encourage disease.”

Palphramand also recommends not feeding your peonies now. Instead, he suggests saving your fertilizer for early spring when the flowers will most benefit.

Frida Kahlo (1907-54) is arguably the most recognisable artist in the world. The Mexican painter’s reputation grew following her death but over the past few decades her image has exploded in the popular consciousness, from being portrayed on the silver screen and referenced in The Simpsons, to having her image spray-painted on walls around the world and plastered on merchandise. This elevation to global fame is explored in the exhibition Frida: The Making of an Icon at Tate Modern (until 3 January 2027). Here, Beatriz García-Velasco, the exhibition’s assistant curator, picks out five key books for learning all about Kahlo’s life and career.

“A good place to start is with Hayden Herrera’s biography. Translated into more than 25 languages, it introduced Kahlo to vast global audiences and inadvertently catalysed the ‘Fridamania’ phenomenon. The book is a meticulously researched, incisive art-historical study that nevertheless reads like a page-turner. Drawing largely on first-hand interviews with those who knew Kahlo intimately, Herrera examines the artist’s work through her lived experience, with particular focus on Kahlo’s accident, lifelong disability and suffering.”

“Gannit Ankori’s writing brings a much-needed, fresh perspective. Questioning the essentialist readings that often rely on autobiographical myth or pseudo-psychoanalytical assumptions, Ankori argues that Kahlo was a trailblazer, who deliberately constructed her own identity and was ahead of her time in breaking taboos by openly portraying the female body, gender, cross-dressing, hybridity, identity and disability—themes that continue to resonate strongly with audiences today.”

“Functioning essentially as a catalogue raisonné, this monumental publication brings together exquisitely reproduced images, documentation and commentaries on every known painting by Kahlo. It’s a book you can lose hours in, turning its lush pages to inspect the detail and texture of her work.”

“For those wanting to get even closer to Kahlo, this accessible facsimile reproduces the notebook she kept over the final decade of her life, which remained hidden until her private rooms were unlocked in 2005. Turning these pages immerses you in her brilliant, and at times tormented, mind.”

“Written in Spanish, this is a personal and vital intervention by the Mexican curator and philosopher, Helena Chávez Mac Gregor. The title takes its cue from André Breton’s famous 1938 characterisation of Kahlo’s work as ‘a ribbon around a bomb’, a phrase Mac Gregor brilliantly upends by arguing that there was perhaps never any ribbon at all: only bombs, and they continue to detonate across time.”

Stimulating the brain with ultrasound could help counter the delusions people experience in schizophrenia, a small trial finds.

The trial involved transcranial ultrasound stimulation, in which a device placed on a patient's head delivers ultrasound waves through the skull and into the brain. Its goal is to change the activity in a targeted brain region.

Delusions are false beliefs that are very difficult to shake; for example, a person might believe they are being stalked when they aren't or think that a major disaster is about to occur without any evidence to suggest so. In the trial, researchers found that a 10-minute session of the ultrasound treatment reduced the severity of delusions for at least a week in patients with schizophrenia or a related disorder.

This targeted ultrasound treatment delivered directly to the brain could avoid some of the full-body effects of antipsychotic medications, such as weight gain and high blood pressure. That said, the new results, published Tuesday (Oct. 20) in the journal npj Acoustics, are a first step and will need further verification in larger trials.

"It's too early to know for certain what the therapeutic benefits will be, but it's very exciting," study co-author Dr. Donald Goff, a professor of psychiatry at the New York University Grossman School of Medicine, told Live Science.

The pilot study included 10 patients with either schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder. In both conditions, patients experience psychotic symptoms, including delusions and hallucinations. Schizoaffective disorder also includes mood symptoms, such as mania.

The researchers interviewed each participant to characterize the content of their delusions, and also documented their cognitive state and experiences with auditory hallucinations — perceptions of sounds that aren't actually occurring. The participants then received both an ultrasound treatment and a placebo treatment, spaced one week apart.

Other noninvasive therapies — such as transcranial magnetic stimulation, which uses magnetic fields — can also stimulate the brain. But the ultrasound technique differs from these therapies because ultrasound waves can penetrate deeper into the brain with greater specificity, study co-author Shy Shoham, a neural engineer at NYU Grossman, told Live Science. He compared the way a transducer directs ultrasound waves toward a given brain region with the way a lens focuses light.

In this case, the researchers focused this lens on a brain network called the cortico-striatal-pallido-thalamic circuit, which includes structures underneath the brain's outer cortex. The relationship between this circuit and schizophrenia symptoms is still somewhat unclear, partly because its deeper components are hard to study in adequate resolution with MRI, said co-author Dr. Wei Qi, a clinical assistant professor of psychiatry at NYU Grossman. However, some past studies have suggested that this network plays a part in schizophrenia.

The ultrasound treatment targeted a brain structure called the globus pallidus internus (orange).

Much of what scientists know about the network comes from research in movement disorders, such as Parkinson's disease, in which there is an imbalance between excitation and inhibition in the neurons that control movement. A similar dynamic could help to explain symptoms of schizophrenia, the scientists think.

"Our hypothesis is that, in schizophrenia, there is an imbalance between excitation and inhibition regarding what in the environment is actually important," Qi told Live Science. "The failure to inhibit irrelevant signals could be the basis of psychosis."

The researchers surveyed the study participants after each treatment, asking about the degree of belief each person had in their delusions and the amount of stress they caused. Compared with the placebo treatment, the ultrasound stimulation was associated with less-severe delusions over the following week. There was no significant change in patients' auditory hallucinations or overall cognition, though.

The ultrasound therapy came with mild side effects, such as fatigue and headache. Some patients had transient increases in delusions, which Qi attributed to normal fluctuations in symptoms that are expected in schizophrenia. These outcomes were just as common after the placebo treatment, so they may not be related to the ultrasound specifically.

The stimulation was aimed specifically at the right side of a brain structure called the globus pallidus internus (GPI). In a previous study, the authors found that greater connectivity between this region and another structure — the dorsal anterior cingulate gyrus — was linked to more-severe psychotic symptoms in schizophrenia. So, they made the GPI their target.

To date, "there haven't been many successful therapeutic attempts at targeting some of these deeper structures," said Dr. Deepak Sarpal, an associate professor of psychiatry at the University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine who was not involved with the study. Ultrasound waves reach brain regions under the cortex in a less-invasive manner than deep brain stimulation, in which a surgeon implants an electrode in a patient's brain, Sarpal noted.

The researchers are not the only group using transcranial ultrasound to influence the cortico-striatal-pallido-thalamic circuit. Earlier this year, another group stimulated portions of the striatum, which is located directly under the cortex, in patients with schizophrenia. They evaluated hallucinations rather than delusions, finding that the treatment reduced symptoms for at least a week after the procedure.

In this line of research, the targeted nature of transcranial ultrasound presents advantages as a research tool as well as a treatment approach.

"If we find that a specific circuit or a node in a circuit seems to be closely linked to symptoms we're trying to treat, then it will be a matter of understanding what pattern of stimulation may allow us to correct that," Goff said. Qi, Goff and colleagues now plan to continue testing their technique on more patients and to experiment with different doses of ultrasound.

"A lot more work needs to be done," Goff said, "but we're really quite optimistic about its promise."

#3717. A Beginner's Guide to Zone 2 Running: What It Is, Benefits, and How to Do It

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Use this guide to maximize the payoffs of your easy days.

For years, runners have used heart rate-based training to maximize their running potential. This method includes five “zones” at which your heart is beating at a percentage of its maximum capacity.

In zone 1, you’re warming up for a run, and in zone 5, you’re at an all-out sprint. Recently, zone 2 has gained MVP status among the five zones, thanks in part to longevity gurus like Peter Attia, M.D., who calls zone training one of the four essential “pillars of exercise” for staying healthy as we age.

The following guide teaches you everything you need to know about zone 2 running—and how to capitalize on its benefits.

🏃🏻‍♀️ Ready to take on your longest run yet? Our How to Run Longer program will help you make it happen!

Zone 2 running means staying within that second of five heart rate zones, which equals about 60 to 70 percent of your maximum heart rate. It’s the zone in which you can have a conversation, but not sing an entire tune.

For runners, zone 2 training calls for sustained efforts at a comfortable pace, and its a key workout on any training plan. “Zone 2 running is the foundation of long-distance running and helps prepare the body to tackle races ranging from a 5K to a marathon and beyond,” says Marnie Kunz, a NASM-certified personal trainer and USATF- and RRCA-certified running coach.

When you see easy runs on your training plan, you’re meant to run them in zone 2, along with most of your long runs.

Zone 2 training is how runners build aerobic fitness, which is essential for improving speed and endurance for race day. “It’s a foundational pace that helps build your aerobic engine, so that more difficult paces are more manageable,” says Jason Fitzgerald, a USATF-certified running coach and the founder of Strength Running.

“Technically, zone 2 is a physiological zone where blood lactate levels are stable,” he continues. At this easy pace, your body has enough time to clear lactate (a substance leftover in muscles from energy creation) from your blood.

Once blood lactate levels begin to rise, you shift into higher training zones, which begin working a different energy system. At lactate threshold, or anaerobic threshold, “this is the point where your blood lactate levels begin rising so quickly that you’re in an unsustainable state—you’re producing so much lactate that you can’t clear,” Fitzgerald says. This reaction creates a burning sensation in your legs that eventually creates too much fatigue for you to continue at that pace.

Higher-effort runs boost your body’s ability to clear lactate, so you can handle faster paces. But adequate zone 2 training helps you maximize the benefits of more intense runs. It also helps you build efficiency at any pace.

That’s because zone 2 training increases the number and size of mitochondria in your cells, Fitzgerald explains. Mitochondria are sites within cells that create the energy—also known as adenosine triphosphate (ATP)—your muscles use when running. With more mitochondria comes a more robust supply of energy you can pull from, enabling you to run longer without tiring.

Having more mitochondria helps you maximize the results from harder-effort sessions, such as VO2 max-boosting interval training (zones 4 and 5). “A VO2 max workout will make those mitochondria work more efficiently,” Fitzgerald says. The more mitochondria you have to work efficiently, the greater your capacity to improve your pace.

“Zone 2 running also offers the benefit of being less hard on your body, making recovery time faster than higher-intensity workouts like speed training,” Kunz says. “This allows you to run more frequently than you could if you were training in higher heart rate zones.”

Finally, practicing zone 2 running helps you become more efficient at using fat as fuel, which takes longer to burn and can also help you go longer. Once you get comfortable in zone 2, you should be able to go almost any distance (as long as you’re adequately hydrated and fueled).

There are a few ways to determine your zone 2 pace. If you’re a tech-focused runner, use a smartwatch or another device to check that your heart rate stays within 60 to 70 percent of your maximum heart rate for zone 2 training, Kunz says.

You can also use different formulas to calculate your maximum heart rate (220 minus age is the most common one), but there’s often too much individual variability for the calculation to be accurate, Fitzgerald says. Instead, he recommends tracking your heart rate during an all-out workout and using your highest heart rate to determine your max, and then estimate your zone 2 range from there.

If you prefer not to watch your heart rate while you run, keep tabs on your effort. “[Zone 2] is a comfortable, controlled, conversational effort where you can speak in mostly full sentences,” Fitzgerald says.

Using the rate of perceived exertion (RPE) scale—which is a method of measuring how hard you feel your body working during physical activity—can also be a useful tech-free method. The scale runs from 0 to 10, with 0 equaling no exertion and 10 meaning pushing yourself to the maximum. Stay around a 4 out of 10 on the RPE scale to tap into zone 2, Kunz says.

It’s also smart to use RPE and the talk test to check the accuracy of your smartwatch zone 2 reading. When you’re running at a conversational pace and RPE of 4, check your pulse to find your heart rate. And then see if it lines up with what your watch is telling you.

Most runners should spend 80 percent of their total mileage in zone 2, while newer runners may benefit from dedicating 90 percent to zone 2, says Fitzgerald. The remainder of your training should be conducted in zones 3 to 5.

As mentioned, you’ll see zone 2 runs on your training plan in the form of easy runs and long runs, which typically make up the majority of your mileage for the week.

One drawback to zone 2 is the time commitment. “You need a higher volume of zone 2 stimulus to see results,” Fitzgerald says.

The ideal zone 2 session length depends on your fitness level and training goals. “If you’re training for a 5K, a 25- to 35-minute zone 2 session will be one of your most common workouts. For longer races, such as half marathon and marathon training, your zone 2 runs may be 40 minutes and more,” Kunz says.

Generally, you’ll start seeing positive adaptations from consistent zone 2 (aerobic) training in two to four weeks, Fitzgerald says. However, it may take a little longer to see results if you’re well-conditioned, typically four to six weeks or longer.

“Aerobic training is one of those things where you’ll continue seeing results even 10 years after you’ve started running,” Fitzgerald says. The key is gradually increasing your mileage or time spent in zone 2 and sprinkling in more strenuous sessions in zones 3 to 5 (making up that 20 percent of your week) so your progress doesn’t stall.

You won’t see gains if you’re not consistent, though. Running in zone 2 at least twice a week, with harder sessions mixed in, is key.

#2093. The separation and expansion of powers between Congress and the president

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

This is the fourth and final part in a series for SCOTUSblog recounting two decades of constitutional change through the five editions of our casebook, the first published in 2008 and the latest to be published this December.

The late 1990s and early 2000s ushered in the Rehnquist federalism revolution, which appeared to place limits on Congress’ powers and expand those of the states. Then came Sept. 11, 2001, and the global war on terror, which seemed poised to expand the president’s powers. Yet, by 2008, Congress’ powers seemed without meaningful limits and the president’s efforts to expand his own powers had failed. And the separation of powers remained, well, separate. Nevertheless, the next two decades would vastly enlarge the president’s powers as Congress’ authority over the executive branch would be greatly diminished.

Late-breaking changes for the legislative power

Most of the chapter on legislative powers has remained the same since our textbook’s first edition was published in 2008. The Rehnquist court’s federalism revolution hit a roadblock three years earlier. Gonzales v. Raich, which Barnett argued, halted the progress made by United States v. Lopez and United States v. Morrison. Those cases found limits on Congress’ powers to regulate guns in school zones and domestic violence through the commerce clause. But Raich upheld Congress’ powers to regulate locally grown marijuana. The second edition from 2013 ushered in NFIB v. Sebelius. This case held that the Affordable Care Act’s individual mandate exceeds Congress’ powers under the commerce and necessary and proper clauses, but the penalty enforcing the mandate could be saved as a constitutional tax.

No further changes were made in the following editions. Indeed, it seemed to both of us that the justices were unwilling to look more closely at the scope of federal enumerated powers. But this year’s edition introduces two unexpected enumerated powers cases. Landor v. Louisiana Department of Public Safety and Corrections clarified the relationship between the spending power and the necessary and proper clause. As a general matter, Congress can only make state officials subject to suits for money damages if the law complies with the necessary and proper clause. Landor ruled that the Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act does not provide a private cause of action for monetary damages against state officials who did not consent to suit, as such a law is not a “proper” exercise of federal power. We also included Justice Clarence Thomas’s concurrence in United States v. Hemani, which proposed tightening the so-called “jurisdictional hook” analysis. He would require a closer fit between interstate commerce and a regulated activity.

The future looks promising for a reconsideration of enumerated powers.

The contracting and expanding executive power

In March 2008, when the first edition of our casebook was published, the Supreme Court was primarily occupied by President George W. Bush’s global war on terror. The first edition of the casebook included Hamdi v. Rumsfeld and Hamdan v. Rumsfeld. These cases rejected early challenges to the detention of suspected terrorists at Guantanamo Bay. In June 2008, the court would conclude the trilogy with Boumediene v. Bush, which held that the writ of habeas corpus runs to that prison facility.

But that case would be a fleeting flash, as the global war on terror faded from relevance – at least jurisprudentially. Moreover, most first-year law students were born after Sept. 11, 2001, and have no living memory of the events that gave rise to the conflict in Afghanistan. What seemed so critical in the decade after 9/11 has waned significantly in prominence before the court.

The realignment of the separation of powers

Perhaps the most significant revisions across five editions of the casebook came in the context of the separation of powers. Labeling these cases is tricky. Cases not only move in and out of the canon but also shift in how they are perceived. Many cases started out under the label of separation of powers – as the court enforced the balance of authority between the branches – but now might be considered executive power cases.

Only three separation-of-powers cases have survived all five editions. First, United States v. Nixon required the president to comply with a subpoena for the Watergate tapes. Second, Morrison v. Olson rejected a challenge to the independent counsel statute. And third, Clinton v. Jones held that a sitting president is subject to a civil suit for conduct that arose before his term began. We pondered at some length whether to retain Morrison, as it has largely been supplanted by Trump v. Slaughter. This 2026 decision formally overruled 1935’s Humphrey’s Executor v. United States and allowed the president to remove members of certain independent agencies.

Three Burger court staples were dropped. Dames & Moore v. Regan upheld the president’s suspension of claims arising from the Iran hostage crisis. Nixon v. Fitzgerald ruled that former President Richard Nixon was immune from suit for certain actions taken while in office. INS v. Chadha rejected the so-called “one house” veto, or the ability of either chamber of Congress to prevent an action by the executive branch. In the 1980s, these cases were landmark decisions. Over time, however, their relevance faded. Still, as reflected in Justice Neil Gorsuch’s concurrence in Learning Resources v. Trump, the 2026 tariffs case, Chadha’s effect has been magnified as the court has ceded more power to the president over executive branch agencies, even as those delegations were originally enacted with legislative vetoes.

Three decisions from the early Roberts court quickly entered and left the chat. Trump v. Slaughter replaced Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB and Seila Law v. CFPB. These cases, which rejected attempts to limit the president’s removal powers, simply lost relevance. NLRB v. Noel Canning was an important case about the original meaning of the recess appointments clause, but it has become largely obsolete as the past three presidents have used other means to make short-term appointments.

President Donald Trump has been the most consequential president for the Supreme Court’s separation-of-powers jurisprudence since Franklin D. Roosevelt. Five cases bearing his name now appear in the separation-of-powers chapter. Trump v. Vance, which concerned the president’s tax returns, was added before the fourth edition went to press. The fifth edition now provides a Trump quartet. Trump v. United States expanded the scope of presidential immunity from the Nixon era. Trump v. Slaughter, as noted, at last overruled Humphrey’s Executor, as Morrison v. Olson remains in a zone of twilight. Trump v. Cook reaffirmed the Federal Reserve's independence. And Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump invalidated the president’s tariffs. These cases, Slaughter in particular, will likely trigger an entire generation of new cases concerning the separation of powers. Who knows what the sixth edition will bring?

Up to the fourth edition of our casebook in 2022, constitutional law followed a somewhat predictable arc. To be sure, there were shifts to the left and to the right, but the doctrine could be explained by a coherent narrative: the New Deal settlement on federal power was untouchable; the court would offer only modest protections for federalism; the administrative state was beyond reproach; attempts to single out unpopular groups would be halted; “benign” racial classifications were permitted; and church and state were strictly separated. Now, each aspect of this narrative has been reconceived. The five years since that edition appeared have brought the most changes to constitutional law since the Warren court.

We began this series by observing that lawyers tend to internalize the constitutional law that existed when they attended law school. The same is true of law professors. Virtually all law professors today came of age in a time of stasis. They could safely assume that the trajectory of the Warren court would continue indefinitely. But the jurisprudence of the Burger and Rehnquist courts was primed for a reversal, just as the Warren court reversed so many precedents from before. Ultimately, the Roberts court continues to chip away at precedents from the 1970s and 1980s, even if the precedents from the 1960s and before remain.

Many of these professors have struggled to cope with sudden changes in the doctrine. Some have voiced their despair of teaching constitutional law at all. Unsurprisingly, their students also struggle to cope with these developments. This trend is regrettable. Our role as casebook editors, and constitutional law professors, is to guide students through the entire corpus of constitutional law, from the framing to the present day, without any nostalgia for, or bias against, times gone by.

It occurred to me that I might be a lousy friend as I turned uphill, abandoning my buddy Ed to shiver it out alone at our agreed-upon meeting place way off on the opposite side of the drainage. The willow-choked ridge above me was pockmarked with the first fresh mule deer tracks I’d seen in miles of churning through this ankle-twisting hellscape of the Idaho mountains. For every craggy brown rock that pimpled up through the snow there were 50 more hidden below — all threatening to send me careening sideways with every step. I followed the tracks upward, scanning the patchwork of evergreens and open glades as I went.

The buck was standing high up on an open bench when I poked my head into a clearing. There was a line of snow-laden ponderosas between us that I’d hoped would provide some cover, but he caught movement and bounded away before I could set up. Under different circumstances I would have taken that moment to kick myself for blowing it, but the late afternoon sun was still well above the frosty peaks and fresh snow means second chances. I was certain I’d be able to track him down.

It had only taken him a heartbeat to top the ridge, so I took advantage of the terrain’s cover and broke into a run. Too fast, it turned out. I hadn’t made it more than 20 yards before I face-planted into the powdery hillside with the kind of silent thud that must’ve looked right out of a Charlie Chaplin flick. I gave myself a quick dusting and checked that there was no snow plugging the barrel of my Tikka, then climbed at a more deliberate pace and hunched low at the crest. There was no trouble finding his course where he had hurried toward the tree line. No longer in a rush, I trailed on.

I caught sight of his legs before I saw the rest of him, ambling through a field 200 yards away. The smattering of drooping firs between us obscured his eyes and I dropped prone to click my bipod into place.

The initial zing of seeing him for the first time had worn off in the chase and my breath was calm as I looked him over. He was a good one: 4×4, thick-beamed antlers that were walnut-dark, with kickers protruding like gnarled antennae. Exactly what I was looking for. I watched him take another step, solitary and alert in the center of that open glade, my finger still well away from the trigger. Then I watched him disappear.

I stared, puzzled, at the vacant landscape. Then in a blur a dark-antlered head came rocketing back up from below the ground into which it had just vanished, breaking through slabs of what I now recognized as ice.

There was a layer of snow on the frozen lake in front of me that I had just mistaken for a field. Despite frigid temperatures, that must have been enough insulation to keep the crust from thickening to a safe depth. The deer had just reached the rotten center when I came up on him.

The buck was a frantic wrecking ball of hoof and antler as he smashed a trail towards the far bank. Every attempt to climb out of the water ended in the snap of cracking ice and a gray body sliding back into the turbid slush. But all that commotion was pushing him, half heaving and half swimming, ever closer to the far shore.

I kept him in the scope, dazzled by what I was seeing but confident I could get a good shot when he made dry land. Onward he went. Crack. Splash. Scrape. Twenty yards from bank. Ten. Four. Four. Four yards, and no further.

From the even way he held his head, he looked to be standing on the bed of the lake with his full body below the neck still immersed in freezing water. The chaotic chorus of splintering ice was gone, replaced in a flash by the deadened silence of the snowy mountains around us.

Whether he was still too deep in the water to get enough upward momentum or whether the lake muck kept sucking his back legs down, he couldn’t summon enough energy to break through the ice that had grown ever thicker the closer he got to shore.

I waited in that eerie silence and watched him for a long time, the wind swirling softly as the sun traced west toward the jagged horizon. Every couple of minutes a flurry would erupt. He was still trying to escape, throwing his forelegs onto the ice shelf and clambering for purchase before slinking back down again in the wake of another failed effort. But soon his breaks were getting longer and longer, and I eventually realized the inevitable: He was going to freeze to death.

I wasn’t in love with the idea of shooting a deer in this kind of trapped extremis. I recalled an old story about hunters who had driven a herd of elk into a deep snow drift in order to kill them, and how that hunt was used in a hunter education class as an example of a classic violation of fair chase. But if I took myself out of this particular situation, I wondered how the buck would fare. Not well, I concluded. I eventually let my finger move toward the trigger and the next time the buck launched his body upward, I fired.

The retrieval was going to be a mess. I had assumed the reeds that circled the shore were on dry land, but the soft crunch of snow gave way to the cracking of ice before I reached them and I was up to my boot tops in watery slush much sooner than I’d hoped. The dead buck was stuck fast, his snout buried firm under the ice shelf. I had nothing strong enough to break the thickening ice and he was locked in there tight enough that my attempt to lasso his antlers with paracord and haul him up did nothing more than cut rope burns into my hands and waist.

“Well,” Ed said as he walked up, guided by the sound of rifle fire, “I’ll help you carry it out — but I’m not going swimming.”

I nodded in resignation. Time to go in after him. I debated taking off my boots, but the serrated ice floating like daggers all around made me think better of it.

“We’ll get a fire to warm me up afterwards,” I said hopefully as I stepped in.

The ice broke beneath my feet and the muck below sucked at my boots as I moved, the water rushing into my gaiters and climbing higher as I tried to keep as much of my body above the water as possible. I was up to my waist by the time I reached the buck’s antler, already frozen and slick and hard to hold onto.

I pressed my hands against the ice, slicing my knuckles in the process but cracking through the slabs that the buck hadn’t been able to free himself of. He may have been the more powerful animal, but I was in shallower water. Being two-legged and six feet tall has its advantages.

The deer was more mobile once the ice was free and I turned to haul him backward, but the bobbing chunks of ice offered no purchase and the puckering slime grabbed my ankles. I had only made it a few feet before feeling a flash of panic as a chunk of submerged ice trapped my foot and sent me tumbling halfway under. Another half yard in the wrong direction and I would have plunged headlong down. But Ed’s outstretched hand was just within reach and I grabbed hold before I fully submerged, leaning forward as he pulled up and shimmying the last couple of yards onto some semblance of solid land.

I mumbled my thanks to Ed as I knelt on the bank and breathed warm air onto my raw fingers, taking a moment to admire the deer before my eyes turned upward to the landscape. The white world around us was beautiful in the failing light but barren and damp and growing steadily colder. The sleeves of my shirt and everything below my belly was soaked through and already starting to stiffen as it froze. I hadn’t felt my feet since stepping in the water.

“It’s your call,” Ed said, “but it’s not going to be very easy to make a fire around here.”

Ed is the kind of organized and resourceful guy who doesn’t have to bother telling you he had been an Eagle Scout, and I couldn’t help but agree with his assessment. After a short debate we decided to make the best use of the daylight we had left and pack out as quickly as we could. We raced the sun to get all the meat into game bags, my hands nimble enough to hold a knife but numb enough that the clumsy cuts I made to my fingers barely registered. Soon enough we were loaded up and on our way.

There are downsides to a long packout over rough terrain in the dark. But one thing it has going for it is that hard work means heat. We hiked with our heads down, following the boot tracks we had made hiking in that morning, and by the second mile I could start to feel the comforting pain of blood returning to my thawing toes. Fear of frostbite fading, we hiked to the faint crackle of my frozen clothes. Somewhere up the mountain, that shallow pond was already starting to freeze over again.

This was the first time I had seen an animal misjudge the safety of a frozen lake, and it ran counter to my general assumption that deer are so mythically in tune with their natural world that a mistake like that would be next to impossible. While not exactly an everyday occurrence, the best answer I got from the deer biologist I spoke with afterward was, “It happens.” A fall over a cliff or through ice is particularly possible if an animal has been pushed or are otherwise distracted, as was doubtless the case with my buck. There are even reports of bull elk coming to grief in this way mid-fight, their focus so fully on their rival that both crash through ice into the frigid depths with their antlers still locked together.

So, it happens. Maybe not often, but in this case things definitely could have gone worse. It was no picnic to be drenched in sub-20-degree weather and miles from the trailhead, but that wasn’t much compared to the pickle I would have been in if I had fired a hair faster while that deer was still standing tall and dry and far from shore on that treacherous surface. So the next time I come across a big buck in a snowy meadow, I’ll still check his antlers — but I’ll also check his feet.

#1044. The Best Trader Joe’s Products to Buy If It’s Your First Visit

I asked our editors what they'd recommend to someone shopping at Trader Joe's for the very first time. They had opinions, and so do I.

When I lived in San Francisco, I had a Trader Joe’s within walking distance of my apartment and visited regularly. I just never bought much in the way of groceries. I went for the wine. (And in my defense, Trader Joe’s has an excellent wine selection.) I was completely unaware of what I was missing in the rest of the store though!

One afternoon, on a whim, I picked up a tub of hummus and a bag of pita chips to pair with my sauvignon blanc. When I got home and started snacking, I realized that for years I’d been walking past an entire store full of things I might love. I went back the next day to fill my cart with things I’d never noticed before. That trip made a convert out of me.

These days, I live on the Oregon coast, two hours away from my nearest Trader Joe’s. My visits require quite a bit more planning, usually involving a long shopping list and a cooler or two, but I still make the trip whenever I can. The wine remains a staple, though I’ve since developed strong opinions about everything from the hashbrowns to the blueberry goat cheese.

Of course, Trader Joe’s has so many interesting products that a first visit can be a little overwhelming. Between the cult-favorite frozen foods, inventive snacks, excellent cheeses and affordable pantry staples, it’s difficult to know where to begin. So I asked some of my Taste of Home colleagues to share their favorites. Here are the products we’d recommend putting in your cart first.

There’s a reason Trader Joe’s Mandarin Orange Chicken has such a devoted following. The breaded chicken cooks up crisp, and the accompanying sauce delivers the sweet, tangy flavors you’d expect from a takeout favorite. It’s an excellent introduction to the store’s freezer aisle, particularly for anyone who appreciates having easy weeknight dinners on hand. Taste of Home editor Lesley Balla has a useful tip: Don’t use all the sauce provided. Start with less and add more to taste.

The Trader Joe’s cheese selection deserves a visit of its own, and this blueberry vanilla chevre is one of my longtime favorites. The soft, tangy goat cheese is coated with a sweet blueberry mixture, with vanilla rounding out the flavors. I love it with crackers, although it’s equally wonderful spread over a toasted bagel or on a charcuterie board. It’s an easy way to make an ordinary afternoon snack feel a little more special.

Senior editor Lindsay Mattison credits this salsa with helping turn her into a Trader Joe’s regular, and it’s an excellent place to begin exploring the store’s condiments. Salsa Autentica is a smooth, tomato-based salsa with a little heat, making it especially appealing to anyone who prefers a pourable salsa over a chunky one. It’s great with tortilla chips, spooned over tacos or added to scrambled eggs. Lindsay discovered it around the same time as another favorite, Trader Joe’s corn taco shells, and the two paired together make an excellent introduction to the store.

Trader Joe’s frozen hashbrowns have a permanent place in my freezer. My kids will eat them just about any way I serve them, but they’re most fond of loading them like loaded baked potatoes with sour cream, shredded cheese and crispy bacon. Lesley is a fan, too, and likes topping hers with scrambled eggs and salsa. Between the two approaches, there’s a strong case for keeping more than one package on hand.

An acai bowl is an excellent excuse to eat something that looks like dessert for breakfast, especially when you’re generous with the toppings. Rosie keeps Trader Joe’s Unsweetened Organic Acai Puree Packets on hand for exactly that reason. She tops her bowls with strawberries, blueberries, sliced banana, coconut, sunflower kernels and honey. The acai itself has an earthy, slightly tart flavor, which makes a lovely contrast to the sweet fruit and honey. And since the puree comes in individual frozen packets, there’s no need to commit to making a whole blender’s worth.

When someone buys a product 10 bags at a time, I’m inclined to pay attention. That’s exactly what editor Lauren Pahmeier does with Trader Joe’s Spicy Alfredo Fusilloni. The oversized, spiral-shaped pasta comes in a creamy sauce with a little heat, making it a particularly appealing freezer find for pasta lovers. Lauren says the enormous pasta shapes are half the fun, and I can certainly respect that.

French butter has a way of making ordinary butter seem like it isn’t trying very hard. Trader Joe’s Cultured Salted Butter has a rich flavor and subtle tang that comes from culturing the cream, making it a wonderful spread on good bread. Lesley is an enthusiastic supporter, and this is an excellent purchase for anyone who believes butter should be more than just a background ingredient.

Hummus was my gateway into Trader Joe’s groceries, and it’s still one of the first things I look for when I visit. Associate community editor Ivis Estrada shares my enthusiasm, particularly for the Mediterranean Style Hummus, which is rich, creamy and generously seasoned. It’s wonderful with fresh vegetables, spread onto sandwiches or served alongside warm pita bread. If you’re new to Trader Joe’s, I recommend making the hummus selection one of your first stops.

You could make a compelling argument that this seasoning blend helped launch an entire grocery-store obsession. A combination of sesame seeds, poppy seeds, dried garlic, diced onion and salt, this blend brings the familiar flavors of an everything bagel to practically anything you sprinkle it over. Try it on avocado toast, eggs, roasted vegetables or homemade crackers. Lesley counts Trader Joe’s spice blends among her favorites, and this one is a perfect starting point.

Sometimes nostalgia is all the encouragement you need to try something. Lindsay spotted Trader Joe’s corn taco shells on the shelf and realized she hadn’t eaten a crunchy taco in years. The shells have that familiar crisp texture that makes an old-fashioned taco night so satisfying. Fill them with seasoned ground beef, shredded lettuce, cheese and a generous spoonful of Salsa Autentica.

Salsa is one of those things Trader Joe’s does exceptionally well, and Lauren’s recommendation is the Pepita Salsa. Made with pumpkin seeds and roasted red peppers, it has a rich, savory flavor that goes well beyond the usual tomatoes and chiles. Lauren likes stirring it into scrambled eggs, although it’s equally delicious with tortilla chips, tacos or grilled meats. This one is a great alternative for anyone looking to mix up their salsa game.

Some grocery store sweets are perfectly pleasant. Others inspire people to hide the container from themselves. Trader Joe’s Dark Chocolate Peanut Butter Cups fall firmly into the second category. The combination of dark chocolate and creamy, salty peanut butter has earned them a devoted following, including editors Lesley and Rosie. Rosie even ventures that they’re better than Reese’s, which is a bold statement about a peanut butter cup. Lesley, meanwhile, warns against buying them if you have any intention of exercising restraint.

A frozen pizza with a deep-fried crust is already an intriguing proposition, but add roasted garlic and pesto, and you’ve got something considerably more interesting than the usual freezer-aisle offering. Ivis calls this one of the best frozen pizzas she’s ever had. It’s a good reminder that a frozen pizza from Trader Joe’s can be every bit as exciting as something you’d order from your local pizzeria.

Hot honey has found its way onto everything from pizza to ice cream, so popcorn was probably inevitable. Trader Joe’s hot honey popcorn coats the kernels in a thin, crunchy layer of honey sweetness with enough cayenne to make its presence known. Editor Caroline Stanko was so taken with the caramel corn-like crunch that she gave it a Taste of Home Award. She even used the popcorn as a cocktail garnish, proving that some snacks have ambitions beyond the bowl.

Fiocchetti are little pasta parcels, and Trader Joe’s version comes filled with cheese and coated in a creamy tomato-based pink sauce. Ivis makes a point of picking up at least one package whenever she shops there. It’s another excellent example of the store’s knack for turning frozen pasta into something that feels a little more special than a last-minute dinner.

Not everything worth buying at Trader Joe’s comes in an inventive flavor or colorful package. Lindsay recently discovered how affordable the store’s nuts and seeds can be and now stocks up on slivered almonds, sunflower seeds and cashew pieces for homemade granola. Sometimes the best grocery store discovery is simply finding a better place to buy something you already use.

This sweet, savory corn salsa is one of Ivis’s favorites for making copycat Chipotle bowls at home. The combination of corn, peppers and spices make it equally useful for tacos, burritos and grilled chicken, and its chunky texture adds a welcome contrast to rice and beans. It also makes an excellent topping for grilled fish. Just add a squeeze of fresh lime!

Trader Joe’s wine section was my introduction to the store, and it remains one of my favorite reasons to visit. Corvelia Cabernet Sauvignon is one of the bottles I regularly bring home, often in embarrassingly large quantities. It’s a true California Cab with dark fruit notes and a little oak, without the price tag that sometimes accompanies wines in this style. It’s lovely alongside a grilled ribeye, where the richness of the beef gives the wine’s tannins a beautiful balance.

Our head of editorial, Ellie Martin Cliffe, nominated Trader Joe’s fresh flowers, a recommendation I wholeheartedly second. The floral section is one of my favorite parts of the store, with seasonal offerings that range from plush peonies and colorful tulips to hydrangeas, roses and fresh eucalyptus, sold in generous bunches. They’re definitely worth one last stop before you head for the checkout.

Between the new foods you’ll be bringing home and the flowers to arrange alongside them, your first Trader Joe’s trip should give you plenty to look forward to once you’ve unpacked the grocery bags.
