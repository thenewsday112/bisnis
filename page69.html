#3657. Fake or Fortune art historian donates major Anglo-Indian art collection to Tate

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Rudolph Swoboda, A Sepoy in HH Maharaja Srinagar’s Indian Regiment, 23 Years Old (around 1886)

A UK collector has donated a significant collection of 50 works by artists who lived and worked in India during the 18th and 19th centuries to the Tate in London. The collection, given by the UK art historian Charles Greig, includes works by Indian artists, such as Sita Ram and Ghulam Ali Khan, and European artists, including Johan Zoffany and Rudolf Swoboda, along with British figures such as Thomas Daniell and William Daniell.

The works throw light on a “period of profound cultural, social and political change brought about by British colonialism in South Asia” says a Tate statement. Key pieces donated include three watercolours by Sita Ram who was instrumental in creating works known as “Company paintings”, watercolours created in a European style for foreign patrons. The British Library in London has a large collection of watercolours by Ram.

Sita Ram, The Palace at Murshidabad from the River (around 1814-15) Presented by Charles Andrew Greig

Greig says in a statement: “Most of the works were made in the vanished world before photography and they provide us with a visual record of our rich shared history from about 1770. Assembling this collection has been my life’s primary passion, but at no stage did I ever see myself as other than a temporary steward.”

Asked why Greig opted for Tate, a spokesperson tells The Art Newspaper: “Tate has long held a few key paintings related to Anglo-Indian history of the period, including Zoffany’s Colonel Mordant’s Cock Match (around 1784) and Indian landscapes by Thomas Daniell and William Hodges”. Greig recently appeared in an episode of the television series Fake or Fortune?, in which he assessed a portrait of an Indian woman by the 18th-century Scottish artist Katherine Read.

A free display of the works drawn from the Greig collection is due to open at Tate Britain in 2028.

#430. The secret to slower aging may be hiding in your gut

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists have uncovered a surprising connection between the bacteria living in the human gut and the speed at which the body ages. Researchers at the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa found that certain gut microbes are associated with slower biological aging, while others are linked to faster aging.

The study, published in Scientific Reports, found that differences in gut bacterial communities accounted for approximately 15% of the variation in participants' biological aging rates. The results suggest that the gut microbiome, the vast community of microorganisms living in the digestive system, may provide valuable clues about aging at the molecular level.

"This study advances our understanding of the relationship between the gut microbiome and biological aging," said senior author Alika K. Maunakea of the UH Mānoa John A. Burns School of Medicine. "We found that microbial patterns in the gut were significantly associated with molecular measures of aging, even independent of chronological age. These findings open important new directions for understanding how modifiable biological systems influence long-term health trajectories and longevity."

Scientists Identify Gut Bacteria Linked to Faster and Slower Aging

To investigate the relationship between gut bacteria and aging, researchers examined stool and blood samples collected from 123 adults across a wide range of ages. They identified the bacteria present in each participant's gut and compared those findings with blood-based molecular markers used to estimate the body's rate of aging.

These biological measurements differ from chronological age, which simply reflects how many years someone has been alive. Instead, they are designed to assess how quickly aging-related changes are occurring within the body.

The researchers found a notable association between the composition of the gut microbiome and DunedinPACE, an advanced biomarker that measures the pace of biological aging.

Some bacterial species stood out for their particularly strong relationships with aging. Bifidobacterium adolescentis was among the microbes most strongly associated with slower biological aging. In contrast, Succinivibrio dextrinosolvens showed one of the strongest associations with faster aging.

These differences suggest that particular patterns of gut bacteria may offer important information about how rapidly the body is aging.

How Diet, Lifestyle, and Environment May Influence Gut Health

Lead author Braden P. Kunihiro said the discovery adds to growing evidence that the gut microbiome could play an important role in precision health research, which aims to better understand individual differences in health and disease.

Unlike chronological age, the composition of the gut microbiome can change in response to everyday influences, including diet, lifestyle, and environmental conditions. This makes it a particularly interesting area of investigation for scientists studying healthy aging.

"The gut microbiome is highly responsive to lifestyle, diet, environment and other everyday exposures," Kunihiro said. "Our findings suggest that the microbiome may reflect important aspects of biological resilience and aging in ways that could eventually help inform future wellness and healthy aging strategies."

The research also included Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander participants, groups that have historically received limited representation in studies of aging and gut microorganisms.

Co-author Ruben Juarez explained that including these populations could help scientists investigate how social circumstances and environmental conditions leave lasting biological effects over the course of a person's life.

"The microbiome exists at the intersection of behavior, environment and human biology," Juarez said. "Studies like this help illuminate potential pathways through which lived experiences and social factors influence long-term health outcomes."

Could Changing Gut Bacteria Influence How We Age?

Although the findings reveal a meaningful relationship between gut bacteria and biological aging, researchers stress that the study does not establish cause and effect. In other words, finding certain microbes in people who age more slowly does not necessarily mean those bacteria are responsible for slowing the aging process.

Further studies will be needed to determine whether deliberately changing the gut microbiome can influence biological aging or improve long-term health.

Still, the findings offer a promising starting point for exploring how the microorganisms living inside us relate to the aging process and whether they could eventually help guide more personalized approaches to healthy aging.

"This is an important early step toward understanding the gut microbiome's relationship with healthy aging," Maunakea said. "Future research will help clarify the mechanisms involved and whether these microbial signatures may eventually contribute to precision approaches for promoting long-term health and wellness."

Most people live with a great deal more suffering than is visible to even the most proximate and sensitive onlooker. Many have survived things both unimaginable and invisible to the outside world. This has been the case since the dawn of our species, for human nature has hardly changed beneath the continually repainted façade of our social sanctions — human beings have always been capable of inflicting tremendous pain on each other and capable of triumphal healing.

There is, however, a peculiar modern phenomenon that might best be described as a culture of competitive trauma. In recent times, the touching human longing for sympathy, that impulse to have our suffering recognized and validated, has grown distorted by a troubling compulsion for broadcast-suffering and comparative validity. Personhoods are staked on the cards dealt and not the hands played, as if we evolved the opposable thumbs of our agency for nothing. In memoirs and reality shows, across infinite Alexandrian scrolls of social media feeds, the unlucky events of life have become the currency of attention and identification.

There is a way, with moderate moral imagination and considerable countercultural courage, to subvert this tendency without turning away from the reality and magnitude of suffering that we do live with — a way to esteem in attention and admiration not the unluckiness of what has happened to us but the luckiness that, despite it, we have become the people we are and have the lives we have by the sheer unwillingness to stay in that small dark place, which is at heart a willingness to be larger than our hurt selves.

It is not a new way of reframing personal narrative (which, after all, is the neuropsychological pillar of identity). It is a very old way, common to many of the world’s ancient traditions but most clearly and creatively articulated by the Stoic philosopher Marcus Aurelius (April 26, 121–March 17, 180).

Because the modern mind calculates validity of vantage point by estimating the comparative value of suffering, it must be observed that, later in life, Marcus Aurelius had it easier than most of his contemporaries, being Emperor; it must also be observed that, earlier in life, he had it harder than most, being a fatherless child and a queer teenager in Roman antiquity, epochs before the notion of LGBTQ rights, or for that matter most human rights. It is hardly surprising that he turned to Stoicism for succor and training in living with the uncertainty of events and the certainty of loss.

His timeless Meditations (public library), newly translated and annotated by the British classics scholar Robin Waterfield, were the original self-help — Marcus wrote these notebooks primarily as notes to himself while learning how to live: how to live with more agency, equanimity, and even joy in a world violently unpredictable at all times and especially so in his time.

In one of those self-counsels, Marcus Aurelius considers the key to regarding one’s own life, and living it, with positive realism:

Be like a headland: the waves beat against it continuously, but it stands fast and around it the boiling water dies down. “It’s my rotten luck that this has happened to me.” On the contrary, “It’s my good luck that, although this has happened to me, I still feel no distress, since I’m unbruised by the present and unconcerned about the future.” What happened could have happened to anyone, but not everyone could have carried on without letting it distress him. So why regard the incident as a piece of bad luck rather than seeing your avoidance of distress as a piece of good luck? Do you generally describe a person as unlucky when his nature worked well? Or do you count it as a malfunction of a person’s nature when it succeeds in securing the outcome it wanted?

With an eye to “what human nature wants” — what life ultimately demands as it lives itself through us, and what our highest answer is — he concludes:

Can what happened to you stop you from being fair, high-minded, moderate, conscientious, unhasty, honest, moral, self-reliant, and so on — from possessing all the qualities that, when present, enable a man’s* nature to be fulfilled? So then, whenever something happens that might cause you distress, remember to rely on this principle: this is not bad luck, but bearing it valiantly is good luck.

Complement with an equally counterintuitive and perspective-broadening modern case for the luckiness of death and Alan Watts on the ambiguity of good and bad luck, then revisit other highlights from the indispensable Meditations: Marcus Aurelius on how to handle disappointing people, the key to living with presence, the most potent motivation for work, and how to begin each day for maximum serenity of mind.

#3589. emma sicher grows biomaterials from wild berries and microorganisms

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

with bacteria and locally sourced plants, biodesigner emma sicher grows microbial cellulose into edible prototypes and packaging.

Berlin-based biodesigner Emma Sicher is experimenting with materials grown by bacteria and yeast, turning microbial cellulose into everything from translucent packaging to edible food prototypes. Some of her samples resemble crinkled pieces of pink glass, while others form glossy, gelatinous spheres. Their unusual colors and textures emerge through fermentation, a process in which microorganisms help produce the material itself.

At the center of her practice is SCOBY, a symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast commonly associated with kombucha. As the microorganisms grow in a nutrient-rich liquid, cellulose-producing bacteria form a gelatinous membrane at the surface. This can be harvested and processed into flexible sheets, or developed into different forms for food and material applications.

Emma Sicher’s EATING SCOBY project explores the culinary possibilities of this cellulose-rich membrane. Developed during her MA in Eco-Social Design, the research involved food microbiologists and consultation with chefs. Among the prototypes are SCOBY chips that begin crunchy before becoming chewy and slightly gelatinous in the mouth. An experimental ice cream cone offers another application, with the possibility of an inner coating of melted chocolate.

A double-flavor popsicle takes the material in a different direction, with a consistency the designer compares to blended aloe vera. These familiar food formats make the unusual ingredient easier to imagine on a plate, while introducing new possibilities for its texture and presentation. The prototypes remain experimental, exploring how microbial cellulose might become part of future food systems.

Sicher first explored bacterial cellulose in her 2017 project From Peel to Peel, which proposed biodegradable food packaging grown from discarded fruit and vegetables. Apple peels and beetroot scraps became nutrients for microbial cultures, producing thin sheets that could be dried into wrappers and small containers. The packaging took on different colors depending on the ingredients, ranging from pale yellow to deep pink.

The dried material can resemble paper or leather, with its finish influenced by the surface on which it dries. Its tendency to absorb moisture presents limitations for conventional packaging, particularly for wet foods. Still, the project established an approach that continues throughout Sicher’s work: allowing biological processes to influence the properties of a material from the beginning.

More recently, Sicher has begun investigating how materials can be grown using microorganisms already present in the surrounding environment. In research published in 2026, she describes experiments with rowan berries foraged around Berlin and mirabelle plums collected in Brandenburg. Both successfully produced cellulose membranes through fermentation using naturally occurring microbial communities, without conventional kombucha starter cultures.

Other experiments combined elderberries with a fermentation starter made from wild mirabelle plums, producing an intensely colored membrane. Sicher’s research also draws on fermentation practices she studied with local practitioners in Thailand. Together, these investigations explore how the plants and microbial populations of a particular region might influence the materials cultivated there, introducing biological variations that would be difficult to achieve through standardized production.

Sicher also brings microbial experimentation into educational workshops, where participants learn to prepare plant infusions and cultivate their own SCOBY membranes. During a workshop at Elisava School of Design and Engineering in Barcelona, students worked with wet and dried cellulose, exploring how its texture and behavior change through processing. Her research with alternative plant infusions has similarly examined how botanical ingredients can influence the density and physical properties of the grown material.

Across these experiments, Sicher works with processes that depend on the behavior of other living organisms. She can select the plants and prepare the growing environment, but the microorganisms play their own part in forming the material. What emerges is shaped by those biological interactions, suggesting a way of making in which the surrounding ecology participates in the design process.

#713. The Best New Kitchen Products of 2026

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If you spend enough time testing and comparing pans, and knives, and air fryers, you will eventually reach the conclusion that it’s really hard to make something for the kitchen that’s both new and great. It’s certainly the conclusion I’ve come to after six years of working as a shopping and reviews editor at Bon Appétit. You can only make the surface of a pan so slick, only add so many presets to an appliance before they become a nuisance. Every year, though, brands try to upgrade their products, and a few succeed.

In our ongoing project to find ways to make the work in your kitchen easier and the results more delicious, we’ve put the year’s new releases through the wringer in the BA Test Kitchen.

The result of hours of chopping, searing, and mixing is our second annual Gear of the Year list. We considered more than 200 appliances, pieces of cookware, tools, and containers, and these 20 made the final cut. From inexpensive, simple (but brilliant) redesigns to the smartest new smart appliances, these are the newcomers that wowed us.

A note on our criteria and testing methodology

Eligible products were released between January 2025 and September 2026 and are available for purchase in 2026. New colorways of existing products—with no features, changes, or additions—are not eligible for consideration.

Our testing methods are designed to evaluate the item’s full range of functions, and therefore vary by product type and category. For more information on the tests performed for each item, you can refer to the relevant "Best of" article on Bon Appétit (which are also linked below).

The winners below are presented in alphabetical order by category, not in a ranked list.

The Artisan Plus marks KitchenAid’s most significant improvement to its tilt-head mixer since 1955 and is the first addition to its stand mixer lineup in decades. Given that the word KitchenAid is practically synonymous with stand mixer, the Artisan Plus had a legacy to live up to—and, boy, does it deliver. It feels like KitchenAid’s answer to the innovations other brands in the space have made (I’m looking at you, Breville), pairing the KitchenAid’s iconic tilt-head design with thoughtful new features that make it a true joy for any baker to use.

Those features include precision speed control, a more powerful motor, a bowl light, and upgraded stainless-steel accessories that feel notably more luxe (particularly relevant given some of the online chatter about changes in material from metal to plastic in newer models). And, of course, the new model remains compatible with KitchenAid’s vast array of attachments, so you can use it to churn ice cream, grind meat, roll pasta, or crank out zoodles as you please.

I’ve tested dozens of espresso machines during my time as one of BA’s most committed coffee nerds, and I’ve found they come in one of two varieties: dummy-proof caffeine injection systems and objects of espresso artistry. Fellow’s first entry into the espresso brewing space somehow manages to be both. If you want it to, it’ll walk you step by step through an almost automatic brewing process that produces coffee-shop-quality shots and cappuccinos. But scroll a little deeper in the machine’s menu and you’ll find options to trick out your shot however you want with different pressure profiles, brewing temperatures, and extraction times.

Fellow’s Espresso Series 1 is designed to solve some of the smaller issues overlooked in nicer home espresso machines. For example, a small boiler inside the grouphead helps heat it up in a fraction of the time it takes most high-end machines (with some, you have to wait 20 minutes for your shot). A squatter body fits comfortably under kitchen cabinets with enough room for you to access the water reservoir—there’s no need to slide the whole machine around the counter to fill it up as with many competitors.

The biggest achievement of the Series 1 is that it can meet someone wherever they are in their coffee brewing journey and help move them along to the next step (should they so choose), giving it a wider audience than almost any other coffee maker out there.

Vitamix is the Ferrari of blenders, with top-tier performance and a price tag to match. So it’s a big deal that the brand’s newest full-size model, the VX1, is both cheaper and more powerful than our longtime favorite, the iconic 5200. Its 2.2-hp, 1,440-watt motor edges out the original’s 2 hp and 1,380 watts. Otherwise, it’s almost identical, down to the footprint, shape, and volume.

The blending magic is in the tapered base of the container, which creates an exceptionally powerful vortex. It easily pulverizes stubborn, fibrous ingredients like raw nuts, whole citrus, dates, and thick kale leaves. In wider containers, those can sit just out of the blades’ reach. During testing, it churned out some of the silkiest smoothies I’ve ever tasted. I also appreciate its old-school analog vibe, with a dial and switches instead of a temperamental touchscreen or presets you’ll never use. It’s our new go-to for most home cooks and first-time Vitamix buyers.

Yes, there is another Vitamix on the list this year, but for good reason. Until now, the brand didn’t have a personal blender in its lineup. This year they changed that, and the Vitamix Nano more than lives up to our expectations (which is saying a lot!). It’s got a powerful 1,000-watt motor that produced the smoothest smoothie I’ve ever made, and I’ve made a lot of smoothies. What really sets the Nano apart, though, is its variable speed control, which allows you to blend at any speed you want by turning a dial, making the appliance far more versatile than any other personal blender I’ve tried. It doesn’t hurt that, like all of Vitamix’s products, the Nano has a sleek, intuitive design that’s worthy of a permanent spot on your countertop.

Ninja’s latest addition to our counters is an air fryer–microwave combo appliance. It’ll pop your JollyTime popcorn just as well as it roasts a chicken, bakes a potato, or reheats a bowl of soup. It’s got three main settings: Air Frying, Microwaving, and FusionCrisp. The first two come with a host of presets as well as manual options, and they pretty much operate as you’d expect. Fusion mode gives you the best of both worlds: the speed of microwave cooking with the crispy-juicy (not soggy and rubbery) results of air-frying. Although there’s no turntable in the unit (which Ninja claims gives you 40% more usable cooking space), the brand’s proprietary “superheated cyclonic air” quickly, and evenly, heated food during testing.

—Kelsey Jane Youngman, senior cooking editor

Pour-over coffee is a joy reserved for people who have both the time and patience to make it. That was the idea that Breville took on with the release of the Spiral Luxe Pour-Over. It recreates and automates the pour-over process, blooming and mimicking the spiral pours you achieve with a gooseneck kettle, without asking anything of a coffee drinker other than the push of a brew button. Thanks to a built-in flat burr grinder, you don’t even need to take the extra step of grinding your beans (I continue to be surprised how much of a road block grinding fresh coffee every day can be for some people). It’s not the first nor only automatic pour-over machine out there, but it is the best.

But, like the Fellow machine, the Spiral Luxe is well equipped for already-advanced coffee drinkers. Scroll through the menu far enough and you’ll find options to tweak coffee-to-water ratios and brew temperatures depending on what kind of beans you’re using.

Few things are as satisfying as the swish of soft, chewable ice in a crisp cold-brew coffee. And if you want the best nugget ice at home, you want a GE Profile Opal ice maker. But the thing is, the size of the original Opal (big) makes it inconvenient for many kitchens. Enter the Opal Mini: It’s a smaller, less expensive version of our longtime favorite countertop ice maker, and it delivers the same crunchy texture we know and love. At under 15 inches tall, it tucks neatly beneath most upper cabinets, making it a perfect match for solo sippers and households of two. Even if you forget to turn it on ahead of time, a fresh batch of ice will start coming out in just five minutes, or about the time it takes to pack your work bag. Plus, it comes in some cute colors, so it’s one appliance you’ll be happy to leave out.

When I want cold coffee, the way I like to make it is by using a method called flash brew. It involves using half the amount of hot water I normally would to make pour-over and brewing it over ice. The result is coffee that has a lot of the flavors I like in a cup of pour-over, served cold but not watered down. The Brezi, which began as a Kickstarter campaign in 2024 and was released more widely this year, reproduces those results, but does it automatically.

The Brezi can handle just about any cold coffee preparation: It can reproduce the brighter flavors of flash-brewed coffee (ready in about five minutes), potent cold-brew concentrate, or smooth, slow-brewed, ready-to-drink cold coffee (that takes several hours).

It’s wonderfully useful for someone like me who brews a lot but wants the occasional shortcut, but because the machine is so user-friendly, it’s even better for cold-brew drinkers who usually buy their coffee.

At bottom, the Ember 3 does what the Ember 2 mug did: keeps coffee and tea warm via an internal heater. If you’ve ever had to walk away from your morning drink to make a noisy child breakfast or suffered through the stale coffee produced by a drip coffee maker’s uncontrollable hot plate, you know how nice that sounds. But I had two qualms with last iteration of the Ember mug: I hated that it made me use an app, and the electronics inside the cup made it feel a little dainty to care for.

Well, apparently, Ember was listening to my silent curses. The Ember 3 has a button to control temperature on the bottom of the mug (just push to cycle through the range from 125℉ to 145℉). If you don’t mind using apps for everything in your life, this might not seem like an improvement to you, but I don’t want to be forced to carry my phone around with me all morning just to drink my coffee. The other upgrade to the new Ember is that it can go in the dishwasher. That bears repeating, in bold: It can go in the dishwasher. It cleans up pretty easily, so you may not need to run it through a cycle—although I’ve run it through almost a dozen times—but the upgrade makes this Ember a more durable piece that I expect to last through many, many mornings.

There will eventually come a time where improved readout speed on a kitchen thermometer won’t seem like much of a leap forward, but that time is not now. ThermoWorks’ latest instant-read thermometer, the Nano, is as close to instant as I’ve ever seen, providing a stable temperature readout as soon as the probe hits whatever it is that needs measuring. There are a number of other nice upgrades from ThermoWorks’ last flagship thermometer, the Thermapen One, like a brighter backlight and integrated magnets that allow you to keep it within arm’s length. But the speed of this thermometer and the way it lets you get in and out of a hot oven or grill is the big win.

A quick search on Amazon will tell you that there are approximately one million handheld milk frothers on the market. Most of them look essentially identical: a plastic body with a coiled wire whisk at the end. Subminimal’s Nanofoamer Go breaks the mold. This frother features three different attachments that turn the simple tool into a jack-of-all-trades, equally adept at creating perfect latte-ready microfoam as it is at whisking eggs, emulsifying dressings, or frothing matcha. And unlike the typical wire whisk, the smooth plastic attachments are incredibly easy to clean.

Given its size, the Nanofoamer Go is incredibly powerful, but unlike most other handheld frothers I’ve tried, it doesn’t send liquid flying everywhere, even at its highest speed. #3857. Pioneering Photographer is Honored With Headstone 102 Years After Death

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Pioneering Photographer is Honored With Headstone 102 Years After Death

A pioneering photographer, who opened one of the first photography studios in the southwestern United States, has been honored with a headstone commemorating his achievements more than a century after his death.

George Herman Rothrock was one of the first professional photographers in Phoenix, Arizona. He operated the first photography studio in Phoenix, photographing Arizona’s people, landmarks, and daily life from about 1876 into the early 20th century. While working in Arizona, he photographed the cliff dwellings at Montezuma’s Castle and Tonto Natural Bridge, transporting his heavy photographic equipment by mule to reach these sites.

Rothrock, who died in 1924, is finally receiving long-overdue recognition. According to a report by Arizona’s Family, the trailblazing photographer, who captured Arizona’s territorial days before it became a state in 1912, was honored on Monday with a new grave marker at Mesa Cemetery. It was exactly 102 years after his death.

The tribute was organized by the Arizona Photography Alliance, whose members noticed the photographer’s previous marker had become dilapidated and eroded over the years, making it hard to read. The news outlet reports that the group raised more than $3,500 to pay for the new headstone by auctioning their own work and collectibles donated by members.

In an interview with KJZZ Phoenix, Jeremy Rowe, a member of the Arizona Photography Alliance says that Rothrock documented Phoenix and the wider Arizona region from around 1875 to 1890, producing small portrait photographs and more than 250 different stereoscopic views that created a three-dimensional effect when viewed through a special device.

Rowe says Rothrock’s photographs provide some of the best visual records of Arizona during this period, but they are often reproduced in books and articles without crediting the photographer or acknowledging the stereoscopic format. Rowe says Rothrock is the second photographer that the Arizona Photography Alliance has commemorated with a headstone, after Frank Hartwell, who also operated a studio in Phoenix in the 19th century.

“I think raising awareness of the photographic history of Arizona is really important, and these [Hartwell and Rothrock] are two of the sort of pioneer photographers that really had an impact that people don’t understand or don’t know about,” Rowe tells KJZZ Phoenix.

Located along Zishan Lake in Xianning, Hubei Province, the project comprises four public buildings designed to complement the guest houses of the Ziyouji resort on the other side of the lake.

The centrepiece of the project is the 3,000-square-metre Zi Museum, which features a multi-faceted, layered structure made from pale concrete and was organised around a central courtyard.

Büro Ziyu Zhuang (BUZZ) created three separate access points into the museum at various levels, to follow the natural elevation of the landscape.

On the ground floor, the museum has flexible gallery spaces and a garden restaurant, with a lower-level entranced located near the main traffic route to make installation setup and collection storage easier.

At the front of the museum, a path from the main building leads to a viewpoint closer to the lake.

Visitors can also go from the viewpoint into the museum’s central courtyard and straight to the viewing ramp onto the museum’s rooftop.

“Visitors who are here for nature viewing are able to bypass the interior gallery space, accessing the rooftop viewing platform directly from the outdoor park, while a different circulation was created for those who are here for the gallery exhibitions,” explained BUZZ.

“This allows serious academic exhibition and relaxed social gathering to co-exist in the same building,” it continued.

BUZZ organised the interiors of the museum as an “open labyrinth”, the studio said.

Galleries, public passages, rest and hospitality areas are intertwined to create a free-flowing viewing experience that was designed to evoke the sense of wandering in a Chinese garden.

Next to the museum sits another viewpoint, described by the studio as a “meditation tower”, which features stairs that circulate around a hollow core at its centre.

These continue underground, where they lead to a sunken courtyard wrapped in metal mesh.

The tower can be used as a viewpoint, as well as for small outdoor gatherings and exhibitions.

The third building, a rammed-earth workshop, was designed to seamlessly embed into the landscape.

Like the museum building, this also has a walking ramp, which leads up to a grass-covered rooftop while slightly revealing the entrance below.

Nearby sits the final building, a bookshop with an overhanging roof design that creates a sheltered entrance facing the main street.

It gently steps down towards the lake, where its glass facade opens up to create views over the parkland and lake.

BUZZ aimed to encourage visitors to explore freely between the four buildings. Rather than designing a set route, the studio wanted to create access to the public art space from every direction of the park.

“The overall project manifests as a continuous, open expansion of green space, which provides the flexibility needed for diverse future operations,” BUZZ said.

“There are no forced axes or rigid zoning between individual buildings; instead, they are connected organically through gentle slopes, undulating lawns, and landscaped pathways, blending seamlessly into the existing meadow,” it continued.

Büro Ziyu Zhuang was founded by architect Ziyu Zhuang in 2016. It now has offices in Beijing, Shanghai and Berlin.

The studio also recently unveiled an UFO-shaped gallery that sinks into grassland in Inner Mongolia, as well as an observatory in China that resembles a “spaceship or alien form”.
