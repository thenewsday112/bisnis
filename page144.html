#1490. The 25 Scariest Movies of All Time

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We used one simple criterion for this list: If you asked someone, “What’s the scariest movie of all time?” could you imagine them saying one of the 25 films below? Some difficult decisions followed. Alien is one of the greatest sci-fi films of all time, but is it one of the scariest films of all time? Paranormal Activity kicked off one of the most profitable horror franchises in film history, but is it still taken as seriously as it was nearly 20 years ago? Does a film like Jaws, once called the scariest movie of all time, leave real and lasting scars?

Naturally, there’s no winning on the Internet. Modern horror heads will say that Psycho isn’t scary anymore. They’ll tell us that Insidious has a terrible ending, or that The Blair Witch Project is only terrifying for a couple of iconic scenes. Let the comments come—we can take it. But we believe the 25 movies below represent the gamut of jump scares, dread, torture, legacy, and everything else you could want in a film deserving of the title of the scariest movie of all time. —Josh Rosenberg

A modern slow-burn masterpiece, Na Hong-jin’s acclaimed The Wailing marinates in the eerie and the otherworldly. Set in a remote village, a police officer (Kwak Do-won) investigates a strange illness that causes the afflicted to viciously attack their own families. But when his own daughter falls sick, he begins heeding warnings from a local shaman and a woman in white. What The Wailing lacks in jump scares, it more than makes up for with abundant psychological dread and eerie atmospherics. —Eric Francisco

The jump scare of the clap alone might have been enough to include The Conjuring on this list, but director James Wan didn’t become one of our greatest horror storytellers simply off of gimmicks. The film follows paranormal investigators Ed and Lorraine Warren (Patrick Wilson and Vera Farmiga), in a supernatural possession story based on the real-life reporting that would inspire The Amityville Horror. Though it contains less thought-provoking material when compared to a classic like The Exorcist, The Conjuring certainly makes up for it in thrills. —J.R.

Another James Wan entry. Insidious may predate The Conjuring, but it’s not any less terrifying. I’ll always remember this film about a family who tries to save their son from becoming a demonic host as one of the first horror movies to break a golden rule: The scares also happen during scenes that take place in the daytime. When that red-faced demon appears behind Patrick Wilson? I haven’t jumped higher out of my seat since. —J.R.

It’s rare for both an original film and its remake to be celebrated, but Hideo Nakata’s The Ring (1998) and Gore Verbinski’s 2002 English-language remake are both exceptional horror films in their own right. There’s only so much you can do to remix a story about a cursed videotape that kills the viewer seven days after they watch it. Still, Verbinski’s remake featuring Samara climbing out of your TV to kill you is a faster-paced and highly stylized update that feels just as worthy as the original Japanese film’s unforgettable imagery. —J.R.

While Japanese director Takashi Shimizu’s American remake is decent enough, his original film made just two years earlier is arguably the better entry. Remember the under-the-covers scare, or the hand that helps her clean her hair in the shower? What about when the dead woman and her son, killed by her jealous husband, just stand lurking over her body while she sleeps? With less cheap effects and more of a focus on the vengeful spirits lurking in domestic Tokyo homes, Ju-On’s practical scares were already perfect. —J.R.

When it seemed like Wes Craven’s burned slasher Freddy Krueger (the immortal Robert Englund) had become a joke, the auteur’s return to his series restored Freddy’s malice and menace. Set in the “real” world, Wes Craven’s New Nightmare features original A Nightmare on Elm Street star Heather Langenkamp playing a fictional version of herself. After she’s approached by New Line to again play “Nancy” for a sequel, Heather becomes haunted by the possibility that Freddy may be real. Between its novelty premise and chilling moments like the screenplay scene, New Nightmare is a sequel unlike any other, where telling oneself “It’s only a movie” feels futile. —E.F.

If I had to pick just one Ari Aster film to include, Hereditary is the undisputed champ. Hell, if we ranked this list, we might have even placed Hereditary at No. 1. Not only does Aster’s audacious debut feature contain some of the most horrifying moments ever put on screen—coupled with an amazing performance from Toni Collette—the film’s story about grief and mental illness passed down through family left viewers with a lot to chew on outside of the scares. —J.R.

It was once called the scariest movie ever made, and you know what? It still might be. Adapted from William Peter Blatty’s 1971 novel, The Exorcist tells of a Jesuit priest (Jason Miller) who is tasked with exorcising a demon from a young girl. A monster success when it opened in December 1973, with moviegoers lining up in the freezing cold for hours (and vomiting out their insides once they saw it), The Exorcist endures as a striking meditation on faith and the existential war between good and evil. Decades of parodies and homages have done little to diminish its power to frighten. —E.F.

I don’t care what anyone says, I’m not going in the ocean. For a generation that made this 1975 Steven Spielberg classic one of the biggest blockbusters of all time, this film about a great white shark that terrorizes a New England beach was the scariest movie of all time. No demons, no exorcisms, and no knife-wielding, masked psychos. Jaws is real-life terror. There’s nothing here that couldn’t actually happen to you. —J.R.

One of the most influential independent films in horror history, The Blair Witch worked because it played on the viewer’s imagination. The found-footage hit followed three film students who venture out into the woods to interview residents about recent disappearances and investigate the truth behind the myth. But like a well-told, scary campfire story, the very idea of the Blair Witch is so frightening that a simple camera trick was all they needed for the film’s final jump scare to rattle audiences. —J.R.

By today’s standards, Psycho likely wouldn’t crack the top ten. Still, Alfred Hitchcock’s 1960 thriller about an eccentric murderer who slashes victims staying at his motel is still one of the most famous horror movies of all time. There are few kills analyzed more than the shower scene, not just for its use of suspense but its artful ending. Plus, to say that viewers at the time wouldn’t have called it the scariest film ever made would be an understatement. After Psycho, nearly every kill came with a screech from a violin. —J.R.

Despite all that horror that director Roman Polanski reportedly caused to real-life women, his work on Repulsion and Rosemary’s Baby surprisingly highlighted the fears of women who feel trapped under patriarchal establishments that strip them of their rights. In Rosemary’s Baby, his horror masterpiece starring John Cassavetes and Ruth Gordon, a recently pregnant newlywed begins to suspect that her neighbor’s satanic cult is taking ownership of her unborn child. When her husband fails to intervene or believe her, she realizes just how little agency she has over her terrifying situation. —J.R.

A teenage Jamie Lee Curtis became a scream queen in this seminal John Carpenter slasher, which follows a crazed killer who escapes the sanitarium and sets out to wreak havoc on the suburban community he once called home. As she’s chased around the neighborhood by Michael Myers, the iconic score’s synthesizer and a horrifyingly deformed William Shatner mask cemented him as one of the greatest movie monsters in film history. —J.R.

A low-budget horror movie that struck Hollywood gold? Call it the American dream. The Texas Chain Saw Massacre from Tobe Hooper dwells in the grit and grime of a politically turbulent 1970s, its story centered on teenagers whose summer road trip takes a dark turn when they encounter a family of cannibals. Among them: The one and only “Leatherface.” Drenched in sweat and blood, The Texas Chain Saw Massacre is a harrowing visit to the underbelly of rural America that in retrospect feels like the overture to the slasher genre’s ascension. Its iconic ending, thirty chaotic seconds of a hysterical teenager and a madman’s thundering chainsaw—against an ironically picturesque sunrise—cements the film as a disturbing classic. —E.F.

Before Backrooms, there was Skinamarink. Kyle Edward Ball’s liminal lo-fi oddity struck fear in audiences when it opened in January 2023. Set inside a suburban home that is apparently changing shape, two young siblings find themselves at the mercy of an evil entity after their parents vanish. Is it a nightmare? A coma? Or is it young eyes interpreting a horrible waking reality? Skinamarink leaves you with a million questions and zero answers, and its abstract imagery—low angles, off-center framing, dim lighting, and ample film grain—prevent coherent understanding. But maybe it’s best that you don’t see what’s lurking in the dark. —E.F.

When it seemed like found-footage horror had worn thin, the Spanish-language cult hit Rec showed there was life in the decaying genre yet. Directed by Jaume Balaguero, Rec is about a television reporter (Manuela Velasco) and her cameraman (Pablo Rosso) who wind up trapped inside a Barcelona apartment building with infected residents. Though Rec’s bag of tricks isn’t totally original, it is greater than the sum of its parts as a claustrophobic zombie horror that makes masterful use of its limited point of view, immersive atmosphere, and airtight 80-minute run time. Rec has rightfully enjoyed recognition as one of the finest, and scariest, found-footage horror movies ever made. Skip the American remake, Quarantine, released two years later. —E.F.

In early 2020, Saint Maud had momentum as one of that year’s scariest movies until the COVID-19 pandemic killed its theatrical release. It’s a shame, because it’s really one of the most disturbing supernatural horror movies in recent memory. Morfydd Clark stars as Maud, a devout Catholic nurse who is assigned to care for a terminally ill former dancer, Amanda (Jennifer Ehle). Maud is convinced that her care for Amanda is a divine order from God, and so she seeks to cleanse Amanda of her degenerate ways. But who is actually the sick one? A stirring portrait of mental illness, religious zealotry, and psychosexual power dynamics, Saint Maud is a sinfully good time that makes maximum use of its very few jump scares. —E.F.

How about something real? The Strangers, starring Liv Tyler and Scott Speedman, follows a couple who experience a break-in while staying at an isolated summer home. What do the intruders want? Who knows! Inarguably, that’s far scarier than a robbery, and the threat of violence emanating from the masked infiltrators is palpable. Take it from Stephen King, who said, “Horror is the scene in The Strangers where Liv Tyler tries to hide beneath the bed…and discovers she can’t fit there.” —J.R.

Hey, wanna have a really bad time? Put on Pier Paolo Pasolini’s provocative, disturbing, and wildly controversial Salò, or the 120 Days of Sodom. Based on the 1904 novel, Pasolini’s vulgar satire sets the story in fascist Italy during World War II, where four wealthy men begin a ritual at a lavish estate. With over a dozen teenage captives at their mercy, the men carry out sadistic acts of torture and sexual pleasure. On the surface, Salò is no better than a snuff film that violates good taste. But get past the shock horror, and Salò reveals the raw inhumanity of the political elite. There are no ghosts or demons or masked killers here. Only the monstrous ways of men when nothing is forbidden, and everything is permitted. —E.F.

A giant of giallo cinema, Dario Argento’s Suspiria is not just an international classic but easily one of the most haunting movies ever made. Jessica Harper stars as an American ballet student who transfers to a prestigious school in Germany, only to learn the academy is run by a coven of witches. Its eye-popping visual palette, gruesome and gory sequences, and sublime synth/prog-rock score by Goblin have made Suspiria an immortal representative for Italian horror around the world. And yes, it’s scary as hell. —E.F.

Being trapped in an Antarctic research station is scary enough. Freezing to death is a largely unexplored way to go in horror films. But John Carpenter’s The Thing ups the ante. Not only are helicopter pilot R. J. MacReady (Kurt Russell) and a team of scientists (including Keith David) forced to survive the bitter cold, they’re also plagued by the discovery of an organism that can mimic living cells. As bodies begin to disappear, the question becomes: Am I talking to you, or am I talking to The Thing? —J.R.

Perhaps the scariest movie ever about familial grief, Lake Mungo emerged out of Australia an instant classic when it released in 2008 and found U.S. distribution in 2010. Directed by the now-reclusive Joel Anderson, who made the movie on a shoestring budget, the movie follows a family trying to cope with the drowning death of their sixteen-year-old daughter Alice (Talia Zucker) only to encounter unexplainable paranormal phenomena. Presented as a mockumentary with “interviews” of the family, Lake Mungo effortlessly immerses you with heavily realistic scares. —E.F.

“If it’s in a word, or it’s in a look, you can’t get rid of the Babadook.” Adapted from director Jennifer Kent’s 2005 short film Monster, The Babadook is a 2010s cult classic about a single mother (Essie Davis) who inadvertently unleashes a monster in her home after reading a pop-up storybook. Though The Babadook follows in the tradition of suburban haunted house movies like Poltergeist, Kent’s film stands on its own as an ode to overcoming grief not by repressing it or even “defeating” it, but simply learning to live with it. The otherworldly design of the Babadook and the movie’s ingenious scares make it an unusually creepy modern masterpiece. —E.F.

#2206. Tajik Activist Given Lengthy Prison Sentence

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Asadullo Boboev. © Private

A court in Tajikistan has sentenced Asadullo Boboev, an opposition activist deported from Germany in June, to 17 years in jail. Human Rights Watch and other groups previously warned the German authorities that activists like Boboev could face mistreatment and criminal prosecution on politically motivated charges if returned to Tajikistan.

Boboev, 54, had lived in Germany with his family since 2017. He was involved in the Reforms and Development of Tajikistan movement, an opposition group critical of President Emomali Rahmon’s government. After Boboev was convicted under three articles of the criminal code, including “extremism through the internet,” a court sentenced Boboev on September 21, according to a media report. Tajik authorities have not published information on the case.

Boboev, who was deported from Germany on June 20, previously told media that Tajik authorities had opened a criminal extremism case against him because of social media posts criticizing the Tajik authorities. He said that the German court gave him until July 1 to prove that such a case exists, but he was deported before this deadline.

Boboev was deported to Dushanbe with his adult son, Muhammad. Boboev was detained on arrival.

Boboev is not the first Tajikistani whom Germany has deported, despite human rights groups’ concerns. Three other opposition activists deported from Germany since 2023 were also imprisoned after being returned to Tajikistan. Abdullohi Shamsiddin, Bilol Qurbonaliev, and Dilmurod Ergashev were sentenced to 7, 10, and 8 years in prison, respectively, on charges that appear to be fabricated.

International law prohibits refoulement—returning anyone to a country where they face a real risk of irreparable harm on account of serious human rights violations. While the German government has committed itself to countering governmental transnational repression, important gaps remain.

Germany has continued to return Tajik nationals even as evidence mounts that doing so delivers critics directly to the hands of the repressive security services they had, in many cases, fled.

German courts and immigration authorities should not readily deport dissidents to Tajikistan. German authorities should explain why Boboev was deported despite the concrete warnings of serious human rights risks and should press Tajikistan to disclose information about his conviction, his treatment, and ensure that he has access to independent legal counsel.

#1499. The 6 Best TVs to Buy in 2026

Every product was carefully curated by an Esquire editor. We may earn a commission from these links. Here’s how we test products and why you should trust us.

After a year’s worth of releases, here’s what impressed us the most.

The TV is the centerpiece of the living room—has been for decades at this point—but I’ll be damned if I ever want to think about buying a new one. It’s just so complicated, even for those like me who speak the language of tech. If you’re not tuned in to the most minute innovations, five years go by but it feels like a lifetime. There are the model numbers that nobody can follow and seemingly meaningless choices between QLED, Mini LED, and SQD-Mini LED. There are identical smart interfaces and feature sets across the board. It’s basically impossible for a normal person to distinguish what’s worth their money. When genuine innovation gets paired with brand-specific marketing, finding the difference between what you want and what you don’t need can feel overwhelming. Thankfully for you, it’s my job to parse it.

Deep down, what most people want is a TV that looks good and sounds good out of the box; one that will stream from all the apps they like and connect to the game consoles and devices they use. That’s something every single one of these TVs delivers. Since I can’t test them all, it’s up to Esquire’s team of testers and editors to help out. Below, I’ve highlighted some of the best options from the brands my colleagues and I have loved over the past couple years of trial and error.

From $500 all the way up to more than $8,000, these are the best TVs at every price range.

Esquire recently called this the best TV of 2026 in our gadget awards, so saying anything else here would be ludicrous. Thankfully, I agree with our tester. This TV owns. With a dynamic, bright SQD-Mini LED display, and audio from Bang & Olufsen, there is nothing left to be desired—not even a soundbar.

The SQ stands for “super quantum,” which is about the dots used to create the image. Science aside, what we can say certainly is this TV blows the other TCLs we’ve tried out of the water in terms of color, contrast, and brightness. For gaming, you’re set with a 144 hertz native refresh rate and a gaming mode that turns on when it detects you have a console connected. PS5, Xbox, or Switch, the TV doesn’t discriminate—you’re getting awesome graphics and smooth performance either way.

Anyone who watches sports (or plays video games) knows the pains of a TV that can’t handle the daylight. OLED TVs have always, for me, felt like the easiest solution to glare. The viewing angles still can’t hold up, but the latest Hisense MiniLED gives that assessment the first real run for its money I’ve seen. Our tester sat this right next to a floor-to-ceiling window in a room with a skylight. No matter the time of day, the screen was clear as can be, and ultra-vibrant in color.

The support for gaming is great too, working as seamlessly as the TCL. Technically, the 165 hertz refresh rate beats out the other TV, but to most players, the difference won’t be noticeable.

When you get to real budget territory, TCL beats out Hisense by just a hair. For under $600, a 55-inch TCL Google TV is the best way to spend your money. If somehow, you’ve never watched a movie at home or played a video game in 4K, we can say with certainly this TV is worth every single penny.

From app streaming for sports and TV to console gaming, the whole experience is super user-friendly, and reliable. I can’t say the same for every manufacturer and OS (looking at you FireTV!). Sure, the contrast won’t be as great as the new TCL, the viewing angles won’t be as numerous as with the Sony OLED, and the sound can’t compete with Bang & Olufsen. But, ask yourself, does that even matter?

When you need a TV that decorates your wall like a work of art, The Frame Pro is everything. Samsung innovated with The Frame—a TV that acts like a piece of art when it’s off—but this time around there have been major improvements to the picture quality, once again placing it at the top of the (now much larger) heap. A glare-proof screen, thin bezel, and partnerships with the Met, MoMA, and a few high-profile artist estates make it the art TV to beat once again.

Samsung always considers how a TV will fit lifestyles against how great the picture is and makes sure both boxes always get checked. That sounds simple, but no other brand does it this well. Take the Wireless One Connect box. It helps keep your Frame setup free of wires and clutter by being an external hardware hub for the TV. Instead of plugging your router, consoles, and Blu-ray player to the TV itself, you can hide all that away in a corner with the Connect box. Truly, it’s still one of Samsung’s biggest game-changers.

Sony’s newest Bravia TV is probably the best entry-level OLED to ever exist. At $2,000 (and that’s before the typical discounts), this 65-inch plays in the same ballpark as the excellent TCL and Hisense TVs I’ve highlighted here. Only, for real display purists, nothing quite looks as dynamic and colorful as an OLED screen. And nothing beats OLED still when it comes to viewing angles—no matter where you are in the room, you’ll be able to see the action. And hear it too, in full detail thanks to the bass reflex speakers in the TV.

Compared to the Samsung Frame Pro and Hisense U7, you will get some noticeable glare that impedes daytime viewing or gaming. It’s really the only disappointing aspect of the TV. Beyond that, you’re getting a ton of smart features, but all are pretty standard for modern 4K TVs.

With the regular priced TVs sorted, let’s talk about the crazy luxury stuff. No one is doing luxury TV like Bang & Olufsen at the moment. The Danish brand has deep roots in electronics but has settled into the niche of sound products. Soundbars, headphones, speakers: B&O is best in class in all of them. A recent partnership with LG has launched the brand back to the top of the television world.

LG televisions (which, full disclosure, are narrowly behind Samsung in our estimation) are paired with built-in Bang & Olufsen audio. The sound is incredible, and LG’s OLED is as good as any is going to look (sans Sony’s QD-OLED). But the real draw is the look of the TV itself. These things are beautiful, and the use of organic materials makes them a real standout in the tech world. As a piece of decoration, it is the rival to art TVs.

And yes, the price point is insane, but the brand is part of a fascinating Cradle to Cradle certification. This isn’t a lifetime warranty, but it’s a way that the company is showing it cares about its customers. Essentially, every Bang & Olufsen product with this certification is future-proofed. This TV will last years—decades with proper care—but if new designs or tech innovations happen, the products are totally modular and allow new parts to replace the old. It’s not a fix to tech’s circularity crisis, but it’s the start of an interesting solution.

LCD: Liquid Crystal Display. The most common type of TV (which includes QLED). A backlight shines through a panel of Liquid Crystal making the colors more refined and clear. It’s pretty basic, but it’s 2025 and we can do better, so no LCD screens on this list.

QLED: Invented by Samsung, the Liquid Crystal panel of LCD is replaced with a quantum-dot panel. This innovation gives TVs brighter, more lifelike color images. For many years, competitors would call what they were working with QLEDs, but you may have noticed in recent years that has changed to Mini LED and ULED. There’s a reason for that.

Mini LED: Instead of using hundreds of tiny LED lights, these are made by using thousands of the little buggers. Compared with LCD and regular LED, this offers improved local dimming and black levels.

QD-Mini LED: This is a Mini LED with a quantum dot layer applied over it. It’s brighter than a Mini LED, while still taking advantage of the black levels that format provides. Therefore, the contrast winds up being pretty stellar on the ones we’ve seen. The new TCL TVs claim to take this to the next level with “super” quantum dots, but at the end of the day SQD-Mini LED is just another branding exercise.

OLED: A distinctly different class of TV. (Organic Light Emitting Diode) Every single pixel creates its own light. There is no liquid or quantum-dot panel, only a shit ton of little lights. The contrast on these TVs is better than what came before it. But the colors are not significantly nicer, and the price is a lot higher.

4K: This is a pixel resolution, with specific reference to the width of the TV. The images on 4K TVs are 3,840 pixels wide, which is why the image is clearer than one in 1080p.

Resolution: Most brands offer 4K TVs as the most common option. This is the new standard in HD, and the days of selling the 1080p TV are almost entirely behind us. But if you’re looking for something with a lower (or higher) resolution, you can still pick up a 32-inch Roku TV on the cheap. That sucker will only do full 1080p, but alternatively it’s currently only $130.

Smart Features: Almost all new 4K TVs you’ll buy will come equipped with their own set of smart features. So what we are talking about here is user interface or operating system. And while you might not expect a Hollywood garden to offer much in the way of practical advice for the rest of us, sometimes the most effective design ideas are also the simplest.

That brings us to one particularly lovely detail in Goldie’s Pacific Palisades garden: the way plants and water work together.

Yes, Goldie Hawn’s Pacific Palisades home was featured in a People magazine shoot, and the outdoor space contained one of the best accidental lessons for garden design you’ll see this year.

Perched on a fountain that flows into a pool, she is surrounded by dense, lush planting on all sides, so much so that it looks like it might swallow her up. And, while the fountain is gorgeous, the lesson has very little to do with it and more with the plants themselves. Take away the plants, and you have a nice water feature, but leave them, and your garden feels like something straight out of Jurassic Park (in a good way, and without the dinosaurs).

Plants and water working together rather than separately is one of the most underused principles in garden design, and it applies to your tiny backyard in suburban North Carolina as it does in a Hollywood fountain at Goldie Hawn and Kurt Russell’s house.

Kendahl Kreps, Director of Marketing at Oase North America, explains why water and plants work so well together. She says that“water has a naturally reflective, open quality, while plants introduce texture, colour, movement and enclosure. A fountain or waterfall gives the eye a focal point; planting softens the hard edges around it and makes the feature feel integrated into the landscape rather than simply placed in it.”

Aiza, sustainability writer at GreenerPulse, emphasizes this, saying, “a still surface doubles the planting in reflection, so the garden looks twice as full for free. Moving water brings sound and shimmer; plants bring stillness and texture. One without the other feels half finished.”

In the case of Goldie Hawn’s garden, the dense green plants around the fountain slow down your eye before it even reaches the water, and that’s not accidental.

As Goldie Hawn's garden has well and truly proven, placement is highly important, as not every plant is super happy with constant moisture around its roots. Jon Graham, CEO of Element Plumbing, Heating and Air, whose team installs outdoor water features professionally, notes that “the humidity around most water features runs roughly 15% higher than the surrounding yard; that’s why moisture-loving plants like elephant ears and Japanese iris thrive close to the source. Their broad leaves and fibrous root systems handle constant splash without rotting at the base.”

Aiza’s list for the wettest positions includes blue flag iris, pickerelweed, cardinal flower, marsh marigold, and dwarf cattail. Slightly further back, where the soil stays damp but not waterlogged: ligularia, astilbe, and hostas in shaded spots.

Kreps adds a practical favorite for smaller water features: “A common plant used near smaller water features is Creeping Jenny, which will actually use the excess splash to grow while walking itself into the feature.”

You’ll want to keep your plant design from becoming chaotic, as there is a fine line between that and lush, and the key is to make like Goldie Hawn and introduce layering. All three experts agree on the same basic formula, and Graham puts it clearly. He says, “Tall ornamental grasses at the back, mid-level sedges in the middle, and low ground covers like blue star creeper along the edge give the eye a clear path to follow.”

Kreps adds, “A lush planting scheme doesn’t mean putting dozens of different plants around the fountain. Instead, repeat a limited number of plants in groups and give each layer a clear job.”

Aiza recommends capping the palette at five to seven plants total, planting in drifts of three, five, or seven, and repeating each plant in at least three spots around the feature. The other essential: “Give the bed a crisp edge, stone or steel, so the planting reads as a designed shape even when it fills in.”

Goldie Hawn and Kurt Russell live in California, where a jungle-like climate is highly achievable in their garden. However, even if you live in a cooler climate, you can still get the same thing; it just requires thinking about leaf scale rather than specific species.

“You do not need California weather, you need big leaves,” says Aiza. Hardy banana, gunnera, giant hostas like Empress Wu, canna lilies, and Fatsia japonica all create that broad-leaf drama in temperate gardens. “The trick is contrast: a few bold leaves rising out of fine ferns and grasses reads tropical even in zone 6.”

Graham adds that “in cooler climates, bold leaf texture does the heavy lifting — hostas, ligularia and hardy hibiscus all handle zone 4 winters and still produce that dense, tropical feel.”

Some plants seem like obvious choices near water, but actually they should be used with caution. Graham is direct on invasive spreaders: “The biggest mistake I see is planting invasive spreaders like mint directly near the water source. They overtake beds in a single growing season and can clog pump intakes within two to three seasons if planted less than 18 inches from the feature.”

Aiza flags purple loosestrife, yellow flag iris, and running bamboo as plants that look innocent near water and then cause serious problems. And lavender or rosemary, however beautiful elsewhere, will rot quickly in a consistently damp splash zone.

The lesson from Goldie Hawn’s Pacific Palisades garden is simple, then. Treat the water feature as an anchor, but remember the planting gives it the atmosphere you’re looking for. If you get the layers right and choose plants that actually want to be there, the result looks far more expensive than it actually is.

#673. Has Substitution Culture Gone Too Far?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Guests are increasingly asking for their meals to be customized. These restaurants say it’s gone too far.

When Chanita Chayaluk decided to open her restaurant and wine bar Taan, she hoped “to showcase Thai food in Chicago in a different light,” she says. “The first [wave of] immigration of Thai people in Chicago was in the 1970s, and a lot of [Thai] food here still feels like it’s from that time.” In September, she opened with a menu including miang, a Northern Thai dish made with fermented tea leaves, and a whole, deep-fried fish in a fish-sauce caramel.

Another quirk of operating in Chicago? “We’re still pretty ‘Midwest nice,’” says Chayaluk. “We try to overaccommodate and appease the masses.” In the early days of service, guests made frequent requests for substitutions or accommodations due to preferences and dietary restrictions. “It would slow down service and speed, and that was not just affecting us, but the guest experience in many ways,” she says. With a small kitchen and staff, it was hard to keep up. “It just felt like we were Chipotle.” When a guest asked for green curry mussels “but without the mussels,” it was a turning point.

Chayaluk implemented a limited substitution policy at Taan, stating on Instagram that ingredients like fish sauce and shrimp paste are “the fabric that defines our cuisine,” and therefore, could not be removed from any dishes that include it.

A decade or so ago, substitution culture was generally frowned upon and derided. Recently, though, some restaurants report it bubbling back up—and in full force. There’s a swirl of factors that got us here: Lunch slop bowl spots primed customers to expect a detailed level of customization with their orders. Unreasonable Hospitality pushed an intensified “customer is always right” mentality. And influencers entering the food-review space have made criticism of anything—including a restaurant choosing not to honor certain dietary preferences—ripe for an immediate, viral takedown. Couple all of that with the fact that it is hard to keep a restaurant afloat these days, and you’ve got several operators giving in, whether to specific requests, or to altering their menus altogether (see also: the great de-spicification of menus.)

At the same time, restaurants like Taan and New York’s Ha’s Snack Bar—which proudly announces in its Instagram bio that everything contains fish sauce—are standing their ground.

For Lom Wong in Phoenix, Arizona, substitution and modification requests have ramped up after receiving national attention. Chef Yotaka “Sunny” Martin was recognized by the James Beard Awards last year, and the restaurant was named a Michelin Bib Gourmand in August. “[After the award,] we had more guests than we were used to picking things apart. They were asking us to modify dishes or not understanding our service style and why we weren’t coursing things in a European fashion,” says chef and co-owner Alex Martin. “[There is a] European distinction of what an award-winning restaurant looks like, so people equated that recognition with a white-tablecloth restaurant. We were just like, ‘Well, that’s not who we’ve ever been, and it’s never what our restaurant will ever be.’”

Like Taan, Lom Wong took to social media to set expectations, including that the restaurant would not “make substitutions simply to make a dish more familiar,” and that certain dishes might differ from Western expectations of them; beef might have gristle, fishes will be whole, and both choices are fully intentional. “Sunny is our guiding light of cultural context,” says Martin. “[She advises, considering if] changing something about a dish compromises the integrity of the dish.” For example, the mango salad can be ordered with the shrimp on the side, but the restaurant won’t remove beef or shrimp paste from dishes that necessitate them.

Chayaluk takes a similar approach at Taan; there is always a vegan, gluten-free option (currently a fried rice) that she describes as a “catchall” for those who can’t have other items on the menu. “We make sure that that dish is also really, really good and substantial; it’s not just an appetizer,” she says. “It’s not an afterthought.”

Staff at both restaurants are encouraged to uphold the boundaries, which both Chayaluk and Martin say are as much in place for protecting their staff’s peace as they are for ensuring quality control for guests. While there has been some backlash, the expectation-setting at the start of the meal (and before, thanks to the social media posts) has for the most part been taken well. “The way we explain [our policy] to guests is kindly saying we respect your preferences, but they’re your preferences,” says Martin, “and steering them toward dishes on the menu that might already align with those.”

#1437. At the Shanghai Grand Opera House, the Gravity-Defying Staircase Was Designed to Look Like a Hand Fan

Along the banks of the Huangpu River in Shanghai, a sweeping spiral staircase winds along the side of a white building, flowing upward toward the roof like a puff of smoke hanging in the air. But journey below the gyratory geometry, and a new image appears: The steps and railing become the canvas and sticks of a hand fan unfurled in the wind. This is the recently unveiled Shanghai Grand Opera House, where the site’s sinuous pièce de résistance can be enjoyed well before the curtain rises.

“Our initial thought wasn’t about what the building should look like, but rather: How can an important cultural institution be integrated into everyday public life in one of the world’s most vibrant cities?” says Kjetil Trædal Thorsen, founding partner of Snøhetta, the firm behind the project. “We wanted to explore how to transform it into a public landscape—a place where people can stop, experience, and enjoy themselves, whether they are watching a performance or not.”

The Shanghai Grand Opera House is located on the banks of the Huangpu River, with a total construction area of ​​approximately 1.6 million square feet. It officially opened to the public on October 17, 2026.

The Grand Opera House’s overall architecture responds to the rhythm of the Huangpu River’s tides with a fluid and dynamic form. The continuously rising spiral roof integrates diverse functions such as dining spaces, exhibition spaces, education centers, and the theater into a cohesive whole.

Overlooking the Shanghai Grand Opera House, this double-track grand staircase, where one can sit and stroll, has become the stage and theater for the entire city of Shanghai.

A decade in the making, the completed project is one of the most significant in contemporary architecture. Spanning roughly 1.6 million square feet, the building encompasses three auditoriums that are equipped for diverse musical performances, including traditional opera, classical music, and even experimental productions. In addition to the venues, the building includes rehearsal, production, education, exhibition, and hospitality spaces, creating an all-encompassing cultural experience.

The double helix staircase is the core and main body of the entire building, with independent two-way circulation routes that cleverly separate the flow of people. From below, it resembles a hand fan.

When conceiving the project, Snøhetta sought inspiration from the building’s site. Located in the Expo Back Beach area, the adjacent Huangpu River with its flowing stream, passing ships, and winding bends prompted the building’s most important design concept: movement. “While the Shanghai Grand Opera House certainly has distinctive visual characteristics, its form stems from considerations of circulation, participation, and public accessibility. The building’s geometry is closely linked to how people enter, ascend, explore, and experience the space,” Kjetil says.

Take the striking steps. While they’re the most visually captivating aspect of the design, they’re also rooted in function, allowing patrons and passersby alike to ascend them and experience the structure in new ways. Once you reach the roof, the views of the Puxi skyline and the river further connect visitors to the environment.

This idea continues throughout the rest of the complex, but as Kjetil explains, “the experience unfolds gradually.” Inside, large glass-curtain walls bring natural light into the sweeping lobby; as patrons move closer to the theaters, the design becomes more intimate, while the colors and textures become increasingly rich. “People move between open public landscapes and more intimate indoor environments; natural light changes with the alternation of day and night and the changing seasons,” he adds.

The indoor plaza of the Shanghai Grand Opera House comprises three theaters with a total seating capacity of 4,200.

Each of the three theaters also possesses a distinct spatial character. The Red Hall, with 2,000 seats, is the heart of the building. A deep burgundy color envelops the audience seating, and its compact five-tiered layout minimizes the distance between the audience and the stage. The meticulously designed balconies, boxes, and walls not only create a visual rhythm but also serve to reflect and propagate sound, turning the theater into its own expertly tuned instrument.

The main theater, the Red Hall, has 2,000 seats and is the core performance space of the entire building. The seating is divided into five levels, including floor seating, orchestra seating, and the three-tier balcony seating.

Shen Hall, with 1,200 seats, draws inspiration from the spatial form of classical opera houses and can accommodate various performance types, including Baroque opera, classical opera, local operas, and dramas. The Hall of Mirrors, with 1,000 seats, offers greater flexibility; the stage and seating can be adjusted to meet different production needs, creating more possibilities for experimental performances. The Hall of Mirrors also connects to the external landscape through a large glass-curtain wall, allowing light and the scenery beyond to become part of the performance.

Shen Hall has 1,200 seats, providing a more immersive viewing environment for smaller productions.

But great design isn’t just about what’s on the surface. At the Shanghai Grand Opera House, a network of interconnected tunnels and paths support behind-the-scenes movement necessary for a building program such as this. Audiences, actors, orchestras, stage crew, and backstage staff each have their own distinct movement paths, and multiple theaters must be able to operate simultaneously without interfering with one another. For example, the symphony orchestra’s rehearsal space and orchestra pit are located on corresponding underground floors, allowing for convenient horizontal transport of large instruments.

The Hall of Mirrors uses the outdoor landscape as a backdrop for the stage. It features 1,000 seats and a flexible stage and seating layout. More like an open art laboratory, it allows for diverse performance possibilities.

As noted, the Shanghai Grand Opera House is more than just a performance venue; it’s also meant to be a touchpoint for anyone in the city, audience member or not. “For us, ‘publicness’ is not just an ideological vision; it must be translated into a spatial reality,” Kjetil says. “People can connect with this space in many ways: watch performances, stroll on the rooftop, meet friends, visit exhibitions, admire the Huangpu River view, and even relive past memories here.”

The Shanghai Grand Opera House uses prefabricated components and partial cast-in-place technology to create the striking curved stairs.

In an era where architectural images spread rapidly through social media, this building, resembling a white folding fan, undoubtedly has the potential to become a popular landmark. But Kjetil is more concerned with whether people will want to return. “Photographs can spark curiosity, but truly meaningful architecture evokes emotional attachment,” he says. “If I return here in 50 years, I hope to see this building still deeply integrated into the daily life of Shanghai; in people’s eyes, it will no longer be just a ‘new landmark,’ but a familiar part of the city.”

#2934. Tick Disease Nightmare: If You Became Allergic to Venison Would You Still Go Hunting?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 22, 2026 2:14 PM EDT

Editor’s Note: This story was originally published in September 2019, before Alpha-Gal disease was as widespread as it is today. Since then, the writer has recovered from his malady and is back to eating red meat. (See the epilogue).

It’s mid-August and my dad, a buddy, and I are tucked into our layout blinds as another flock of Canada geese swings in low toward our spread. When they line up perfectly, I holler “Take ’em!” and we greedily get to shooting, dropping four birds out of the flock. We’ve already piled up about 15 geese by now, but the early-season limit here in North Dakota is a generous 15 birds per person.

Many hunters—many waterfowlers, even—would look at so many honkers with a tinge of reservation. Goose meat isn’t valued as much as, say, a venison backstrap or elk tenderloin. But to me, our growing pile of geese looks like a mound of gold. That’s because Canada goose is one of the few red meats I can eat without getting sick. I have Alpha-gal syndrome, a tick-borne illness that makes its victim allergic to any mammal-based food product. The disease is carried by the Lone Star tick, and it’s becoming more prevalent all around the country (more on that later). For hunters like me, contracting Alpha-gal means no more elk backstraps, no venison cheeseburgers—not even a fried squirrel leg.

“Get down and load up,” I holler, watching another flock of geese appear over the horizon. “Here come some more.”

I started getting sick in December. I’d get hit with a crippling stomachache that would start an hour or two after almost every meal. Sometimes I’d get sick enough to spend a few hours vomiting in the bathroom. Other times I’d feel a tightness in my chest and a rash of hives would break out on my skin.

After a few days of this, I went to a doctor who ran a series of blood tests (all fine) and an ultrasound of my gall bladder (also fine). After narrowing my diet to only chicken and rice, and more tests from my doctors, it became clear that I have Alpha-gal syndrome.

I did some research and learned my reactions are relatively mild on the spectrum of this condition. According to the Mayo Clinic, symptoms of an Alpha-gal allergy include: hives and itching, swelling of the lips, face, tongue and throat, wheezing or shortness of breath, runny nose, abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea or vomiting, headaches, and anaphylaxis—a severe, potentially deadly allergic reaction that restricts breathing.

I read stories about Alpha-gal victims who were rushed to the emergency room as their entire body ballooned up, or others who passed out from throat swelling. I also learned that this is a relatively new condition: The meat allergy was first formally described in 2009, according to The New York Times Magazine. On its info page, the Centers for Disease Control describes Alpha-gal syndrome as “a serious, potentially life-threatening allergy to alpha-gal that can develop after a tick bite.” There is no cure. But I also found out that if I am not bitten by a carrier tick again, there’s a chance that my allergy could fade over time.

So I cut red meat out of my diet for the rest of the winter and spring. By mid-summer I summoned the courage to try a few slices of grilled venison backstrap. I was cooking the meat for my wife, but the sizzling cut looked and smelled too good to resist. So I gobbled down three thin, medium-rare slices—and waited. A few hours went by, and I felt fine. Progress.

So what is this allergic disease, exactly? And how are ticks transmitting it?

Technically, I’m allergic to a complex sugar called galactose-alpha-1,3-galactose, or Alpha-gal for short. The New York Times Magazine published an in-depth report in July on mammalian-meat allergy that explains how the allergic disease is spread. This is an excerpt from the Times report:

“When the lone-star tick feeds, alpha-gal leaks from its mouth into the wound, exposing the victim’s immune system to the sugar, prompting the immune system to remember and pursue alpha-gal. But exposure to alpha-gal alone probably doesn’t achieve this feat. [Dr. Scott] Commins, who is at the University of North Carolina, at Chapel Hill, has identified a candidate, an enzyme in the tick’s saliva called dipeptidyl-peptidase that works as an adjuvant. It’s also common in bee and wasp venom. This enzyme, Commins argues, is what tells your immune system to see alpha-gal as the type of threat that warrants the itching and swelling of the allergic response.”

Lone Star ticks are most common in the southeast, where Alpha-gal syndrome is more prevalent, but large numbers of Lone Star ticks have been found as far north as Maine and as far west as Oklahoma, according to the Mayo Clinic. I live in Minnesota, where there aren’t established populations of Lone Star ticks, but even so, Alpha-gal cases have been cropping up here since 2017.

Some researchers think it might not just be the Lone Star tick that spreads the disease.

“I think most of these allergic responses are due to the Lone Star tick, but that doesn’t account for problems that we’re having in Australia,” Commins says in a podcast on his website. “People that we know about in southern Sweden…have this same allergy, but they don’t have Lone Star ticks. So one of the myths… is that Lone Star ticks are the only cause, and that’s certainly not true.”

As tick numbers continue to grow around the country, researchers expect to see more Alpha-gal syndrome cases in even more locations.

In some ways, contracting Alpha-gal disease is better if you’re a hunter. I have access to large quantities of wild duck and goose meat, which (when cared for and prepared properly) can be a killer substitute for red meat. Don’t believe me? Try this recipe for a sous-vide Christmas goose, this recipe for wild-duck ramen, or this tutorial on how to take your Canada goose cooking to the next level.

On the other hand, my condition makes big-game hunting trickier. I’ve been a deer hunter since I was 12, and one of the things I’ve always loved about that is the freezer full of venison after a successful hunt. So am I going to take this deer season off? Hell no. Sure, I hunt deer for the meat, but even more so, I do it for the experience. I’ll fill my freezer with ducks and geese, and I’ll spend my deer season enjoying the hunt itself.

Plus, I’ve got a big extended family of wild-game eaters, plus some friends from the city who would love a few venison roasts and steaks. And there are all kinds of great venison donation programs that turn deer into food for folks who need it. One of the reasons that researchers believe ticks and tick-borne diseases are spreading is because of growing populations of whitetails (which carry breeding ticks) around urban and suburban areas. If I kill a couple deer in high-population areas and donate the meat to folks who need it, that’s my small way of helping reset the natural balance of things. Maybe it will even earn me some good karma with the deer gods.

I still consider myself a meat hunter, but this fall I’ll just be passing the meat along to someone else. And when I throw the next venison backstrap on the grill for my wife, I might even try four slices.

As the months and years went by, my sensitivity to red meat subsided. I’m now able to eat beef and pork without any problems. Unfortunately, this is not the case for everyone who is struck with Alpha-gal. Some folks remain allergic no matter how much time passes. So while I had a few tough years of passing on ribeyes and pork-chops, I still consider myself one of the lucky ones.

Raman spectra rarely go directly from the spectrometer to a model. Before extracting quantitative information, we usually need to go through some preprocessing steps, including cropping the useful spectral region, removing spikes, subtracting the baseline and normalizing the data.

In this tutorial I will go through that workflow using real Raman spectra from our recent publication, “Process monitoring of zeolite crystallization: Bridging in situ Raman spectroscopy, ex situ XRD, and predictive machine learning models.”

Interzeolite transformation occurs during zeolite synthesis when one crystalline phase transforms into another, making it useful to track how the contribution of each phase evolves with time. This process can be tracked with Raman spectroscopy, however, the spectra must be deconvolved. #2039. When denying a stay denies the rule of international law

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

Just as the 2026-27 term was getting underway, the Supreme Court once more sided with the Trump administration with a stay order in what has been variously called the emergency, shadow, interim, or equity docket. The case, Department of Homeland Security v. D.V.D., involved the government’s plan to deport undocumented persons without a hearing to third countries where they might be subject to torture or other persecution.

Though the stay generated some publicity, the court permitting a Trump policy plausibly seen as violating fundamental rights pending further review is, by now, hardly news. Yet the D.V.D. order is newsworthy in another regard. It brings together the court’s now familiar emergency docket leanings with another tendency – its increasing disregard, if not antipathy, to international human rights law. That combination is not only news. It’s news that spells trouble for anyone concerned with immigrant rights and the rule of international law. Still less does any of this bode well given that the court has now granted cert to consider the merits.

D.V.D. involves a class action on behalf of undocumented aliens subject to a February 2025 Department of Homeland Security policy mandating immediate removal from the United States to a third country. Immediate means just that. Those subject to the new initiative would have no opportunity to argue that they have a valid fear of torture or other forms of mistreatment to the new destination, so long as DHS received “credible,” non-individualized “diplomatic assurances that [any person being deported] will not be persecuted or tortured.” For all other countries the detainee would have 24 hours to demonstrate that it would be “more likely than not” that she or he would be subject to persecution or torture. If so, the case would be referred to an immigration judge. If not, Immigration and Customs Enforcement must “consider” filing a motion to reconsider or select another country.

The named plaintiffs demonstrate the dangers the third-country policy poses. Each one goes by only initials. D.V.D. is a Cuban national with severe mental illness who is afraid of deportation to a country where he will be deprived of psychiatric treatment and be imprisoned on arrival. O.C.G is a gay man from Guatemala who already “has faced multiple death threats on account of his sexual orientation" in his home country and “was kidnapped and raped” in Mexico. M.M., a citizen from Honduras, had been severely beaten by her husband and dreads him finding her in a “third country,” as he already did once. As members of a certified class, they, by definition, share common questions of law and fact with countless others.

The key common question of law and fact for the current stay centers on whether DHS must provide persons it intends to remove to a third country: 1) notice of which third country to which it intends to remove the individual; as well as 2) a meaningful opportunity to show the basis for a fear of likely being subject to torture or persecution.

Those challenging the DHS policy rely on two federal statutes. In particular, section 241(b)(3)(A) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, codified as 8 U.S.C. section 1231(b)(3)(A), provides that “the Attorney General may not remove [a noncitizen] to a [third-country] if the Attorney General decides that the [noncitizen’s] life or freedom would be threatened in that country because of the [non-citizen’s] race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.” Likewise, section 2242(a) of the Foreign Affairs Reform and Restructuring Act, codified as a statutory note to 8 U.S.C. section 1231, states that that it shall be the policy of the United States “not to expel, extradite, or otherwise effect the involuntary return of any person to a country in which there are substantial grounds for believing the person would be in danger of being subjected to torture, regardless of whether the person is physically present in the United States.” The regulation implementing this policy provides that “[i]f the immigration judge determines that the alien is more likely than not to be tortured in the proposed country of removal, the alien is entitled to protection under the Convention Against Torture.”

Neither statute expressly requires notice and an opportunity to be heard. Nor do the related regulations to implement the statutes, which provide for individualized consideration of persecution and torture claims, but do not expressly prescribe prior notice nor an opportunity of such.The U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 1st Circuit nonetheless held that they are best read as implicitly requiring those protections. These holdings asserted that the two laws, together with associated regulations, are most sensibly read to require procedural protections giving a person notice of which third country to which they will be removed and an opportunity to demonstrate that they would face torture or persecution there. Bolstering these interpretations was the venerable cannon of constitutional avoidance: Not to require notice and an opportunity would risk interpreting the two statutes in a way that would violate procedural due process under the Fifth Amendment. As Justice Louis Brandeis perhaps most famously articulated the canon, “it is a cardinal principal that the Court will first ascertain whether a construction of [a] statute is fairly possible by which the question [of its constitutionality] may be avoided.”

The Supreme Court subsequently granted a stay on the ground that the government would likely succeed on the merits. The court’s lack of any substantive explanation for its ruling, which has become commonplace on the emergency docket, was troubling enough. But even more disturbing was any apparent engagement with international law, much less international human rights. Even as background, that body of law would appear to be relevant to a situation involving one country detaining asylum speakers claiming to escape severe persecution from their nation, only to send them to another potentially authoritarian state without any opportunity to demonstrate that their removal would subject them to the same type of mistreatment that caused them to flee their home country to begin with. As it happens, this case invites and even compels consideration of international law as a matter of well-settled U.S. legal doctrine.

A cornerstone of both international refugee and human rights law goes under the French term, non-refoulment. Under this doctrine, a nation may not oblige a person to return – refoule – to a territory where “where his life or freedom would be threatened on account of his race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion." This prohibition appears in several landmark treaties, including the 1951 Refugee Convention, its 1967 Protocol, and the 1987 Convention Against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment. It is also widely accepted that the principle of non-refoulment has attained the binding status of customary international law. Through both its treaties and international custom, this obligation applies to the United States.

International non-refoulement also implicates corollaries directly relevant to the Supreme Court’s consideration. The UN Committee Against Torture, which implements CAT, has stated that diplomatic assurances from a state are not sufficient to justify refouling an individual where there is clear evidence that the receiving state would engage in torture or other mistreatment. More generally, the Executive Committee of the UN Office of High Commissioner for Refugees has set out extensive procedural protections to enable a person to argue against deportation to a state likely to engage in persecution, including some, like the right to consult a UNHCR representative, that go well beyond the opportunity sought by the asylum seekers in D.V.D. These bodies, like the district court and 1st Circuit on domestic grounds, concluded that the right against non-refoulement would mean little unless the person facing persecution had some opportunity to contest the removing government’s say-so.

How a nation’s international law obligations do or do not come down into its domestic legal system could well fill up a year of columns. Suffice is to say in the present case several well-settled American legal doctrines invite, if not mandate, their application.

The most obvious route involves FARRA and congressional intent. In 1988 President Ronald Reagan, with the advice and consent of two-thirds of the Senate two years later, made the U.S. a party to CAT. Congress enacted FARRA in particular to incorporate CAT’s provisions with respect to non-refoulement. Indeed, the statute tracks CAT’s language closely, making it the policy of the United States “not to expel, extradite, or otherwise effect the involuntary return of any person to a country in which there are substantial grounds for believing the person would be in danger of being subjected to torture, regardless of whether the person is physically present in the United States.”

Both the lower federal courts in D.V.D. noted FARRA’s general purpose to incorporate CAT’s non-refoulement prohibition en route to holding that the substantive limit on removal would have little practical meaning without the procedural protection of a deportee’s opportunity to be heard. They could have gone even further. A more powerful argument would have been to emphasize Congress’ intent to bring the U.S. in line with not just CAT, but with the global legal commitment to non-refoulment – a commitment at least worth a mention by name.

Then there is the Charming Betsy. In that celebrated 1804 case, Chief Justice John Marshall proclaimed that “an act of congress ought never to be construed to violate the law of nations, if any other possible construction remains.” Applying the Charming Betsy canon, any ambiguities in the INA, which is not directly tied to CAT, or for that matter FARRA itself, should be interpreted in a way that avoids placing the U.S. at risk of violating evolving customary international law. Among other things, that means not naively relying on a state’s diplomatic representations that it is not repressive. No less importantly, it also confirms that argument that the right not to be refouled means little without an opportunity to contest the refoulement.

Finally, there is the federal court’s authority to effectively fashion national common law rules to draw upon even those international standards that have yet to achieve the near universal status of international custom. Here the precedent is the court’s landmark 1964 decision in Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino. In that case, the court applied that act of state doctrine, which holds that the courts of one nation state will accept the official acts of another undertaken within its own borders, to side with a Cuban state bank over a U.S. corporation. This it did even though the majority held that the doctrine had not achieved the status of binding customary international law. But the court applied the doctrine in any event using its Article III judicial power to fashion rules out of respect for international comity and widespread practice.

So too with D.V.D. Even if one views the procedural protections against non-refoulement as not quite a matter of binding international custom, Sabbatino affords the federal courts a rationale to mandate them nonetheless. Doing so, moreover, would have the further basis of the well-established power of the federal courts to fashion common law rules to fill, in the words of Judge Henry Friendly, the “interstices” of federal statutes.

In its holding in D.V.D., the Supreme Court did no such thing, ignoring the requirements of international law altogether. Sadly, this is nothing new from the court, which – in its present incarnation in particular – has grown hostile to this body of law. The justices would do well to recall the words of the first chief justice, John Jay, who wrote, “We had become a nation. As such we were responsible to others for the observance of the Laws of Nations; and as our national concerns were to be regulated by national laws, national tribunals became necessary for the interpretation and execution of them both.”

“Food is fuel.” I’ve heard the cliché my entire life, and it never resonated. Food has always represented much more to me: a reason to gather, a way to celebrate holidays, a source of nostalgia and novelty. To see food as proverbial gas in the tank seemed clinical.

But food has also been a source of deep personal anxiety, and in moments, downright pain. For many years, I was obsessive and compulsive with my eating behavior. A binge-and-restrict cycle became my norm, with food acting as the currency for doling out punishment and rewards.

As I worked with a therapist and mended my relationship with food, I started seeing it as nourishment for my spirit. This was actually fairly easy—I’ve always loved to eat. But it was finding the resolve to intentionally fuel my body, especially as I’ve established a deeper practice of strength training and running, that remained a struggle.

When I learned that red meat was higher in saturated fat than legumes, I limited my intake of the former. After reading that too many carbohydrates could spike my blood sugar, potentially leading to more hunger and weight gain, I swapped my morning oatmeal for yogurt. Even though I had no medical predispositions to warrant slashing these foods, I saw the ingredients as barriers to optimal health.

At the time, my definition of “healthy” was misguidedly synonymous with “being thin.” I logged hours of endless, directionless cardio, the only intention for which was to burn the minimal calories I consumed the rest of the day.

After a brief respite in the 2010s when body-positivity and neutrality were in vogue, the pendulum has swung back with a vengeance. Myriad celebrities who once touted body-positivity are taking and becoming advocates for the FDA-approved versions of these weight-loss drugs, often known as GLP-1 drugs—choices that are, of course, theirs, and none of my business, but ones that are public and influential.

This is not to bash properly taking a prescribed medication: Many users report that, since taking a GLP-1 drug, they are able to comfortably exercise, or found it easier to avoid alcohol and stay sober, or even manage a binge-eating disorder. My main concern with these medications is how easy it is to access and abuse them. I’ve been in several social settings in the past months where someone has mentioned they are “microdosing” a GLP-1 drug. These are folks who do not medically need the prescription, but take the shots or pills purely to lose that “last” 5 or 10 pounds. And where things get especially sticky for me is when these conversations shift to food noise. And they almost always do.

Yet I fear many people, especially those who have a history of disordered eating, conflate food noise with any thoughts of food, even as a result of hunger—something your body should feel throughout the day, especially if it’s expending energy. When someone praises a drug for helping them forget about food altogether, something is wrong. And blaming the medication alone is a Band-Aid on a gushing wound. What we should really be focusing on is why people are ashamed to fuel themselves.

This has become something of a mantra for me lately: It is human to feel hungry, and what a privilege it is to be able to satiate myself. Look at what I can accomplish when I am truly nourished.

My strongest, most capable body might not be its most lean version. What’s more, I don’t want to rid myself of thoughts of food at the expense of losing my appetite. I love food. And I want to learn why certain ingredients, even those I’ve been conditioned to fear in the past, are beneficial to my body.

Do I eat more on particularly lively days, when I can really reap the benefits of the extra energy? You bet. Do I force myself to curb my appetite on lower-activity days because I haven’t “earned” my sustenance? Not anymore. Diet culture may have turned calories into something to fear or avoid, when in reality they are simply units of measurement for energy. And we all need energy!

Sometimes it’s as simple as that. Reorienting my perspective to see food not as a reward, but a power source, changed everything. And fueling both my body and brain are equally worthy. I need complex nourishment after finishing a 10K just as much as I will after writing this essay. And it won’t just be a collection of the appropriate dose of macros to keep me going. It will be salty (or sweet) and rich, crunchy and colorful, something I genuinely crave.
