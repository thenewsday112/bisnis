#812. Patrick Stewart, Star Trek and X-Men actor, dies at 86

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Sir Patrick Stewart played Captain Jean-Luc Picard in the Star Trek series. (Supplied: CBS TV)

Sir Patrick Stewart, the beloved Star Trek and X-Men actor, has died aged 86.

According to a statement on his X account, he died at his home in Los Angeles after a period with Alzheimer's disease and Lewy body dementia, a progressive brain disorder.

Stewart's diagnoses had not been previously revealed.

Sir Patrick Stewart died at his home in Los Angeles surrounded by loved ones. (Supplied: X)

"He has been held in the warm embrace of family and friends, surrounded by live music, prayer, tears, laughter, and the kind of profound emotion that only he could inspire after a lifetime of devotion to us all," the statement said.

"We will forever cherish the peaceful ease and reflection on his face, an enduring reminder of his invincible grace."

The British actor was best known for playing the erudite Captain Jean-Luc Picard on Star Trek: The Next Generation and the telepathic Professor Charles Xavier in the X-Men movies.

Sir Patrick Stewart with his fellow X-Men: Days of Future Past cast-mates at the film's UK premiere in London in 2014. (AP Photo: Jonathan Short)

Marvel Studios, behind the X-Men movies, shared a statement on X paying tribute to Stewart.

"Sir Patrick brought Professor Charles Xavier to life with extraordinary grace, intelligence, and humanity, defining the character for generations of fans," the statement said.

"He was a singular talent, a generous collaborator, and a true gentleman.

"Our hearts go out to his wife Sunny, his children, his friends and the countless fans around the world whose lives he touched with his art."

Raised in Mirfield in Yorkshire, a working-class corner of northern England, the future 24th-century commander of a starship grew up without a bathroom in his house.

Stewart joined an amateur drama group at 12 and memorised Shakespeare for fun as a teenager. He won a scholarship to the Bristol Old Vic Theatre School, launching a career as a character actor.

He had been bald since he was 19. Alopecia runs in his family.

Among his Star Trek and X-Men cast-mates, Stewart is being remembered as a "tremendous friend", "magnificent actor" and "irreplaceable legend".

Hugh Jackman described Stewart as "one of the greats of stage and screen".

"I learned so much from him. I will miss you, Patrick Stewart, all of my days," he said in a social media post.

William Shatner, who played Captain James T. Kirk in Star Trek, also paid tribute, saying Stewart "brought intelligence, grace, and a deep sense of humanity to everything he did."

Another Star Trek cast-mate of Stewart's, George Takei, said he "took the starship Enterprise to places it had never gone before".

"He inspired a whole next generation of stargazers who looked longingly ahead to a brighter future for all humanity. Our hearts are heavy, indeed. Rest in the heavens, Captain," he said in a social media post.

"The stage would prove to be a safe space, a refuge from real life in which I could inhabit another person, living in another place and time," Stewart wrote in his memoir Making It So.

Stewart joined the Royal Shakespeare Company and stayed for 14 years, playing everything from Mark Antony to Henry IV to Shylock to Oberon.

"People who were not in the company would say to me, 'Give it a break. Why don't you go somewhere else?' And I would say, 'To do what? Telly?'" he told the Associated Press.

Patrick Stewart at the Lyceum Theatre, where he starred in a production of Macbeth, in New York in 2008. (AP: Rick Maiman)

In 1987, he received an interesting offer.

"I found myself in Hollywood shooting a syndicated science fiction series, which I was assured would fail," he said.

But thanks to Stewart's rich baritone and noble bearing, the Star Trek franchise ran for seven years, 178 episodes and four feature films.

He was known for his slogan "Make it so."

Sir Patrick Stewart married songwriter and singer Sunny Ozell in 2013. (AP Photo: Evan Agostini)

His love of Shakespeare served him well when he began playing Jean-Luc Picard.

"There is a formality to the way they speak and comport themselves that reminds me of numerous Shakespearean situations I'd been in onstage," Stewart wrote.

"I should play Jean-Luc, I realised, as if he were a character in Henry IV, which is about brave men."

The British actor was knighted by Queen Elizabeth at Buckingham Palace in 2010. (Reuters: Dominic Lipinski)

Stewart died just a few days after Walter Koenig, another Star Trek icon, who played Ensign Pavel Chekov on the original NBC TV show from 1966 to 1969.

Stewart also often performed a one-man rendition of A Christmas Carol and did West End and Broadway runs of Harold Pinter's No Man's Land and Samuel Beckett's Waiting for Godot in repertoire with friend Sir Ian McKellen.

He earned some of the best reviews of his career when he finally tackled Macbeth.

Rupert Goold directed the production, which debuted in May 2007 at England's Chichester Festival Theatre, moved to London's West End, then to the Brooklyn Academy of Music, and finally to Broadway in 2008.

Stewart's performance earned him his sole Tony Award nomination.

The Times of London said his Macbeth was "an enthralling creation of frailty, appetite, egotism and grim humour".

"Nearly 50 years I've waited," Stewart told The Associated Press in 2008.

"I learned it when I was 14. I memorised all the soliloquies just because I thought they were great. And they stuck, so I didn't have to learn those bits of the play, which is a blessing, because learning lines has now become the one curse of this job."

Lewy body dementias is an umbrella term for two types of dementia that involve damage to the brain caused by abnormal deposits of Lewy bodies.

A Lewy body is a tiny tangle of protein that can appear in a brain cell.

It can cause damage that affects a patient's movement, thinking and behaviour, Dementia Australia says.

There are a range of symptoms associated with the disease including inattentiveness, difficulties planning and reacting, and memory loss.

While it is known that Lewy bodies cause the disease, Dementia Australia says researchers still do not understand why Lewy bodies form.

The charity says anyone can develop the disease, but it is more common in older people.

It estimates more than 100,000 Australians have Lewy body dementia.

#820. Antisemitism envoy proposes mask ban and visa review measures

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Australia's envoy to combat antisemitism, Jillian Segal, with her latest report. (ABC News: Dan Guia)

Australia's envoy to combat antisemitism is proposing visa review measures and bans on face coverings in a report delivered 10 months after the Bondi Beach terror attack.

Jillian Segal is also calling for creative industries to take steps to re-engage with Jewish artists.

The measures are among a raft of proposals building on an earlier plan to combat antisemitism.

Bans on face coverings, exclusion zones for protesters and extra visa review measures are among a raft of proposals being put forward by Australia's envoy to combat antisemitism.

Jillian Segal's report comes 10 months after 15 people were killed in a shooting targeting a Jewish festival at Bondi Beach.

It builds on an earlier plan released by Ms Segal and suggests dozens of new measures to tackle antisemitism in response to emerging threats.

The report singles out progressive antisemitism, incorporating elements of far-left and Islamic extremist ideology, as the "most pervasive" form of antisemitism facing Australia.

The report calls for the introduction of restrictions on protests, such as bans on face coverings.

Israeli president Isaac Herzog's visit to Sydney sparked heated protests. (ABC News: Jack Fisher)

Victoria has already given police powers to remove masks at protests and the NSW government brought in laws to restrict protests that the state's top court deemed unconstitutional.

Ms Segal has also proposed safe access exclusion zones for protests, citing similar measures that prevent protests outside abortion clinics.

"Antisemitism doesn't start with violence, it starts with words and then those words progress through different stages if they're not appropriately dealt with," she said.

The report also identifies areas where progress on tackling antisemitism "remains insufficient" and names the arts and cultural sector as one of particular concern.

Ms Segal said Jewish people continued to feel excluded in creative industries and she recommended institutions take steps to address this.

"Those Jewish artists have not been re-embraced and we have to find a way to make that happen," she said.

The Royal Commission on Antisemitism and Social Cohesion heard evidence from Jewish artists who said they had been blocked from professional opportunities and lost friends after the leaking of private messages.

The report has recommended the government trial a "modest" grant program to help re-engage Jewish creatives and stories into the arts sector.

It also recommends enforceable conditions for arts funding from organisations such as Creative Australia that would allow grants to be terminated if a recipient engaged in antisemitism.

Ms Segal argued the university sector had been previously stalling efforts to tackle antisemitism, but new racism standards, set to come into force on January 1, had since allayed her concerns.

Jillian Segal wants Home Affairs to check visa holders for antisemitic views. (AAP: Sarah Wilson)

The report also calls for further efforts to strengthen the visa processing system.

The Department of Home Affairs has already begun training staff involved in visa processing to help them recognise antisemitism.

But the report recommends setting up a dedicated group within the department to review previously granted visas where there are "credible concerns" the visa holder may hold antisemitic views.

It also calls for extra risk-based filters to determine whether a visa applicant may hold views "antithetical to Australian values", along with public reporting on the number of refusals and cancellations.

The report includes further detail on a proposal for extra oversight of public broadcasters the ABC and SBS, to prevent "the spread of antisemitic ideas and narratives".

Ms Segal previously floated the idea of an independent oversight committee to review the public broadcasters' handling of complaints about its coverage.

During evidence at the royal commission in July, she accused the broadcasters of "marking their own homework" in regard to internal complaints processes. The ABC and SBS have rejected that claim.

Now Ms Segal is calling for the existing responsibilities of the ABC and SBS ombudsmen to be transferred to an external ombudsman.

"It is in no way to attack their independence, because their independence is incredibly important for all of us, but to assure everyone that it's working effectively," she said.

The royal commission will deliver its final report on December 18.

#27. 'The city is alive': A brief peace in Kherson, but residents fear what comes next

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The temporary ceasefire in Kherson has provided its residents with the first glimpse of peace in nearly five years

The Ukrainian city of Kherson has known its first two days of quiet after many years, as a US-brokered temporary ceasefire has allowed residents rare moments of respite from the constant Russian aerial attacks that plague the front line region.

Videos posted on social media showed people strolling down streets covered in anti-drone nets and gathering in the city's squares - ordinary activities that usually mean keeping one eye on the sky and one ear out for incoming drones.

"Ever since the ceasefire was announced, there's been total silence," said Olga, 42, speaking by phone from Kherson. "We are all, of course, overjoyed."

"I don't even know what to call it," she added, her smile coming through in her voice. "It's as if everything has finally come to an end."

Since Tuesday, Kherson has "literally come back to life," Ukraine's human rights ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets said.

"People are calmly going out, walking, enjoying the autumn sun. What for some is ordinary everyday life, for residents of the Kherson region today is the greatest desire."

The temporary pause in hostilities - due to end on Sunday - is the result of efforts by US First Lady Melania Trump to broker a safe passage out of the Russian-occupied Ukrainian city of Oleshky, which lies on the other side of the Dnipro river from Kherson.

For several days, Russian forces have been working to clear the road of mines, ahead of a planned evacuation of Oleshky's civilians on Friday.

But there are fears that, alongside carrying out the well-publicised "humanitarian operation" â which some American journalists will be invited to witness â the Russians are using the truce to bolster their forces in the area ahead of a renewed onslaught of Kherson from next week.

"They are... using this lull to strengthen their positions, bring in more military supplies, weapons, ammunition, and drones, and carry out some redeployment of their forces," an anonymous source working in local government told the BBC.

Petro Andriushchenko, Director of Ukraine's Center for Studying the Occupation, says Kherson locals have told him that they witnessed a military build-up around Radensk, some 20km (12 miles) from Oleshky.

Its residents will be saved but "we are simultaneously giving the enemy an opportunity to exploit the ceasefire for its own military purposes," he said. Once the ceasefire ends, the situation may be "worse than we saw before, because Russia's units will be stronger. They have enough time to prepare to prolong this action."

Though Kherson residents are clear-eyed about what lies ahead, for a brief period this week the city caught its breath.

No violations have been reported on either side since Tuesday. There have been other ceasefires in the past, but these were generally not respected, Kherson resident Dima said.

"This is the first time the shelling has stopped, people don't quite believe it," he told the BBC. "They are going to the shops, drinking coffee, walking around... The city is alive."

On social media, a woman named Yulia wrote about her unusually uneventful trip to the supermarket with an edge of disbelief. For once, she said, she returned home from the shops "whole and calm, not as usual - scared, sweating from the horror and with a lump in my throat."

"We've managed to adapt to this way of life," Olga said. "We've been living under these conditions for nearly five years now."

"Some are venturing into places where nobody has dared to go for years, filming areas where simply being present has meant - and will once again mean - almost certain death," said Denisa Bohackova, a Czech volunteer who lives in Kherson.

Dima described scenes of "girls, boys, parents, grandmothers" walking down the streets of the red zone carrying suitcases and bags, returning to their flats to see what they could retrieve.

Some of those who returned to the red zone found buildings reduced to charred, empty shells; in other cases, homes had been looted. The scale of destruction in some of these places, Bohackova said, is "breathtaking".

Others were rushing back for reasons beyond material concerns. Angela Dobrovolska's husband Serhiy was killed two years ago by a grenade dropped by a piloted Russian drone - an instance of a "human safari" attack of the kind that Kherson has become sadly infamous for.

On Thursday, Angela headed back from Kyiv to Kherson to visit his grave for the first time in months. "They say that all the people have rushed to Kherson," she said. "And I'll be going to the cemetery to my husband."

The population of Kherson has dwindled from over 250,000 to about 65,000 since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, which will enter its sixth year in February.

Though civilian life in the city never stopped, Bohackova said, it also carried on under the conditions of an active battlefield, with schools, theatres, kindergartens and hospitals moved underground.

"An ordinary civilian from Kherson has experienced more of this war than many soldiers," Bohackova said.

Olga, who is raising two children, described regular blackouts, mines dropped by drones and concealed by fallen leaves, drone fragments ricocheting, and a city that typically shuts by the afternoon.

But since silence fell on the city, Olga has been able to live a normal life. She drove to a different town and took the bus to another district to visit her grandmother with her son. Around her, the shops were full, as were the cafes. After years of quiet streets, "there was even a small traffic jam," she smiled.

The evacuation of nearby Oleshky is due to be completed by Sunday, after which the ceasefire will be over. "The concern is that, starting Monday, things could turn truly chaotic," Olga said.

"We know the strikes will resume, and that they will increase," said Dima, as he and his wife prepared to visit their flat in the red zone for the first time in several months.

"These few days showed us how it could be if it all finally ended."

#458. Cleric arrested in Iran protests sentenced to death twice

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An Iranian cleric and former Revolutionary Guards member arrested during January's nationwide protests has been sentenced to death twice in separate cases, including for apostasy, US-based rights group HRANA reported Thursday.

The cleric, identified only as Mehdi, was sentenced to death alongside two other protesters, Amirreza Rezaei and Vahid Ghahremani, by Branch 23 of Tehran's Revolutionary Court, according to HRANA.

The charges against the three included allegedly carrying out operations on behalf of Israel, HRANA said. They were informed of their sentences in prison on Monday.

Mehdi, whose surname remains unknown, received a second death sentence from Iran's Special Clerical Court on a charge of apostasy. HRANA provided no further details about the circumstances of that case or his previous service in the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC).

All three were arrested separately by security forces during the nationwide protests in January. They were transferred from Greater Tehran Prison to Ghezel Hesar Prison in Karaj on Wednesday.

The sentences come amid warnings from the United Nations that Iranian authorities have intensified executions and prosecutions linked to the protests during the war with the United States and Israel.

UN Special Rapporteur Mai Sato told the General Assembly on Wednesday that at least 34 people had been executed in connection with the nationwide protests.

Sato cited one case in which 34 defendants were tried in just five days, with 12 sentenced to death and seven already executed. She raised concerns about mass trials, forced confessions and the lack of due process in capital cases.

"The war has provided cover for the authorities to intensify their crackdown and tighten the repression," Sato said, adding that protesters had been portrayed as agents of foreign powers and peaceful dissent prosecuted as a national security threat.

The protests began in late December with strikes by merchants in Tehran before spreading across the country. At least 36,500 people were killed during the subsequent crackdown by security forces, according to Iran International's reporting, while tens of thousands were arrested or summoned.

Some Iranian families are paying thousands of dollars to put their children in foreign online schools, buying an international education from home and stepping outside the Islamic Republic’s school system, despite the education ministry’s warnings.

The government-affiliated newspaper Iran reported on Thursday that institutions offering foreign curricula and diplomas from countries including Canada and Britain were attracting Iranian students.

A mother whose 15-year-old daughter attends an online high school based in Toronto told the newspaper her family pays $7,000 annually to prepare her daughter for admission to Canadian universities without leaving Iran.

Another father cited shortcomings in Iranian schools’ approach to practical skills and creative thinking as reasons for enrolling his son in a foreign online college alongside his education in Tehran.

Mohammad Salimi, head of the Education Ministry’s Center for International Affairs and Overseas Schools, warned that the foreign online institutions in question lack authorization to operate in Iran and that their diplomas are not recognized by the domestic education system.

Students attending only these institutions are not registered in Iran’s official education databases and could face difficulties continuing their studies domestically, Salimi told Iran daily.

For families planning to send their children to universities abroad, however, recognition by institutions in the destination country may matter more than approval from Iranian authorities.

A diploma’s lack of recognition in Iran does not necessarily make it invalid internationally. Its acceptance depends on the school’s accreditation and the admission requirements of individual universities.

The expansion of international online schools allows students to follow American and British curricula without relocating.

International Virtual Learning Academy (IVLA), a private American school, provides online education from elementary through high school, allowing students to select courses and progress at a pace suited to their circumstances.

Annual high school tuition starts at $2,679 for its basic program, rising to $3,799 or $5,279 for packages offering additional educational support.

Britain’s King's InterHigh offers live and recorded online classes, with subjects including computer science, economics, psychology and film studies.

For the 2026–2027 academic year, annual tuition is £7,250 for students aged 14 to 16 and £7,585 for the pre-university program covering three principal subjects.

Crimson Global Academy also provides American and British curricula online. Annual full-time tuition for its American high school program ranges from $22,375 to $25,500.

The differences show that foreign online schooling does not carry a uniform price. Costs vary according to grade level, course selection and the educational services provided.

Ideological education and alternative choices

Some families are turning to foreign education in a country whose school system was restructured around the Islamic Republic’s religious and political objectives after the 1979 revolution.

UNESCO has documented how Iranian textbooks were revised after the revolution to incorporate Islamic teachings, with religious and political content extending beyond religious education into subjects including history, social studies and Persian literature.

Iran’s Fundamental Reform Document of Education also identifies the cultivation of Islamic-Iranian identity and religious values among the education system’s central objectives.

In a study published in July 2025, education researcher Golnar Mehran examined Iranian social studies textbooks and the role of Islamization and politicization in their content.

By contrast, the international online schools examined offer greater flexibility in course selection, different academic pathways and preparation for universities abroad.

The YouTube generation and English-language learning

The spread of the internet and social media has also exposed Iranian students to English-language material and international educational resources from an early age.

YouTube, online gaming and English-language social media content have expanded opportunities to learn beyond formal classrooms, despite extensive internet filtering in Iran.

A 2024 survey by the Iranian Students Polling Agency, ISPA, found that roughly half of the university students surveyed used YouTube.

Another official survey published in 2026 found that around 23 percent of children and adolescents aged six to 15 used tools to circumvent internet restrictions.

A 2026 study involving 120 English-language learners in Tehran and Isfahan found that structured educational use of YouTube, Instagram, Telegram and WhatsApp over 12 weeks significantly improved English proficiency among participants in the experimental group.

Such resources can make international online education more accessible to students who develop sufficient English-language skills.

IVLA, for example, expects international applicants to have adequate English proficiency but does not require a language-test certificate at enrollment.

Dollar-priced education and a future beyond Iran’s job market

The cost of foreign online schooling means it is largely accessible to families with greater financial resources, some of whom may be considering educational and professional futures beyond Iran.

English-language instruction, exposure to international curricula and experience with remote learning could help prepare students for university education abroad and potential participation in the global labor market.

The growth of remote employment has also created opportunities for people to work for foreign companies or clients without emigrating. Language skills, digital proficiency and familiarity with international educational and professional environments can support access to such work.

However, Iran’s internet restrictions, difficulties receiving foreign payments because of limited access to international banking networks, and overseas employers’ hiring policies can still obstruct those opportunities.

More than 16.5 million students were enrolled in Iran’s education system at the beginning of the 2026–2027 academic year, but no comprehensive figures have been published on the number attending foreign online schools or whether enrollment is increasing.

It is also unclear how many intend to pursue education abroad and how many regard foreign courses as a supplement to their domestic schooling.

Although the newspaper Iran focused on tuition costs and the risks associated with diploma recognition, the parents it interviewed described different priorities, including alternative curricula and preparation for education overseas.

For families already investing substantial sums in international schooling, recognition by Iranian authorities may carry less weight than access to foreign universities, migration opportunities or employment beyond the domestic market.

The Education Ministry’s warning about unrecognized diplomas therefore addresses only part of a broader issue: how far Iran’s education system can meet the expectations of students increasingly using the internet, English-language resources and online education to explore academic and professional opportunities beyond the country’s borders.

Young Iranians are turning from opium, their parents’ drug, to cannabis, methamphetamine and a widening range of synthetic substances, treatment specialists and officials say, as unemployment, hardship and uncertainty about the future reshape the country’s drug use.

Opium is still common among older users, Tejarat News reported. The younger generation’s drugs go by other names: cannabis, known locally as gol (bud), and methamphetamine, known as shisheh (glass).

In July, the head of prevention at Iran's Drug Control Headquarters, Zahra Abedini, said the highest prevalence of drug use was among people aged 24 to 35.

She estimated the prevalence of drug use among university students at between 2.5 and 2.7 percent, although the figures were based on existing studies. A new nationwide survey is underway to provide updated data on addiction in Iran.

Kamran Fateh, an addiction specialist, told Tejarat News that drug consumption should not be viewed simply as a matter of personal choice.

Behind many cases of drug use lies a combination of social, economic and psychological factors, he said.

"A young person facing anxiety, unemployment, a sense of having no future, emotional setbacks, economic pressure or family tensions may look for a quick way to temporarily escape those pressures," Fateh added.

Cannabis use and the changing drug market

The Drug Control Headquarters' director of treatment has identified cannabis and hashish as among the country's most commonly consumed substances, warning about the rapid emergence of synthetic drugs and new psychoactive compounds.

One concern highlighted in the report is the growing normalization of cannabis use among young people, some of whom regard it as less dangerous than traditional narcotics or believe occasional consumption carries little risk of dependence.

Fateh warned that misconceptions about the safety of certain substances were among the biggest obstacles to prevention.

#72. What killed 500-plus penguins? How scientists solved the mystery

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A small group of Adélie penguins return to their colony. A mega-colony of these penguins was hit by a big die-off in early 2024. Ralph Vanstreels hide caption

Sailing toward the Danger Islands, Dr. Ralph Vanstreels had a good idea of what he might find: A lot of dead Adélie penguins.

It was March 2024 and a virus had been sweeping through the area, leaving deceased birds in its wake.

Vanstreels got the crime right but the culprit wrong. There were dead penguins but a virus was not to blame.

As his vessel pulled up to a landing ramp on one of the islands — located just off the Antarctic Peninsula — Vanstreels and his fellow researchers immediately noticed bumps in the snow.

"Was each bump a rock or was it a carcass? We had to do quite a bit of intensive searching [through the snow]," says Vanstreels, who is based in Argentina and is a wildlife veterinarian with the University of California, Davis.

The team ultimately found more than 537 penguin carcasses on just the ramp, which he estimates was about the size of an airport runway.

There were many more carcasses elsewhere on the Danger Islands, which are home to two mega colonies for penguins. The researchers estimate there were thousands of dead Adélie penguins and other species of birds. But they had to work quickly and couldn't do a complete count, Vanstreels says.

A view of the Adélie penguin colony at Beagle Island, one of the Danger Islands. A few penguin carcasses are scattered on the rocks. Ralph Vanstreels hide caption

"You cannot stay there very long because you might be locked down with an iceberg blocking your way out," he says.

As the team has pieced together this tragic penguin mystery, Vanstreels says, the case holds broader lessons for tracking the spread of diseases globally.

Now let's time jump to Australia a few months ago for another bird disease conundrum.

In June, H5N1 — high pathogenicity avian influenza also known as bird flu — arrived in mainland Australia. Up until that point, Australia had been the only continent to dodge the virus which has traversed the globe, leaving a trail of carcasses in its path.

Since late 2021, the virus has led to the death of nearly 300 million poultry birds. Either the virus killed them or they were culled as a preventive measure to stop the spread of the disease, according to the World Animal Health Information System. Further, bird flu is estimated to have killed millions of wild animals and marine mammals, although it's hard to get an exact figure.

Researchers were amazed that Australia could stay bird-flu-free for six years. And the virus didn't take the path that scientists expected.

"We have a lot of migration of birds between Australia and Southeast Asia, so for the first few years, we're like: That's where the virus is coming from," says Dr. Amandine Gamble, an assistant professor at Cornell University in the Department of Public and Ecosystem Health.

The way bird flu eventually got there was from the other direction. It came from Antarctica likely via migrating birds.

"This is a virus that's not assisted by airplanes," Michelle Wille, senior research fellow at the Center for Pathogen Genomics at the University of Melbourne, told NPR in 2025. "This is a virus that's traveling by mass mortality after mass mortality after mass mortality after mass mortality."

So when Vanstreels set sail to check on the penguins, he assumed bird flu would be the culprit.

Bird flu arrived in Antarctica in late 2023 and early 2024. Vanstreels and his colleagues rapidly arranged for a trip to try to figure out exactly what was happening.

"The expedition was initially fully set up to look for bird flu because that was the panic," he explains, adding that ongoing fear of a continuing global panzootic — or pandemic among animals — is justified.

In several layers of warm clothes covered by protective gear — double gloves, coveralls, glasses and masks — Vanstreels and the team started taking samples of the penguin corpses they found.

"We looked a little bit stiff when walking — like a penguin walking almost — because of all the gear," he says. "We had very strict biosafety standards."

They brought samples from the deceased penguins back to a mini-lab on their boat. By evening, they had early results. But not the results they expected.

"There is bird flu here, but the virus loads are low," he recalls. "That was a little bit puzzling."

A scientist conducts the necropsy of an Adélie penguin chick collected at Heroina Island, one of the Danger Islands where disease struck. Ralph Vanstreels hide caption

The carcasses were not very old, so Vanstreels would have expected to find far more bird flu virus if that had killed the birds. Perhaps something else was the main driver? "We were unsure what was happening," he recalls.

So what killed the 500-plus penguins on the landing ramp and the many others on the island?

Back on land several months after the voyage, in the Netherlands, Vanstreels' colleagues did more investigation. "Under the microscope, you can see that there's bacteria in the tissues," he says — not virus.

They discovered a different disease, a bacterial disease that causes avian cholera (which is distinct from human cholera).

Vanstreels and his team's findings were published in October in the journal the Proceedings of the Royal Society B.

So how worried are they about avian cholera?

The disease is not considered a major threat to humans since the strain that is specialized to birds is distinct from the strain specialized to mammals. "The risk for human infection is not really that high when you see an outbreak on birds," Vanstreels says. But, it does kill birds worldwide — especially in cold environments where the bacteria thrives.

While there's not enough research to know exactly how common avian cholera is, the disease has been on the radar of scientists for decades. However, it's not exactly top of mind. The last time researchers looked for it in the Antarctic Peninsula was 25 years ago.

"One could be misled to think that: Well, that was a unique thing that happened back in 2001, and nothing's happened ever since," says Vanstreels. Instead, it is possible that avian cholera die-offs could be occurring regularly, only scientists haven't been checking.

One of his big takeaways from this bird-flu-that-turned-out-to-be-avian-cholera event?

"Yes, there is a big thing with bird flu. But we still want to keep checking for the other diseases because they might be mixing up in the middle," Vanstreels says.

Like any good mystery, the case of the cholera-stricken penguins has got scientists wondering: Whodunit?

There's a theory that avian cholera might have been introduced to Antarctica decades ago from researchers who brought poultry from the U.S., Vanstreels says.

The Adelie penguin mega-colony at Heroina Island at the peak of the breeding season. The photo was taken in January 2025, a year after the outbreak. hide caption

Gamble, of Cornell University, adds that the strain of avian cholera found in penguins in Antarctica is "very close" to the strain found 5,000 miles away on Île Amsterdam in the Southern Indian Ocean.

"Antarctica is way more connected to the rest of the world than we think it is," she says.

It's hard to track diseases in Antarctica but, she says, it's turning out to be an important piece in the global pathogen puzzle.

The work of keeping tabs on what's happening has fallen to a number of different countries since Antarctica is not governed by any one government. It was designated as a continent of peace and science by the mid 20th-century Antarctic Treaty, which now has 58 parties — among them are the U.S., France, Japan and Russia. Many nations have research stations and team up on scientific undertakings, including tracking pathogens — be it bird flu or avian cholera — that travel great distances and don't respect borders.

"So this Avian cholera outbreak is just showing this is not a unique event," says Gamble. "We really need international collaboration."

His central political concern is the weakening of the democratic centre. Powderfinger are back! What better time for fans old and new to revisit the legacy of the Brisbane rock icons?

"We got together and said, 'Let's think about if we want to do it. Is this the right time for everyone?" Middleton says.
