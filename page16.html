#32. Serbian police shoot dead assailant at Israeli Embassy

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A "minor" has been killed by police after attacking a guard at Israel's embassy in Belgrade with knives, the interior minister says. Ivica Dacic said police had declared the highest terror alert after the incident.

A knife-wielding assailant attacked a police guard outside the Israeli Embassy in the Serbian capital, Belgrade, overnight to Wednesday, slightly wounding the officer before being shot dead by him, Serbia's interior minister said.

Interior Minister Ivica Dacic said the attacker, whom he described as a minor, had been monitored by security agencies as a supporter of the terrorist organization "Islamic State" (IS).

Israel's Foreign Ministry later confirmed an "attempted terrorist attack" near its embassy in Belgrade, adding that no personnel were injured and the incident was being investigated.

The attacker was thought to come from the western Serbian town of Sabac and "kept the black flag in support of the 'Islamic State' on his Instagram” account," according to the minister.

Dacic said the assailant shouted "Allahu Akbar," or "God is great" in Arabic, during the attack on the guard. According to Dacic, the guard killed the attacker in self-defense.

He said police were treating the incident as a terrorist attack, with the terrorism threat level raised to its highest, or "red alert."

"This means tighter security checks and an increased police presence at sensitive sites, including infrastructure facilities and places where large crowds gather," he said.

The attack came  just days after key early parliamentary election in Serbia and one day after Israel held its parliamentary vote, with no clear result yet in sight.

In June 2024, an attacker with a crossbow seriously wounded a Serbian police officer guarding the same embassy in Belgrade before being shot and killed by police.

That attacker was identified as a 25-year-old Islamic convert from a village near the capital who had become radicalized.

#23. Africa's first Youth Olympics open in Senegal amid hope and scrutiny

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Senegal is hosting Africa's first Youth Olympics, with 2,700 young athletes competing in Dakar, Diamniadio and Saly. The Games bring pride and hope, but also questions over cost, rights and legacy.

On the final day of October, what may well be the final Summer Youth Olympics ever will begin. It will be the first on African soil as around 2,700 athletes aged 15-17 arrive in Senegal to compete across a total of 153 events in 25 sports.

Hosted in eight venues across three locations – Dakar, Diamniadio and Saly, 80 kilometers (50 miles) from the capital – these Games are seen by many as a prelude to Africa hosting a full Olympics one day in the future.

"I'm really excited about the Youth Olympic Games because, for the first time, I'll be representing our country," 15-year-old judoka Aisha Diop who is competing for Senegal told DW.

"I am very proud to be able to represent my country at these Games. This will really motivate young African athletes because they will improve, and they will want to continue playing sports now that the Youth Games have been held here."

The 2026 Youth Games in Senegal will also be a first beyond the history it is making in Africa.

"Of the 2,700 athletes who will be competing in Dakar, there will be a 50-50 split between men and women," Olivia Nloga, Chief Brand Promotion Officer on the organizing committee, told DW.

"But this parity isn't just reflected in the overall figure. This gender parity will also be reflected in each event and in each sport where such parity actually exists."

Several sports are also making their debut at the Youth Olympic Games, such as five-a-side baseball, kung fu and the Senegalese specialty of beach wrestling. There's also a refugee team at the Youth Olympic Games for the first time.

"As I said earlier, the Youth Olympic Games aren't just about sports, and they aren't just about infrastructure," said Nloga.

"For us, it was also important to use this time to build on and reinforce the idea that these Games in Senegal are a celebration for the people. We did this by investing in culture—for example, through a fashion show and a program called Yi-sol, which is a mural project on the city's walls."

Other projects designed to celebrate the people include music, and a local student designed the mascot. The biggest focus of all though, is on creating sustainability for the young people of Senegal.

"We did so through a program that is very important to us – one that is close to our hearts, namely, the Olympic Civic and Sports Certificate," Nloga said.

"We traveled to schools throughout Senegal and worked with more than 1,000 instructors to reach and make an impact on more than one million young Senegalese children who, during this hiatus, were exposed to and educated about the values of the Olympic Movement."

With recent flooding in the country, there were concerns that infrastructure would have been affected, but Nloga insisted that flood risk had long been in the planning.

"The prime minister held an interdepartmental meeting during which he established a task force to ensure that the necessary arrangements and follow-up measures are put in place to safeguard both the competition venues and the accommodation sites."

With Formula 1 confirming its return to Africa earlier in October, global sport is slowly becoming a more regular event in Africa. As a result though, there have been questions asked about the legacy of such events.

Many in Senegal concerned by the high cost of living and unemployment will see these Games as an unnecessary expense. For the Senegalese authorities though, they are an opportunity for long-term investment in the country's young people as well as infrastructure. There is also the hope that this will make Dakar a major tourist destination.

There was further criticism of the Games being in Senegal from Human Rights Watch (HRW), with the NGO calling on the Senegalese authorities to change their stance on the LGBTQ+ community.

"You are welcoming all of the world. It shouldn't be 'you are welcome to our country, except this, except that,'" Larissa Kojoué from HRW told DW.

"Anti-LGBT legislation and that crackdown that is going on, they contradict the Olympic movement, the Olympic Charter, and also various human rights convention that the country has signed."

Earlier this year, Senegal President Bassirou Diomaye Faye signed into law new legislation on same-sex relations that have proved contentious abroad despite broad political support within the majority Muslim country's parliament. Several human rights groups had voiced concerns about the bill, including warning that it would dissuade people at risk of illnesses like HIV/AIDS from seeking help.

The Games also see the return of Russians to Olympic competition under their own colours after the International Olympic Committee (IOC) lifted their suspension in July. Nevertheless, each sport's international federation can decide whether to authorize the Russian athletes' participation. World Athletics, for example, is upholding its ban, while judo and swimming have allowed Russians to return to competition with their flag and national anthem. According to the Russian Olympic Committee, 13 athletes will compete at the Youth Games in Senegal accompanied by 10 coaches.

Despite the various concerns, there is an overriding hope that the Games will be prove beneficial for all involved.

"What makes the Youth Olympic Games in Dakar special is that they are also rooted in Senegal's extremely strong sporting culture," Nloga said.

"Every evening, when you take a stroll along the Corniche in Dakar, you're struck by the number of people you see exercising, regardless of their age or physical condition. What we'll be offering during these Olympic Games is also an immersion into Senegalese life – into the way sports are just as much a part of Senegalese identity."

#4384. Please Let the Internet Die

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The internet must be put out of its misery.

Any rational observer can admit that the current iteration of the web—and the social fabric woven there—is not what they signed up for. Symptoms of advanced cultural decay can no longer be ignored. This thing is eating itself.

Giants including Meta and Google have been forced to settle massive consumer lawsuits brought over privacy violations and mental health harms allegedly caused by the addictive mechanisms of their platforms. Schools are banning smartphones as multiple countries institute social media restrictions for under-16s. Parents are in a panic about their kids aspiring to careers as social media celebrities, losing themselves in a vast ecosystem of online casinos, or joining extremist movements.

Generative AI, briefly considered an amusing novelty, is now a turbocharged slop engine for the web, filling feeds and comment sections with useless, grotesque content. As humans scroll past headlines about AI taking their jobs, confusing fantasy for reality, and contributing to cognitive decline, any consumer appeal the technology might have is turning to anguish. The data centers that power it have become increasingly unpopular, and politically radioactive.

To be online, many now admit, is to be endlessly manipulated and exploited. In December, Oxford University Press declared rage bait to be the word of the year, noting that use of the term had tripled over the previous 12 months. The lexical spike represented a heightened awareness that monetized social accounts churn out bad-faith takes and extremist content, automating this abusive behavior with generative AI, simply to profit off hostile engagement. Billionaire oligarchs have opened the floodgates to such inauthentic activity, conspiracy-mongering, and hate speech by rolling back moderation.

In short, when you hang around online in 2026, you see little worth saving. Perhaps a decade ago, you felt that the glimmers of community and joy, of artistic and intellectual exchange, were enough to balance out the toxic junk. No longer.

It’s not necessarily that people have gotten worse. “Dead Internet Theory”—the proposition that a vast majority of the interactions on the web are fake, bots talking to other bots—has accrued more supporting evidence recently. One cybersecurity firm concluded this spring that agents already outnumber human users, their traffic surging by 8,000 percent through 2025. No wonder seemingly everyone has at some point caught themselves scrolling through a wasteland of brainrot and asked, “What the hell am I even looking at?” Underlying that question is another: “Is it always going to be like this?”

With this anxiety hammering at my prefrontal cortex day in and out, I was more than intrigued by the absolutist title of technologist and Pulitzer-winning journalist John West’s new book, The Internet Will Die, and So Will You: On Learning to Live in Our Digital Age. In his discerning treatise, West confirms what many have suspected for a good while now: The platforms once seen as enduring archives of knowledge and communication are being cannibalized by ideological warfare and basic greed.

But that’s not the whole story. For one, West argues, people misperceive the “vastness” of the internet, which really amounts to “a vanishing fraction, a rounding error, of the human experience.” Moreover, it is remarkably fragile—West opens with the story of how a 2001 train derailment in a Baltimore tunnel took out a major bundle of fiber-optic cables, leading to internet slowdowns nationwide—and continually undergoes cycles of death and rebirth as a result of this natural instability.

If the internet is much smaller and less solid than imagined, West’s book suggests, then it’s crucial to recognize the forces that determine how those deaths unfold, or which pieces are preserved while others crumble. Because the market has falsely sold us the web as a comprehensive library of accumulated wisdom, each transient phase of this global network comes to define the present, for better and worse. Today we witness a dismal paralysis brought on by algorithmic incentives to churn out inflammatory garbage: Full-time influencers are in a losing arms race for attention with low-effort, high-volume channels that streamline production by delegating the creative process to bleeding-edge software.

West draws a sharp contrast between this holographic environment of monetization and a passionate, personalized internet that spanned from the primordial GeoCities to Tumblr, describing how these innovative communities have been gradually erased. While some endure in half-dead and highly corrupted form, others are lost. In middle school, West maintained a web page devoted to his love of anime; later, he kept a blog documenting his college years. Looking back as an adult, he’s vexed by the fates that have befallen each.

“I really wanted to delete my anime site,” West tells WIRED. “I don't have access to the AOL account anymore, and I couldn't do it.” Instead, it lives on as a zombie page, cluttered with spam ads and dead-end links. As for the college blog, he had neglected to pay domain fees toward the end of his time writing it, and so the final year of posts disappeared. “I kind of got obsessed with this idea that the things we want to last really don't, and then oftentimes the things that we kind of wish would go away hang on for way too long,” West says.

These shifting sands are a far cry from the flawless, collective, permanent memory that internet users originally believed they were building. “‘Forever’ is such a strong word, and it's the wrong word,” West says. Online spaces are memento mori, the same as individuals who self-promote across them (and that includes certain longevity-obsessed biohackers). Sure, there was a time when a celebrity could be “canceled” for tasteless posts unearthed years later, but in 2026, it’s not so easy to surface specific messages when you go hunting for them in your AI-powered Gmail account. Zeitgeist-defining platforms like Vine can be vaporized in an instant, a fact reinforced in January 2025, when Americans posted farewell TikTok videos ahead of an expected US ban on the app.

This random ephemerality, West points out, has eroded any confidence in claims previously backed up by hypertext cross-references, and thus any kind of consensus truth. An analysis of hyperlinks in articles on The New York Times website between 1996 and mid-2019 found that 25 percent of all links were inaccessible. According to a 2016 study, “three out of four citations in scholarly articles have drifted—the websites they purport to cite no longer say what they once did,” he writes. “Over half of Wikipedia’s entries contain at least one link in its references section that goes nowhere, a Pew report found.”

The impulse to drive engagement spreads to users themselves: West noticed, for instance, that when he recently reread a favorite book, Marilynne Robinson’s Gilead, he had the nagging thought that while he loved returning to the novel for the fourth or fifth time, he wouldn’t be able to count it toward his annual Goodreads stats. West says this fixation on the internet’s hollow validations creates “the exact conditions for a space to be filled by something that isn't human.”

Wouldn’t it be liberating, then, to actually deny our attention to astroturfed outrage, engineered virality, and influencer sponcon? To let the mainstream digital economies propped up by AI-enabled surveillance finally fall to ruin? If what the titans of the industry have to offer us these days are “pervert glasses” and the suppression of ads for a probably unflattering documentary, why are we helping them stay in business? Leaving their platforms for dead might be the last lever of power available to the masses.

Doing so could free people to explore dormant corners. West notes that some of the most interesting parts of the internet are the ones that nobody sees, like web portals to streams of YouTube videos that have almost no views. “Some of it’s fucking weird and creepy, but a lot of it’s just people singing a song and having a blast,” he says. West also cites the bustling, zero-revenue, circulation of poetry in web journals as proof that “you can find meaning and make meaning online all the time,” provided there is no financial motivation.

When you eliminate what the platforms want you to fixate on, it’s clear what you’ve been missing, and how you were steered away from it. Your opinions are shaped in a bubble by avatars you’ve never met, weaponized against equally hermetic rival camps. The internet doesn’t really have winners, unless you count the few without the sense to stop chasing relevance at any cost. That’s why the main characters are so malleable in their beliefs; never afraid to pivot, betray, and discard what they once held to be the core of their identity. And that’s when they stood for anything to begin with.

Absent a sudden, lasting outage due to a fresh and severe catastrophe, the internet as it exists won’t perish anytime soon. There are, however, splintering cracks in its facade, which may create room for emergent and strangely evolved life-forms. There’s no reason to assume that the present tech empire will rule the century to come; the organic and anarchic web stands to mount a triumphant renaissance if (or when) it falls.

“Look, the internet is rotting,” West says. “The question is, where are we going to put our energy?” A metaphor he often turns to in his book is compost. “Compost is just rot harnessed for a purpose,” he says. Can a decaying internet be fertilizer for something better? It’s a rosy notion, but speaking as a perennially failed composter—it’s just too gross, and I’m too lazy—I have my doubts about whether enough hardy, patient gardeners can be mobilized to make our digital landscape bloom once more.

Then again, as West’s book sought to remind me, I am destined to die as well. Maybe I’ll be mixed in with the soil.

#3669. Remembering Ursula von Rydingsvard

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The monumental sculptor dies at 84; plus, how an artist snuck his own work into The Met, and a dispute over watercolors of Roma victims of the Holocaust.

Tonight’s the night! If you haven’t gotten your tickets to Hyperallergic’s Halloween Party, you still have a few hours to RSVP (and cobble an art costume together — or maybe just tape a banana to your shirt?)

Today, we remember the tremendous legacy of Ursula von Rydingsvard, whose monumental, organic sculptures bear the heavy trace of loss and the ever-present possibility of life. Born in Germany to Polish and Ukrainian parents, the artist and her family moved among displacement camps for Polish refugees before immigrating to the United States in 1950. Working across mediums — most spectacularly cedar beams — she lived a life of art in deep harmony with the natural world.

The thread of memory also runs through Roma scholar Cristiana Grigore's must-read essay on the battle over watercolors by the late Holocaust survivor Dina Gottliebova Babbitt. As the artist’s family fights to recover Babbitt’s haunting paintings of Roma prisoners of Auschwitz, currently housed in the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum, Grigore asks: “What would justice look like for them?”

The decades-long ownership dispute between the family of late Holocaust survivor Dina Gottliebova Babbitt and the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum over seven watercolors depicting Roma prisoners is back in the news. That’s because the dispute culminated in a California lawsuit initiated by her daughters 17 years after their mother’s death. Nicknamed the “Angel of Death,” Dr. Josef Mengele ordered Babbitt to portray the traits of Roma prisoners as part of his pseudo-scientific experiments to prove what he perceived as their racial inferiority. Under these extreme life-and-death circumstances, Babbitt created the watercolors to save her life as well as her mother’s. All the Roma people forced to pose for these portraits were later executed. The news coverage and conversations around the ownership dispute have long missed an essential frame through which to view this tragedy: the rights and dignity of the Roma people who were depicted, killed, and forgotten.
