#3197. Bargiel Abandons Dhaulagiri Ski Attempt

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Andrzej Bargiel and his team have also retreated from Dhaulagiri, citing bad weather and poor conditions. No one will summit Dhaulagiri this season.

Bargiel’s team, outfitted by Seven Summit Treks, reached Camp 2 while other climbing teams returned to Base Camp. The other teams turned back because of a lack of fixed ropes, and subsequently ended their expeditions.

But Bargiel still hoped to summit the peak and ski down to Base Camp. He planned to move up to Camp 3 yesterday and summit today, according to The Tourism Times. Bargiel did not share his exact location during the expedition, and his team often reported his progress with some delay. However, the skier reportedly retreated to Base Camp yesterday evening.

“Unfortunately, I had to give up on reaching the summit given the conditions we encountered,” Bargiel’s team quoted him saying. “Over the past few days, we’ve had significant snowfall accompanied by extremely strong, almost hurricane-force winds, and this created a high avalanche risk, which was the main reason I decided to turn back.”

This aligns with conditions described by the rope-fixing team last weekend, when they stopped work on the route. Soon after, commercial teams pushing for the summit were told to return to Base Camp.

“On a positive note, I had a great ski descent from Camp 2, with plenty of fresh snow. We’re all safe and heading home, and that’s what matters most,” Bargiel said.

The skier will not launch any further attempts this season to ski Dhaulagiri. Seven Summit Treks has officially closed the expedition with a rare statement explaining their decision to halt rope-fixing work. They cited their “guide’s decision” to turn around after assessing that conditions were not safe on the upper sections of the mountain.

The expedition’s director, Chhang Dawa Sherpa, was countering accusations by climbers that the rope-fixing team had lost their motivation after Seven Summit Treks’ biggest clients abandoned their summit attempts. You can read the full statement below:

This year, the highest point climbers reached on Dhaulagiri was around 7,100m, only slightly above Camp 3. No one has seen the conditions on the traverse below the summit ridge and the couloirs leading to the summit area.

Dhaulagiri is the second 8,000’er that no client summited this season, after a record number of teams halted their Manaslu attempts because of excess snow on the route. Only the rope fixers summited Manaslu. Two Russian climbers eyeing Makalu also decided to go home. With no permits issued yet for Kangchenjunga or Lhotse, the only summit chance on Nepal’s highest peaks now lies with the Russian team on Everest.

#3207. Jordanian Man Trekking 23,000Km From South Africa to Siberia

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Saddam Al Alaya is almost a year into his attempt to walk 23,000km from Cape Town, South Africa, to Magadan, in Russia’s Far East. The 35-year-old Jordanian left Cape Town on January 1, 2026, on a trek that he estimates will take three years. He covered the 5,500km to Rwanda in nine months, so he is on schedule.

This year, Al Alaya has already passed through South Africa, Botswana, Zimbabwe, Zambia, Tanzania, Burundi, and now Rwanda. He expects to transit 18 countries in all, including his home country of Jordan, which he will cross from south to north.

Al Alaya grew up in Wadi Musa, beside Petra, and began guiding tourists around the famous archaeological site and the nearby canyons when he was about 16. He continued to guide while working for a business degree. He conceived the idea for this long walk in 2021, and spent almost five years organizing it.

Al Alaya meets a supporter. Photo: Facebook

Like so many adventurers, he wants to give his project a social angle, but Al Alaya seems particularly good at this. He founded an organization called Hike With Me, and many of his social media images show him glad-handing officials, walking with local people, or visiting regional schools. He often carries a white flag, symbolizing peace.

Al Alaya has official sponsorship from several Jordanian ministries, national organizations, and Jordanian embassies in various countries along the way. These have helped him with visas, security, and logistical support, according to the Jordanian News Agency.

#2994. Why Aggressive Venture Investing After FIRE Isn’t Reckless

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

After my posts on being broke again at 49 and feeling silently stressed, some of you let me have it. Irresponsible. Reckless. Top of the market psychology.

All fair points. Please keep the respectful criticism coming.

I appreciate the pushback because it forced me to look hard at my risk profile and explain it out loud. So here’s my defense. Part of it is financial. The other part is psychological, and that’s the part almost nobody talks about.

I have a liquidity problem. I don’t have a solvency problem. One makes me annoying at Red Rocks. The other gets your house foreclosed on.

My hope is that this post also gets you to review your own risk profile too. Leave a portfolio alone for a few years, and your actual asset allocation can drift far from what you really want. Rising markets, new commitments, and life changes all quietly rewrite your risk tolerance for you.

I left the permanent labor force in 2012. Since then, AI has accelerated the decline of my ability to earn supplemental retirement income. So the only realistic way to outpace inflation is to invest aggressively until my free cash flow runs dry.

And if AI is the biggest threat to my income, and potentially my children’s future income, it only makes sense to own the very companies capable of ruining our lives. If you can’t beat them, become an LP.

I’ve been investing since 1996 and have survived multiple bear markets. During the global financial crisis, I lost about 35% to 40% of my net worth in roughly six months. Money that took 10 years of aggressive saving and investing to build, gone. That experience taught me a risk-appropriate asset allocation isn’t optional. It’s survival.

So I cap private investments at 20% of my investable capital. That includes venture capital, venture debt, and angel investing. These investments are opaque, illiquid, and charge higher fees. I’m OK with that. I’m happy to pay up for access to potentially higher returns, and I enjoy outsourcing the work so I don’t have to think about it.

I also cap my private and public equity exposure to 50% of my net worth. At my age, I prefer less volatility, not more.

My goal is to outperform the average index investor. You don’t do that by owning only what the average index investor owns. At the same time, my other goal is to stay unemployed until my youngest leaves home in 12 years.

If 100% of my 20% goes to zero, I’ll be battered but still survive. Yes, private and public markets are correlated, so a bad year for one is probably a bad year for both. But ring-fencing a fifth of my capital protects the other 80% from my worst instincts. More on that below.

To show you how ferocious the capital calls have been, here’s a five-week snapshot of every wire I sent to my venture funds.

This may also be an ominous indicator of bubble behavior in the private markets as too much liquidity chases too few promising deals. Venture funds must get bigger to successfully compete.

Five capital calls in five weeks totaling $111,150. That’s way more than my passive income and online income combined. Hence the financial constraint.

Even a Managing Director at an investment bank making $1.5 million a year would wince. An MD’s base salary is about $500,000. Most of the pay comes as a year-end bonus withheld at close to 50% in a high-tax state. Then up to half of that bonus may be paid in deferred cash and stock that vests over three or four years. On paper, $1.5 million. In the checking account, a lot less.

And this was just one flurry. I’ve had four more like it this year, and the year isn’t over. Carrying silent stress is inevitable.

Before making any investment, I spend hours modeling the risk-reward potential and stress-testing my liquidity. Then I assign every investment a specific purpose. If I can’t explain why a dollar is going somewhere, it doesn’t go.

Finally, I talk each investment through with my wife and my father, and sometimes with the Financial Samurai community. Three sets of eyes catch what one set of overconfident eyes misses.

Here’s why I’m comfortable investing in venture so aggressively post FIRE.

I’m a terrible trader. Before 2011, I’d rack up 1,000 trades a year and walk away up or down $20,000. All that time and stress for a coin flip. My old big boss eventually flew in from New York to tell me to stop. So I did. Please don't day trade.

Since then, I’ve mostly been a buy-and-hold investor in index funds and individual stocks like Google and Apple. Mostly. I’ve still made some dumb trades that lost me plenty of money.

Money committed to venture can’t be traded. It’s in the hands of general partners who are incentivized to find the best private companies to make their LPs, and themselves, money.

So I don’t view that 20% as being at greater risk. Ironically, I view it as being in safer hands. With GPs incentivized to make money and raise new funds, I see them as better fiduciaries than me, who opted out of the grind. Further, when selling is one tap away on your phone, the biggest risk to your portfolio in a bear market is usually you.

In 2009, I wrote that I should buy rental properties in Vallejo, California, which was getting obliterated by the financial crisis. I didn’t buy a single one. Too scared, and too much of a PITA. Opportunity lost.

In 2012, I even tried to sell my primary residence because I feared a housing relapse and I had just left my day job. Luckily, nobody bid on it. I sold it in 2017 for a lot more.

Venture helps with consistently investing, even during bad times. Funds typically call about 90% of committed capital over three to five years, whether I feel brave or not. When the next downturn hits, I’ll be investing at lower valuations even if every fiber of my body wants to hoard cash.

Every major S&P 500 decline has eventually been followed by new all-time highs. Knowing that was never the hard part. Acting on it while everyone panics is. Capital calls act on it for me.

Most venture capital firms have not beaten the NASDAQ over the past 10 years. However, the very top tier have and can continue to do so. Therefore, if you can get invited to one, jump at the chance.

So far, they are doing well with investments in names like Instinct and Etched. However, these venture returns are not real until they exit. All the same, the markups feel good anyway as they keep FOMO at bay.

Now for the part that gets lost in the investment allocation debate.

I’m a thrill-seeker. At 18, I got in trouble with the law, largely from taking stupid teenage risks. #1540. Selling Your Home? Here Are the Biggest Mistakes to Avoid

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Parting with your home can be a difficult decision tinged with fraught emotions and unrealistic expectations. In today’s economy, luxury homebuyers hold much of the power, with properties taking longer to sell and price cuts appearing with increasing frequency. Looking to dodge that fate? We reached out to brokers at some of the nation’s top firms, asking what mistakes sellers are making—and how to avoid them.

Hands down, overpricing is the most common mistake luxury sellers should avoid, according to real estate experts. They argue that it’s better to set the value appropriately right from the beginning in order to avoid slashing the price later on. “Many sellers say they’re not in a rush to sell, and feel the ‘right’ buyer will come along,” says Etta McLendon of Aspen Snowmass Sotheby’s International Realty. “However, when you lack urgency, so do your buyers, who will happily watch from the sidelines for you to drop the price—three or four times.”

Adds Nicole Hay of Compass, “Every week that listing sits, buyers assume something is wrong.” In Manhattan, where she works, homes entering the market at an optimal price point can set the stage for buyers to swoop in. “The first few weeks on market are your best shot at top dollar, and we always strive to price accurately to garner maximum interest for a quick sale.”

Sellers would do best to set expectations before even reaching out to a real estate agent, according to Lyndsi Armenio, a mid-Atlantic broker with Douglas Elliman. “I completely understand it; if you’ve spent $300,000 renovating your kitchen, you want that investment to show up in the price,” she says. The issue? “Buyers don’t necessarily care what something cost you—they care about what it’s worth to them.”

Sellers should avoid “testing the market,” explains Liza Caminiti, a North Carolina–based agent with Ivester Jackson Christie’s International Real Estate. “When a property launches at the wrong price, it can lose the momentum and sense of exclusivity that are so important at this level,” she says. “You can always create competition around a beautifully positioned property, but it is very difficult to recreate the excitement of a new listing once that initial opportunity has passed.”

It’s no secret that buyers today have a wider array of choices and more room to negotiate than in years past—but some homeowners haven’t gotten the message. “I think the biggest mistake sellers make today is assuming they still control the narrative,” says Cory Weiss of Douglas Elliman in Beverly Hills. “Whether it’s pricing, choosing between buyers, or deciding how much to invest in presentation, the market has shifted.”

He says homeowners can often benefit in the long run by going the extra mile. “Sellers who resist thoughtful cosmetic updates, overlook presentation, or aren’t realistic about pricing can very quickly get lost in the inventory,” he adds. “In this market, character can absolutely command a premium, but dated for the sake of dated generally does not.”

For Helene Barre of Sotheby’s International Realty in Greenwich, Connecticut, the worst transgression involves decor. “The biggest mistake today is refusing to have a house staged if the agent has recommended it,” she says. Douglas Elliman’s Megan Sullivan, also based in Greenwich, zeroes in on the issue. “Too often, sellers either over-personalize the home before listing or, conversely, strip it of the details that make it distinctive,” she says. “The goal should be thoughtful presentation, not genericization.”

Sullivan’s advice? “A well-executed listing needs to tell the story of the house—who designed it, how it has evolved, what has been preserved, and why its design is meaningful,” she says. “Buyers need to envision themselves in the space and how it will work for their family.”

A final note: Be wary of including an overabundance of listing images, which can continue living on the internet long after a property has traded hands. “Many sellers in this market prefer privacy,” says Chicago-based Annika Valdiserri of @properties Christie’s International Real Estate. “For most properties, you don’t need 70 listing photos, and you don’t need pictures of all seven bedrooms and baths.” Her guidance is to stick to a tighter edit. “Include professionally shot photos and video of the main living areas and amenities—enough to get buyers through the door.”

#3623. I Counted Gear at the 2026 Ironman World Championship. This is What I Saw.

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

TJ Tollakson counts power meters at the 2026 Ironman World Championship bike check in. (Photo: Brad Kaminski/Triathlete)

How we reported: Editors from Triathlete, alongside other media outlets and tri industry reps have sent counters to Kailua-Kona, Hawaii, to count bikes and bike components at the Ironman World Championship for decades. This year, former pro and tri bike expert T.J. Tollakson assisted in counting power meters, while managing editor Abby Levene counted wheelsets.

I worked the first shift of the bike count at the 2026 Hawaii Ironman World Championship, and I’ve been part of the bike count at the world champs going back to 2006. You see a lot of trends come and go in that time, and some that stick. Sure, the winning bike brand is the headline grabber, but the most interesting stuff lies way beneath the surface.

My job this year was counting power meters as racers walked from Palani to the Kona pier, where the transition area and bike racks live for the Ironman World Championships. Fifteen years ago, the gold standard, an SRM crank-based power meter, cost about $3,400 and was an elite luxury item. Today, power meters are abundant and affordable, and the vast majority of the 2,000-plus bikes we counted on the pier had one. In fact, many brands sell power meters as a part of a moderately affordable complete bike — something nearly unthinkable less than a decade ago. This means the power meters blend gracefully into the overall bike’s design, making the count much harder than ever. There are also more brands than ever, and left-side-only meters make it tricky to spot them from the drive side (difficult, but not impossible for someone who has spent most of their life racing and working on tri bikes).

After hours of wiping the sweat from my brow, swatting buzzing fruit flies, and dodging “Banyan tree fruit droppings” (probably poop, but oh well), I’ve been able to sit down and truly dive deep into the depth of data. Here’s what I’ve learned.

Canyon won the Ironman World Championship bike count for the second time in Kona history (the Nice events notwithstanding). For most of the time I’ve been counting, Cervélo owned the Kona pier with a quarter of all bikes in the Ironman World Championship transition area. Canyon had four bikes in 2010. This year it had 509. What exactly does that mean? And how did the German brand get there?

Canyon is the story of this count: 509 bikes, 22.5% of everything that came through transition, and 122 more than Cervélo. That’s not a fluke year either; they were at the top of the 2024 Kona list with the men (483) and were right there with the women in 2025. (As a somewhat interesting side note, Canyon had a bigger in-person brand team in Kona than we’ve seen in years, so they’re paying attention.)

How Canyon got here matters, but there’s more to it than “they make great bikes.” Canyon sells direct-to-consumer (DTC), so there’s no brick-and-mortar shop margin built into the price, and they’ve been aggressive with discounts on top of that.

They also spend (very) big where world-championship-caliber triathletes can see it (the triathletes who roll their bikes out onto the Kona pier in October): pro sponsorships, age-group activations, and a presence in Kona race week that is hard to miss. That combination gobbled up market share. It is also expensive, and it hasn’t necessarily been profitable.

Follow the money and it lines up with the chart above:

Canyon already had good forward momentum before the big money showed up — and it’s likely that was what made it an attractive investment. With TSG as a minority investor, it went from 18 bikes in 2013 to 179 in 2019 (8.1% of the pier), but the first big jump was in 2017 (39 to 102).

Then GBL and LeBron came in in 2020, and it went to 15.5% in 2022 and 21% in 2024 after the start of the COVID pandemic. That’s the steepest part of the line, and it starts right after the checks cleared.

Now let’s look at the other side of the ledger:

GBL wrote its Canyon stake down 43% in one year, from €460M to €261M ($514.9M to $292M USD). According to GBL’s own numbers that takes the whole company from roughly €925M to around €525M ($1B to $587.6M USD), and it slipped again in 2025, even after GBL bought more of it.

Revenue fell 6-7% in 2025, EBITDA was down 34%, and in January 2026 Canyon announced up to 320 job cuts. The stated target is €1B (roughly $1.1B USD) in revenue by 2028.

So here’s my read. The market share on the pier is real; I counted it. But it was bought with private equity money (and a little bit of LeBron’s) at a loss. That works as long as the owners keep believing. The question for the next few counts is whether Canyon can hold 20%+ of Kona when the discounting has to stop and the books have to balance.

It’s also very important to note that Canyon makes more than just triathlon bikes — they have a popular road line, a gravel line, and a mountain bike line — and it’s unclear how much of their total sales come from triathlon. Canyon even sells e-bikes and commuter bikes. Cervelo, on the other hand, mostly sells tri, road, and some gravel (plus one e-bike model). Similarly, Canyon sponsors athletes from each of their racing-related categories, and specifically stakes in road cycling teams like CANYON//SRAM, Alpecin-Premier Tech, and Movistar.

DT Swiss was the number-one wheel brand on the pier: We counted 421 DT wheelsets (23.2%), with Zipp second at 327, and HED third at 149.

DT got there largely because of Canyon. Canyon specs race-day DT wheels on a lot of its tri bikes, so every Canyon that rolls onto the pier is very likely a DT wheelset too. That’s the power of OEM. Zipp sells to the athlete who chooses Zipp (whether aftermarket or on a few stock-ish completes). DT sells to Canyon, and Canyon sells 509 bikes.

And here’s the kicker. Canyon lost €38M ($42.5M USD) in 2024. DT Swiss is privately held, so we don’t see their books, but I’d bet they made money on every one of those wheels. Canyon took the risk and the losses to grow market share; DT Swiss got paid.

If you’re asking who really won the Kona bike count, I think it’s DT Swiss.

Eighteen years ago, hands up high and angled toward the sky was a radical position. People laughed at it. Now it’s the default for both the pros and for the age-groupers. Walk down the rack and you’ll see extensions tilted up, pads stacked, elbows narrow, bike after bike.

What’s changed recently is how people get there: Profile Design was still the number-one aerobar on the pier (497, 23.3%). Canyon’s integrated cockpit was second (284, 13.3%), then Bontrager (169) — both directly due to the fact that they come OEM on the first and third most popular brands on the pier. FastTT (125) was fourth with its custom 3D-printed extensions built to your arms, and that’s the direction everything is heading. Custom 3D-printed, carbon-integrated cockpits with hydration built right into the front end. The position stuck, but also the hardware got a lot more personal.

Bottles everywhere, and storage everywhere: behind the saddle, between the arms, inside the frame. The bike that just has two cages on the downtube is basically gone.

The biggest jump this year was integrated rear hydration, storage built into the frame or seatpost by the bike company itself. It was 5.9% of rear setups in 2025 and 19.7% this year (307). EZ Gains was the number-one rear setup at 353 (22.7%), then Profile Design (280) and X-Lab (266).

Up front, “Other” was the biggest category at 558, which indicates mostly custom, 3D-printed, and integrated systems that don’t fit any one brand. (2025 was a women’s-only race, so it’s not a perfect comparison, but the trend is obvious standing on the pier.)

The brands have figured out that the bottle can be part of the aero package, instead of a penalty on top of it. Hang the fuel where it’s out of the wind, fill the gap between your arms, and you’re actually faster. The average bike splits keep getting faster, and this is a big part of why.

We did see one age-group athlete attempt to check in a bike with a disc wheel cover, but he was forced to remove the cover before racking his bike. Kona still bans disc wheels because of the wind. Fine.

But look at what the fastest folks rode: Marten Van Riel (BEL), Frederic Funk (DEU), and Lisa Perterer (AUT) all had the HED Jet 180 out back; men’s winner Vincent Luis (FRA) rode an Evolve Charger Trispoke, and Norwegians Gustav Iden and Kristian Blummenfelt rode a Cadex prototype. The Jet 180 is a disc with a few holes in it. The Evolve and Cadex rears are the same deep-carbon idea but with a fat carbon tri-spoke.

These wheels exist because of the Kona rule, and they’re essentially discs made for Kona. It’s actually a wonder that these deep-dish configurations — or the arguably more aggressive double tri-spoke setup that athletes like Nick Thompson (AUS), Cam Wurf (AUS), Solveig Løvseth (NOR), and Regan Hollioake (AUS) all rode — aren’t completely ubiquitous in the pro bike racks, but of course everyone has varying degrees of handling skills in the gusty Big Island crosswinds.

If the wheel companies have already engineered their way around the ban, what is the ban doing? Athletes are buying a Kona-only wheel to ride the one race on the calendar that won’t let them use the wheel they already own? It’s time to allow discs — if nothing else than for the pros.

There were still plenty of rim brake bikes on the pier, but the number is dwindling fast. Zipp, ENVE, and DT Swiss don’t even make rim brake aero wheels anymore. If you see a rim brake bike in Kona now, it’s either at least five years old or the athlete is going for a retro vibe. I don’t expect to be counting many of them in a few more years.

This was my station at the 2026 count, so I’ll spend a few minutes here.

We saw 1,486 power meters for all bikes counted; that’s about 66% of the field. Last year it was 900 power meters on 1,529 bikes, about 59%. Remember: 15 years ago an SRM power meter was a $3,400 luxury item.

Quarq was number-one by a mile with 532 (35.8% of all power meters). Then Assioma (353), Garmin (201), Shimano (111), and 4iiii (102). Back in 2010 we counted 51 Quarqs. In 2011 it was 119 and in 2012 it was 191. Now it’s 532.

Here’s the number that jumped out at me. There were 776 SRAM cranksets on the pier, and 532 Quarqs. Not every Quarq is on a SRAM crank, but nearly all the modern ones are. That means SRAM is selling roughly 68.6% of its cranksets with a power meter built in. When SRAM bought Quarq in 2011, that’s precisely what they were buying.

Shimano is the opposite story: 1,004 Shimano cranksets, and only 111 Shimano power meters. That’s 11.1%. Shimano has struggled to make a power meter that is affordable and that people trust, so Shimano riders go to Assioma and Garmin pedals or a left-side crank arm meter instead. (Which also makes counting harder!) For example, we had 489 Assioma pedals but 353 Assioma power meters, so we have a data discrepancy. Left-side meters and power pedals are a lot easier to miss than a big Quarq spider.

In 2009 SRAM had 16.4% of the pier, Campagnolo still had 6.1%, and Shimano had literally everything else. SRAM climbed to about a quarter by 2013, then slid back when Shimano’s Di2 took over the tri world (Shimano peaked at 83.8% in 2016), but SRAM has been climbing ever since.

This year it was 913 SRAM groupsets to Shimano’s 1,511. Campagnolo had two. Two!

The reason is simple. SRAM has new wireless 12- and 13-speed options built for tri bikes. Shimano still doesn’t have a 12-speed tri groupset. If you want a 12-speed Dura-Ace on a tri bike, you are running 11-speed bar-end shifters with wires, adapters, and firmware updates to make it work. It works, but it’s a workaround. SRAM is the one making it easy right now, and if Shimano doesn’t answer soon, I think SRAM passes 40% next year.

#1552. Inside Lucille Ball and Desi Arnaz’s Legendary Homes

In honor of the 75th anniversary of I Love Lucy, see the real-life homes of its central couple.

This month marks the 75th anniversary of I Love Lucy: the legendary sitcom that invited America into the marriage, friendships, and homes of Lucy and Ricky Ricardo. The show made household names of its stars and co-producers, Lucille Ball and Desi Arnaz, who were married during its legendary run. But while I Love Lucy’s nonfiction parallels made millions feel part of the couple’s private world, the life and legacy they built off set took a much different shape than the soundstages we love to revisit.

While their fictional counterparts lived on Manhattan’s Upper East Side, and later in a Connecticut suburb, Ball and Arnaz squeezed three California homes—and three distinct design styles—into their 20-year marriage. From the idyllic homestead where they built the foundations of both their family life and production company, to their eventual Beverly Hills property and glamorous vacation home, their real estate investments and tastes evolved with their growing fame.

But glimpses inside each Arnaz-Ball estate also share a comforting air of familiarity. Though so much of I Love Lucy’s charm is in its detachment from reality, there are glimpses of Lucy’s eccentricities and Ricky’s flair in snapshots of the couple’s actual homes.

Ahead, we rounded up photographs showing Lucille Ball and Desi Arnaz’s life at home—plus a bit more color on the spaces they shared.

Before Arnaz and Ball founded their portmanteau production company, Desilu, the name belonged to their home. According to the Library of Congress, they bought a five-acre ranch in Chatsworth, California, in 1941, shortly after their elopement. They remained at Desilu Ranch for a formative 15 years, using it as a rural retreat through the 1950s while I Love Lucy skyrocketed and the couple cemented their television legacy.

The Chatsworth ranch was designed by prolific Los Angeles architect Paul R. Williams, whose other clients included Frank Sinatra, Cary Grant, and more of Hollywood’s elite. Williams’s architectural style ranged vastly (spanning Hollywood Regency to Space Age futurism over the course of his career), but Desilu ranch was a California classic: its exterior featured white clapboard siding and a sweeping wall of windows, looking out onto an oval-shaped backyard swimming pool.

Interior photos capture Ball’s eclectic, feminine influence in loud floral wallpaper and an abundance of frills: “I decorated it in Early Victorian and Bastard American; with cabbage-rose wallpaper, red plush chairs, white ruffled curtains, trailing ivy and beribboned lampshades," she wrote about Desilu ranch in her autobiography, Love, Lucy.

Desilu ranch was the first home of Ball and Arnaz’s two children, Lucie Arnaz and Desi Arnaz Jr. “[Ball] was a working mom, and when she was home, she was very businesslike about running her house,” Lucie Arnaz said in a 2011 interview. She noted that Ball’s onscreen Lucy was much more “outrageous” and humor-ruled than her actual mother was at home, but she savored the weekends and summers she could slip into the role of homemaker—presiding over dinners, homework, and garage cleaning projects.

The Chatsworth ranch had all the makings of a rural fantasy, with vegetable gardens, avocado and orange trees, a cow (reportedly nicknamed “The Duchess of Devonshire”), dogs, and chickens raised on the property.

Desilu also reportedly saw Hollywood parties, magazine photoshoots, marital fights and reconciliations, and even a Red Scare press conference. But the couple’s scrapbook photos from the ranch capture the same lived-in warmth they conjured on set for millions of viewers.

In 1954., Ball and Arnaz commissioned Paul R. Williams to build a new property for them: a Rancho Mirage retreat. The home, situated on the golf course of the Thunderbird Country Club in Palm Springs, was an example of Williams’s explorations in desert modernism.

A complete departure from the lush landscape and sitcom-worthy interiors of Desilu, the Thunderbird ranch’s open plan and window walls placed its modernist interior in conversation with the sweeping desert and mountain views outside.

Photos of the home from the Getty Research Institute document exposed stone walls, dark wood accents, a party-primed lanai patio space, and a swanky swimming pool. Here, it seems that Ball traded doilies and dusters for sculptural and spare decor elements, while Arnaz was reportedly so involved in the architectural planning that he claimed collaborative design credit with Williams.

The couple used the Palm Springs property as a retreat for five years during the height of their fame, and sold it when they divorced in 1960.

After selling Desilu ranch to former child actor Jane Withers, Ball and Arnaz took primary residence at 1000 North Roxbury Drive in Beverly Hills. The 7-bedroom home was bought by the couple in 1954 for $85,000; today, its estimated value is over $10 million.

According to a Beverly Hills Cultural Heritage Commission Report, the home was built in 1936 by Carleton Burgess in classic Colonial Revival style. Arnaz and Ball lived with their children in the home until their 1960 divorce.

While there are fewer intimate glimpses of Arnaz and Ball’s interior life (both literally and in design terms) at the Roxbury house, the property featured prominently in their public image. The home appeared on a beloved episode of I Love Lucy, centering actor Richard Widmark. According to the Cultural Heritage Commission, Ball’s famous neighbors also recall her handing out Halloween candy from the front door while dressed as a witch. Ball maintained ownership of the Roxbury estate through her second marriage to Gary Morton and until her death in 1989.
