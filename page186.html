#1446. SailPoint Intros New Capabilities to Address Agentic AI Identity Security Challenge

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI agents are moving from experiments into everyday enterprise environments, but the security controls around them are not keeping pace, according to enterprise identity security company SailPoint. In its latest Horizons of Identity Security report, SailPoint found that 79% of enterprises already have AI agents running in production, while just 2% have deployed identity security tools specifically designed to govern them.

That gap could become harder to ignore as organizations give agents more autonomy. Agents can increasingly access applications, sensitive data, and credentials, and in some cases take actions on a user's behalf, effectively creating a new class of identity that IT and security teams need to manage.

SailPoint, which develops identity security and access governance technology for enterprises, is responding with a collection of new identity security capabilities announced at its Navigate 2026 conference. The company is expanding its Agentic Fabric to discover "shadow AI" agents and provide runtime authorization and controls capable of immediately disabling an agent.

The company also introduced what it calls SailPoint Autonomous Agents, specialized AI agents designed to govern other agents. The security agents can monitor runtime activity, intercept threats, and adjust permissions while allowing legitimate agents to continue operating, SailPoint said.

The idea reflects one of the challenges created by increasingly autonomous AI systems: Traditional human approval processes may not be fast enough to govern software capable of taking large numbers of actions on its own.

"The idea that a security admin is going to review and approve access for a group of autonomous agents making 10,000 tool calls a second isn't just outdated — it's a fantasy," SailPoint CTO Chandra Gnanasambandam said.

Another part of SailPoint's approach focuses on visibility.

New endpoint and browser sensors, along with SIEM and XDR telemetry and non-human identity discovery capabilities, are designed to uncover agents, MCP servers, credentials, tools, and data stores that haven't been brought under an organization's identity program.

The company is also introducing Autonomous Identity Security Posture Management, or A-ISPM, which uses identity information from its Atlas platform to identify dormant accounts, orphaned access, shadow administrators, and other potentially risky access paths. Organizations can then revoke access, disable an identity or AI agent, assign an owner or automate enforcement.

Just-in-Time provisioning is another part of the strategy. Rather than giving people, service accounts, and AI agents permanent access, permissions can be granted when needed and subjected to conditions such as reauthentication or a business justification.

Capabilities across SailPoint's Agentic Fabric, Human Fabric, and Atlas platforms are beginning to roll out globally. A-ISPM and several other announced features are expected in the company's fourth quarter of fiscal 2027.

"Autonomous agents are no longer just experimental copilots; they are becoming the primary execution layer of modern enterprises," SailPoint President Matt Mills said.

Suppose you ask Copilot to add a custom page size to an application. Making the change is only part of the job. The new code should also follow the patterns your team already uses.

Repository custom instructions give Copilot that baseline context. Add a .github/copilot-instructions.md file to define guidance that applies across the repository, such as your preferred architecture, naming conventions, or expectations for testing and validation.

You can also create path-specific instruction files inside .github/instructions. These files use an applyTo field to tell Copilot when the guidance is relevant.

When Copilot works on a matching file, it can use both the repository-wide instructions and the more specific guidance. That lets you keep the broad rules in one place without forcing every file type into the same mold.

Custom instructions are useful for standards that should shape many tasks. But some guidance only matters when Copilot is performing a particular procedure, such as running quality checks, preparing a release, or debugging a failed workflow.

That’s where agent skills come in. A skill is a folder containing instructions and, optionally, scripts and other resources that Copilot can load when they’re relevant. Project-level skills can live in .github/skills, with each skill in its own subfolder.

#2583. Mental health experts say you shouldn’t ban your kid from group chats. Here’s what to do instead

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Group chats are often rife with bullying, exclusion and inappropriate content. Here's how to teach kids to use them wisely.

By Lauren Ramakrishna Updated Oct. 12, 2026

I’ll never forget the day my seventh grader got off the school bus with his head hung, walking at a snail’s pace up to the house. “What’s wrong, bud?” I asked. “Mom, I don’t want to wear my new shoes to school anymore.”

We bought those shoes the night before and he was ecstatic about them. They were flashy for sure—bright neon yellow covering every inch—but he absolutely loved them.

“What happened?” I asked. “The kids on the bus were making fun of them,” he replied. “And then one of the kids in my grade showed me that people were talking about them in the group chat.”

The group chat. I’d heard about it before. It started around fifth grade and had only grown as more and more kids got cell phones. My smartwatch-wearing son wasn’t in it, but that didn’t save him from falling victim to group chat bullying.

His experience follows a trend that pediatric mental health experts know all too well. “I commonly see increased anxiety, fear of missing out, disrupted sleep, social comparison, and increased peer conflict connected to group-chat activity,” says Cara Toretta, MD, an adolescent psychiatrist and Director of Silver Hill Hospital’s adolescent residential program in New Canaan, Connecticut.

Yet Dr. Toretta and other experts acknowledge that group chats aren’t all bad. “The benefit is that group chats can support friendships, social connection and coordination around school and activities,” Dr. Toretta says.

So, what’s a parent to do? Your instinct may be to forbid group chats outright, but experts agree that’s not the way to go. “Banning your kids from group chats could make them feel isolated and resentful toward you,” advises Vinay Saranga, MD, a child and adolescent psychiatrist and founder of Saranga Comprehensive Psychiatry in Apex, North Carolina.

“From a mental health perspective, a gradual approach with supervision, clear expectations and boundaries, and ongoing conversations is often more effective than unrestricted access or an outright ban,” Dr. Toretta adds.

Think of the group chat as another space for social learning. “Group chats give children a real-life window into group dynamics, peer pressure, and what we call the groupthink mentality—that feeling that you need to go along with the group in order to belong,” says Liz Nissim, Ph.D., a psychologist at Psychological and Educational Consulting in Livingston, New Jersey.

If you approach group chats with care, they can become a space where kids can practice relationship-building skills, boundary setting, how to navigate peer pressure, and build their own identities, Dr. Nissim says.

That’s clearly a necessary skill, sadly illustrated by the devastating events at Cornell University. Members of the Chi Phi fraternity posted pictures and derogatory language in a group chat, announcing that an intoxicated female student was sexually available. Not a single member of the group chat was brave enough to intervene, notify the authorities or even express disapproval. Now the student, known as Jane Doe, is suing Cornell University, the Chi Phi fraternity chapter at Cornell, and seven student members of the fraternity who she alleges participated in her gang rape.

“This story definitely reinforces that we need to teach these skills before kids are in the middle of a difficult group chat situation. They need to know what is clearly inappropriate and be ready to make smart choices when these situations arise,” Dr. Saranga says.

Learning how to handle difficult group chat situations develops skills that transfer beyond their digital life. “Teaching children how to pause before responding, consider how their words might affect someone else, recognize when a conversation has crossed a line, and have the confidence to step away are skills that will serve them far beyond the group chat,” Dr. Nissim says.

Assess your child’s responses to social situations that happen offline. Are they able to handle disagreement, stand up for themselves, and pull away from group activities when they start to head in a bad direction? These skills are necessary to make the jump to online discourse.

Dr. Toretta says other signs of readiness include the ability to follow family technology rules and seek help when something uncomfortable happens. “A child or teen who can tolerate feeling left out without becoming consumed by it and who shows kindness and good judgment is often better prepared for the challenges of group-chat participation,” she adds.

“Before giving a child that access, parents should have conversations about digital citizenship, privacy, kindness, peer pressure, and what to do when a conversation crosses a line,” Dr. Nissim advises.

Saranga says it’s wise to come up with potential scenarios your kids might encounter in group chats—gossip, bullying, inappropriate content, conflict—and discuss how they should handle them.

Begin with a smaller group chat with your child’s closest friends so they can dip a toe in the shallow end before trying to wade in the deep end.

“I think it’s a smart idea to start slow,” Dr. Saranga shares. “Before you allow your kids to chat with friends on all different platforms whenever they want, start with one platform and set a time limit of maybe 20 or 30 minutes per day so they have to prove they can handle it. Then you can slowly raise the limits.”

Don’t monitor every message going in and out. Instead you should check in with your child about what’s happening in the chat, the topics of discussion, how people are responding, and how your child feels about the chat, Dr. Nissim says. “The goal is to help children develop the judgment to manage these conversations independently, not to make them feel that an adult has to monitor every interaction forever,” she explains.

As you have these conversations, take advantage of opportunities to react to teachable moments. “We want children to learn that they don’t have to participate in every conversation, they don’t have to agree with the group, and they don’t have to stay in a space that makes them uncomfortable,” Dr. Nissim says.

Group chats can also help kids understand the nuances of digital communications, which lack the body language and other context clues of real-world interactions. Leadership expert Tara Furiani recalls a time when her daughters and their friends were playing a pretend game involving a “hostage.” “One of my daughters went to the group chat and said, ‘I know where you live.’ In the context of their game, it was pretend. Another mom understandably read it very differently when going through her daughter’s phone later,” Furiani says. While she was able to clear up the confusion with the other parent quickly, she used the incident as a teachable moment with her kids, ages 15, 13, 12, 9 and 9.

“I don’t expect a child to instinctively understand how something that made sense in a game could sound threatening on a screen. That’s something we have to teach,” she says.

If grades are falling, your child’s behavior changes, or they seem disconnected or depressed, something happening in the group chat could be to blame. “These are all signs that parents need to intervene,” Dr. Saranga says.

If your child comes to you to talk about hurtful interactions they had in the chat (or even just heard about, like my son), respond with interest and care. “Be curious with your child about what happened, help them put the experience in perspective, reinforce healthy friendships, and intervene with the school when the behavior crosses into bullying or significantly impacts their emotional well-being,” Dr. Toretta says.

Just because you make a decision to allow group chats doesn’t mean you can’t take it back. If you notice that participating in the chat negatively impacts your child’s happiness or discover they aren’t following your family’s expectations while chatting, it’s okay to put a pause on group chats.

Sometimes technology can be your friend. Parental apps like Bark and Quistodio use AI to scan your kids’ messages, flag potential risks and send you alerts when something concerning happens.

Or there’s Moxies, a research-backed social platform for girls ages 8–12, designed to help them learn how to be good digital citizens and practice digital communication with real-life friends through small, private, parent-approved groups. It was founded by Juliette Blake, a mom of three, who spent more than 20 years working the youth digital media landscape.

It’s important to remember that you should use these tools to flag particularly egregious or dangerous behavior while your child develops the skills to become their own group chat supervisor. It’s OK, and even advisable, to back away as your child proves they can handle group chat access without constant parental oversight.

My own experience with my son’s group chat struggle led to a valuable conversation about groupthink bullying, peer pressure, individual expression, and determining who is a real friend. I gave my son the space to express his disappointment and hurt, and a really hard moment brought us even closer. Plus, he knows he can come to me with hard conversations about group chats and I won’t fly off the handle.

He took a day off from his new shoes. But by day two post drama, they were back on his feet.

#4295. Struggling with impulse control? Try these 7 tips

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

You send the text before you finish thinking it through. You put in an online order before asking yourself whether you really need it. You eat three bites of the snack before you realize you’re not even hungry.

Impulsive moments like these aren't signs of a character flaw. They reflect your brain's tendency to act before it fully evaluates the consequences. For some people, that tendency shows up during a stressful stretch and eases once life calms down. For others, especially those with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), it's more of a constant struggle. Either way, impulse control is a skill you can improve.

So, what does this mean for you? Let’s break down what drives impulse control, and what you can do about it.

Impulse control is your ability to pause before you act on an urge, thought, or emotion — that split second where you decide whether to follow through.

Your prefrontal cortex, the part of your brain responsible for planning and thinking ahead, mostly handles this. When it's working well, it helps catch an impulse before that impulse becomes an action.

If your impulse control feels shaky at times, that’s not necessarily a willpower problem. It usually means your brain is under strain from stress, poor sleep, or emotional overload.

Most people who act impulsively on occasion don’t have a diagnosed disorder. For others, though, struggling to resist harmful urges is a persistent pattern. Impulse control disorder covers conditions like intermittent explosive disorder (sudden, disproportionate anger), kleptomania (compulsive stealing), pyromania (compulsive fire-setting), and compulsive gambling. These conditions may respond well to professional support, such as therapy or medication.

Your brain relies on two systems that don't always agree. The limbic system reacts fast, driven by emotion and instinct. The prefrontal cortex works slower, weighing your options before you act.

When you're calm and rested, the prefrontal cortex can keep impulsive urges in check. When your brain is pushed past its capacity, the limbic system takes over instead. Impulse control shifts throughout the day depending on how you're feeling, which is why the same urge can feel manageable to sit with one moment and impossible to resist the next.

A few factors can make impulse control harder:

sleep deprivation, which weakens your brain's ability to regulate behavior

chronic stress, which keeps your brain in a heightened, reactive state

strong emotions that feel too intense to ignore or resist

substance use, which affects your brain's decision-making centers

ADHD and other neurodevelopmental conditions, where pausing may be a challenge throughout daily life, not just under pressure

Like any skill, impulse control strengthens with practice. The tips below work at different layers: some help in the moment, while others ease the mental strain that makes urges harder to resist in the first place. Feel free to start with one or two that feel relevant to you.

Impulsive urges can feel pressing, but they don’t have to dictate what you do next. Often, just pausing for a moment is enough to break the pull.

Try: Counting to ten, taking a slow breath, or naming what you're feeling out loud. Some people find a go-to phrase useful, such as "not yet" or "wait and see" to delay their response. Over time, this habit starts to feel more natural.

Most impulsive behavior follows a pattern. It tends to spike around conflict, boredom, overwhelm, hunger, or fatigue. Once you learn your triggers, you can work with them instead of reacting in the moment.

Try: Keeping a brief log for a week or two. Note when impulsivity shows up, what leads to it, and how you feel. Once you spot a pattern—say, you're most reactive before meals or after a difficult conversation—you can plan around it.

Eat before that difficult conversation. Save demanding tasks for your sharper hours. Take a breath before responding to messages that tend to trigger a reaction.

Under pressure, your brain defaults to what's fast and familiar. If-then planning gives it a prepared response instead, so you're not improvising when emotions are running high.

Try: "If [situation], then I'll [response]." For example, "If I feel the urge to reply right away, then I'll wait 10 minutes first." Or "If I'm about to make an unplanned purchase, then I'll add it to a list and revisit it in 48 hours." The more specific the plan, the harder it is to talk yourself out of it later.

Impulse control runs on limited fuel: sleep, stress, and mental bandwidth all shape how much of it you have on a given day. That's why impulsive urges tend to show up most at the end of a long day or after a stretch of bad sleep.

You can't eliminate every source of strain, but you can cut down on low-stakes daily decisions. Try prepping meals or laying out clothes the night before — that way, you're not making choices when you're already running on empty.

It’s also worth protecting what restores you, including sleep, movement, time away from screens, and nourishing meals.

Regular mindfulness practice builds your ability to pause as an ongoing habit, not just in the moment an impulse hits. Start with a few minutes a day, and keep at it on a schedule that works for you.

Try: Focusing on your breathing during practice. When you notice an urge come up (to shift position, check your phone, stop early), gently return your attention to your breath instead of acting on it.

A therapist trained in cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) can help you identify what's driving your behavior and develop more effective responses. If ADHD is part of the picture, an assessment can open the door to options like medication.

If you have ADHD, tools such as if-then planning give your brain a prepared response before the moment of temptation arrives. Consistent mindfulness practice can also help you build the capacity to pause in those moments.

If impulsivity is getting in the way of your work, relationships, or daily life, it's worth talking to your doctor or an ADHD specialist. For some people, medication can meaningfully help.

Impulse control disorder may involve anger that feels bigger than the situation calls for, compulsive stealing, or gambling that's negatively impacting your life. If resisting an urge feels out of your control, that's worth bringing to a doctor — this condition is treatable.

Stress puts your brain into threat-response mode, which interferes with the part of your brain that handles careful decisions. That heightened stress state is useful in an actual emergency, where speed is more important than reflection. But in everyday situations, it makes thoughtful decision-making harder.

Mindfulness trains the same skill impulse control relies on: noticing an urge without immediately acting on it. Regular practice improves attention and emotional regulation, which makes it easier to find that pause in tempting situations. This can be especially useful if you have ADHD or you’re working on substance-related impulsivity.

The amount of time it takes to improve impulse control depends on your situation and your approach. Tools such as if-then planning or pausing before big decisions can help within a few weeks, while mindfulness and therapy tend to take longer — often several months of consistent practice.

Self-control is broader than impulse control: it covers habits like managing emotions and staying on task. Impulse control is more specific: it's about what you do in the gap between having an urge and acting on it. You can have strong self-control overall and still struggle with impulses in the moment, or be quick to pause in the moment while your broader self-control could use work.

Travel has always been a shortcut to adventure to me, and, because of that, I’ve often shorthanded adventure as requiring distance. I wondered if I could tap into the same thrills, all without ever stepping foot on a jet plane.

After a 10-hour train ride from New York City to Buffalo, I’d ride two main sections of the Empire State Trail—the west-to-east Erie Canal Trailway and the chain of rail trails and backroads running south from Albany back to my Harlem apartment—some 550 miles in all. Burdened by corporate vacation policies, Maggie decided she’d ride the Erie Canal section to Albany with me, then hop on the train home. In the months leading up to the trip, I pored over guides and online forums, of which there are many, building out a plan that involved a mix of camping and staying at bed-and-breakfasts in the towns along the way. Veterans of the trail warned of heavily trafficked sections of road around Kingston in the Hudson Valley and of the mosquitoes that swarmed the campsites at the various lock stations of the Erie Canal. Insiders cautioned against underestimating the coma-inducing qualities of a Rochester “garbage plate,” a bucketload of ground meat smothered in a fridge-tray’s worth of condiments. I took fastidious notes and drew up packing lists, weighing my panniers until I got to a weight I felt comfortable with. I plotted out days on the trail that would run us an average mileage of around 60, with a few “rest days” in the 30s baked in.

Seasoned bikepackers and bike tourers may find this all a little overdramatic. But having really only fallen hard for cycling in the last few years, I still don’t understand many things: a bike’s head-tube angle and trail; heart-rate zones and watts. But I do know travel. I know that so many of the clichés are true: that the best travel moments often appear out of the haze of the unexpected; that the journey can mean much more than the destination; that risk almost always reaps rewards; that who you travel with is often more important than where you go; that everywhere, no matter how seemingly mundane or obscure, has a little bit of magic waiting to reveal itself, if you just let it.

When it opened in 1825, the Erie Canal was the longest artificial waterway in North America and, in how it opened up the interior of the country to enterprising East Coasters, it was an embodiment of a pioneer mindset that saw a burgeoning country shape the land to its will and expand farther west. While it wouldn’t take long for the railway to replace canal barges as the go-to freight hauler, the political will and sheer toil that went into the canal’s construction would be a precursor to the hyper-capitalist nation emerging in the following centuries. “In time, this skinny ditch in upstate New York would demonstrate that trade and commerce are keys to the expansion of prosperity and freedom itself,” writes the historian Peter L. Bernstein in his 2005 history of the canal, The Wedding of the Waters.

Many of the settlements along the canal aren’t thriving like they were when this was a major thoroughfare of trade. Once tracing a path between vibrant industrial hubs, the waterway now closely mirrors the contours of the Rust Belt. Just as the grandeur of Victorian mansions points to what was, so do empty streets and faded “For Rent” signs on empty storefronts.

One such town, Brockport, is at least partially buoyed by the state university campus—or so I was told by Valerie Ciciotti, who runs Park Ave B&B in a painstakingly restored 1851 mansion that we stayed in. Graduation is the busiest time, she says, but bike tourists like us are becoming more common every summer. As we rolled out of Brockport the next morning, I wondered—not for the last time—whether a steady influx of tired, hungry cyclists could be enough to keep towns along the canal alive. (When I caught up with Ciciotti recently, she said the trail was already making a difference—the village government has become more directly involved with tourism, including designating Brockport an official Empire State Trail Town.)

We spent the next week on the Erie Canal in a kind of meditative rhythm that only comes from long trips, drifting in and out of conversations with each other, keeping count of kingfishers flitting along the water, and gasping when a heron swallowed a fish whole.

Outside Fairport, the skies turned black and dumped rain on us until we were laughing at the absurdity of trying to cycle through mud so dense it made our tires seize. Bystanders, wise enough to take shelter, cheered us on as we rode. We stopped under an awning and pried mud from our chains with our fingers. When the rain showed no sign of stopping, we just kept going—pulled along by the promise of a hot shower and a cold beer. We rode through sheets of rain until water poured out of our shoes. Once the deluge slowed, we tied wet clothes to our bikes. We looked like sailboats rolling down the trail.

One night, we camped at one of the locks along the trail, where boats are lifted to account for elevation changes in the ground. (Cyclists can camp at certain locks for free but are encouraged to call the lockkeeper ahead of time as a courtesy.) As the light faded and the mosquitoes emerged, I spoke to a pair of retirees on a boat who were following the same path as so many goods-bearing barges once did—from the Great Lakes to the Hudson and the sea. We woke up in the middle of the night to the infernal shrieks of nearby foxes. The following morning, we rode into Utica for breakfast at Golden’s diner, itself a throwback, where one man behind a counter flipped eggs and pointed to the self-service coffee station while bebop played from tinny speakers.

For our last night on the canal, we stayed in the Amsterdam Castle, which looms over the tiny town of Amsterdam like a tyrant’s keep. A bizarre manifestation of some sort of Arthurian fantasy, the hotel features suits of armor in the lobby and velvet bedding in the rooms. New York State truly contains multitudes.

I thought about all the many things I’d seen and done in my travels to far-flung places—witnessing a solar eclipse in Chile, kayaking alongside humpbacks in Antarctica, hiking through prehistoric rainforests in New Zealand. If novelty is what drives our travel decisions, it was rapidly becoming clear to me that I didn’t always need to fling myself halfway around the world to find it.

Because the Empire State Trail is mostly a consolidation of existing paths and an effort to fill the gaps between them—and because of the vast distances the network covers—the character of the trail changes the farther along it you ride. I noticed this as soon as I said goodbye to Maggie in Albany at the eastern terminus of the Erie Canal and turned south on the first of a number of rail trails that would take me back to New York City over the next three days. With the canal no longer a constant companion, I found myself instead passing through bucolic suburbs, open farmland, and, occasionally, dense sections of forest. Riding alone after days with someone else was an adjustment, too. I found, just a few hours after turning south, I was talking to myself—making observations about the lack of shade and the odd, ashy taste in my mouth from forest fire smoke drifting south from Canada. It made for tough riding—and I took much more frequent rest stops.

When planning my trip, I knew I’d pass through major regional hubs like Hudson and Kingston. But I also wanted to experience corners of the state I hadn’t seen before, and I chose my stops for the night with that in mind. That’s how I ended up veering a mile off the trail into the storybook village of Kinderhook. I checked into the luxurious Old Dutch Inn, a new hotel housed in a modernized 19th-century building with views of the village square. It had only just opened a few weeks prior. The co-owner, Jennifer Ose-MacDonald, showed no trepidation when she welcomed my mud-streaked, sweat-drenched self into her immaculate establishment, where snow-white linens hung from the windows. Knowing I was one of the first of what she hoped to be many bike-traveler guests, she asked me for advice on bike racks. I told her that though the exact model of bike rack wasn’t that important, alas, a lot of cyclists might not be as comfortable locking up their bikes outside overnight as I was. (Ose-MacDonald says she has since carved out space for indoor bike storage and has added a trove of tools for cycling guests.)

Ose-Macdonald’s partner, Jake Samascott, runs a much-visited orchard and farm shop in town but is also the president of the Columbia Friends of the Electric Trail, an organization of volunteers who maintain the section of trail that passes through Columbia County, mowing and trimming the greenery around the path. For years it was an unused, overgrown corridor through which power lines ran. More than a thoroughfare for bike tourists like me, the trail is a community asset, Samascott says. “I have three little girls who wanted to learn to ride bikes—I want them riding on a trail and not on the road.”

I saw how these paths build community throughout my trip but especially on the rail trails that run one into another through the Hudson Valley until they end in Van Cortlandt Park in the Bronx. Travelers like me were few and far between, but I passed dozens of families out on bikes, joggers with their dogs, even summer-school groups. Several, seeing my bags, and the level of grime on my face and body, would offer a thumbs-up or shout, “You’re almost there,” though they didn’t know where I was going.

New York State isn’t all smooth-rolling. Idyllic sections of tree-shaded gravel paths on rail trails are broken up by hilly roads and, at one point around Kingston, a traffic-snarled bridge. Unlike the Erie Canal section, campsites along the Albany-NYC axis of the trail are largely nonexistent, which means budgeting for hotels, and that can be pricey, especially during the summer high season. The penultimate stretch of trail, leading to the town of Brewster, inches steadily uphill for at least 15 miles, which, after more than a week of long days, felt like I was conquering the Dolomites. Arriving in Brewster—still about 50 miles from the northern edge of New York City—I found none of the charming, independent B&Bs or idyllic campsites I had depended so much on during my trip. I could have opted to make a break for it and ride a century straight from the Poughkeepsie area to the city on my last day, but I didn’t have it in me. I bought a gas-station beer the size of my head, checked into a soulless Comfort Inn, and ordered a pizza.

Over the course of the whole trip, 11 days in all, I thought often about infrastructure and movement; about how much effort has gone into inventing new ways to travel, ever faster, ever cheaper, ever more efficient. By the time the Erie Canal was completed, the railroad was already ascendant. On large sections of the Hudson Valley’s various rail trails, the rusted remains of iron rails sit, fossilized and inert. Alongside them, on the very beds of dirt where they once ran, the gravel is crisscrossed with bike-tire tracks and packed down with the footprints of millions of pedestrians.

I stopped often, to stretch, to photograph, to think. As it does when traveling on your own power, every mile felt like an achievement, no matter how repetitive and, at moments, no matter how difficult the actions were. Even while choking on smoke, with my legs burning and my bike making new and unfamiliar sounds (brake rub, it turned out), I found myself drawn onward by a sense of discovery—the feeling that I was getting a more solid picture of a concept that has eluded me for most of my life: home.

The closer I got to New York City, the more anything resembling silence was replaced with the constant whir of nearby interstates. Planes rumbled overhead. How much faster can we possibly go? What comes next in our constant hunt for peak efficiency? And what will we do with all that extra free time, anyway? The bike and all the places it can take me beckon.

#3254. Controversy Continues on Dhaulagiri as Expeditions Called Off

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

After Manaslu, Dhaulagiri is the second 8,000’er this season with no summits. The rope-fixing team says they stopped working for safety reasons, but some climbers suspect other motivations and describe a tense atmosphere.

Paul Clarke of Australia — who had been waiting at Camp 2 for five days — shared his frustrations with ExplorersWeb. Clarke had just learned that the rope fixers had stopped working above Camp 3 and were retreating. He described a lack of motivation among the Sherpa rope fixers after VIP clients with Seven Summit Treks, the company in charge of fixing the route, had called off their summit attempt. Soon after, all 20 climbers waiting at Camp 2 were told to return to Base Camp.

“Makalu Lakpa came through the lower camp this morning after the decision had been made,” Clarke said. “It was a pretty uncomfortable atmosphere, and I think everyone understands just how badly this has affected the rest of the teams on the mountain.”

Clarke’s outfitter, 8K Expeditions, sent a Sherpa team to retrieve their gear and tents from Camp 3 at 7,100m.

A helicopter lands at Dhaulagiri Base Camp. Photo: Paul Clarke/@outdoorpirate

Makalu Lakpa Sherpa is leading the 14 Peaks Dhaulagiri Expedition, one of the two largest teams, together with its sister company, Seven Summit Treks. Today, he posted a message explaining that increasing winds on October 10 resulted in a higher risk of slab avalanches on October 11, when his team was due to attempt the summit. He says that is the only reason his team returned to Base Camp.

In an unusual remark, Lakpa stated that he looks after his own group and cannot be held responsible for “other teams’ summit ambitions, climbing strategies, or rope-fixing arrangements.” He also noted that the rope-fixing work was assigned to Seven Summit Treks, not his team.

Finally, he spoke out against climbers who had expressed their disagreement yesterday:

To those criticizing our decision from a distance, I ask you to understand the actual mountain conditions before passing judgment. Assessing risks firsthand and making decisions that determine whether a team returns home safely is very different from commenting on social media.

Guides working for Lakpa’s company and for Seven Summit Treks have posted messages on social media supporting his decision. Benny Lieber, an American guide working for 14 Peaks, posted his assessment of avalanche risks on the mountain:

A post on Dhaulagiri conditions by Benny Lieber on Instagram. Photo: Benny Lieber/Instagram

Chhang Dawa Sherpa, leader of Seven Summit Treks, argued that the weather window was too short, given that a summit push would take “several days.” The plan was to move up to Camp 3 on October 11, reach the summit on the morning of October 12 (with October 13 as a buffer day), and return to Base Camp the following day.

Chhang Dawa, a director at Seven Summit Treks, posted about Dhaulagiri’s weather forecasts. Photo: Chhang Dawa/Instagram

Still, these explanations have not satisfied some climbers. Their biggest frustration came from watching the mountain in perfect, windless weather (though a wind plume eventually grew on the summit, according to Benny Lieber).

“From my perspective, one of the biggest frustrations is how badly the weather window appears to have been misread by Makalu Sherpa,” Paul Clarke said. “The strong winds and poor climbing conditions yesterday were not a surprise, since we had all been watching the forecasts for days, and winds in the 40–50kph range had been clearly visible well in advance.

“Despite that, Makalu Sherpa sent the rope-fixing team and his VIP clients higher on the mountain yesterday in conditions that were obviously going to be difficult. That has left climbers from multiple other operators stranded by a decision they had no control over. The consequence is that the rope-fixing team has now come back down, the VIP clients are no longer continuing [a helicopter was waiting for them as they arrived in Base Camp], and we have just been told that all our expeditions are over.”

Vincere Zeng of Singapore also shared her pòint of view with ExplorersWeb. Zeng has summited seven 8,000’ers, and was the first Singaporean woman to summit K2 and Annapurna. “I attempted it [Dhaulagiri] last autumn as well, so I was fully aware of the risks involved, which is why I had been monitoring the weather closely every day,” she said.

Zeng explained that her group was aware of high winds on October 9 and 10, and therefore waited at Camp 2. They planned to attempt the summit today, on October 12, in the morning. “We also considered Oct 12-13 as a possible buffer, as the next strong wind cycle was forecast to arrive around the afternoon of Oct 14,” Zeng explained.

“What I did not understand was why the 14 Peaks team chose to move toward Camp 3 (~7,000m) on Oct 10, which was the windiest day in the forecast, and then cancelled the rope fixing based on the conditions they encountered that day,” Zeng said. “From our side, we hoped we would at least have the opportunity to assess conditions during the originally planned Oct 11-12 window.”

Both Clarke and Zeng said that the 8K Sherpas who went to Camp 3 yesterday to retrieve equipment reported good conditions to that point.

“Of course, that does not mean conditions higher up, particularly around the traverse and summit slopes, were necessarily safe,” Zeng noted. “But it was one reason I hoped conditions could be reassessed once the forecasted wind had passed, especially as we still had time to do so. Instead, we were informed that the expedition was cancelled before we had that opportunity.”

Vincere Zeng (with green down suit) waits at Camp 2 in good weather last weekend. Photo: Vincere Zeng

Zeng explained she was “absolutely prepared to turn around if an assessment showed that conditions were unsafe.”

Clarke confirmed that all commercial expeditions on Dhaulagiri have called off their attempts. With the big teams pulling out, there is no manpower to launch another commercial-style attempt.

“At this point, nearly all of the teams have already packed up their Base Camp operations, and almost all of the climbers have flown out by helicopter, either yesterday afternoon or this morning,” Clarke said.

One team, however, has no intention of leaving. Andrzej Bargiel’s expedition, which includes a significant support team and film crew, is still hoping to summit and ski down the mountain, as he explained to fellow climber Xenia Santha of Hungary today.

“We wish Andrzej [Bargiel] the best of luck,” Clarke said. “If he can continue and summit, it will obviously provide another very clear picture of what the conditions on the mountain were actually like during this window.”

Risk assessment is always a tricky issue on commercial expeditions. Climbers pay large sums of money and expect every possible summit chance. It is often hard to explain when conditions are too risky, considering that factors include avalanche risks and wind, but also group sizes, the skills and endurance of guided climbers, complex logistics, and the disastrous consequences of a wrong decision.

However, in this instance, it is the lack of choices rather than the conditions that has frustrated climbers. Ironically, commercial expeditions on big mountains are more interdependent that ever, but communication between them, and sometimes with their clients, can be poor.

“As a client, I have no exact knowledge of the rope-fixing arrangement or collaboration agreed between the different outfitters,” Zeng said. “As we were a relatively small team with less manpower [8K Expeditions], we were to some extent dependent on the main rope-fixing team and whatever cooperation had been agreed between the operators. So, once the main fixing effort stopped, in practical terms it also affected our ability to continue.”

“Seven Summit Treks and Makalu Lakpa are now, from our perspective, trying to justify the decisions that were made by saying the weather was the reason they stopped,” Clarke concluded after reading the posts by Makalu Lakpa and Chhang Dawa today. “Based on the forecasts we had been watching for days, what we saw ourselves, and now the reports from climbers and Sherpas who were actually higher on the mountain, I simply don’t agree with that explanation.”

Large expedition operators sometimes react only when climbers openly share their experiences and thoughts, and their reaction is often to try to stop the dissemination of news from the mountains.

Clarke and his climbing partner, Xenia Santha, said media coverage had caused tension. Santha received a text message asking why she and Clarke had been speaking to the press. “I found that quite surprising; we have only spoken publicly about what we experienced firsthand and about decisions that directly affected our expedition,” Clarke said.

Adventure Has No Retirement Age: Adventure is continuing well into the ninth decade of life for a growing number of paddlers and hikers. Ninety-one-year-old Dale “Greybeard” Sanders only took up extreme endurance challenges at 80. Since then, he has paddled the Mississippi River from source to sea twice, completed the Missouri River Race, and this year reclaimed the record as the oldest person to thru-hike the Appalachian Trail.

The challenge of competing and breaking records keeps him going, but not everyone is chasing endurance records. For Wendy Eden, 77, the goal is just to be out in nature and to keep exploring her beloved Canadian wilderness on her own. That means adapting her approach as she gets older.

I Hitchhiked From Sydney to Perth: Aston Brown describes his experience of hitchhiking across Australia from Sydney to Perth. He sees hitchhiking as an opportunity to surrender control and connect with people he would otherwise never meet.

Far more common in the 1970s and 1980s, the practice plummeted in Australia because of its association with serial killer Ivan Milat, who targeted hitchhikers. Now it is starting to pick up again, as online communities and social media introduce it to a new generation.

Brown recognizes that hitchhiking carries dangers and that his experience as a man does not reflect the risks women traveling alone face. Yet his journey left him with a sense of connection. He spent three days and 1,900km traveling with Neil, a 79-year-old widower, on a four-month road trip around Australia. He also met a Zimbabwean family who had left the U.S. because of gun violence and immigration crackdowns, a retired tractor enthusiast, and Doug, head of Australia’s national science agency, who invited him birdwatching to see one of the country’s rarest birds.

She had set out on a multi-day camping trip and was expected home within four days. When she failed to return, friends and family raised the alarm. Rescuers found her camper van at the trailhead and launched a search. They found her one day later, at the bottom of the embankment. Despite suffering injuries from the fall, dehydration, and hypothermia from the cool nights, Granbery was still conscious when the rescue team reached her.

On the RN7 highway, they drove through rainforest, highland rice paddies, canyons, and desert, encountering rare species including golden bamboo lemurs, ring-tailed lemurs, and Verreaux’s sifakas.

Leaving the highway, they ended up on rough tracks with tricky river crossings, mud pits, and routes so difficult to navigate that they hired a local guide, known as a pisteur, to find their way.

#4058. Scientists find hidden driver of aging and a simple amino acid that fights memory loss in mice

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

What if some of the changes we associate with aging begin with the loss of a single protein deep inside the brain? Scientists investigating that possibility found that restoring a protein called Menin in older mice improved memory, balance, bone mass, and skin thickness in just 30 days. Even more surprisingly, supplementing the animals with a naturally occurring amino acid improved their learning and memory in only three weeks.

The findings, originally published in March 2023 in PLOS Biology, came from researchers led by Lige Leng of Xiamen University in China. Their experiments identified a previously overlooked connection between inflammation, brain chemistry, and aging throughout the body. Although the results were obtained in mice, they raised an intriguing possibility: Some effects of aging may be influenced by biological processes in the brain that scientists could eventually learn to modify.

Research published since then has added evidence that the brain plays an important role in regulating aging. It has also revealed why the seemingly simple supplement findings deserve careful interpretation.

How a Hidden Brain Protein May Influence Aging

At the center of the discovery is the hypothalamus, a small but powerful region near the base of the brain. It helps regulate essential functions such as body temperature, hunger, metabolism, sleep, and hormone production. Because these systems affect nearly every organ, changes in the hypothalamus can have consequences throughout the body.

Scientists have increasingly recognized the hypothalamus as an important player in aging. One possible reason involves neuroinflammation, the brain's inflammatory response. Although inflammation helps protect against infections and injuries, persistent inflammation can interfere with normal cellular function and contribute to age-related deterioration.

Earlier work by Leng and colleagues had identified Menin as a protein that helps restrain inflammation in the hypothalamus. Menin also participates in regulating gene activity, allowing it to influence multiple biological pathways.

The researchers wanted to understand what happens when this protective protein begins to disappear with age.

They discovered that Menin levels gradually declined in certain hypothalamic neurons as mice grew older. Interestingly, the same decrease was not observed in astrocytes or microglia, two other important types of brain cells involved in supporting neurons and managing immune responses.

To investigate whether this decline actually contributed to aging, the team developed genetically modified mice in which Menin could be selectively disabled.

The results were striking. Mice with reduced Menin activity developed greater inflammation in the hypothalamus, thinner skin, reduced bone mass, and worsening cognitive performance. Their lifespans were also modestly shortened.

Rather than simply accompanying aging, the loss of Menin appeared capable of helping drive several of its characteristic changes.

Restoring Menin Reversed Several Signs of Aging in 30 Days

The researchers then asked an even more interesting question: If declining Menin contributes to aging, could restoring the protein reverse some of the damage?

To find out, they used a gene delivery technique to increase Menin production in a specific part of the hypothalamus in older (20-month-old) mice. This region, called the ventromedial hypothalamus, helps coordinate metabolism, energy balance, and other bodily functions.

Just 30 days after treatment, the animals showed measurable improvements.

Their skin became thicker, bone mass increased, and performance improved on tests measuring learning, memory, and balance. Researchers also observed reductions in inflammation and improvements in certain metabolic changes associated with aging.

In a separate survival analysis, mice receiving the Menin treatment also lived longer than untreated controls.

These findings suggested that some biological changes associated with old age could be at least partially reversed by restoring Menin activity in a specific group of brain cells.

But the researchers also uncovered an important clue about how Menin produced these effects.

A Simple Amino Acid Offers a Surprising Clue

When Menin levels declined, the researchers noticed that concentrations of D-serine, an amino acid involved in brain signaling, also fell.

Amino acids are best known as the building blocks of proteins, but some perform other important functions. D-serine helps regulate NMDA receptors, specialized proteins on nerve cells that are essential for communication between neurons, learning, and the formation of memories.

D-serine is closely related to L-serine, an amino acid commonly found in foods such as soybeans, eggs, fish, and nuts. The two forms have different biological roles. Although D-serine is also available as a dietary supplement, consuming ordinary serine-rich foods is not equivalent to taking the form used in the experiments.

Further investigation revealed that Menin helps control the production of PHGDH, an enzyme involved in the biochemical pathway that generates serine (which was in turn regulated by Menin). When Menin activity decreased, this pathway became less active, reducing the amount of D-serine available to the brain.

Restoring Menin increased D-serine levels in the hippocampus, a region especially important for learning and memory.

This connection suggested that the cognitive effects of declining Menin might be partly explained by disruptions in D-serine signaling.

To test the idea directly, the scientists gave older mice D-serine in their drinking water for three weeks.

The supplementation improved their performance on learning and memory tests, even without directly restoring Menin.

However, there was an important distinction. While increasing Menin improved both cognitive function and physical signs of aging, D-serine supplementation primarily improved cognition. It did not restore the age-related losses in bone mass and skin thickness.

That difference suggests Menin influences aging through several biological mechanisms, with D-serine accounting for only part of its effects.

Later Research Strengthens the Link Between the Brain and Aging

Since the original 2023 discovery, additional studies have provided evidence that the hypothalamus helps coordinate aging across the body.

In a 2024 study published in Cell Metabolism, researchers at Washington University School of Medicine identified a different group of hypothalamic neurons that communicates with fat tissue. By maintaining or stimulating this communication pathway, they increased physical activity, improved age-related metabolic function, and extended lifespan in mice.

That work involved a different protein and neural circuit from Menin, but supported the broader idea that signals originating in the hypothalamus can influence how the rest of the body ages.

Another 2024 study in the Journal of Physiology and Biochemistry provided additional insight into Menin itself. Researchers found that increasing Menin helped protect cultured hippocampal cells against inflammation and a form of programmed cell death triggered by exposure to the stress hormone corticosterone. Although these laboratory experiments did not establish an anti-aging effect, they supported the idea that Menin has important protective functions in brain cells.

An even larger picture emerged in January 2025, when scientists published a detailed analysis of approximately 1.2 million mouse brain cells in Nature.

The researchers discovered that some of the brain cells most sensitive to aging were concentrated around the third ventricle, a fluid-filled cavity in the hypothalamus. Many aging cells showed reduced activity in genes involved in normal neuronal function and increased activity in genes associated with inflammation and immune responses.

These findings highlighted the hypothalamus as a potential center of vulnerability during brain aging, although they did not directly test the Menin pathway.

New Findings in 2026 Reveal More Aging Pathways

Research published in 2026 has further strengthened the connection between hypothalamic activity and longevity.

In a study published in Cell, scientists found that strengthening daily biological rhythms in a specific hypothalamic region improved several aging-related measurements and extended lifespan in male mice. The intervention also reduced estimates of biological age based on DNA methylation, chemical modifications to DNA that change with age.

Another 2026 study in Cell Metabolism identified a protein called Mimecan that helps skeletal muscles communicate with the hypothalamus and brown fat, a specialized tissue involved in generating body heat. Restoring circulating Mimecan levels in older mice improved this communication and extended lifespan.

Together, these discoveries suggest that aging involves complex communication between the brain and the rest of the body. Rather than depending on one master switch, multiple neural circuits, hormones, and metabolic signals appear to help determine how age-related changes unfold.

Could D-Serine Supplements Protect the Human Brain?

The possibility of improving memory with a relatively simple amino acid is especially appealing. However, evidence that D-serine can reverse age-related cognitive decline in humans remains limited.

A small randomized clinical trial published in 2016 tested D-serine in 50 healthy older adults. Participants performed better on one assessment of spatial learning and problem-solving after receiving the amino acid, but researchers did not find significant improvements across the other cognitive tests or mood measurements.

More importantly, later research suggests that the effects of D-serine depend on the biological circumstances.

In an April 2025 study published in Cellular and Molecular Life Sciences, scientists investigated D-serine in mice genetically engineered to develop features of Alzheimer's disease.

Unlike the Menin experiments, which associated reduced D-serine with age-related memory impairment, this study found that an early increase in D-serine accompanied disruptions in brain signaling. Removing the enzyme responsible for producing D-serine prevented or reduced several later cognitive impairments in those mice.

These findings do not invalidate the earlier results. Instead, they suggest that D-serine signaling is more complicated than simply having too little or too much. Its effects may depend on the condition being studied, the timing of treatment, and how the brain's signaling systems are functioning.

There are also unanswered questions about the long-term safety of D-serine supplementation. High doses have raised kidney safety concerns in animal experiments, and research has not established whether prolonged supplementation is a safe or effective way to prevent age-related memory loss in otherwise healthy people.

For now, D-serine should be considered an experimental approach rather than a proven treatment for brain aging.

Can Scientists Eventually Slow Aging by Targeting the Brain?

Despite the encouraging mouse experiments, researchers still need to answer several fundamental questions.

Why does Menin decline in the hypothalamus as animals age? How many of its effects could be reversed by treatment, and how long would those improvements last? Could restoring Menin influence other biological systems in unexpected ways?

It is also unclear whether treatments targeting Menin or D-serine could reproduce similar results in humans.

Nevertheless, the original researchers believe the pathway deserves further investigation.

"We speculate that the decline of Menin expression in the hypothalamus with age may be one of the driving factors of aging, and Menin may be the key protein connecting the genetic, inflammatory, and metabolic factors of aging. D-serine is a potentially promising therapeutic for cognitive decline," said Leng.

Leng adds, "Ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH) Menin signaling diminished in aged mice, which contributes to systemic aging phenotypes and cognitive deficits. The effects of Menin on aging are mediated by neuroinflammatory changes and metabolic pathway signaling, accompanied by serine deficiency in VMH, while restoration of Menin in VMH reversed aging-related phenotypes."

The growing body of evidence points toward a potentially important shift in how scientists understand aging. Instead of viewing it only as the gradual deterioration of individual organs, researchers are investigating how specific brain pathways may actively shape the process.

The possibility that restoring those pathways could improve memory, physical function, or longevity remains experimental. But the Menin discovery, together with subsequent findings, offers a compelling reason to explore whether some of the biological changes associated with aging are more reversible than previously thought.
