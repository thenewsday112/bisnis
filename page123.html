#950. Copycat In-N-Out Burger

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A copycat In-N-Out burger, served "Animal Style," isn't just a burger. It's a way of life.

When I get back to Los Angeles, my first stop is at In-N-Out Burger for an Animal Style Double-Double. I live in Chicago now, but when I worked in L.A. restaurants, In-N-Out was always the reward waiting on the other side of a closing shift. This copycat version brings that exact order to my midwestern kitchen, from the mustard-fried patty to the precise way everything gets stacked.

“Animal Style” in In-N-Out parlance means the patties get fried with a smear of mustard (which gives it a tangy, caramelized crust you can’t get any other way), covered in cheese, and served with a heap of caramelized onions. Most important is the spread. I am so obsessed with copycat In-N-Out sauce that I’ll put it on just about anything. Like most Double-Doubles, this one has crisp iceberg lettuce, tomato slices, dill pickle chips and a soft, buttered, grilled bun that holds everything together.

Every bite of this burger hits salty, sweet and tangy all at once. That combination is exactly why I still crave this burger more than almost anything else in Southern California. Pair this burger with a batch of Animal Style fries, and you’ve got the whole drive-thru experience (minus the incessantly long lines) without ever leaving your kitchen.

Combine the mayonnaise, ketchup, sweet pickle relish, white vinegar and sugar in a small bowl. Cover and refrigerate the sauce for at least one hour.

Editor’s Tip: Make a double batch of this sauce. You’ll want extra for dipping fries.

In a large cast-iron skillet, heat the oil over medium heat. Add the onions and the remaining salt. Cook and stir the onions until they’re softened. Reduce the heat to medium-low and cook them for 30 to 35 minutes or until they’re deep golden brown, stirring occasionally. Remove them from the skillet and keep them warm.

Editor’s Tip: If you’re short on time, try caramelizing onions in the microwave. It works!

Spread 2 tablespoons of softened butter over the cut sides of the buns. Toast the buns in the skillet over medium-high heat, cut sides down. Remove them to serving plates, and wipe the skillet clean.

Shape the ground beef into four 2-ounce, 1/2-inch-thick patties. Sprinkle them with salt and pepper. Add the remaining 2 tablespoons of butter to the same skillet. Cook the burgers, covered, over medium heat until a thermometer reads 160°F, four to five minutes on each side. While cooking, brush them with mustard. Top each patty with a slice of cheese. Continue cooking the patties until the cheese melts.

Editor’s Tip: To get a nice mustardy caramelization, add the mustard to the patty before flipping it, then swipe a little more on the top of the burger before adding the cheese.

Spread the chilled sauce over the bottom half of each bun, then layer on the lettuce, tomato slices and dill pickle chips. Place one patty on top, add a generous spoonful of caramelized onions, then stack on the second patty and finish the burgers with the bun top.

These burgers are best eaten fresh out of the skillet with the buns still warm and the patties hot. That said, you can store the components separately in airtight containers in the refrigerator for up to two days. Keep the sauce, caramelized onions, cooked patties and fresh toppings apart so nothing gets soggy, then reheat the patties in a skillet before reassembling. The sauce alone will keep in the fridge for about five days.

No In-N-Out-inspired meal feels complete without a side of Animal Style fries, smothered in the same combo of spread, caramelized onions and melted cheese. A crisp, cold milkshake (make mine malted) is another classic pairing if you’re going all in on the fast-food nostalgia. For something a little lighter, a simple green salad or a pile of dill pickle chips on the side keeps things balanced.

Animal Style is part of In-N-Out’s “Not So Secret Menu” that turns a regular burger into something a little more indulgent. The patty gets cooked with mustard on it, then the burger is loaded up with extra spread and a hearty helping of grilled onions. This copycat recipe builds that method right into the directions, so you get all that flavor without needing to know any insider lingo.

To make this recipe ahead of time, start by making the sauce and caramelizing the onions a day or two in advance. Store both in the refrigerator, then cook the patties and toast the buns fresh right before serving so everything tastes its best.

#3513. Running All the Marathon Majors Didn’t Live Up to the Hype. It Made Me Rethink Why I Run.

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

This article originally appeared in the Fall 2025 issue of Runner’s World.

I RUN PAST Buckingham Palace and, after 26 miles, can finally see it: my sixth World Marathon Major finish line, the moment many runners dream about. After running through the arch, I am shepherded through the chute with hundreds of other marathoners. A volunteer hastily puts a London Marathon medal around my neck, and another adds the Six Star Medal. I hobble behind other finishers in a queue waiting to get their photo taken in front of an Abbott World Marathon Majors poster. A fleeting thought comes to mind when it’s my turn to pose for the photographer: Is this it?

The next day, I sit in Heathrow Airport, scribbling on a blank page in my training journal: I just did something amazing—two marathons in one week. But I feel a little hollow. It’s just all so…pedantic? Silly? I’m going home with this gigantic medal and another even more gigantic medal—and for what?

I was hesitant about running the London Marathon. Even though I wanted to race it someday, I was daunted by the idea of it being six days after Boston, which is held on Patriots’ Day on the third Monday in April. I’ve qualified and raced every Boston Marathon since 2013 without fail. Skipping a year just to focus on running London was nonnegotiable.

My partner Paul and I had already gone through an IVF cycle and were planning on going through another round. I was starting to become more mindful of how races interrupted my menstrual cycle and how taxing the effort was on my body months after. Pregnancy and motherhood would also eventually set some limitations on racing and travel. Runner’s World Content Hype Director Aly Ellis, who was also running Boston and London, spoke frankly: “We’re at that age where we want to start families; you have to take these opportunities now.”

Though the Abbott World Marathon Majors series was on my radar, I honestly didn’t really care about earning that colossal medal at first. The series began in 2006 with five marathons: Boston, New York City, London, Chicago, and Berlin. It wasn’t until the Tokyo Marathon was added as a major in 2013 that runners who completed all six would be awarded a special medal. I thought, maybe, I’d eventually win entry to these races (all marathons except Boston have lotteries) and afford to travel to those places, but it seemed like an extravagant goal.

By 2022, I had run all of the stateside majors. Riding off of a sub-3:00 high in Chicago, I researched the Berlin website and saw there was another way to gain entry into that marathon: qualifying. I entered the 2023 race with a time under 3:10, the cutoff time for women ages 18 to 44, and got in. Then, Asics, the Tokyo Marathon sponsor, offered me a 2024 bib. New Balance, the brand sponsoring London, followed with an invitation to participate in this year’s race. Suddenly, earning my sixth star seemed like it would happen sooner rather than later.

And then it happened, and instead of feeling accomplished, I felt empty.

Maybe I felt depressed because I wanted a do-over. I didn’t wear the second bib Abbott provided that read: “Today is the day! Cheer me on” with all six majors listed, ending with London 2025 in big, bold red letters. I left it in my hotel room, unable to stop the safety pins from popping off the back of my sports bra. As I raced and saw fellow Six Star hopefuls with the bib on their backs, I felt both camaraderie and regret that I wasn’t as recognizable for attempting to complete the same feat.

Maybe I felt alone. As I was shuffled through the Abbott booth, straining a smile at the camera and walking around with two heavy medals clanging against my chest, I had no one to share the moment with.

The Six Star hardware had also lost its luster this year. Abbott had announced the inclusion of Sydney as a seventh star—but there would be no Seven Star Medal. At press time, the Shanghai and Cape Town marathons have also been evaluated as additions to the majors. In a Black Mirror turn, eventually you may be able to go the deluxe route, earning a Nine Star Medal. Which begs the question: When does it end?

For me, at least, money is always a factor. Participating in the Majors is an expensive commitment. Runners who aren’t NYRR members had to pay $315 for last year’s NYC Marathon. Travel and housing costs can’t be left out of that equation when it comes to international races. Majors are becoming more difficult to get into. The London 2026 ballot broke a world record with over 1.1 million applications. Those accepted are a mere fraction of that number; this year’s race had approximately 56,000 finishers.

You wouldn’t notice these race caps attending these events, however: Major races are event-filled weekends, with live shows that sell out fast, multiple shakeout runs vying for shared space on narrow sidewalks, and overcrowded activewear pop-ups that run out of extra-smalls.

I know I sound ornery. The fact that these events have reached music festival proportions is a good thing for our sport. But I can’t help feeling that the Nine Star Medal will be more possible for the privileged—those who can afford it and, yes, those with connections. I was able to gain access to two of the six races thanks to my job.

Ironically, while I was tipsy on a holiday cocktail last December, my fingers started tapping my info on the Sydney ballot form, thinking my chances of scoring a bib were slim. (I’ve never won a marathon lottery.) “Could you imagine if I got in?” I asked Paul, sitting in the middle of the restaurant while eating dinner, my eyes glued to my phone’s screen.

Weeks after my night of being the worst dinner date ever, I received an email from Race Roster with the “Claim this registration” button.

At this point, it doesn’t matter to me whether or not Sydney is a major. For years, I’ve loved running races—marathons especially—because it’s something athletic I’m actually good at, unlike ball sports like tennis, volleyball, or basketball that I’m very bad at. Now running is a reason to travel, the actual event a footnote in my itinerary. I honestly never thought I’d visit Japan or Germany. I fed monkeys in Kyoto. Ate apfelstrudel in the Literaturhaus Berlin garden. And I got to experience it with Paul, who supports my spur-of-the-moment race registrations, then cheers for me at the finish line. We celebrated my PR together in Chicago, shared the free pint every runner gets after crossing the finish line in Berlin, and toasted with sake after Tokyo. In London, I sat alone in the New Balance VIP tent, sipping on a gigantic lager I couldn’t even finish.

After an air traffic control delay prolonged my six-hour flight to Newark to 14 hours, I used the extra time to reassess. Even though Paul wasn’t there, I had so many people who helped me see that race through: Aly sitting next to me on the bus to the start, the runner offering his hand to me after I fell on the course, fish and chips with a grad school friend postrace.

I realized that sometimes, when you’re a small part of completing something big, you start to forget the reason you’re really doing it. For me, it was never for the hardware. It was about proving myself. And I did—several times. Now these races hold more meaning when I share them. And I’ll do that with Paul in Sydney.

Have you measured how many of your website’s visitors come from LLM tools like ChatGPT?

I looked at 12 months of GA4 data across 100 different websites of varying sizes with over 145 million sessions. In this dataset, sessions from ChatGPT amounted to just 0.62% of the volume driven by Google Organic.

When all other LLMs were included in this number, it increased to 0.73%.

While it’s interesting to measure and try to influence whether your content is being cited by ChatGPT, for the vast majority of sites this traffic is miniscule compared to Google organic visitors!

We can all see that AI is changing how businesses are found on the internet, but I think we’re missing the point by putting so much emphasis on people finding us via ChatGPT and similar LLMs.

The AI search that is actually sending people to businesses today isn’t ChatGPT. It’s Google.

Between AI Overviews, AI Mode, and a core ranking system that is now largely driven by AI, millions of people are using AI search every day without ever leaving Google.

We can lament over the fact that our clicks to informational content are drying up, but the reality is that the web is changing. If people are looking to engage with a business like yours, then Google Search is by far the most likely way they will find you.

I would encourage you to work on figuring out what a conversion or key event looks like for your business and tracking it. Applegate explained that losing the diagnosis didn’t mean losing the symptoms, leaving her feeling like she was “back at square one.” She’s one of a shockingly large number of people who receive MS misdiagnoses every year. A study in 2019 found that roughly 18 percent of MS cases resulted in a misdiagnosis (though the results originated from a relatively small sample size). Another meta-analysis five years later refined that number to roughly 15 percent.

“Diagnosing MS is a little bit like putting a puzzle together,” National Multiple Sclerosis Society’s Bruce Bebo recently told Scientific American. “There’s not a single diagnostic test that tells you, ‘You have MS.’” Many other diseases, such as fibromyalgia, neuromyelitis optica, muscular dystrophy, Lou Gehrig’s disease, or even simple migraines, share similar symptoms with MS and need to be ruled out.

Historically, doctors have been more likely to dismiss the symptoms of women than those of men. In fact, before the arrival of magnetic resonance imaging in the 1970s, diagnosing MS based on symptoms alone led the medical profession to believe the disease was more prevalent in men (MS is in fact significantly more prevalent in women). It’s unknown whether such gender-based bias played a role in Applegate's own diagnosis. But there’s evidence that it still happens. “We think that it will take less time to reach a diagnosis after a patient has the first symptoms of MS,” Cleveland Clinic’s Daniel Ontaneda said in 2025. “And also, since we’re incorporating more specific tests, we believe that the diagnosis will really be more efficient. Here’s hoping that the pain and confusion of the misdiagnoses experienced by Applegate and thousands of other women will soon be a thing of the past.

Body recomp (increasing muscle while decreasing fat) is no easy task. In the kitchen, you have to prioritize protein while consuming just few enough calories to maintain a caloric deficit. And in the gym, you need to load your muscles a few times a week to stimulate growth. When doing both at the same time, some people might find that the dumbbells that once felt manageable suddenly feel way heavier. That's because losing strength can be an unexpected side effect of body recomposition.

A loss of strength is one of the less obvious effects of body recomposition that many people don’t anticipate. You may assume gaining muscle automatically means gaining strength, but the two don’t always go hand-in-hand when you’re underfueled.

Even with the slightest calorie deficit, weights that once felt easy may feel heavier than usual. That’s because calories provide us with the fuel our muscles, heart, ligaments, and more need to exercise. When less energy is available, it can be harder to work out at your usual capacity.

I’m sure you’re thinking, “one low energy day in the gym is nothing to worry about.” And you’d be right—but when that feeling of weakness remains consistent, it can become more of a problem than you might realize. That constant feeling of defeat, unable to pick up the same weights you were before, might make you start to resent your routine or lifting in general. I’ve seen it in my clients before, and they tend to be more apt to skip their gym sessions when they’re feeling down on their capabilities in the gym—and that can be the start of a downward spiral that’s tough to break out of while still maintaining a deficit.

One of the best ways out of it is to get ahead of it. If you’re noticing a decline in strength while pursuing body recomposition, here are a few ways to minimize it and stay excited about your routine.

Maintaining strength during body recomp (and recomping successfully!) means setting up a system that works with you. If your calorie deficit is too large, you run the risk of feeling underfueled during your workouts, which can leave you lethargic and weak.

The good news is you don’t need an extremely large deficit to reach your goals. A deficit of 250 to 500 calories a day is enough to make sustainable progress, Dana White, RD, previously told Women’s Health. Body recomp is a marathon, not a sprint—so treat it like one.

Food fuels your daily life, your workouts, and so much more. Eating a meal or snack a little before exercising can help you power through a workout, especially when you’re in a calorie deficit.

Power up with a carb-heavy snack about an hour or so before you hit the gym, like some peanut butter on toast, veggies and hummus, or a bagel. If you are having a whole meal, make sure it’s not so heavy it trips you into a food coma, and try to eat closer to two to three hours before working out. Eating shortly before your workout can help ensure you have energy when you need it most.

Increasing the amount of weight you lift may be particularly difficult during body recomp. One day it might click, the next it doesn’t. Your muscles need sufficient stimulus to grow, which generally means training close to failure (two to three reps away from it, to be exact!). But increasing your weights is only one way to achieve that. Experiment with other metrics to create challenges instead.

Aim for a higher rep range, play with slower tempos (slow on the “down” portion of the rep), or swap in some more difficult variations of certain exercises. For example, try mastering an archer push-up instead of increasing the weight of your bench press, or trying to get your first pistol squat instead of loading up a back squat.

When you’re body recomping, it’s actually a great time to experiment with your routine. Doing so can help prevent burnout, and allow you to build strength in ways you may not have considered before. That empowerment will make you want to keep going, even if you’re not pushing the weight you used to.

This may seem obvious, but now is not the time to neglect your strength workouts. They might look a little different than they did before, but consistency will be key to maintaining your strength during a deficit. To do that, make your workouts as manageable as possible.

Is your workout taking up too much time? Streamline your program to three or four compound exercises, aiming to finish each set a few reps short of failure. Struggling to make it to the actual gym while juggling work, children’s schedules, and other responsibilities? Do some bodyweight workouts at home. Hate working out alone? Find a workout class you like.

Every workout counts when it comes to body recomposition. Find the routine that feels easy to return to over and over again—that’s the one you’re most likely to stick with, even when you’re feeling weak.

#3349. COROS Doubled the Price of Its Bike Computer. Can the DURA 2 Compete With Premium Rivals?

COROS kept the feature that made the original stand out and rebuilt nearly everything around it. Now the DURA 2 has to justify its place among other premium options.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

The original COROS DURA stood out for two reasons: battery life and price. At $249, it was a (relatively) affordable alternative to the Garmin and Wahoo computers most riders already knew. Just with a solar charging feature that made charging almost optional. But it also felt like the first-generation product that it was. The DURA 2 is COROS’s attempt to move beyond that niche and compete with computers from Garmin, Wahoo, and Hammerhead.

The DURA 2 has a new, unusually slim form factor, a larger 3.5-inch touchscreen, updated maps, and redesigned controls that replace the original’s thumb dial. It also adds voice commands, music controls, and a built-in bike bell. The screen is a memory-in-pixel display, rather than the bright, phone-like panel found on OLED displays on the Karoo and latest Garmin head units. COROS’s priority here is a legible readout while minimizing the power demand of the screen.

Battery life remains the headline. COROS rates the DURA 2 for up to 120 hours in standard GPS mode, or 70 hours with dual-frequency GPS, before factoring in solar charging. After 30 hours of riding with paired sensors, occasional navigation, and the screen at full brightness, my unit still had 77 percent battery remaining.

The price of the DURA 2 has changed along with the ambition. The computer costs $499 on its own, or $549 with COROS’s aluminum mount—a steep jump from the original, though you can skip the bundled mount if you already have a compatible one. The question is whether the DURA 2’s much longer battery life, improved interface, and the COROS ecosystem make it a compelling alternative at this price. After riding it, I think the new DURA is a much more complete and compelling computer. But it hasn’t quite closed the gap to its competitors.

The DURA 2’s memory-in-pixel screen won't win anyone over with vivid colors or smartphone-like detail. That’s fine with me. I don’t use a cycling computer to watch videos or scroll social media. I want to glance down, find the information I need, and get back to riding. The DURA 2 does that well, with arguably the most legible screen in direct sunlight I’ve seen.

The screen struggles most in inconsistent, high-contrast light, such as bright dappled sunlight through tree cover. This is when I wish the DURA 2 offered any kind of meaningful backlight. To my eyes, changing the screen brightness from full to all the way off made no noticeable difference. This wasn’t a huge issue on basic numerical data pages, which stayed very legible, but when you’re trying to discern map detail, it felt a little tough, especially while riding.

Some riders will still prefer the brighter, higher-resolution screens on the Hammerhead Karoo or Garmin’s high-end computers. That’s a valid preference. How beautiful a screen looks isn’t a huge priority for me.

The maps themselves are a big improvement over the original DURA. In my experience, the Hammerhead is still the standard for map legibility and ease of on-device navigation, but the DURA 2 is now quite good. It shows street names at closer zoom levels, and the map responds quickly when you pan or zoom. Both the processing speed and touchscreen response are dramatically better than on the first DURA, and are now close to what I expect from Garmin and Hammerhead.

One screen quirk is that Coros has no way to manually lock the screen. COROS says it will automatically lock when it detects too much rain. I would still like to have a manual lock setting, even if the automatic system works as promised. I suspect COROS could add that option in a future firmware update.

Good riddance to the dial. I gave the original DURA’s dial a fair chance, and it could be useful once I got used to it, especially with gloves. The screen is a little less responsive with gloves on, but not dramatically less than other touchscreens I’ve used. The physical buttons help, and the new layout feels more straightforward. The DURA 2 is simply easier to operate on the bike compared to the original.

The offset Garmin-style mount connection is a small but useful design feature. Larger computers can run into clearance problems with some proprietary out-front mounts. Because the DURA 2’s connection point sits farther back, I’m less concerned about that here. Even with the caveat that battery usage will vary from user to user based on factors like location and use case, the battery performance is extraordinary. For shorter rides, the advantage is less dramatic but still useful: charging becomes one less thing to remember. And Coros deserves kudos here for maintaining battery-life performance with the DURA 2 while adding a larger screen and more processing power to the unit.

Navigation on the DURA 2 with the updated maps is a clear improvement. The faster processor makes the device feel much less sluggish when zooming or scrolling the map, and the map is easy to read while riding. Street names at tighter zoom levels are a welcome addition compared to what the original DURA offered.

My main reservation is that rerouting still depends on your phone. I get the practical argument: most of us ride with a phone anyway, and the phone can handle it. But it still feels like a shortcoming in a $500 cycling computer. Other units can reroute on their own, and I’d rather not rely on a Bluetooth phone connection when I’ve already paid for a dedicated device.

The rerouting itself is kind of hit or miss. Most of my testing has been in Southeastern Pennsylvania, and so far the DURA 2 (or, I guess I should say, the Coros app on my phone) has sent me down a trail into a stream crossing on my commuter bike only once. Following a route I designed and loaded onto the DURA 2 has gone smoothly, as expected. But I wouldn’t trust the Coros-suggested routes when navigating to a point on a map or when rerouting in an unfamiliar place.

Finally, I personally don’t mind manually loading a route onto my head unit. But in 2026, route sync should be mostly automatic. Hopefully, they can add that in a future firmware update.

Sensor pairing and ride syncing have been trouble-free for me. I’ve had no issues with the sensors I use, and activities push to the app smoothly after a ride. Which is what I expect from a core cycling computer function in 2026.

Structured training is now more than passable. I don’t use my head unit to guide workouts, but I appreciate that COROS has made the feature more useful for riders who do. You can now pause an interval without pausing the ride recording, which feels like it should have been possible from the start, but I’m always happy to see improvements.

The COROS app and Training Hub are also strengths. In some ways, the experience is simpler than Garmin Connect, and I find parts of it easier to use. If you already wear a COROS watch off the bike, that integration is useful. As with Garmin Connect, though, you get the most from the system when you use the company’s hardware.

The new music controls are great, and they’re seamlessly integrated. The electronic bell sounds like an actual bike bell, which is great. But it can’t currently be paired to a bonus button on Di2 or AXS, which Coros can fix in a future firmware update.

The voice control is puzzling to me. Garmin just unveiled this feature on its latest 1060 unit, and I’m just not sure why. I’ve spent 20 years riding without talking to my cycling computer. I would much rather companies perfect their UI and button interfaces so riders can quickly and simply access the functions they need instead of implementing voice controls. I’m sure some riders will find it useful, but I can’t see myself talking to my cycling computer any time soon. Especially since every time I tried to talk to the DURA 2 while riding, it couldn’t understand me, so there's definitely room for improvement.

The price has doubled, and there's really no getting around that. I think the improvements, plus the new form factor, justify an increase. But at $499, the DURA 2 is basically the same price as the Hammerhead Karoo 3 and not much cheaper than the Garmin Edge 850. Both units have their own strengths and trade-offs, but they're polished and fully functional. By contrast, I have written the phrase “hopefully this gets fixed in a future firmware update” three times in this review.

The DURA 2’s biggest strength is that it no longer feels like a budget computer asking you to overlook its rough edges. It feels like a serious head unit with exceptional battery life, and if you regularly ride 20 hours straight, it’s the easy pick. If you don’t, the DURA 2 is still a big step forward over the original, but the rough edges still need a bit more sanding given the jump in asking price.
