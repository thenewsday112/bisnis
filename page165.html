#3950. Your aging brain may be trading memory for something more valuable

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Growing older is often associated with memory loss and declining mental abilities. However, scientists are questioning whether these changes tell the whole story. Rather than simply becoming less capable, the brain may be adapting to a new stage of life in which accumulated knowledge, wisdom, and understanding become more important than remembering every detail.

Researchers at the University of Arizona have proposed a different way of understanding cognitive aging. Drawing on findings from molecular biology and brain imaging, they suggest that the changes occurring in older brains may reflect a natural stage of human development rather than deterioration alone. Their work, published in Perspectives on Psychological Science, offers a new perspective on why thinking and memory change with age.

The researchers call their proposed explanation the adaptive aging hypothesis. It suggests that the brain gradually adjusts its priorities to make better use of a lifetime of accumulated experience. This could help explain why older adults often have valuable strengths in teaching, mentoring, storytelling, and passing knowledge to younger generations.

"If we look at normative aging as a story of just decline, we are really missing the boat," said study coauthor Fabian-Xosé Fernandez, an associate professor of cognition and neural systems at the University of Arizona. "The older brain is optimized for different things than the younger brain, predominantly the sharing of stored expertise and social relationships."

How the Brain Shifts From Memory to Meaning

Two brain regions play important roles in how people remember and interpret their experiences. The hippocampus helps preserve specific information about events, including their details and circumstances. The frontal cortex, meanwhile, is more involved in extracting broader meaning, recognizing patterns, and understanding the central message of an experience, sometimes called its "gist."

According to the researchers, the balance between these systems changes during healthy aging. As people grow older, the brain tends to place less emphasis on activity in the hippocampus and rely more on the frontal cortex. This shift may allow the brain to focus more on interpreting information through existing knowledge rather than recording every new experience in precise detail.

"The fact that you see this across people and animal models suggests there is an evolutionary driving force," Fernandez said. "One could argue you are developing your entire life, and development occurs in lockstep with the needs of your brain. When you're younger, the hippocampus turns on because you don't have a knowledge base or know how the world works. A kid absorbs information like a sponge because they are building an archive of information. In early life, the balance is tilted toward recollecting declarative memories with precision, detail, and context."

Young brains need to gather enormous amounts of new information because they are still building a foundation for understanding the world. By later adulthood, however, people have already developed extensive stores of memories, concepts, and experiences. Connecting new information to what they already know may become more useful than retaining every individual detail.

The researchers suggest that this changing balance reflects a shift in how the brain processes experience. Instead of concentrating primarily on collecting detailed memories, it increasingly emphasizes understanding relationships, identifying underlying meaning, and applying previously acquired knowledge.

Why Evolution May Favor Wisdom in Older Adults

Evidence of similar changes in animal models raises the possibility that this transition has an evolutionary explanation. Fernandez suggests that the aging brain may be adapting to help individuals preserve and share the knowledge they have acquired throughout their lives.

From this perspective, the ability to draw on decades of experience could be especially valuable for making judgments, guiding others, and transferring important information between generations. Older adults may therefore play a distinct role in supporting younger people through mentorship, teaching, and storytelling.

"Given those goals, it makes sense that you aren't interested in nitty-gritty details," Fernandez said. "You are interested in safeguarding the vast knowledge you already have. Evolution may be saying that if you make it long enough, there's a need to transmit this information, so we put into motion biological changes that enforce and protect your semantic and autobiographical memories."

Under this hypothesis, changes in memory and thinking are not necessarily signs that the brain is functioning less effectively. They may instead reflect a different set of priorities, with greater emphasis on preserving meaningful knowledge and using it to understand new situations.

Why Healthy Brain Aging Is Different From Alzheimer's

The adaptive aging hypothesis could also provide scientists with another way to examine the differences between normal cognitive aging and Alzheimer's disease.

Although both involve changes in memory, Fernandez emphasizes that Alzheimer's should not be understood as ordinary aging taken to an extreme. The biological processes involved are fundamentally different.

"When I was in grad school, people thought about Alzheimer's as a more intense version of cognitive aging, but that is just not the case," Fernandez said. "They are completely different."

During typical aging, the brain's gradual shift away from relying heavily on the hippocampus and toward greater involvement of the frontal cortex generally unfolds between the fifth and seventh decades of life. In Alzheimer's disease, however, abnormal processes involving proteins such as amyloid and tau may interfere with this transition.

One important distinction involves activity in the hippocampus. While healthy aging is generally associated with reduced emphasis on this region, Alzheimer's can involve unusually high levels of hippocampal activity. Rather than supporting the normal progression of cognitive development, disease-related changes may disrupt the brain networks responsible for memory and understanding.

"In normative aging, hippocampal activation is deemphasized, which is probably what should be happening in a 70-year-old. But in many cases of Alzheimer's, it turns out the hippocampus is hyperactive," he said. "This beautiful developmental transition is completely disrupted in Alzheimer's disease by pathological processes that mess with episodic memory and interfere with circuits that allow you to think about your own life, lexicon, and vocabulary."

These disruptions can affect the ability to recall personal experiences and access stored knowledge, including words and their meanings. Understanding how these changes differ from the adaptations associated with healthy aging may help researchers develop a clearer picture of normal brain development and neurodegenerative disease.

Embracing the Hidden Strengths of the Aging Brain

The researchers argue that society may need to reconsider what successful brain aging looks like. Encouraging older adults to process information exactly as younger people do may not always be the most productive approach.

Fernandez suggests that placing excessive emphasis on remembering precise details could work against the brain's changing priorities. "For many individuals, the weight of how you process information should probably not be so hippocampal-centric by the sixth or seventh decade."

Instead of judging older adults primarily by abilities that tend to weaken with age, the researchers encourage greater recognition of the capabilities that may become especially valuable later in life.

Creating more opportunities for social interaction could be one way to support these strengths. Relationships between older and younger generations may be particularly beneficial because they provide opportunities to exchange experiences, share expertise, and preserve knowledge that might otherwise be lost.

"People see older individuals and look at them as not being on par with younger individuals across a lot of metrics," Fernandez says. "The way I look at it is that you've got someone with a brain that has been active for several decades and has seen a lot. There is tremendous value there, and we don't do a good job of unlocking it."

The central message of the adaptive aging hypothesis is that growing older may involve more than a gradual reduction in cognitive abilities. Although some mental skills become less reliable with age, the brain may also be adapting to make better use of the knowledge and experience accumulated over a lifetime. Recognizing those strengths could change how society understands aging and the contributions older adults can make.

So, if you don’t have the time to devote to deep-cleaning the car every other week, what can you do to keep the car looking good until the next time you get a free afternoon to devote to a full clean? Instead of vacuuming every surface in the car, you can stick to the high-traffic areas for this car hack. Parham Koukia, lead car detailer and operations manager at Panda Hub in Toronto, Canada, says you should “vacuum carpet and mats using a soft brush attachment and use compressed air to dislodge debris stuck in vent openings and seat tracks.”

Simply remove the mats from the vehicle, vacuum the driver side floor and passenger side floor. You can skip the rear floor if you haven’t had anyone in the backseat recently. Make sure to vacuum or use compressed air to clean out the seat tracks, then vacuum your floor mats before returning them to the car to give your car floor a quick refresh.

Cleaning the car windows and mirrors does more than give your car a well-maintained appearance. The task also makes it safer to drive, since you won’t be struggling with hazy glass or smudges when you are trying to see your surroundings. Grab a microfiber cloth and use a store-bought cleaner or make a vinegar and water solution, depending on personal preference and convenience.

“Wipe down all glass, inside and out, to remove haze, beginning with vertical strokes and ending with horizontal strokes to remove any remaining streaks,” explains Koukia. It’s also worth it to spend a few minutes cleaning off your headlights and tail lights to ensure that they are clean and clear enough to help you see and to help others see your car when driving at night.

Since you won’t be breaking out the pressure washer for this quick clean, focus instead on which parts of your car get dirty the fastest due to daily use. In most cases, that’s the interior driver side of the car. Target high-touch areas, like the dashboard, steering wheel, shifter, door handles, and cup holder to remove oil and grime that might have built up over the week.

“Use a damp microfiber cloth to gently wipe the dashboard and other surfaces with an interior cleaner. Never spray any cleaning solution directly onto the interior surfaces, since that will oversaturate and potentially damage the materials,” Koukia says. You can also use a compressed air canister to blast dust out of the air vents, followed by a quick swipe over the dash and steering wheel to remove any dust particles.

Organization is just as important as cleaning, but one area car owners often overlook is keeping the various compartments inside the car tidy. You might have a tendency similar to your approach to closets: simply stuff things inside the glove box, console, or door pockets, then forget about them entirely.

To keep your car clean and organized, Koukia says to “empty and clear all compartments, including consoles, glove boxes, and door pockets. Wipe each compartment with a damp microfiber cloth. Put back only the items you need for daily operation of the vehicle, like the registration, owner’s manual, and insurance cards.” If you do have other items you need, like gloves for winter driving, make sure the compartments are organized enough so that these items are easily accessible.

Cleaning out the trash inside your car on a weekly basis is quick, easy, and necessary. Take time to clear out the coffee cup that’s been sitting in the cup holder, packaging from a snack your kids were eating in the back seat, or even a receipt that found its way to the floor of the car.

For items that you use regularly, Koukia notes that you should “give each item a spot in the vehicle to make it easier to stay organized. #4016. Legal troubles and family feud keep Klimt portrait off the market

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Gustav Klimt, Portrait of Fräulein Lieser, 1917 © Auktionshaus im Kinsky GmbH, Wien

The consignor of Gustav Klimt’s Portrait of Fräulein Lieser (1917), whose €30m sale at the Vienna auction house Im Kinsky in 2024 was later cancelled because of disputes over its tangled provenance, now faces yet another hurdle to selling the painting in the form of a lawsuit filed in the United States that could keep it off the market for years to come. This latest dispute centres around a bitter 1942 divorce and a betrayal whose repercussions have reverberated across generations and continents.

The identity of the young woman portrayed in the portrait has long been a puzzle and the subject of debate. It is also key to determining which members of a Jewish family owned it before it was lost during the Nazi era.

Little is known about the portrait’s provenance. What is known is that it was still in Klimt’s studio at the time of his death in 1918 and is not quite finished. The name of the person who commissioned the portrait from Klimt was documented only as Lieser, a family of important industrialists in the Austro-Hungarian Empire.

The authors of the Klimt catalogue raisonné believe it was commissioned by Adolf Lieser and portrayed his daughter, Margarethe. Im Kinsky, however, said at the time of the 2024 auction that there were indications it was instead commissioned by Henriette Lieser-Landau, the ex-wife of Justus Lieser, Adolf’s brother, and portrays one of her two daughters. Lieser-Landau was deported in 1942 and murdered by the Nazis in 1943.

Heirs of both Adolf Lieser and Henriette Lieser-Landau were included in a potential settlement that would have divided the revenue of the sale between the claimants and the current holder of the painting, Eva Ropper. According to correspondence between lawyers representing claimants recently obtained by The Art Newspaper, 60% of the revenue was to go to the claimants, and 40% to Ropper.

At the April 2024 auction, the advisory Patti Wong & Associates bid €30m on behalf of an anonymous Hong Kong buyer. While this was at the low end of Im Kinsky’s estimate, it was the highest price ever bid at an auction in Austria. But the sale fell through when another claimant emerged: Patricia Leahy, the granddaughter of Hans Lieser, who was Margarethe’s brother. Her lawyer filed a complaint in April in the Supreme Court of the State of New York against Im Kinsky and Ropper.

The lawsuit alleges that the Hong Kong buyer “sought indemnification from all potential claimants”, including Leahy. The claimants all “signed agreements disclaiming further claims in exchange for payment” except for Leahy, who was not included in the original settlement, according to the complaint. When attempts to negotiate an agreement with Leahy failed, the Hong Kong buyer withdrew, it says.

Leahy’s suit requests a declaratory judgment that “the plaintiff is a rightful heir and a rightful owner of Portrait of Fräulein Lieser”. It urges Im Kinsky and Ropper to “immediately deliver Portrait of Fräulein Lieser to plaintiff”.

By mid-October, six months after the complaint was announced and widely distributed to news media, it had not yet been served on Ropper, according to her lawyer in Vienna, Robert Bachner. “It appears that the plaintiff has so far taken no steps to do so,” he said. “From Ms Ropper’s perspective, the complaint is without justification. The background to the complaint appears to be primarily a dispute between the Kraft Estate/Patricia Leahy and Hannes Staal, the sole legal successor to Hans Lieser.”

The Austrian newspaper Der Standard reported in June that Ropper has also commissioned a more detailed provenance report in an attempt to uncover which part of the family owned the painting.

The reason Leahy was not included in the original settlement is that, despite being Hans Lieser’s granddaughter, she is not—according to the lawyer for another claimant, Hannes Viktor Staal—his rightful heir. Hans Lieser’s 1968 will, a copy of which was made available to The Art Newspaper, states that his sole heir is his second wife, Margareta Lieser.

Some dark family history explains why Leahy’s claim is disputed by the other claimants. Hans Lieser married his first wife, Edith Böhm, in 1934. She was not Jewish. Their son, Nikolaus, was born in Vienna in June 1938, three months after Adolf Hitler’s annexation of Austria.

According to a court affidavit written by Hans Lieser and dated 23 January 1947, members of the Lieser family had entrusted Edith with their company and with shares to protect their assets from “Aryanisation” by the Nazis. Hans and Edith Lieser moved to Budapest in September 1938.

Hans Lieser’s affidavit states that his initially harmonious marriage soured when Edith began an “intimate relationship” with a Gestapo and SS officer, Tibor Collas. In the affidavit, Lieser wrote that Edith, who was often in Vienna as the nominal manager of the company business, ended up moving there permanently with their son Nikolaus in 1939 and “from that point onward regarded the company as her own property”.

Not only that, but according to the affidavit and to a Hungarian newspaper article from 1946, Collas used his Nazi connections to pursue Hans Lieser, who was arrested and deported to Slovakia.

The Liesers’ marriage was dissolved in 1942 and Lieser was once again arrested on the basis of a complaint filed by Collas, he wrote. Edith established a new company and transferred to it the assets and rights of the original family-owned company. In November 1945, after the end of the war, Hans Lieser filed a police complaint and, in the affidavit, estimated that Edith had appropriated more than 1m Reichsmarks from the Liesers—the equivalent of several million dollars today.

Edith married a US army colonel, James Kraft, and moved to the US after the war with Nikolaus, who adopted his stepfather’s name—the recent US complaint identifies him as Nickolas Johann Kraft. Nickolas Kraft had little contact with his biological father but sued him in 1967, before his marriage, for payment of a dowry, according to documents made available to The Art Newspaper. Under Austrian law, parents are, under certain circumstances, obliged to give a settlement to their children on marriage. The draft of a letter Hans Lieser wrote to Kraft shows that he responded with outrage.

When Lieser died in 1978, his widow Margareta inherited his estate. Lieser’s will states that Nickolas Kraft should only receive the “compulsory share” of his estate because he “gravely violated his duties towards me as a son, making contact with me only when he expected to gain financially”. Under Austrian law, even disinherited children are entitled to a share of the inheritance, which Kraft duly received.

As she had no children, Margareta left the estate to her sister, who, in turn, bequeathed it to her son, Hannes Staal. According to his lawyer, Louis Rönsberg, Staal says he remembers Hans Lieser and had a good relationship with his uncle. Rönsberg says his client is the only rightful heir to Hans Lieser’s 15% share of the revenue of Portrait of Fräulein Lieser—when and if it is eventually sold.

While disinherited children are entitled to a share of the estate under Austrian law, they do not qualify as heirs and must claim their share from the beneficiary of the will.According to a 2024 letter from Leahy’s Austrian lawyer to Rönsberg that was made available to The Art Newspaper, that share in this case is 37.5%. Rönsberg, however, argued in his response to the lawyer that Leahy’s claim has by now expired due to statutes of limitation.

After the failed 2024 auction at Im Kinsky, the consignor and claimants included in the original settlement agreed that the next attempt to sell would be at a major international auction house. But as long as the threat of a US court case against Im Kinsky and Ropper is pending, “a sale is impossible,” Rönsberg says. “Ms. Leahy and her lawyer are blocking everything. The case is not advancing and they are not helping to find a solution.”

Leahy’s New York-based lawyer, Oren Warshavsky, did not respond to repeated requests for comment. A spokeswoman for Im Kinsky declined to comment on Leahy’s suit, saying only that the auction house is no longer in possession of the picture and no longer has a relationship with the consignor.

Following the auction and the failure to reach an agreement, “Ms Leahy has apparently now decided to use the complaint, together with the accompanying PR campaign, to exert pressure”, says Bachner, Ropper’s lawyer. Her failure to advance the suit, he adds, “reinforces the impression that the plaintiff’s primary objective was to achieve the greatest possible publicity with minimal effort”.

A new study out of Japan appears to have identified how pterostilbene, an enzyme found in fruits like blueberries, bilberries, grapes, and cranberries, could be helping to burn excess fat stored inside the muscles.

Experts have long known that pterostilbene is a powerful antioxidant that could offer several benefits including reduced inflammation and even DNA repair but this breakthrough study, led by Shinshu University in Japan, has cast new light on previous theories that suggest this enzyme could also be a vital tool for the war against excess fat.

Until now, little was understood about the way that pterostilbene (a naturally occurring polyphenol) might accelerate fat metabolism in skeletal muscle. But among the compounds tested during the study on cultured mouse cells, pterostilbene was hailed as producing the most dramatic reduction of “intracellular lipid accumulation.” And, while pterostilbene was shown to reduce fat within the muscle cells, the enzyme had no detrimental effect on muscle function, allowing for normal development and growth to continue.

Identifying ways of burning intramuscular fat is critical as experts link excess storage to serious heart health issues. Earlier studies have shown that high levels of fat within the muscles can lead to heart dysfunction, heart attacks, and preventable death.

According to the latest research, when pterostilbene was introduced to the muscles, it seemed to trigger the release of glycerol on the outside of the cells, a telltale sign that fat was being broken down. Warren Buffett has spent decades telling anyone who’ll listen that he lives simply. Same house in Omaha he bought in 1958 for $31,500. No mansion, no car collection, nothing flashy.

He eats at McDonald’s, drinks Coke by the case and has said more than once that a person could live like him on a fraction of his fortune and lose nothing that actually matters.

A physician two decades into a cardiology career already has a six-bedroom house on two acres with a three-car garage. Something that would put Buffett’s Omaha home to shame.

But Buffett flies private. On his personal jet. Which he owns. (He also owns the company that made his plane just FYI).

That cardiologist couldn’t touch a private jet with a ten-foot pole. Not on a $600,000 salary carrying a jumbo mortgage, two kids in private school and a home equity line he opened for the kitchen renovation.

Buffett could write a check for that physician’s entire house and still have money left for the furniture.

My point is, quantifying wealth is not a black and white endeavor. McMansions don’t lead to private jets. And “modest” lifestyles don’t rule them out.

It’s a strange world we live in. One where most people are blindly chasing perception rather than what actually matters.

The Federal Reserve’s Survey of Consumer Finances states that the median American household net worth is $192,084. The average is more than 5x that at $1.06 million due to a relatively small number of extremely wealthy households that pull the average up.

Physicians, as a group, are ahead of that median and the gap keeps widening.

Medscape’s 2026 Physician Wealth & Debt Report found that 24% of physicians now report a family net worth of $5 million or more — largely thanks to stock market gains and rising home values.

However, the same report makes it clear this wealth isn’t evenly spread. The physicians in the $5 million group are mostly in procedure-based specialties which have the highest earning ceilings to begin with.

Plenty of physicians in lower-paying specialties spend most of a career well short of that mark despite carrying the same title, the same debt load at the start, and the same years of deferred income during training.

All physicians start with some sort of student debt — small or big — regardless of specialty. The median medical school debt for the class of 2025 is $215,000, not counting undergraduate loans.

Or any non-education debt, credit cards and car loans that tend to accumulate during residency for that matter.

A newly minted attending earning six figures can still have a negative net worth. It takes years to pay down debt and bring it up to zero.

Years more to enter the black. Those early years are the reason for the gap between a $5-million-net-worth radiologist and a physician twenty years into practice who still hasn’t crossed seven figures.

Let’s talk about two doctors who have the same degree and the same starting debt but two very different philosophies on spending money.

For this example we’ll play into the most common stereotypes doctors have faced — and fought hard to shed — for years.

Dr. Flashy “invests” in a new Range Rover every three years. He’s got a lake house two hours outside the city, a boat he uses maybe four weekends a summer — if the weather cooperates.

And surprisingly to some, he’s not broke. He makes real money and a part of his lifestyle is funded from that same income.

Almost every single one of these major purchases generates its own follow-on bill which continues to recur exponentially.

The Range Rover’s routine maintenance costs more than a lot of people’s monthly car payment. The lake house needs a new roof eventually, landscaping every season, comes with a property tax bill that rises every time the county reassesses and someone to physically check on it every time a storm rolls through three states away.

The boat comes with marina fees, winterizing and a marine mechanic who bills by the hour. Buying something nice is the equivalent of signing up for a subscription with an indefinite term and a rising rate.

This is called the hedonic treadmill. Once someone upgrades something in their life, the old version stops being satisfying, permanently. And the new version only delivers that same satisfaction for as long as the novelty lasts.

Eventually the purchase itself becomes pointless. It was the feeling of upgrading that felt good but that feeling fades fast enough that the next upgrade becomes necessary just to get back to the baseline.

Luxury goods are sneakily good at triggering this because so much of what they deliver is the status attached to the price tag. Any functional improvement or value they bring is usually beside the point.

Dr. Flashy’s income may be large. He might even be a neurosurgeon earning $829,161. But at some point his lifestyle needs additional funding to keep up. That’s when he turns to financing.

Eventually, the bank becomes something he is increasingly dependent on.

To be clear, I’m not speculating on his character. Forbes reports that the highest-income American households, those above the 90th percentile, carry the largest average credit card balances of any income group (over $11,000 on average) despite being the group least likely to carry a balance at all.

So data shows us that the people who look the most financially comfortable are disproportionately dependent on revolving debt to stay there. Not all high earners are predisposed to open lines of credit, but when they do, they tend to rack up more of it because the lifestyle they’re financing costs more to maintain than anyone else’s.

The thing is, money is finite even with a large income. Spending more of it means you need more just to sustain the spending at its current level. And that treadmill doesn’t slow down as the years go by.

Dr. Net-Worth has the same income as Dr. Flashy. Same specialty, even, same call schedule, same patient panel.

But Dr. Net-Worth drives a Honda he’s had since fellowship. It’s got a cracked bumper he keeps meaning to fix. He lives in a house that’s nice but completely unremarkable from the street — not something you’ll see featured in Architectural Digest.

Still, his investment accounts feature a sum that would make your eyes water. No one except him and his accountant know what it is.

We have come to perceive net worth as some concrete evaluation of a person’s wealth. But it really isn’t. In fact, the more zeroes it has, the less real it is.

I mean, this year we saw Elon Musk become (briefly) the first ever trillionaire. But does that mean he’s got a Scrooge McDuck vault full of gold coins stacked to the ceiling?  Well, he might — but he won’t have an actual trillion in there to dive into and swim around in.

That number is mostly the market’s opinion of his equity stakes on a given day. An opinion that can — and does — swing by tens of billions of dollars before lunch and back again by dinner. All without a single dollar changing hands or a single decision on his part.

Dr. Net-Worth’s number works the same way just on a smaller scale. Most of it lives in a house he’s still making payments on every month, a retirement account he can’t touch before a certain age lest he trigger a penalty, and an ever-growing brokerage account he could never imagine drawing from.

All of it is his. Yet, he doesn’t use a dollar of it beyond the mortgage payment and the grocery bill. He’s rich! Technically. Psychologically, it might as well be someone else’s money.

You can’t fault him for being this way. Sometimes it’s just temperament. Plenty of high earners are simply frugal by nature, genuinely uninterested in the lake house and actually happier to keep the Honda running for another hundred thousand miles. Nothing wrong with that. At least Dr. Net-Worth doesn’t need rescuing from the life he has chosen.

But sometimes the hesitation to spend stems from fear. Constant worry that the money could vanish overnight, that one bad market year or one malpractice suit could erase everything you’ve built.

That spending on anything beyond the bare essentials is an invitation for disaster to strike. Dr. Net-Worth worked his butt off to build a financial fortress that classifies him as financially free. Yet the day may never come when he actually gets to taste that freedom.

Dr. Flashy is one bad year/one slow season at the practice/one unexpected repair bill on the boat away from a very uncomfortable conversation with a lender.

Dr. Net-Worth has already won the game by any objective measure but refuses to enjoy it.

Financial planner Derek Hagen says that money that isn’t pointed at anything in particular just accumulates, and accumulation without a purpose behind it isn’t freedom. Dr. Net-Worth has plenty of money with no real purpose for it.

Dr.  Flashy has an answer: status and visible proof that he has “made it”. But his answer costs more to maintain with every passing year, never quite delivering the sense of arrival he’s chasing.

There’s no handbook that outlines exactly where the divide between enjoying the money and hoarding it is. And anyone who claims there is one wants to sell you something.

You, the physician reading this, are probably nothing like Dr. Flashy or Dr. Net-Worth. And asking which one is better or worse isn’t the point either.

Your money, no matter how it is accumulated or spent on, deserves to be used to fund a life you chose on purpose. Not one assembled by accident out of habit, fear or…what the neighbors might notice.

Buffett kept the house and bought the plane because both of those choices added up to a life he actually wanted. That is rich (of course being worth $144.6 billion doesn’t hurt).

One of the best fall meteor showers peaks this week, when pieces of the famous Halley's Comet streak across Earth's atmosphere as fast-moving "shooting stars."

The Orionid meteor shower will peak overnight Wednesday (Oct. 21) through Thursday (Oct. 22), potentially producing around 20 meteors per hour under a dark sky, according to Time and Date. The Orionids are caused when Earth passes through the debris left by Halley's Comet, which last passed through the inner solar system in 1986 and will return in 2061.

Halley's Comet is presently beyond the orbit of Neptune, according to The Planets Today, but it doesn't need to be close to Earth to cause "shooting stars." Every time the comet travels through the inner solar system, heat from the sun causes it to shed dust and rocky particles along its orbital path. Earth's own orbit takes the comet through streams of that ancient debris several times a year, with the particles vaporizing as they slam into our planet's atmosphere.

The Orionids are particularly fast meteors, striking Earth's atmosphere at around 41 miles per second (66 kilometers per second). They can produce bright streaks, occasional fireballs (extremely bright, sometimes colorful meteors) and "persistent trains" — glowing trails that remain visible briefly after a meteor has disappeared. The best way to view them is with the naked eye, although a nice pair of stargazing binoculars or a small backyard telescope will help you enjoy more sights during peak skywatching season this fall.

Halley's Comet produces not one but two annual meteor showers. Its orbit comes close to Earth's at two different points, so our planet encounters the comet's debris twice each year — once during the Orionids in late October, and again during the Eta Aquariid meteor shower in early May.

There's a slight complication with the Orionids this year. A 71%-illuminated waxing gibbous moon will brighten much of the night, making it harder to spot meteors. However, the moon will set early Thursday morning, creating a valuable window of darkness before dawn. That's also when the constellation Orion will be high in the sky, making the predawn hours the best time to watch.

You can trace the paths of Orionid meteors back to their radiant: the famous red supergiant star system Betelgeuse in the constellation Orion. (That's why the shower is called the Orionids.) However, there's no need to look directly at Orion. Meteors seen farther from the radiant can produce longer streaks across the sky; each one comes from a tiny piece of Halley's Comet completing a journey that began many centuries ago.

Vivienne’s agency used to publish client posts as “written by Acme Shop” when ghostwriting, but no longer does. “Authority gets you noticed,” Vivienne says. “Credibility gets you trusted.”

​Add an Author Box: Include an author box with a short bio on every post that presents the author as a subject matter expert. The named author doesn’t have to write the post. An agency or in-house writers can do the writing, as long as the author can stand behind the content.

​Link to a Standalone Author Page: Link each author box to a dedicated page that covers the author’s years in business, credentials, education, experience, and the organizations they belong to. This is the place to toot their own horn. The page doesn’t need to appear in the top navigation bar because AI will still find it.

Write Title Tags of 50 to 60 Characters: A title tag is the text shown on a browser tab and as the blue headline in Google search results. It doesn’t appear on the page itself, but Google’s crawlers and AI use it to understand what the page is about. The maximum is 70 characters, and the sweet spot is 50 to 60. Use that space to say who the business is and what it does.

​Put Location at the End: Location-based businesses should include their location at the end of the title tag, not the front.

​Give Every Page a Meaningful H1: Treat page headings like an essay outline, a step many business owners skip. An H1 like “Celebrating 25 years in business” conveys credibility but doesn’t specify whether the business is a dog groomer or a wedding venue. Instead, the H1 should state what the business does and who it’s for. Leading with impact is fine.

​Use Keywords Only Where They Fit: Two years ago, Vivienne would have recommended putting keywords in every H1. Now she recommends a compelling H1 that tells the machines more about the business, with keywords included only if they fit naturally. Never leave an H1 blank or fill it with something unhelpful.

The final step is proving the business is who it says it is. A Google Business Profile is one of the first places AI looks for information about a business because it treats it as a verified source, making it one of the most important assets for getting recommended. Virtual businesses that once couldn’t get a profile without a physical address should check again, because that barrier appears to have eased.

Write a Business Description: Describe what the business does.

​Choose the Right Primary Category: Pick the category that makes the most sense and reflects the customers the business is trying to reach. Add secondary categories if the profile allows them.

​Complete the Standard Information: Add business hours if appropriate, an address, a phone number, and a website link.

​List Products and Services With Images: Use the profile’s product and service categories and add images for each.

​Post to It Like a Social Media Platform: Treat the profile as a mini social platform. Post small billboards for upcoming sales, behind-the-scenes content, videos, and client testimonials. Many businesses don’t take advantage of this.

Reviews are where a business proves its positioning. Make asking every customer or client for a review a regular habit, because if the business doesn’t ask, the answer is always no.

​Then respond to every review. A review response isn’t written for the reviewer. It’s written for every future buyer who reads it before making a decision, and it gives AI platforms more information about the business. A reply like “Thanks for the five-star review. It was a joy to work with you” wastes that opportunity because it could apply to any business anywhere. Vivienne recommends this formula instead:

For example, if a bridal shop customer writes, “I found my dream dress and Kaylee was great,” the response could open with “We’re so glad we helped you find the perfect wedding dress for your big day,” and then mention that Kaylee loves working with brides who want long sleeves. That reply celebrates the customer while reinforcing exactly what the shop offers.

​Use the same formula for negative reviews. Many buyers decide whether to work with a business based on how the owner responds to criticism.
