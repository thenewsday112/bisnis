#624. Why Do We Have Necks? Scientists Uncover a 380-Million-Year-Old Evolutionary Twist

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

The first necks may owe more to the beginnings of walking than to looking around, according to models showing how little our early ancestors could move their heads.

In fish, the shoulders attach directly to the skull, so looking left or right means turning the whole body. Some of the animals making the transition from water to land about 380 million years ago began to develop a separate neck. Yet new research suggests that this change did not give them much more freedom to move their heads.

Models combining new fossil evidence with mechanical tests on living fish indicate that these early necks remained stiff, challenging the long-standing idea that they evolved to let the head move more freely. The international study, which included Tom Stewart, an assistant professor of biology at Penn State, was published October 14 in Nature.

Fossils from the Devonian Period, about 420 million to 360 million years ago, capture animals with a mixture of aquatic traits and features associated with life on land. Some had robust fins that might have supported their weight, allowing them to prop themselves up or push along the bottom in shallow water. Distinct necks also began appearing in these transitional fish.

“This is a really interesting time in Earth’s history where some of the first forests are forming, and there’s a really radical reorganization of the Earth,” Stewart said. “So, understanding exactly when our lineage made that transition from water to land, how it was achieved, and which anatomical systems were changing is an important part of that story. What’s cool is that many of the changes that were occurring in the skeleton at this time, about 380 million years ago, are still reflected in our own bodies today.”

One of those transitional fish was Tiktaalik, whose fossils come from Ellesmere Island in Nunavut, Canada, in the northern Arctic.

“The materials have been really important because Tiktaalik is one of the closest relatives to limbed vertebrates,” Stewart said. “It helps us to reconstruct a lot of intermediary steps in the transition to life on land, such as the move from suction feeding in water to biting on land or from swimming to walking.”

For the ancestors of four-limbed animals, known as tetrapods, the ability to look down or from side to side could have helped them find food and avoid predators in shallow water or on land. Scientists reasoned that these environments might make independent head movement more useful than it was in deeper water.

“Tiktaalik is one of these fossil fish, and it was first described in 2006,” Stewart said. “It has been reasonably assumed for decades that this separation of head and shoulders must have allowed the head to move more freely, so the hypothesis that neck mobility was an evolutionary driver for this change in the tetrapod body plan has prevailed, at least until now.”

Testing that explanation required reconstructing a backbone unlike those of most vertebrates alive today. Early tetrapods and their close relatives had a long rod of tissue called the notochord running through the entire vertebral column. Their vertebrae were small and arranged around this large core of soft tissue, which behaved differently from the joints between vertebrae typical of modern animals.

“The fish that were undergoing these changes had a vertebral column that was largely made up of soft tissue, which, unlike bone, is rarely preserved in the fossil record,” Stewart said. “In these cases, we can look to modern animals to serve as analogs for the past, like how living birds can teach us about now extinct dinosaurs.”

Backbones built around a notochord are rare today, although they were common throughout the history of life on Earth. Sturgeon, distantly related fish sometimes called living fossils because of their primitive features, retain this kind of backbone. That made them a useful way to investigate the mechanical properties missing from the fossils.

“We want to understand how these early tetrapods were living and how that is reflected in changes to their bodies,” Stewart said. “Trying to reconstruct where an animal lived, how it moved, what its behavior was, is a really difficult thing. The cool part of this story is that it combines data from fossils and studies of living animals in a mechanical, functional perspective to reconstruct what the changes we see in the animal’s anatomy mean for their behavior.”

The researchers measured the stiffness of modern sturgeon notochords, then applied those material properties to models of the necks of early tetrapods and transitional relatives such as Tiktaalik. The models allowed them to test whether separating the shoulders from the skull would have freed the head to move despite the backbone’s structure.

“I undertook this study to find out how big of a difference this change in anatomy made for these animals,” said Dana Korneisel, a postdoctoral researcher at Uppsala University in Sweden and first author of the paper. “The vertebral columns of sturgeon are built a lot like those of early tetrapods, but they didn’t seem terribly stiff. So, after doing the modeling, the lack of significant head mobility for almost all of these animals was a huge surprise.”

The estimates considered only the backbone. Muscles, skin, and, in many of these animals, bones embedded in the skin could have restricted movement further, meaning the living animals may have had even less neck mobility than the models suggest.

“Head elevation in these animals was no more than what you would expect in fish and, sometimes, even less,” Korneisel said. “We found that the kind of head twisting that we can do when we shake our heads was not feasible in any of these animals.”

With little extra head movement available, the researchers suggest that a distinct neck likely arose instead from anatomical changes associated with using the front limbs for locomotion. Greater flexibility appears to have been a later benefit, rather than the advantage that initially drove the separation of head and shoulders.

“We know that tetrapods did eventually gain higher neck mobility,” Korneisel said. “Clearly it was advantageous eventually, but it was not an advantage available to these early tetrapods yet because of the limitations of their anatomy.”

Reference: “Early tetrapod neck mobility modelled using an extant analogue” by Dana E. Korneisel, Cassandra M. Donatelli, Linfang Han, Thomas A. Stewart, Andrew L. Traynor, Benjamin K. A. Otoo, Marcos Martinez, Tetsuto Miyashita, Emily M. Standen and Hillary C. Maddin, 14 October 2026, Nature. DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-11158-3

The research was supported by The Steven Cohen Award for Student Research presented by the Society of Vertebrate Paleontology and the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada (RGPIN-06442).

#4540. OpenAI rolls out budget-priced mid-tier GPT-6.1 Terra

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Not a bombshell, but it rivals pricier models on some tasks at a tenth of Astra's API price

OpenAI has added a mid-range model to its GPT-6.1 family, offering strong performance at a lower price.

The AI business' general-purpose models span four tiers. Products named "Sol" or "Astra" are its high-end offerings and come with price tags to match. "Luna" models are its cheapest offerings. This new model gets the "Terra" treatment the company reserves for mid-range models.

"Terra continues to advance the cost-intelligence frontier across our model families, providing a new balance of speed, capability, and cost," OpenAI said. "Its standard API prices are 50 percent lower than those of GPT-5.6 Terra and GPT-6.1 Sol, making it practical for developers to automate high-volume work, like reading incoming invoices and entering their details into accounting software."

Artificial Analysis, a site that tracks model performance and cost, notes that GPT-6.1 Terra pushes the cost efficiency frontier. "It is the first Terra-class model since GPT-5.6, and is more intelligent for less than a third of the cost," the site says.

GPT-6 Luna ($0.10/M input tokens, $0.50/M output) occupies the lowest tier of OpenAI's model lineup in terms of cost and capability, followed by GPT-6.1 Terra ($1/M, $5/M), GPT-6.1 Sol ($2/M, $10/M), and GPT-6 Astra ($10/M, $50/M).

In a comment posted to Hacker News, Ted Sanders, a member of technical staff at OpenAI, framed the differences between the various GPT-6.1 models thus: "In the case of GPT-6.1 Terra, I'd say Terra is probably better than Astra and Sol at technical communication, similar on vision and computer use (but much faster!), and a bit worse on most other things. For API developers, it's a nice midrange option if you want something cheaper than Sol but a lot smarter than Luna (especially for vision/computer use/communication)."

Terra's knowledge cutoff date, June 1, 2026, means it is the most up-to-date of its model family. Perhaps that counts for something if you're asking questions about events after April 30, 2026 – the training data cutoff for Astra and Sol.

In terms of model safety, Terra handles harmful requests a bit less frequently than GPT-6.1 Sol but also avoids unnecessary refusals more often than its more capable sibling.

But as noted by Max Woolf, a data scientist, in a recent blog post, the real story here is the economics of AI models. Despite the widespread expectation that OpenAI and Anthropic will have to raise the cost of AI inference (or reduce the value provided via subscription) to bring revenue closer to the extravagant sums these companies are spending on model training, inference, and infrastructure, he notes that prices keep coming down and model performance keeps going up.

"The idea that token prices will suddenly rise is more a fantasy that is not supported by both behavioral economics and the actual trends of LLM prices going down significantly over the last few months for better models, and not just from OpenAI and Anthropic," he observes. "As long as scalers can price at a cost-per-token lower than the revenue-per-token, then those inference providers will reduce token costs to compete with each other to a point that OpenAI/Anthropic will have to lower their prices to compete too."

#1763. More than half of US adults use social media to get their news

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A new report from Pew found that the percentage of Americans who use apps to stay connected to current events has risen sharply over the last four years.

Pew Research published a new report on Oct. 23 that showed that more than half of Americans now consider social media to be a key way to get news and information.

Based on a survey of more than 3,500 U.S. adults, conducted in June this year, the data underlines the significant influence social media platforms have on the flow of information. It also reiterates the role that social media continues to play in terms of how the voting public stays informed of key events.

As shown in this chart, 58% of Americans consider social media to be an important connector to current events. Within that, 13% of Americans consider social apps to be their most important information source. Based on the total U.S. adult population of about 270 million, as estimated by the Annie E. Casey Foundation, that suggests that around 35 million U.S. adults now rely on social media to keep them informed.

That’s higher than when Pew Research conducted the same research in 2022. In that report, as cited in the above chart, 43% of U.S. adults said social media was an important way that they got news, while only 5% said that it was the most important news resource.

As noted, the data underlines the growing reliance on social platforms, while also further underlining the power that social networks have to influence media narratives, depending on each app’s policy and moderation approach.

That’s been a key point of contention for some time. Elon Musk, for example, purchased Twitter in 2022 based partly on his own concerns that the Twitter management team had been interfering with democracy by manipulating what posts gained traction in the app.

This type of speculation has coalesced into what some have referred to as the “censorship industrial complex,” which is allegedly a loose collection of activists that has sought to police online behavior by attempting to sway public discourse through crackdowns on speech, and trying to limit what can be amplified within social media apps.

Indeed, in December U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced that the State Department was taking action against individuals whom it claimed had led organized efforts “to coerce American platforms to censor, demonetize, and suppress American viewpoints they oppose.” That announcement was titled "Actions to combat the censorship-industrial complex."

Given the influence that social media platforms now have, it makes sense that the government wants to ensure that they remain free and open to all perspectives. Yet, at the same time, allowing information to flow through these platforms, enabling potentially huge amplification of any claims, can facilitate the spread of misinformation and false narratives.

As such, it’s important to consider the role that social platforms should play in establishing the rules around what people can and cannot say, and who should be making those calls.

The platforms themselves have been keen to take a more hands-off role on this front, and allow users to dictate what’s acceptable and what’s not. But again, given the significant influence that social platforms have on the flow of news and information, that could set a dangerous precedent. In addition, platform algorithms are designed to boost conversations that drive more engagement, regardless of what those specific claims and conversations may be, which generally leads to the most controversial discussions getting more exposure.

The theory that people will always engage more with borderline content was outlined by Meta founder Mark Zuckerberg in 2021, in a post in which he argued that more needs to be done to address attempts to weaponize misinformation.

As per Zuckerberg: “One of the biggest issues social networks face is that, when left unchecked, people will engage disproportionately with more sensationalist and provocative content. This is not a new phenomenon. It is widespread on cable news today and has been a staple of tabloids for more than a century. At scale it can undermine the quality of public discourse and lead to polarization.”

And yet, in January 2025, Zuckerberg changed his tone. In a post about Meta’s move away from fact-checking, he argued that the company has an obligation to allow more speech, and facilitate more discussion on borderline topics. As a result, Meta then relaxed some of its rules that had previously restricted the discussion of certain controversial topics, including immigration and gender identity.

By Zuckerberg's own logic, this shift will lead to more polarization, which will result in more harm.

It’s a significant change in perspective, and as social media platforms become even more critical sources of information, it begs the question of why Zuckerberg had a change of heart on this, and decided to go the opposite way allowing more of this discussion, despite acknowledging potential harm.
