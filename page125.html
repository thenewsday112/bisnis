#1089. 3 Impeccable Fall Outfits for Peak Admiring-the-Foliage Season

Fall outfits take many forms. There’s a certain point, right around the time when the temperatures start dropping, that getting out of town starts to sound like a very good idea. Maybe you’re heading for the hills for some fresh air or simply taking a break from the city before the holiday madness kicks in. Whatever the excuse, there’s something to be said about spending a couple of days somewhere where there are more trees than people. (Whatever gets you out the door, really.)

And while a little fresh air is reason enough to get out of town, we’d argue that getting dressed for it is half the fun. Think chunky knits, substantial outerwear, sturdy boots, and all those rich, tactile fabrics that make dressing for the colder months so appealing. Of course, a weekend away can take you from a slow morning at the cabin to a muddy trail to a nice dinner in town, sometimes all in the same day. So we’ve put together a few fall outfits for dressing the part.

If you’ve gone through the trouble of renting a cozy hideaway for the weekend, you might as well make the most of doing nothing. This territory is prime for the softest of flannels and, say, a shawl-collar cardigan from Elder Statesman, the reigning kinds of impossibly good knitwear. And pajamas at noon? Perfectly acceptable, provided they’re Derek Rose’s brushed plaid bottoms. Add Charvet’s suede slippers and a pair of marled wool socks, because cabin floors are notoriously unforgiving to bare feet.

You’re here for some fresh air and a nice view, and chances are, you’ve got a well-marked trail to work with, so you don’t gotta go overboard. A thrashed sweatshirt will do just fine, but a pair of trail-ready pants from Portal will give you more freedom than your usual jeans. Trail or not, there’s a case to be made for a proper pair of hikers from Diemme, the Italian brand that’s been making exceptionally handsome footwear in the foothills of the Dolomites for decades. Better still, they’re the kind of boots you’ll wanna wear after you’ve made it back to civilization.

Arguably the best part of any weekend getaway to the country is the chance to indulge in a little small-town living. Poking around antique shops, finding a good cup of coffee, or settling in for an early dinner. You might be the only person in town wearing a shearling-collared coat from Yves Salomon, or a pair of cords from De Bonne Facture, but that ain’t a reason to leave either at home. And do you need to wear a pair of rugged Chelsea boots to browse the stacks at the local bookshop? Probably not. But damn if you won’t look like a well-dressed son of a gun doing it.

#3733. As riots scar Paris’s luxury storefronts, Maison Guerlain opens art exhibition on Champs Elysées

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Installation view of L’Heure des Possibles at Maison Guerlain in Paris

On Tuesday 20 October in Paris, among all the luxury boutiques on the avenue Champs-Elysées bearing the scars and graffiti sprayed on shopfronts by rioters just days before, Maison Guerlain opened L’Heure des Possibles, an exhibition of works by 33 artists.

The pieces, four of which were specially commissioned and the rest loaned, were sourced in response to one of Guerlain’s signature perfumes, L’Heure Bleue, and the period of twilight the fragrance takes its name from.

Wall-based works by well-known names including James Turrell, Annette Messager, Christian Boltanski and Ugo Rondinone have been installed throughout the historic maison alongside up-and-coming contemporary artists such as Marie Bovo, Xie Lei and Valentin Fougeray.

First founded in 1828 by Pierre-François-Pascal Guerlain, the Guerlain family built their hôtel particulier on the site in 1914.

“We need a moment of blue after all the black,” says Guerlain’s director of art, culture and heritage, Ann-Carolin Prazan, regarding the demonstrations that protested the state of education and cost-of-living in France, and which have been overshadowed by violent outbreaks by some ultra far-left groups. A VIP evening event marking the opening of the exhibition on 20 October at Maison Guerlain was cancelled.

“Paris is the capital of culture,” Prazan adds. “People want to support artists and culture; we must not be felled. I think we have to say ‘enough!’ now.”

Installation view of L’Heure des Possibles at Maison Guerlain in Paris

Despite President Emmanuel Macron saying “illegitimate” demonstrations will be banned over the coming days, France is bracing for another round of rioting this weekend, with Art Basel in Paris announcing yesterday that it is closing two days early as a safety precaution (Guerlain is a host partner of Art Basel Paris). Steps have already been taken to remove and protect some of the public sculptures that are dotted around the French capital as part of the Art Basel public programme.

Maison Guerlain, however, will remain open all weekend, according to a spokesman.

The perfume designer has an almost 200-year history of collaborating with artists from Matisse to Yves Klein and Lee Ufan. Its famous Bee Bottle, created in 1853 as a wedding present to Empress Eugenie de Montijo with Napoléon III, has been reinvented by contemporary and street artists including JonOne, Wassim Mraihi and Géraldine Gonzalez.

The company now has a vast archive of 25,000 works in its warehouse, many of them perfume bottle designs.

For the past 20 years, Guerlain has also held contemporary art exhibitions in the flagship boutique—a programme that has supported contemporary artists. The Paris-based artist Alexandra Mocanu, who has collaborated on an intricately embroidered work with the Swiss creative studio Retinaa, says Guerlain’s support has been “crucial” while she looks for a gallery in the French capital.

Prazan says she was unsure when she first started installing Modern and contemporary art in the maison in 2006. “It’s hardly a white cube,” she points out. “But it works.”

Unlike other luxury brands, Guerlain has remained streamlined in the products it makes, sticking to fragrances, skin care and makeup. This is perhaps unsurprising, given that the company was bought out in 1994 by LVMH, the world’s largest luxury goods conglomerate which manages more than 75 brands and made more than €80.8bn in revenue in 2025. Bernard Arnault, who owns LVMH, is one of France’s biggest art collectors, though the Guerlain collection remains a distinct entity.

The focus is now on the 200th anniversary of Guerlain in 2028, when a global exhibition featuring 20 artists is due to take place in Korea, Dubai, the US, China and Japan, among other places. “It’s going to be big, and it’s going to be totally unique,” Prazan says.

That choice transforms what is usually a neutral element into the starting point for an interior that isn’t afraid to play with color, even within the confines of a 750-square-foot space. A pink bathroom, playful furnishings, and colorful accents take center stage without needing to be perfectly coordinated: They meet, contrast, and interact, creating a cheerful, personal, and surprising atmosphere. This floor isn’t just a backdrop in this home. It sets its rhythm.

The famous Lampe de Marseille, designed by Le Corbusier for Nemo Lighting, hangs above the dining table.

The blue chosen for the floors is more than an interesting aesthetic element—it was also the starting point for the entire design of the project. Colored resin covers most of the existing floor, transforming a blank surface into the signature element of the home. The color flows seamlessly through the hallway and living area, guiding the eye and creating a visual continuity that makes the different rooms feel like part of a single, unified space.

In the living room, a Teddy sofa by OMHU sits above a Super Rock rug featuring a graphic and textural design inspired by the shapes of rocks, by Bethan Laura Wood for CC-Tapis.

The blue floor takes center stage, with the rest of the house revolving around it without ever matching it precisely. White, wood tones, red, and pink alternate and play off one another. Red tiles brighten up the kitchen, while the pink ones in the bathroom introduce a softer, unexpected touch.

The result is a deliberately imperfect balance, made up of contrasts and playful combinations, in which that light-blue floor ends up doing much more than simply covering a surface: It brings the sky into the home.

The custom kitchen, constructed by local artisans, features a minimalist design and red tiles that echo the home’s color palette.

There are also Plia chairs by Giancarlo Piretti for Anonima Castelli, a plush Teddy sofa by OMHU, and a vintage LC4 chaise longue by Cassina, along with custom pieces crafted by local artisans. The result is a blend of design, vintage items, and artisanal creations that follows no specific rule except one—everything must have personality.

#979. I Own Both A Keurig &amp; Nespresso—Here's Which One I Actually Prefer

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The best pick depends on whether you want lattes, drip coffee, or cheaper pods.

Delish editors handpick every product we feature. We may earn commission from the links on this page.

When it comes to my morning caffeine fix, I’m a traditionalist. I’ve always preferred a homemade cup of black coffee over a latte or cold brew from a coffee shop, so I’m particular about how I make it.

I’ve cycled through my fair share of brewing methods over the years, from classic drip coffee makers and espresso machines to French presses and pour-over setups. These days, the single-serve coffee maker reigns supreme in my kitchen.

I’ve tried several Keurig models before switching to a Nespresso, and now, I own both. It’s safe to say I’m quite the expert on the two brands. However, this isn’t a one-size-fits-all thing. One machine isn’t necessarily better than the other—each has pros and cons, and the right pick depends entirely on what you’re looking for.

Look, Nespresso has a better reputation for overall coffee quality. That's not bias, just facts! It typically brews a stronger and more café-style bevvy (with a stunninggg crema top), which for many is the priority, while Keurig tends to brew a milder version. Though there are different sizes and strength options to tweak it to your palate.

The Nespresso is great for lattes or a post-dinner espresso, but personally, I prefer the simplicity of a classic Keurig cup in the mornings—call me a drip coffee purist. I don't care if that makes me boring.

If, like me, you enjoy both versions for different reasons, the Keurig K-Crema is a great compromise. While it might not brew quite as strong a cup as the Nespresso, it allows you to choose between the "original" style and a "pressure" version, which has a crema top that mimics its competitor. Do with that info what you will.

Keurig has soooo much more variety (over 75 authorized brands make K-Cups), while the Nespresso Vertuo pod selection is a little more limited (they’re sold exclusively by the brand and Starbucks).

From a sustainability perspective, Keurig also wins. The brand sells reusable pods, so you’re not stuck with single-use plastic. Nespresso does not sell reusable pods, though there are workarounds: Amazon has a few trusty tools that make it easy to make your own, like this Reusable Vertuo Pod Refill Kit.

The Nespresso Vertuo Pop+ is so simple and easy to use (a huge perk!) and brews different sizes—including espresso, double espresso, gran lungo, mug, and carafe, as well as "over ice." This is all based on barcode recognition from the pod. The Keurig, however, requires you to select your brewing option manually. The sizes range from four ounces to 12 ounces (and even 30 ounces with a larger model). It's not a huge difference, but the Keurig lets you adjust the strength of each cup. More control!

Let's break down the $$$ of it all, shall we!? There are plenty of different machines from both Keurig and Nespresso, and I'm going to talk about the two (very similar!) machines that I own. The Nespresso Vertuo will run you a very reasonable $130, while the Keurig K-Crema is a little pricier at $220. Again, it comes down to priorities! If you prefer high-quality lattes and espresso, the decision is easier—Nespresso will make that for you at a lower cost. However, the Keurig has more features. If that's something you need, the extra $90 is easy to justify.

As for pod costs, the Keurig version is slightly cheaper on average, around 40 cents to $1.10 per pod, while Nespresso is more like 80 cents to $1.30.

In case you couldn't already read my obvious bias, I am a Keurig girly through and through. Despite my attempt to embrace Nespresso, the Keurig is like that familiar ex you keep going back to. And while I went back and forth for a bit between the two—I didn't want to give up that fancy schmancy crema top but also really missed boring drip-style brews—the Keurig K-Crema made my final decision simple. I get the best of both worlds!

However, what matters to you—cost, simplicity, strength, style, etc.—is so subjective. If you only drink café-like bevvies or prefer stronger coffee, the Nespresso is probably a better fit. If you'd rather spend less per pod, Keurig is your girl. Choose your own fate, and stay caffeinated!

#2869. The Best Shotguns for Bird Hunting of All Time

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The latest for gun hunters and competitive shooters.

You could shoot a lifetime of roosters with a beat up Remington 870 Express. And you could make a good argument that any gun you’re willing to carry and can shoot well in the uplands is an upland bird gun.

However, there are some shotguns that are better suited for the task. A good bird hunting gun should be pleasant to carry (meaning it’s light and well balanced), it should mount naturally, and it should swing through targets with ease. A bird gun should look nice too, because as longtime shotgun columnist Phil Bourjaily says, pheasants and grouse don’t like to be killed by ugly guns.

So for this story, and the video above, Bourjaily helped me assemble a list of our favorite bird hunting shotguns, both new and old. We carefully considered value when making our selections. None of the guns on our list are cheap, but they are still attainable for the working bird hunter. They are also the kind of gun that you will want to pass down to your kids one day.

We picked the 12-gauge version of the 825 as our Editor’s Choice award-winning shotgun in 2025, but it’s quite possible that we like the 20 gauge version of the new Citori even better.

Shotgun nerds know that the Browning Citori was introduced in the early 1970s as a more affordable, Japanese-manufactured alternative to John Moses Browning’s Belgian-made Browning Superposed (which you’ll find in our list of classic guns). The modern Citori maintains that legacy of quality and attainability. The Citori 825 has very nice aesthetics. It features a glossy American walnut stock, quail and dove engravings on the receiver, blued barrel, and ivory bead.

With the 28-inch barrels I thought the gun handled perfectly. Even at just 6 pounds, it did not feel whippy at all. Compared to the 725, this upgraded Citori has a lower-profile receiver and lower-bore axis. This should mean less muzzle jump and faster recovery between shots. While shooting this gun on clays, I did notice that it recoiled very lightly.

Lastly, the Citori has a tapered locking bolt that is designed to self-adjust — in other words, tighten — with time. This is meant to prevent a very ambitious shooter from wearing out the action. If you are looking for a do-it-all upland bird gun, it’s hard to beat the Citori in this price range.

The Benelli Montefeltro is probably my all-time favorite pheasant hunting shotgun. It’s light, reliable, and it comes to my shoulder more naturally and quickly than any other semi-auto out there. And sometimes, two shots are just not enough. For Bourjaily’s part, he says that if he had to hunt pheasants for money, this is the gun he would shoot (luckily, or perhaps sadly, that is not the case for Phil).

The Montefeltro was first introduced in 1983, but it was updated most recently in 2023 with an easier-locking bolt system, a two-piece shell latch, and improved ergonomics and grip.

The inertia action in this shotgun allows for a wonderfully sleek and slender shotgun (because there is no need to make room for a gas system in the forend). I like inertia guns best for upland hunting. Typically, you will not be shooting very much, so punishing recoil is not as problematic as it is on a fast and furious duck hunt. Plus you’re less likely to encounter the mud, sand, and dirt that can jam an inertia gun on a waterfowl hunt.

The 20-gauge version of the Montefeltro with a 28-inch barrel weighs only 6.1 pounds. The 12-gauge version at the same barrel length weighs only 6.6 pounds. So take your pick and off to the cattail sloughs you go.

The Auto 5 was a marvel in its day. It was the first successful mass-produced semi-auto shotgun and it was made in Belgium starting in 1902. The Auto 5 utilizes a long-recoil system, in which both the bolt and the barrel move to the rear to re-cock the hammer after a shell is fired. Then the barrel moves forward, the bolt follows and chambers the next shell from the magazine. Friction rings on the magazine tube were designed to allow the firing of light and heavy loads, which was a major hurdle at the time.

The 16-gauge version of this shotgun, aptly named the “Sweet 16,” was introduced in 1936. Browning lightened the gun by removing steel from the barrel ring and receiver and wood from the stock. The promise was a semi-auto shotgun that carried like a 20 gauge and hit like a 12 gauge. And on this promise, Browning delivered.

Today, 16-gauge shotguns are not quite as appealing, since modern 20 gauges can fire 3-inch shells delivering just as much payload in an even lighter platform. But our love for this gun is more about nostalgia than practicality. As Bourjaily wrote for us in his full review of the gun: “No other shotgun feels quite like an original Browning Auto 5 when it goes off. As Bourjaily writes, before the Browning Superposed, over/unders were handmade, bespoke sidelock shotguns priced far out of reach of the average person. John Browning saw the O/U and realized that American shooters, who were accustomed to looking down the single barrel of a pump or semi-auto, would like what he called the single sighting plane of an O/U shotgun. He set out to make an O/U that an average person could afford if they saved for it. Browning wanted to make an aspirational gun. He designed it to be simpler than an English gun, to be made with some mass-produced parts and he worked with FN in Belgium, as he did with many other guns.

Browning’s work on the Superposed began in the 1920s, when the economy was strong, skeet shooting was a fad, and there was a wide variety of game to hunt.

Browning died in 1926, literally while at work on the gun. His son Val finished it, but the Superposed was born into a global depression, then a world war that resulted in the occupation, then the destruction, of the factory where it was being built. In the 1950s, the Superposed became the gun that Browning always thought it would be, until the 70s when high cost of Belgian labor forced Browning to move production to Japan and simplify the design into the Citori, which is largely the same gun.

The Superposed is more than an artifact of shotgunning history. In its Lightning and Superlight configurations, John Browning’s O/U still makes a great upland bird gun as it nears its 100th birthday. The heavier 3-inch magnum guns have to be babied with bismuth, but they can drop ducks and pheasants as well as any modern gun. In any configuration, the Superposed is durable, reliable, well-made and good-looking. The 20-gauge version is especially well-suited for carrying in the uplands.

The Ruger Gold Label might not be everybody’s pick for the top classic side-by-side, but we have our reasons. The Gold Label was one of Bill Ruger’s last guns and it was only made for a short time. This gun might be the last classic American double.

All of the Foxes and Parkers and Ithacas became casualties of WWII. They became too expensive to produce, and no one wanted them in postwar America. Fifty years later, Ruger aimed to make an affordable, high quality side by side for American hunters, and the Gold Label was the result.

His design was inspired by Scottish round actions, in which all the parts of the lock sit on the trigger plate so the receiver can be rounded and petite.

Ruger guns were made with investment casting rather than machining to reduce costs.

Bill intended this gun to sell for $2,000 in 2004, which would be about $3,500 today. And that is about what these guns sell for on the used market. Unfortunately, Ruger had a hard time manufacturing the Gold Label under that price point and ultimately the guns lost money.

The Gold Label was introduced as a svelte 12 gauge that weighs only 6.5 pounds. Since the frame is so light, the gun has a very weight-forward balance, which makes it easy to shoot.

We shot Bourjaily’s Gold Label and he admits that he doesn’t love the stainless frame, but he does love the lines of the round action, which we agree are the most beautiful shotguns ever made. We can’t afford the great Scottish round actions — like a MacNaughton or a David McKay Brown — but we can afford this gun, and so can you if you really want one. Ruger eventually gave up Bill’s pet project after he died. There were only 3,000 Gold Labels made, which leaves 2,999 out there for you to find.

#2957. The Mountain That Looked Impossible: Muztagh Tower, 1956

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For nearly half a century, a single photograph defined one of the Karakoram’s most striking peaks. Taken in 1909 and reproduced in books and journals, it showed Muztagh Tower as a vertical blade, a slender tooth of rock and ice rising from the glaciers with no obvious weakness. Generations of climbers studied it and concluded that the mountain was impossible to summit.

In the summer of 1956, two independent expeditions arrived on opposite sides of the peak, each convinced they were looking at one of the range’s last great unclimbed problems. But the photograph had misled them.

Vittorio Sella was born in 1859 in Biella, Italy, into a family connected to the mountains. His uncle, Quintino Sella, had helped found the Club Alpino Italiano, and his father, Giuseppe Venanzio Sella, was a pioneer of early photography who published the first important Italian treatise on the subject.

Vittorio Sella combined serious alpinism with large-format photography. He made the first winter ascent of the Matterhorn in 1882, followed by the first winter traverse of Mont Blanc. Throughout his career, he carried heavy glass-plate cameras into the high Alps, the Caucasus, Alaska, the Ruwenzori, and the Karakoram.

Sella’s photographic technique was excellent. His plates were large, the detail exceptional, and the compositions carefully chosen. His work was much admired, including by American photographer Ansel Adams. In a 1946 article for the Sierra Club Bulletin, Adams paid tribute to Sella’s work, writing:

“The purity of Sella’s interpretations move the spectator to a religious awe. Sella has brought to us not only the facts and forms of far-off splendours of the world, but the essence of experience which finds a spiritual response in the inner recesses of our mind and heart.”

Vittorio Sella. Photo: Alpine Club Photo Library

Sella’s most important Himalayan photographs came from the 1909 K2 expedition led by Luigi Amedeo, Duke of the Abruzzi.

Filippo De Filippi’s official account of the expedition, Karakoram and Western Himalaya 1909 (published in English in 1912), was illustrated throughout with Sella’s images. Those photographs remain among the finest early records of the range. One of them, a telephoto view of Muztagh Tower, became famous far beyond the expedition report, and inadvertently sparked a legendary mountaineering myth.

Muztagh Tower (7,276m) is located in the Baltoro Muztagh of the Karakoram, on the border between Pakistan’s Gilgit-Baltistan and China’s Xinjiang. It rises between the Baltoro and Sarpo Laggo Glaciers, just north of the main Baltoro corridor leading toward K2.

The peak has two summits only a short distance apart. The eastern peak is the true summit, and the western summit is a few metres lower. From most angles, the mountain is a substantial massif with steep but not uniformly vertical faces. From one particular viewpoint it collapses into something that looks far more extreme.

Muztagh Tower taken from the Upper Baltoro Glacier in 1919 by Vittorio Sella. The tower appears as an impregnable tooth. The photo inspired mountaineers for decades. Photo: Vittorio Sella

Sella took the image of Muztagh Tower from the upper Baltoro Glacier, looking northwest toward the mountain. The twin summits lined up almost perfectly. The long telephoto lens compressed the intervening ground and presented the peak as a narrow, knife-edged tower whose walls appeared to fall nearly vertically from summit to glacier.

From other angles the peak revealed broader ridges and less precipitous slopes, but this single compressed view was the one that circulated widely. The effect was really dramatic, but as later climbers discovered, also misleading. In 1941, Sella’s photo of Muztagh Tower appeared in a mountaineering book under the caption “The Last Citadel.” The image fixed the mountain’s reputation for decades.

Muztagh Tower, 1909. Photo: Vittorio Sella

In 1956, a British team and a French team both set out for Muztagh Tower, drawn in large part by Sella’s photograph.

The British team, led by John Hartog and consisting of Joe Brown, Ian McNaught-Davis, Tom Patey, and liaison officer Captain Riaz Mohammad, chose the Northwest Ridge. They planned to approach via the Muztagh and Chagaran glaciers on the western side of the peak.

The French party, led by Guido Magnone, included Andre Contamine, Paul Keller, Robert Paragot, Francois Florence, and liaison officer Ali Usman. They worked from the eastern side, aiming for what became the eastern arête after reconnaissance ruled out the North Ridge. The two groups operated independently on opposite flanks of the mountain.

The British established Base Camp on the Muztagh Glacier on May 28, 1956, at 4,282m. Through the first half of June, they placed camps on the Chagaran Glacier, avoiding the worst of the lower icefall by climbing the northern moraine and then threading seracs. Camp 3 was set on the west col at 6,355m after a steep ice wall. They reached the col on June 16, but took until the end of the month to move tents, fuel, and food up.

From there the route followed the exposed crest of the West Ridge, a mixed line of rock, snow and ice that required fixed ropes on the steeper sections. Brown made a key traverse around a rock cliff on the South Face. Camp 4 was placed on the ridge itself at 6,439m.

Above the high camp, the climbing continued up a narrow couloir and then mixed ground. The final technical obstacle was a short rock leaf between the two summits, graded alpine V and exposed on both sides.

On July 6, Brown and McNaught-Davis reached the western summit. The following day, Hartog and Patey, after supporting the first pair, crossed the connecting ridge and stood on the slightly higher eastern summit. Both pairs had to bivouac on the descent. Hartog suffered frostbitten toes. All four climbers reached the top.

Muztagh Tower. Photo: Hunza Adventure Tours

The French established their Base Camp at 4,496m on June 12 after approaching from the Younghusband Glacier. Their reconnaissance quickly showed the North Ridge was impossible, so they moved onto the eastern branch of the glacier.

They fixed ropes through an icefall and up a rock-and-ice spur, placing successive camps until Camp 4 stood at the foot of the summit pyramid at 6,300m. On July 6, as bad weather closed in, they spotted the British climbers moving on the upper heights.

After the two-day storm cleared, the French regained the ridge, overcame two substantial rock towers, and bivouacked high on the mountain. On July 12, Magnone, Paragot, Contamine, and Keller reached the main summit through deep snow, six days after the British first topped out. After summiting, they descended to Camp 4 after dark. Dr. Florence had waited at high camp for more than 40 hours without radio contact during the summit push.

The British party therefore made the first ascent of Muztagh Tower. The French completed an independent second ascent six days later. Both teams discovered that the mountain, while technically demanding and exposed, offered viable lines. The route, while serious, proved less relentlessly steep than Sella’s photograph had suggested.

The two expeditions were not engaged in a formal competition. They approached from opposite sides, followed separate routes, and maintained cordial relations. The British simply reached the summit first. The French, seeing the British already on top, continued their own line to completion.

Far from a bitter nationalistic race, the two teams demonstrated alpine solidarity. The French team chose the east side specifically to avoid interfering with British progress on the western side. Rather than abandoning their push in defeat after spotting the British on the summit, the French celebrated their colleagues’ historic achievement and resolutely pushed forward to fulfill their own objective. When Hartog suffered severe frostbite during his descent, the French team stepped in to provide vital assistance and support during the evacuation.

The southeast side of Muztagh Tower, showing the route. Photo: Guido Magnone for The Himalayan Journal

Since 1956, Muztagh Tower has remained one of the least visited major peaks in the Karakoram. A British team in 1984 repeated the Northwest Ridge (Mal Duff, Tony Brindle, Jon Tinker, and Sandy Allan), though they reached the slightly lower west summit rather than the true east summit. In 1990, a Swedish party consisting of Göran Kropp and Rafael Jensen also successfully scaled the Northwest Ridge.

In August 2008, Slovenian alpinists Pavle Kozjek and Dejan Miskovic made a groundbreaking alpine-style push up the steep Northeast Face. They successfully reached the upper ridge crest, but ferocious winds ended their push. Tragically, just after beginning their descent, a ridge cornice collapsed, and Kozjek fell to his death.

The 2,000m Northeast Spur of Muztagh Tower showing the line “Think Twice.” Photo: American Alpine Journal

Four years later, Russians Sergey Nilov, Dmitry Golovchenko, and Alexander Lange made the historic first ascent of the massive Northeast Spur. Their grueling 17-day climb via a new route named “Think Twice” reached the true summit, earning them the prestigious Piolets d’Or. The Russian team indicated in their expedition report that Muztagh Tower was 7,284m.

The 1956 French route has never been fully repeated to the main peak, and no further ascents of the true summit have been confirmed since the 2012 Russian expedition. Still, climbers continue to push limits here, most notably in 2019, with the first ascent of the Black Tooth (6,718m), a difficult lower spire on the mountain’s ridge.

Muztagh Tower (top left) and its Southeast Ridge, with a high point on the Black Tooth (6,719m). The solid line, with the two marked bivouac sites, is the line of ascent to Black Tooth followed by Simon Messner and Martin Sieberer in 2019. Photo: Simon Messner/American Alpine Journal

#4061. What Narcissists, Psychopaths And Sadists Have In Common

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Different dark personalities may have more in common than their labels suggest.

Different dark personalities may have more in common than their labels suggest.

Narcissists, psychopaths, sadists and manipulative people share a dark personality core that predicts selfish and antisocial choices.

This common tendency helps explain whether people exploit others, retaliate against them or refuse to trust them.

The crucial feature is a willingness to pursue personal interests at other people’s expense, accompanied by beliefs that justify doing so.

Known as the dark factor of personality, or D, this tendency brings together traits that can look quite different on the surface.

Narcissism involves an inflated sense of self-importance, Machiavellianism centres on manipulation, psychopathy includes callousness, and sadism involves enjoying others’ suffering.

The idea that these traits share an underlying disposition was established in earlier research examining their overlap and links to harmful behaviour (Moshagen et al., 2018).

The latest findings suggest that this shared core is more consistently associated with antisocial decisions than any of the four traits considered separately.

In other words, knowing what these personalities have in common may reveal more about their choices than knowing which particular label fits them.

The research involved over 10,000 people across ten studies using eight types of decision-making game.

Participants completed personality questionnaires before making hypothetical choices involving another person they did not know.

Depending on the game, they could decide how much money to share, whether to return someone’s trust, whether to retaliate or whether to cooperate.

Some situations allowed people to benefit themselves at someone else’s expense, while others explored whether they would trust another person when cooperation could benefit both.

People scoring higher on the dark factor consistently made fewer prosocial choices across all eight games.

The individual dark traits also generally predicted less prosocial behaviour, but their associations were weaker.

Once their shared dark core was taken into account, the separate traits usually added little to the explanation.

There were a few exceptions, particularly involving Machiavellianism in situations where distrust could influence decisions.

One possible explanation is that dark personality traits combine harmful tendencies with other characteristics that are not inherently antisocial.

For example, boldness does not necessarily involve harming anyone, while wanting admiration does not automatically mean exploiting others.

Earlier research proposed that these additional characteristics help distinguish dark traits from one another, while their shared core captures what makes them socially harmful (Bader et al., 2023).

Measuring a broad trait such as psychopathy may therefore mix callous self-interest with features that have little relevance to a particular moral decision.

Focusing on the common core could provide a clearer picture of someone’s willingness to disregard other people’s welfare.

Importantly, the dark factor extends beyond wanting a larger share of the rewards.

It also encompasses a readiness to hurt others and beliefs that can make selfish conduct seem acceptable.

Distrust may fit this pattern because expecting other people to behave selfishly can provide a convenient justification for doing the same.

That is a proposed psychological explanation, rather than evidence that distrust caused the choices observed here.

The findings do not mean that everyone with dark traits will behave selfishly in every situation.

Nor do they establish that personality caused the decisions participants made.

All the games were hypothetical, so participants were describing what they would choose rather than making decisions with real financial consequences.

This matters because associations between dark traits and antisocial choices can be stronger in hypothetical situations than when money is genuinely at stake.

Participants also volunteered through a website offering dark-personality assessments, so they were not a representative sample of the wider population.

Even with these qualifications, the results support a simpler account of apparently different dark personalities: much of their connection to harmful choices lies in the same underlying disregard for others.

And now, nearly a year after those first pieces broke the surface, more questions than answers are emerging from the wreck. The biggest one is: has somebody been down there already?

The San José, sunk by the British Royal Navy in the Caribbean during the War of Spanish Succession in 1708, was full of value. The ship was laden with 10 years’ worth of gold, silver, and gems—tribute from Spanish colonies in Latin America headed back to the Spanish king. The Colombian Ministry of Culture, Arts, and Knowledge used a robotic underwater vehicle to secure the first samples of that cargo in late 2025, picking up three coins, a bronze cannon (along with fragments of wood, rope, and sediment), and two porcelain cups—each with a different style—without disturbing the structure of the galleon itself.

Alhena Caicedo Fernández, director of the Colombian Institute of Anthropology and History (ICANH), said in a translated statement that collecting the objects “opens the possibility that citizens can approach, through material testimony, the history of the San José galleon.”Not everyone sees it that way.

Earlier this year, a Colombian oversight group dedicated to the country’s submerged cultural heritage issued an open letter to the nation’s attorney general, alleging looting and unauthorized interventions at the wreck site in 2016 and 2022—and claiming the site’s coordinates, officially a state secret since the government announced its discovery in 2015, have been disclosed. The group’s suspicions trace partly to an analysis by a University of Southampton deep-sea archaeology expert, who compared imagery of the wreck from 2015 and 2016 and found “selective areas with sediment removal.” Even the rope fragment recovered with the cannon sparked a fight: the watchdog’s director called it “most likely modern,” while the research team countered that rope has survived on far older wrecks, including a 12th-century Viking vessel. ICANH has repeatedly denied any looting.

Other experts questioned why the extraction happened at all. One Colombian archaeologist who has challenged the recovery plan since 2018 said “no scientific reasons were given” for pulling up the artifacts, while the research team maintains the objects help determine the site’s conservation through physicochemical studies.

Then there’s the custody battle—or battles, really. Current estimates of the on-board value have ranged from $10 billion to $17 billion in today’s currency, sparking competing claims from the Spanish government, which considers the galleon a sovereign warship and war grave, and Bolivia’s Indigenous Qhara Qhara nation, whose ancestors mined much of the silver aboard. The biggest legal fight is happening at the Permanent Court of Arbitration in The Hague, where U.S.-based salvage company Sea Search Armada—which claims it located the galleon back in the early 1980s—is seeking $10 billion, roughly half the cargo’s value. Colombia disputes that the company ever found the real site. A ruling against Colombia could mean the most expensive treasure recovery in history will end up costing the country more than it winds up getting from the wreck.

But whoever gets custody of the San José probably won’t come up totally empty-handed. It’s estimated that 200 tons of gold, silver, and uncut gemstones were aboard the ship, the result of 10 years of taxation saved up before the fleet’s planned voyage back to Spain. The macuquinas—known as cobs in English—were the main currency in the Americas at the time, typically cut from gold or silver ingots, and were the primary way the Spanish transported large volumes of wealth from the Americas to Europe. These coin hoards likely formed part of the royal treasure dispatched from Peru.

But when the fleet of 18 ships left Cartagena bound for Spain on June 8, 1708, it was attacked by five British warships. The cannon battle resulted in the explosion of the San José’s gunpowder stores, sinking the 150-foot-long ship and nearly 600 sailors with it.

The shipwreck—and its treasured cargo—was thought to have sat untouched on the ocean floor ever since. Now, bit by bit, what’s left of it is reaching the surface once more. But as the legal fight over its ownership rises faster than the artifacts themselves, the real tragedy may be that the world’s greatest sunken treasure has already been plundered.
