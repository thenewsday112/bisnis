#4200. How to stop nightmares and fall back asleep after bad dreams

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, CDCES, MBA

Nightmares can be really tough. They jolt us awake in the middle of the night, often in a cold sweat with a racing heart, and the residue of the bad dream leaves us feeling shaken and on edge, making it hard to fall back asleep. And we all know how much more challenging everything else becomes when our sleep is disrupted.

Recurring nightmares can wreck your sleep and leave you feeling drained, anxious, or unsettled the next day. If you’ve found yourself googling, “why do I keep having nightmares?” or, “how to stop bad dreams every night,” you’re not alone.

Nightmares can be triggered by everything from stress and sleep disorders to late-night snacks or certain medications. Thankfully, there are proven strategies to reduce their frequency and help you get back to sleep after a bad dream.

Let’s explore common nightmare causes, tips to prevent them, and techniques to calm your mind and body when you wake up in the middle of the night.

Sleep disruptions, like frequent nightmares and bad dreams, can take their toll on our well-being. It’s not just about feeling tired the next day. It’s about how those disturbances affect our mood, energy levels, productivity, and our mental health. Getting enough sleep is crucial when it comes to rejuvenating our bodies and resting our minds, but when you’re experiencing nightmares on the reg, peaceful sleep can feel like a distant dream.

But there is hope. With awareness and understanding of what’s causing your nightmares, along with some effective strategies, it’s possible to manage, if not prevent, bad dreams. Tools such as mindfulness and meditation can help you cultivate a more peaceful mind and significantly improve the quality of your sleep.

Nightmares don’t tend to be random. They’re usually the product of various factors at work in our subconscious or conscious minds. So a key factor in tackling nightmares is being able to understand what’s prompting them.

Stress and/or anxiety: Perhaps unsurprisingly, frequent nightmares can be the product of stress or anxiety that’s present in our waking life. It’s like your mind extends the day’s worries into your sleep. That upcoming work presentation, conflict with your partner, or just the sheer weight of day-to-day tasks can all find their way into your dreams, causing nightmares.

Eating too close to bedtime: That late-night snack might be more trouble than it’s worth. And no, it’s not just cheese, which, by the way, doesn’t actually cause nightmares. Heavy or spicy meals, in particular, can lead to physical discomfort that disturbs your sleep, often inducing vivid and sometimes bad dreams.

Scary movies, TV shows, or books: Love a good horror movie or thriller book before bed? You might want to rethink that. Your subconscious can weave elements of the contents of whatever you’ve been reading or watching into your dreams, creating some pretty unsettling mental visualizations when the lights go off.

Traumatic experiences: If you’ve been through a particularly challenging event either recently or in the past, the trauma of whatever happened could show up in your dreams. This is normal. It’s your brain’s way of processing the trauma, but it can lead to recurring nightmares, which can be distressing and even prevent sleep.

Alcohol, drugs, and/or caffeine: It may feel like alcohol and drugs ease you into sleep more easily than going to bed unassisted, but these substances often disrupt the quality of your sleep and can lead to vivid, intense nightmares.

Medications: Some prescription drugs, including certain types of antidepressants, blood pressure medicines, and beta blockers, can lead to bad dreams. If you’ve started a new medication and are experiencing frequent nightmares, you might want to talk to your doctor.

Sleep disorders: Sleep conditions like sleep apnea and restless leg syndrome can disrupt sleep and lead to nightmares. If you’re experiencing symptoms such as daytime sleepiness or morning headaches, please check in with a healthcare provider.

Knowing what triggers your nightmares is the first step in gaining control over them. Remember, the goal isn’t to eliminate all dreams but to cultivate a more relaxed sleep experience. After all, dreams are a natural part of sleep, and many of them can be magical, rejuvenating, and creative experiences.

Now that we know what might be causing nightmares, here are some strategies to help keep bad dreams at bay.

Routines aren’t just for kids, and the value of a consistent sleep schedule can’t be overstated. Your body thrives on routine, so a regular sleep and wake time helps your body understand when it’s time to wind down and when it’s time to rise.

Consider your sleeping environment. Is it dark, quiet, and cool? These are ideal conditions for restful sleep. Also, consider investing in a comfortable mattress and pillows. These small changes can significantly impact your sleep quality and help prevent bad dreams.

If stress or anxiety is the culprit behind your nightmares, it’s important to tackle the cause head-on. Implementing stress management techniques, such as deep breathing exercises or guided mindfulness practices (such as this one), can work wonders in soothing your mind before bed.

Try to wind down at least an hour before bed. This means turning off the TV, stepping away from work, and switching off any other devices. This quiet time allows your mind to transition smoothly into sleep mode.

Regular physical activity can promote better sleep, helping to prevent nightmares. Just ensure you don’t exercise too close to bedtime, as it can have the opposite effect.

As mentioned before, late-night snacking, especially heavy or very spicy food, can lead to bad dreams. Finish eating at least a few hours before hitting the hay.

Incorporate relaxing activities into your bedtime routine. This could include reading, listening to relaxing music, or practicing mindfulness exercises. It’s all about calming your mind and preparing your body for sleep.

If something’s bothering you, don’t let it stew in your mind. Talking it out with a trusted friend, family member, or professional can help release some of the burden.

Both these substances can disrupt your sleep cycle and might increase the likelihood of having nightmares. Try limiting them or avoiding them altogether.

If you suspect your medication is causing nightmares, discuss it with your healthcare provider. They might be able to adjust the dosage or suggest alternatives.

Implementing these strategies can make a big difference in preventing nightmares and improving the overall quality of your sleep. Everyone deserves a good night’s sleep, and you’re no exception. Remember, it’s not about perfection but progress, so celebrate small victories and keep experimenting to find out what works best for you.

The biggest challenge after waking up from a nightmare is often trying to calm your body and mind enough so you can get back to sleep. If you’ve ever found yourself wide awake at 3 a.m., staring at the ceiling, you know exactly what we’re talking about.

So how exactly do you drift back into the land of nod once you’ve been rudely awakened by a nightmare? Here are seven strategies that might help you fall back asleep after a bad dream.

Deep, controlled breathing can be a powerful tool in helping to calm your mind and body. The practice works by shifting your focus away from any disturbing images from your nightmare and redirects it onto the rise and fall of your breath. An easy-to-follow technique is the 4-7-8 breathing method, where you inhale for 4 counts, hold your breath for 7 counts, and exhale for 8 counts.

This technique involves tensing and then releasing different muscle groups in your body. Start from your toes and work up to your head (or vice versa). This can help you feel more grounded and take your mind off the images or content of your nightmare.

Picture a peaceful, calming scene. It could be a beautiful beach, a serene forest, or any place that brings you joy and tranquility. Focusing on images that make you feel relaxed and at ease can steer your mind away from whatever you were dreaming about.

If you’re finding it hard to fall back asleep, get out of bed and do something relaxing. This could be reading a book, listening to calm music, or doing a low-intensity activity like drawing. Do whatever feels soothing to you, and try to do it somewhere quiet and in low-lighting so your body can ease into relaxation and sleep. If in doubt, try listening to a Sleep Story.

If you wake up scared, turning on a soft, gentle light can feel comforting. In some ways, it feels like you’re banishing the darkness from your dream, and being able to see your surroundings makes you feel safer and more at ease. Just make sure its not too bright or it may, ironically, end up disrupting your sleep.

Writing down whatever happened in your nightmare can be therapeutic. In some ways, the process of writing helps to drain away the power the bad dream has over your emotions. Keep a notebook and pen beside your bed and if a nightmare wakes you up, write it down. Here’s a Dream Journal you can download and print out.

If you’ve been startled awake by a nightmare, take a moment to center yourself. Notice your surroundings, the feeling of the sheets, the sounds you can hear. This can bring you back to reality and help separate the images of your nightmare from the safety of your bedroom. Repeat to yourself: “I am here. I am safe.”

Finding it difficult to fall back to sleep after a nightmare is perfectly normal. Don’t pressure yourself to get back to sleep immediately. Take your time, be gentle with yourself, and use the techniques above to help guide your mind and body back into a state of ease.

#918. A tiny defect in a crystal reveals a big quantum surprise

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When light strikes a tiny imperfection inside a material such as diamond or silicon carbide, it can transfer energy to electrons and push them into an excited state. These imperfections, known as spin defects, could become valuable building blocks for future quantum technologies, including quantum sensors. Because they can function as stable qubits whose states can be read, scientists are eager to understand exactly how they behave. However, predicting how their energy changes over time has remained a major challenge.

Researchers at the University of Chicago Pritzker School of Molecular Engineering (UChicago PME) have now developed a computational method that provides a more complete picture of what happens when these defects interact with light. Their approach can simulate the entire optical cycle, including a particularly difficult process known as internal conversion.

The findings, published in Physical Review Letters, could help scientists design better materials for quantum technologies. The method may also prove useful for studying materials involved in renewable energy conversion and storage.

"This internal conversion process is not only relevant to spin defects -- it's also related to processes of interest to energy technologies," said Giulia Galli, the Liew Family Professor of Electronic Structure and Simulations in UChicago PME and the Department of Chemistry and the senior author of the study. "That's one of the reasons this work is so exciting."

When a laser excites a spin defect, the defect can return to its original, lower-energy state through several different processes. It might release a photon of light, transition between states with different spins, or transfer its energy into vibrations of the surrounding atoms.

That final pathway, called internal conversion, has been especially difficult for scientists to model accurately. Although the fundamental equations of quantum mechanics can describe the other two processes, calculating how energy is lost through atomic vibrations presents substantial computational obstacles.

"Although the equations are known, it's hard to describe the process accurately because of computational difficulties," said Stefano Villani, a UChicago PME postdoctoral researcher and the study's first author. "You need to account for complex interactions between the electrons and surrounding atoms, and that's challenging in realistic solids represented by models with hundreds or thousands of atoms"

To overcome this problem, Galli, Villani, and Yu Jin combined several computational techniques that their research group had developed over many years. Jin, a former graduate student in Galli's group, is now a postdoctoral scholar at the Flatiron Institute in New York.

The researchers used mathematical descriptions known as many-body wavefunctions to represent the excited states of spin defects. Through exact mathematical derivations, they determined how strongly each excited state interacts with the movements of nearby atoms.

This allowed them to calculate how individual electronic states connect with all the possible vibrational configurations of hundreds of surrounding atoms, rather than having to analyze those configurations individually.

"In the past, a clear mathematical derivation and efficient algorithms were not available," explained Yu Jin. "The new derivation came together after years of work on the optical cycle of spin defects."

The researchers incorporated their new framework into WEST, an open-source simulation program optimized for graphics processing units (GPUs). Galli's group has spent the past decade developing WEST through the Midwest Integrated Center for Computational Materials (MICCoM), which has received funding from the U.S. Department of Energy's Office of Basic Energy Sciences since 2015.

A Surprising Discovery Inside Silicon Carbide

To test their approach, the researchers first examined a nitrogen-vacancy center in diamond, a type of crystal defect widely studied for quantum applications.

Their calculations closely matched recent spectroscopy measurements. For triplet states, internal conversion contributed relatively little to energy loss. In singlet states, however, it was the primary mechanism responsible for releasing energy. Both findings agreed with what scientists had previously expected.

The results changed when the team investigated a divacancy defect in silicon carbide.

Their calculations revealed that internal conversion has a much greater influence on the lifetime of the defect's excited triplet state than earlier models had accounted for. This previously underestimated contribution helped explain a long-standing disagreement between theoretical predictions and experimental observations.

"We were expecting to see an effect, but of a smaller magnitude," Galli said. "What we found is that this internal conversion process turns out to be more important than we thought."

Understanding this unexpected energy-loss pathway could help scientists more accurately predict the behavior of silicon carbide defects and improve their potential use as qubits in future quantum devices.

A New Tool for Quantum Computing and Energy Research

With the framework now available through WEST, researchers at other institutions can begin applying it to their own studies. However, Galli noted that the simulations still demand considerable computing power.

The team is exploring ways to reduce those requirements, including the use of artificial intelligence.

"We are working on ways to integrate machine learning into excited states calculations to further improve the efficiency of our method," Villani said.

Meanwhile, the scientists are expanding their approach to investigate spin defects in silicon, as well as materials used in energy technologies where internal conversion influences how energy is transferred or lost.

By addressing one of the most difficult parts of modeling how spin defects respond to light, the researchers have made it possible to predict their behavior more completely and accurately.

"We provided the missing piece of the puzzle needed to completely describe the optical cycle of spin defects," Galli said. "Our work can now be used to make more accurate predictions about many materials of interest for quantum technologies," added Yu Jin.

#902. Scientists discover a surprising double benefit of exercise for Parkinson’s

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

People with Parkinson's disease who are more physically active may experience slower declines in memory, thinking, and movement, according to a new study published October 7, 2026, in Neurology, the official medical journal of the American Academy of Neurology.

The findings suggest that staying active could offer two important advantages for people living with Parkinson's. However, researchers emphasize that the study identified a connection between physical activity and slower symptom progression. It does not establish that exercise directly causes the disease to progress more slowly.

"Parkinson's disease is a brain disorder that causes movement and cognitive problems that gradually worsen leading to disability and a loss of independence," said study author Dong-Woo Ryu, MD, PhD, of the University of Cincinnati in Ohio. "Previous research has shown associations between physical activity and movement symptoms. Our study found physical activity may have dual benefits, with associations with slower declines in movement and cognition."

Tracking Exercise and Parkinson's Symptoms Over Seven Years

The research involved 294 adults with mild to moderate Parkinson's disease. Participants were 66 years old on average, and researchers monitored their health for up to seven years.

To understand their activity habits, participants completed questionnaires estimating how many hours each week they spent being physically active. The questions covered activities performed at work, around the home, and during hobbies or recreation.

Researchers classified activities by their intensity. Moderate activities included brisk walking, doubles tennis, leisurely swimming, and household tasks such as cleaning windows, vacuuming, or mowing the lawn. More demanding activities included running, cycling at higher speeds, participating in team sports, playing singles tennis, shoveling, and carrying heavy objects.

The team examined physical activity in three ways. First, they calculated the total time participants spent doing moderate or vigorous activities. Second, they estimated overall activity volume by combining duration with intensity, giving greater weight to strenuous exercise. Third, they separately measured the amount of time spent doing vigorous activities.

Based on total weekly activity time, participants were placed into three categories. Those in the least active group reported up to four hours per week, the moderately active group reported four to eight hours, and the most active group reported more than eight hours.

Each year, participants also completed assessments designed to track changes in thinking and memory, along with tests evaluating the severity of their Parkinson's symptoms.

More Activity Linked to Slower Memory Decline

One of the most striking differences emerged in cognitive performance. Researchers used a 30-point test of thinking and memory, with lower scores reflecting poorer cognitive function.

Among participants in the least active group, scores fell by an estimated average of 0.478 points each year. The moderately active group experienced a much smaller annual decline of 0.090 points.

For the most active participants, the estimated average yearly decrease was only 0.007 points, indicating that their cognitive scores remained nearly unchanged over time.

The researchers also examined changes in Parkinson's movement symptoms using an assessment scored from zero to 132. Higher numbers represented more severe symptoms, and participants began the study with an average score of 23.

In the least active group, symptom scores increased by an estimated 0.143 points annually, indicating a slight worsening. By contrast, scores decreased by 0.225 points per year in the moderately active group and by 0.783 points in the most active group.

Even after accounting for participants' age, sex, and how long they had been living with Parkinson's, the researchers found that the most active group experienced slower cognitive decline and less worsening of movement symptoms than the least active group.

The connection was not limited to one measure of exercise. Higher total activity time, greater overall activity volume, and more time spent exercising vigorously were each associated with slower deterioration in both thinking abilities and movement symptoms.

Why Consistent Physical Activity May Matter

The results suggest that remaining physically active over time could be especially important for people with Parkinson's disease. Researchers noted that even relatively small differences in the rate of decline might become more significant when they accumulate over several years.

"The possible benefits of physical activity for people with Parkinson's disease may depend on how long they stay active and whether they continue exercising over time," said Ryu. "While our study found small differences between activity levels, these differences may become more meaningful over time. Since participants generally had good thinking and memory skills at the start of the study, our results should be interpreted with caution and further studies are needed."

The researchers also identified an important limitation. Physical activity levels were based on participants' own reports rather than direct measurements, meaning some estimates may have been inaccurate.

Although the findings strengthen the evidence connecting physical activity with better long-term outcomes in Parkinson's disease, additional research will be needed to determine whether exercise itself can slow the progression of symptoms.

You don’t need to have opened a Batman comic to know the Joker, the Penguin, Catwoman, and the Riddler. But even some hardcore comic fans and/or nerds might scratch their heads at the mention of Clayface. With a film of his own in theaters today, Gotham’s resident shape-shifter is about to become considerably better known, although putting a face to the name will remain a problem.

DC, home of Batman, Superman, and Wonder Woman, is hoping that may turn out to be an advantage. Arriving in cinemas this Friday, Clayface takes a less familiar corner of Gotham City and gives it to James Watkins, the director of Eden Lake, The Woman in Black and Speak No Evil. His leading man is Tom Rhys Harries, whom you might remember as the doomed Ibiza DJ Axel Collins in Netflix’s White Lines. Here, he plays an actor whose attempt to repair his ruined face turns out to be a terrible, terrible idea.

Even if you're totally unfamiliar with comic book lore, there is still a straightforward proposition here: an ambitious man accepts a medical miracle, then discovers what it costs.

There have been several, which is the sort of answer that makes people regret asking questions about comics. But bear with: the relevant history is fairly simple.

The first Clayface appeared in Detective Comics in 1940. Basil Karlo was an actor enraged by the prospect of his old horror film being remade without him. In his wrath, he adopted a monstrous disguise and began murdering members of the production. Originally, the monster was a costume.

A later version, Matt Hagen, acquired the ability to morph his body into different shapes. Then Batman: The Animated Series brought elements of the two together. Its 1992 two-parter “Feat of Clay” made Hagen a disfigured actor dependent on an experimental cream that allowed him to restore his appearance. His supplier exploited that dependence; the eventual result was a shape-shifting monster.

That is the essential appeal of the character. Beneath the extraordinary powers is a performer who can become almost anyone, and yet has lost control of himself.

Harries plays Matt Hagen, whose rise from Gotham street kid to Hollywood leading man ends when a crime boss leaves him disfigured. With his career collapsing, he turns to a scientist, played by Naomi Ackie, for a treatment that restores his appearance. Alas, the treatment also begins to affect his mind, leaving him hell-bent on revenge.

Eddie Marsan, Max Minghella and David Dencik also star. The screenplay is credited to Mike Flanagan and Hossein Amini, from a story by Flanagan, whose work includes The Haunting of Hill House and Doctor Sleep. Watkins directs, with The Batman filmmaker Matt Reeves among the producers.

Those names offer a useful indication of the intended audience. This is being sold as a horror thriller, with an actor’s physical and psychological deterioration at its center.

Clayface’s history supplies a ready-made subject for body horror: a man whose own flesh turns against him. For an actor, the loss of control over his face also threatens the thing that pays the bills.

“Probably the biggest inspiration was Cronenberg,” Watkins told Esquire Philippines. He singled out The Fly, in which a grotesque transformation matters because the audience has become invested in the people experiencing it. Watkins also flagged “the horror of losing your livelihood and your identity”.

The cast’s account suggests the film has other interests alongside its transformations. Minghella describes it as "a Gotham crime thriller," invoking Heat, while Ackie has discussed its noir atmosphere, plus the characters’ psychological decline.

There may be some commercial calculation here, too. The Associated Press reports a production budget of about $40 million, relatively modest by superhero standards. Horror has repeatedly shown how far a smaller budget can go: Longlegs took more than $125 million worldwide against a reported $10 million production budget, while this year’s Backrooms has taken about $400 million on a similar budget. Keeping costs down means a film does not need superhero-sized ticket sales to succeed, even allowing for marketing costs and the cinemas’ cut.

The hope is that someone might buy a ticket because they like horror, then discover they have taken a sidestep into the DC Universe.

The filmmakers say no. “A lot of people wouldn’t necessarily know who Clayface is, but they wouldn’t need to,” Watkins told AP. “It’s its own movie.”

The character’s Batman connection should be enough to explain where he comes from. It does not mean you need to work through decades of comics, films and television before understanding a desperate actor’s decision to undergo an experimental treatment.

And will Batman himself turn up? The film takes place in Gotham, so it’s a reasonable question. Asked whether his character knows about the city’s most famous resident, Harries hinted at footage he couldn’t discuss. Whether that means an appearance, a passing reference or something that never made the final cut remains unclear.

Yes. Clayface belongs to the DC Universe overseen by James Gunn and Peter Safran, the shared setting of Superman, Supergirl and Peacemaker. Despite Reeves’s involvement as a producer, it is separate from the world of Robert Pattinson’s The Batman and Colin Farrell’s The Penguin.

Clayface has already appeared in the DCU’s animated series Creature Commandos, voiced by Alan Tudyk. And Gunn has made clear that there are further plans for the character. Speaking to Cinemex at the film’s premiere, he said: “This isn’t the last we’ll see of Clayface.”

Where he turns up next has not been announced. Man of Tomorrow, Gunn’s follow-up to Superman, is scheduled for July 9 next year, but there is no confirmed Clayface appearance. Nor has a role been announced for him in The Brave and the Bold, the planned DCU Batman film. For now, we know he will return, but not where or when.

While it’s not always given the recognition it deserves, space has fast become a critical infrastructure layer, underpinning nearly all systems that enable modern life as we know it. Therefore, it’s critical that a safe and clean operating landscape is maintained.

Yet this is becoming increasingly challenging, particularly given that, alongside the rapid increase in space traffic, there are also more than 1.2 million pieces of debris over 1 centimeter (0.4 inches) cm in Earth’s orbit, each of which could damage satellites and spacecraft and potentially cause disastrous consequences. With the number of active satellites and debris objects in space continuing to increase, the space environment is under growing pressure.

Space was once the sole domain of nation states, but this is no longer the case. As technology has advanced, launch costs have decreased, and satellites have become more efficient, smaller and cheaper to build. These changes have democratized space, resulting in a massive increase in space traffic, not least constellations in low Earth orbit (LEO) that comprise thousands of satellites.

Plans are underway to further expand these constellations, and new and even bigger constellations are also expected to launch in the coming years. Added to that, we’re also likely to see the first space-based data centers within the next few years, which may well mark the start of the wholesale shift of data centers from Earth to space, which will further add to the congestion already being felt.

To ensure that space continues to meet our current and future needs, this continued expansion must happen in a sustainable way. Maintaining a safe and clean landscape is more important than ever before, and mitigating the impact of space debris is critical to that effort.

Mitigating the impact of debris involves a multipronged approach. Whether intentional or unintentional, the creation of new space debris by future space missions must be minimized as much as possible. This applies during launch, operation, and end of life phases, and satellites and other spacecraft need to be designed with this in mind. Additionally, satellites that are no longer active should not remain in orbit for extended periods, because this only increases the chance of collision events which would create new debris. The standard rule has been 25 years, but there are recommendations now that this should be lowered to five years.

Also critical is the prevention of in-orbit collisions between active satellites and other objects. This is only achievable through effective Space Situational Awareness (SSA) and Space Traffic Coordination (STC) systems. SSA maps out and characterizes objects in space (both satellites and debris), determining their location, trajectory and velocity, while STC actively manages the space traffic identified through SSA to prevent collisions. When a conjunction is probable, SSA/STC service providers alert the operators involved, who then decide whether to undertake a collision-avoidance maneuver.

Extending the operational life of satellites is another important aspect of debris mitigation because it reduces the need for so many new launches, thereby reducing the likelihood of more debris being created. Innovations such as electric propulsion systems that allow for greater maneuverability and extended mission durations are part of the solution, as is in-orbit servicing, maintenance and refuelling.

While these strategies focus on preventing new debris from being created, we also need effective methods for removing existing pieces of debris. This is a rapidly growing sector attracting much investment. Debris-removal technology being developed ranges from physical capture and tethered deorbit to destruction with ground and space-based laser ablation and plasma systems, as well as the emerging concept of mining debris to extract metals and gases for in-orbit processing to feed into the circular space economy.

The UN views space safety and space sustainability as urgent priorities. Its treaties and agreements focus on preserving the peaceful, safe, and sustainable use of outer space, preventing collisions, managing space debris, and ensuring responsible operations. Governments, space agencies and international organizations are also strengthening their focus on space sustainability.

ESA has launched its Zero Debris strategy, which is aimed at achieving a debris-neutral orbital environment by 2030. As part of this approach, ESA has updated its debris mitigation requirements and standards that will govern how its missions are designed, built, flown and disposed of. It also sets requirements for any company or institution that works with ESA on its missions.

In the U.K., the UK Space Agency is also taking a proactive role in space safety and sustainability through its Active Debris Removal (ADR) program and through the development of space sustainability standards in partnership with industry. It is also in the process of developing a space sustainability strategy to ensure a coordinated approach to sustainable practices in space.

On the other side of the pond, NASA’s Space Sustainability Strategy identifies five key challenges that are felt nationally but also within the wider space community. These include the absence of a single framework for space sustainability that has been accepted by the space community and the fact that space sustainability may be in tension with other mission interests. The strategy defines six goals that are intended to address each of the challenges alongside corresponding objectives to guide NASA’s achievements of the goals.

The U.S. is also building on the National Orbital Debris Mitigation Plan, which was released in 2022, with the Orbital Sustainability (ORBITS) Act. The Act, which has been introduced to the Senate but not yet been enacted into law, seeks to establish a program for the active remediation of orbital debris as well as requiring the development of uniform orbital debris standard practices. The Office of Space Commerce has also developed TraCSS (Traffic Coordination System for Space) to provide basic SSA data and services to civil and private space operators in support of spaceflight safety, although the system is currently in beta mode.

The International Standard Organisation (ISO) is also working on a standard for STC (Space Traffic Coordination) so that SSA activities can be standardized. Titled Space Systems — Space Traffic Coordination (STC), the standard is moving toward final publication, and its core focus is on establishing baseline protocols, data exchange formats and operational requirements for space safety, on-orbit conjunction assessment and collision avoidance.

While these strategies are certainly moving the industry in the right direction, there is a real need to act quickly. Success will rely not only on speed, but also on sustained and collaborative effort, as well as engagement from all space users. The decisions made and actions undertaken over the next decade will shape the space environment for generations to come.

As to whether sustainability becomes a core principle rather than an afterthought, that remains to be seen. There is, however, reason to be optimistic.

With artificial intelligence (AI), scientists have a powerful new tool for creating never-before-seen proteins, which can be used to make either groundbreaking new medicines or devastating bioweapons.

To help guard against nefarious uses of these proteins, scientists have recently figured out a way to "watermark" them without compromising their function. A team from Google DeepMind developed this technique to better track the provenance of AI-designed proteins.

"This has the potential to reduce biosecurity threats, such as unauthorized toxin creation, and may help with database curation, preventing artificial designs from cluttering the standard databases," said Roy Sleator, a biology professor at Munster Technological University in Ireland, who was not involved in the research.

"In practice, it could help trace a design's origin, flag engineered proteins for extra biosecurity checks, and reduce misleading claims about how a protein was made," Sleator told Live Science in an email.

However, in its current form, the same AI technologies that add watermarks can also strip them away, other experts warned.

The unveiling of the AI program AlphaFold in 2020 was a milestone in protein engineering. The tool, which won Demis Hassabis, John Jumper and David Baker the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2024, can reveal the 3D structure of a protein from the sequence of its building blocks, called amino acids. Proteins fold like origami, adopting 3D shapes that dictate their function.

However, AlphaFold's technology also opened the door to generating brand-new proteins that could be used to create biothreats, including viral proteins that can evade our immune systems and existing vaccines.

"Anything that can be used for good can be used for bad," said George Rose, professor emeritus of biophysics at Johns Hopkins University.

Current screening tools identify novel dangerous or restricted molecules by seeing how similar their amino acid sequences are to those of recognized threats. But the problem is that AI could come up with proteins that don't look like anything we've ever seen. That's what the new watermarking technique aims to solve.

The DeepMind team has framed the watermarking effort as a "family" of related techniques they call SynthIDBio. The first member of this family is SynthIDBio-sequence, which encodes watermarks into the amino acid sequences of proteins created by a program called ProteinMPNN.

The 3D shape of a protein is largely determined by its amino acid sequence, or the order of its building blocks. In cells, these blocks are assembled by ribosomes (pictured).

When users tell ProteinMPNN the desired 3D shape for their protein, it chooses amino acid options that ultimately fold into that shape. SynthIDBio-sequence subtly influences how the program chooses each amino acid according to a secret watermarking "key." Once the sequence is generated, anyone who knows the hidden code can assess how likely the sequence is to have been created under the key's bias. In their recent study, the DeepMind team showed that proteins built with this watermark could still bind to their targets as well as non-watermarked proteins.

A sister technique, called SynthIDBio-structure, instead embeds a watermark directly into the protein's structure. It does this by altering the function of AlphaFold 3, DeepMind's flagship protein prediction algorithm.

When AlphaFold predicts a structure, SynthIDBio-structure gives it a side mission: The AI must make minuscule tweaks to how the atoms in the protein are arranged. These tweaks can be picked up by a detector later, and they're small enough that they don't look like obvious distortion.

In a test, these two systems created hidden watermarks that could be detected with incredible accuracy, flagging more than 99% of watermarked proteins and correctly ignoring 99.9% of non-watermarked proteins.

Live Science spoke to several experts who agreed that SynthIDBio was an impressive technical feat. However, they said it wasn't ready for widespread implementation yet.

The two techniques are "effective but brittle," Bruce Wittmann, a principal applied scientist at Microsoft, told Live Science in an email. That's because, as the DeepMind team also notes in their paper, the watermarks can be easily removed.

I imagine it would be very difficult to ensure the regulations were being followed.

"For sequences, watermarks can be removed by 'resequencing' — effectively, altering the protein amino acid sequence such that the watermark is no longer detectable," Wittmann said. "For structures, watermarks can be removed by adding noise to or otherwise modifying the spatial coordinates of the structure." Screening tools wouldn't be able to detect if a watermark had been removed from a protein.

Experts are also skeptical that watermarking would provide a long-term solution for biosecurity.

An indelible watermark could identify who originally made a nefarious protein, or identify when a user was accessing a protein generation software without permission. This might deter some criminals, but terrorists looking to create a novel protein for a biological weapon would be less likely to care about being identified after a successful attack, said Max Brackmann, a chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear arms expert at the Spiez Laboratory in Switzerland.

Additionally, the vast majority of software for protein prediction and generation is open-source, meaning much of the code is freely available to use and modify. That makes it far harder to bake watermarking into these programs. Wittmann warned that the field would have to shift toward closed-source models before watermarks could become an industry standard.

He added that watermarking could act as one layer of a biosecurity defense shield, rather than offering standalone protection. Top-down regulation would be challenging to enforce. "I imagine it would be very difficult to ensure the regulations were being followed," he said.

Rose was even blunter about regulating the use of generative proteins.

"I'm convinced it won't work," he said. "Once there's a sweet technology that does something, government regulation to keep it under wraps has never been really successful."

The scale of the decline is particularly striking over the past five years. During that relatively short period, Switzerland has lost nearly 20% of its total glacier ice volume, illustrating just how quickly the country's mountain landscapes are being transformed.

These findings come from the annual report of the Swiss Academy of Sciences (SCNAT), which draws on long-term glacier observations collected by the Glacier Monitoring Network, known as GLAMOS. The monitoring program is based at ETH Zurich and the universities of Fribourg and Zurich in Switzerland.

Extreme Summer Heat and Low Snowfall Accelerate Ice Loss

A combination of unusually little snowfall and prolonged summer heat contributed to the extensive melting. The winter of 2025 to 2026 ranked among Switzerland's ten least snowy winters since records began, leaving glaciers with less snow to protect them from warmer temperatures.

Conditions became even more unfavorable between May and September 2026, when persistent heat and dry weather accelerated ice loss, even at the highest elevations.

The enormous amount of melting also temporarily helped ease Switzerland's summer water shortages. With the variety of edged weapons on the market today, we might tend to think of the tomahawk as an outdated relic that only belongs on the American frontier. Well, think again. Lightweight throwing axes predate Colonial America and they’re still being used today by military and law enforcement professionals and everyday outdoorsmen and women. Discover the ancient origins of this simple tool, and learn how it can still be a useful part of your outdoor kit.

The word “tomahawk” is based on a phrase from the Native American Algonquian language. This widely spoken tongue has been used by dozens of Native nations up and down the East Coast, and the language has given us many common English words we still use today (like moccasin and hickory). Some scholars credit Captain John Smith of the Jamestown settlement for first jotting down the Powhatan phrase “tamahaac,” though different versions and spellings of the word can be found throughout history. These names essentially translate to “a tool for cutting things off” and that’s just what a tomahawk does.

These one-handed axes have straight handles and small axe heads. Lightweight and versatile, they have been used (and still are used) as handheld tools and thrown weapons. Native people of the New World had been using stone axes for thousands of years prior to European contact, but when the sharper and more durable metal axes became available as trade goods in the 17th century, the popularity of these tools surged. Today’s tomahawks are sometimes made as replicas of these original iron trade items, though more modern construction techniques are also used. Some modern tomahawk heads are made from drop forged steel and heat treated for shock-resistance and durability, with synthetic handles replacing the traditional hardwood axe handles. Tomahawk enthusiasts today can choose the traditional styles and construction, or something more modern and updated.

When iron tools and implements first came to the New World, the Native people must have been amazed. Iron pots could be dropped on the ground without shattering like an earthenware pot, and iron axes were much sharper than stone axes (cutting down trees in a fraction of the time). Native Americans had been making small axes with straight wooden handles for centuries, but so had the Europeans (especially the French). The design of the tomahawk isn’t strictly Native American. The Frankish warriors of the early Middle Ages were known to carry lightweight throwing axes known as “franciscas,” which were used as close-range projectiles and also employed as hacking weapons during hand-to-hand fighting. A sixth century historian named Procopius once wrote of the francisca that: “…they are accustomed always to throw these axes at one signal in the first charge and shatter the shields of the enemy…” Axes like these continued to be popular into the late Middle Ages, being used by both lowly foot soldiers and knights.

Once traders and blacksmiths saw how excited First Nation people were about iron tomahawks, they started experimenting with new varieties and variations. Some tomahawks were simply adorned, while others were elaborately decorated. Some had decorative file work on the edges, and others had shapes punched through the blade (hearts were quite popular punched shapes, for some reason). But these all pale in comparison to the creativity of the pipe tomahawk. With a functional smoking bowl as a poll, and a hollow channel through the handle – the user could either smoke a pipe with their allies or chop the heads of their enemies. And occasionally, the pipe bowl was used as a weapon too. One story (which may or may not be embellished) recalls the famous frontiersman Simon Kenton (a younger contemporary of Daniel Boone) being struck in the head with the bowl side of a pipe tomahawk, smashing in a coin-sized disk of his skull and nearly killing him.

World War II Marine Corps veteran Peter LaGana is credited with bringing the tomahawk out of obscurity and back into military use at the end of the Vietnam War. In the latter half of the 1960s, he updated the age-old tomahawk design and sold roughly 4,000 tomahawks to selected units of the U.S. Armed Forces. LaGana’s tomahawk head featured a classic axe blade on one side, and a straight spike on the other. Similar to nautical “boarding axes” from Colonial days, the spike could be used for puncturing, prying, and breaching doors. As the war (and public opinion) soured, LaGana’s company closed in 1970, but not before the tomahawk had been revitalized and reintroduced to a new generation. Today, several companies still make “Vietnam tomahawks” for commercial sale.

Jump forward a few decades from the Vietnam War, and the “tactical” tomahawk has enjoyed something of a resurgence among U.S. soldiers in Iraq and Afghanistan. This heritage tool had obvious utility as a back-up weapon for hand-to-hand combat but it was also a useful tool in the field. The U.S. Army Stryker Brigade in Afghanistan, the 172nd Stryker Brigade Combat Team based at Grafenwöhr (Germany), the 3rd Brigade, and the 2nd Infantry Division out of Fort Lewis began carrying and using tomahawks as part of a rescue kit and as “modular entry tool.” And soldiers aren’t the only modern users.

Law enforcement agencies, particularly SWAT teams, have adopted tomahawks as a multi-use tool that is both lightweight and versatile. Favored models include prybar features for breaching doors and windows, rope cutting notches, and cutouts that allow it to be used as a wrench.

But here’s the foundational problem with tomahawks as a projectile weapon: Even if you are a great thrower, would you really want to throw your weapon away? Some fighters consider this strategy a bit like throwing your empty rifle at the bad guy, instead of reloading it. While axes like these have been thrown for ages, an ill placed strike may do little damage to an adversary. A miss won’t hurt them at all, and it may even hurt you (since you just tossed a weapon to them). Sure, historians have documented instances where tomahawks were thrown (such as the Frankish battle strategy of lobbing volleys of axes at enemy lines), but there are even more scenarios in which you might want to hang onto your weapon.

With all of that in mind, there’s no denying that axe and tomahawk throwing has been a pastime in America since the earliest recorded times. Not only is it popular among historical reenactors in North American, enthusiasts are seeking out hand-forged tomahawks from master craftsmen and competing in high stakes tomahawk throwing competitions. Things can get pretty serious, with high dollar ‘hawks being thrown for big money prizes.

But these aren’t the only modern people using this heritage tool. Tomahawks have been embraced by those interested in camping, bushcraft, and wilderness survival. Since many tomahawks weigh less than comparably-sized axes, they are easier to carry into the field for chopping and camp tasks. And while the axe head geometry doesn’t split wood as smoothly as other axe styles, that doesn’t stop the tomahawk from being chosen as a lightweight hatchet alternative. With a hammer poll on the ax head, the tomahawk can be just as useful in the woods today as it has ever been.

For people with asthma, switching from a gas stove to an electric one might be a breath of fresh air. The combustion that creates the flame of a gas stove or oven releases pollutants, including nitrogen dioxide, which can trigger asthma symptoms. After a research-backed electric stove replacement, people reported more symptom relief than is often achieved with asthma medications.

“We were really surprised at how large the benefit was,” says Ashwini Sehgal, a clinician who studies community health at Case Western Reserve University in Cleveland. With funding from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency during the Biden administration, Sehgal and colleagues spearheaded a project to install electric stoves in Cleveland households that had at least one person with asthma.

Eighty-five participants who had poorly controlled asthma were given a questionnaire a few months after the switch. They reported symptom improvement that was roughly two to three times as large as what’s typical with medication. Those with the worst symptoms saw the biggest improvement. Participants also reported fewer emergency room or hospital visits and said they missed work or school less often.

One 38-year-old woman told Sehgal’s team that whenever she cooked on her gas stove, she felt like her throat was closing. But the sensation disappeared after she switched to electric. Another participant, a 36-year-old man, found it difficult to walk uphill and play with his kids. With the stove swap, “he described how much better he felt as ‘magical,’ ” Sehgal says.

While heating, steaming or frying on a gas stovetop, nearby NO2 levels can skyrocket, reaching hundreds of parts per billion. That’s much higher than the EPA’s one-hour outdoor air quality standard of 100 parts per billion. NO2 exposure is linked to increased asthma symptoms. For example, research in children has found the pollutant can boost wheezing, coughing and asthma attacks.

The project was initially funded to follow 1,200 households for 12 months. But the Trump administration terminated the EPA’s Community Change Grants early, which drastically reduced the number of study participants. Yet even with this small group, the symptom improvement was significant.

The decision to replace a gas stove “depends on how much you are struggling with your own illness,” says environmental epidemiologist Carrie Breton of WashU Bursky School of Public Health in St. Louis. As a trial run, Sehgal suggests using an electric hot plate and a toaster oven to see if keeping the gas stove off helps improve symptoms.

If a stove swap isn’t in the budget, reducing NO2 exposure is still worthwhile, says pulmonologist Laura Paulin of Dartmouth Health. She recommends using kitchen ventilation, either via an exhaust hood that vents to the outside or an open window. A HEPA filter with a carbon insert can trap NO2 to decrease airborne concentrations. And try to keep children with asthma out of the kitchen when the stove is on, Paulin says. “It is a daunting task to replace a stove, but it doesn’t have to be an all-or-nothing thing.”

For researchers, the next step would be to measure lung function before and after a stove switch, Breton says. It would also be helpful to account for more factors, such as seasonal effects. “Many things can trigger people’s asthma,” Paulin says. Researchers could do a randomized trial in which some people get the electric stove right away while others wait for six months, Sehgal says, to allow time for comparison.

If electric stoves continue to be linked with fewer trips to the emergency room, fewer hospitalizations and better symptom control than medication can provide, “health insurance companies should consider paying for or subsidizing” stove swaps, Sehgal says. “If it truly works this well,” Breton says, “it’s got a bigger bang for your buck.”

#3744. "Getting people into space is one thing. Creating somewhere they actually want to live is quite another"

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For most of the space age, architects could fantasise freely about building off-world. Inflatable lunar villages and glass Martian domes could be sketched out without anyone having to worry too much about who cleaned the toilets.

That luxury is disappearing as we start to seriously consider the possibility of living in space. Next year, the first commercial space station will be launched – Haven-1 – and will be joined by a second, Axiom, a few months later.

NASA will return to the moon in 2028 with plans to start building a semi-permanent base. A nuclear reactor will follow by the end of the decade, as might the Chinese, and by the time the International Space Station (ISS) is retired in 2030, there will be tens of private stations available to replace it.

The elegant visions of 2001: A Space Odyssey are now giving way to the messy business of everyday existence

And that’s before we talk about Elon Musk and his ambition to create a city of a million people on Mars by the 2050s.

But getting people into space is one thing. Creating somewhere they might actually want to live is quite another.

That is where architecture comes in, and firms such as Foster + Partners, BIG, SOM (pictured top) and others have already turned their attention to what might be possible beyond Earth. The elegant visions of 2001: A Space Odyssey and the like are now giving way to the messy business of everyday existence and the problems of extraterrestrial construction.

The obvious challenges are formidable but well-rehearsed: how do you shield people from radiation? Where does the oxygen come from? How do you recycle water?

Engineers have mostly answered these questions. But once people are asked to live somewhere for years rather than just visit, other questions arise. Such as: who gets a window? It may sound trivial. It isn’t.

A window in space is both expensive and a potential point of failure. The engineers would prefer a solid metal tube.

Yet astronauts value their views of Earth enormously and “Earth-gazing” is a favourite way to unwind aboard the ISS. In fact, being able to see out is considered essential for morale.

So imagine the first lunar settlement. There are six bedrooms but only two windows. Who gets them? The commander? The tourist who pays the most? Does everybody receive 10 minutes of Earth-gazing a day?

History should certainly make us wary of who gets to dictate what extraterrestrial living looks like

Or, does the window belong to everyone, because something so precious cannot become private property?

The same applies to almost every spatial decision. Who gets a room with a door? How large is it? Can inhabitants personalise it? Where can somebody go to be alone? Where must they encounter other people? Who controls the thermostat, lighting or music?

In a lunar base containing 30 people these are potentially bones of contention. There has been at least one stabbing in the Antarctic, so the danger is real.

And that’s before we even decide who gets to run the place. Would you prefer a government body or a corporation like SpaceX or Blue Origin, which, after all, are manufacturing the rockets that are likely to haul people and materials to the moon and Mars?

History should certainly make us wary of who gets to dictate what extraterrestrial living looks like. Take Henry Ford’s Fordlandia, established in the Amazon in the 1920s to secure rubber.

That was conceived not simply as an industrial settlement, but as an attempt to export Ford’s version of American life: workers received company housing – segregated, of course – but had prescribed diets and had to follow strict behavioural rules. The American managers had running water and got the best views, while the Brazilians had to rely on wells.

The company town is an old model: housing, employment, shops and infrastructure controlled by the same organisation. On Earth, at least, you can leave. What does “leaving” mean when the exit is miles across the moon and stepping outside without permission might mean dying?

This matters because the economics of space point towards precisely this danger. The first extraterrestrial settlements will require immense investment. Governments will fund some, but corporations will provide most of the transport, communications and habitats.

Architects should be arguing about (and hopefully agreeing) extraterrestrial design standards before settlements exist

Commercial necessity can gradually become governance. The East India Company provides an extreme historical precedent: a trading firm that accumulated taxation, administrative and military powers as business slid into tyranny.

Space could easily witness a 21st-century version. Imagine a Martian settlement where one corporation supplies your employment, accommodation, oxygen, water, communications and return ticket. You are simultaneously worker, tenant and customer. Architecture could make that unequal relationship physical, as in Fordlandia.

None of this is an argument against commercial spaceflight. Without private capital, much of the extraordinary acceleration now taking place would be impossible.

Nor can architects wish away physics. A window genuinely is more dangerous than a wall.

But if we are to start producing extraterrestrial architecture, it must do more than ensure survival. A habitat that maintains oxygen while steadily degrading its inhabitants’ mental health has failed.

Circadian lighting, vegetation, privacy, views, places for leisure and for ritual are not decorations to be value-engineered away. They contribute to people’s ability to function and remain sane.

This is why architects should be arguing about (and hopefully agreeing) extraterrestrial design standards before settlements exist. I would argue the time for that is now.

The danger is not that our first extraterrestrial settlements will look insufficiently futuristic. It is that they will reproduce the worst terrestrial arrangements: workers’ barracks, gated communities, company towns and luxury enclaves – only this time with airlocks.

If we leave those questions until the rockets arrive, someone else will already have answered them for us

Conversely, architects rarely get to establish the principles of an entire built environment before it exists. Here we can.

We should use that opportunity to ask difficult questions now, while the answers are still cheap. Who owns the habitat? Who can modify it? What constitutes an acceptable home in space?

What must remain communal? What degree of privacy ensures dignity? And, yes, who gets a window?

If we leave those questions until the rockets arrive, someone else will already have answered them for us. You might not like who that person is.
