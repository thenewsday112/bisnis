#505. The Best Meta Smart A.I. Glasses, Ranked Based on Whether I’d Actually Be Caught Dead Wearing Them

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s about more than the cameras, speakers, and built-in artificial intelligence. I ranked nine frames based on style, wearability, and features that make them worth considering.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. Why Trust Us?

If you’re going to drop a few hundred dollars on a pair of smart glasses, they’d better look good. What’s the point of putting tech in your glasses if you don’t actually want to wear them? I wear prescription glasses, so I’m especially picky: I will automatically dismiss a set of frames if it doesn’t fit my face, and I’m drawn to designs that look trendy and make me feel like less of a nerd. I’m going to wear these every day. I need to like how they look, so that I can like how I look.

As they’ve grown in popularity, Meta has expanded its lineup of smart glasses, both from partner brands such as Ray-Ban and Oakley and its own line of Meta Glasses frames. From the classic Ray-Ban Wayfarers to retro-inspired frames and fashion-forward cat eyes, there are a lot of options to choose from, whether you want something more subtle or a pair that does a little more of the talking.

Meta’s now on its third generation of smart glasses. There are important tech differences among the three iterations of the tech, but that’s not what we’re here to talk about today. I’ve spent so much time looking at these glasses in all their different forms, and most of the time I’m fixated on what’s going on inside. But I also have some thoughts about how they look—because there are plenty of styles that I can (and do) actually wear, but also a few that would never leave my bag. These are my notes, of course, which means they reflect my taste and feelings about what glasses look good on me. Your mileage may vary.

The Scribers (which I reviewed) are my favorite because they look and feel like traditional glasses, rather than some big tech statement on my face. The round, shiny black frames feel a little more distinctive than the usual chunky tech specs, but aren’t so bold that they’d clash with any outfit. I’d wear them even if they weren’t smart glasses.

And they get extra points from me because they’re prescription-ready, so glasses-wearers can get them for everyday eyewear. They are Gen 2, not the new Gen 3, so you’re missing out on some features, but I think that’s fine for now. What’s important is that you don’t feel like you’re wearing a costume (or an iPhone) on your face.

Meta’s second-gen special edition collab with Kylie Jenner is perfect for me. Their slim, oval frames are almost identical to the sunglasses I already wear regularly. Though the stems are a bit chunky (like most Meta glasses), they have clean lines and come as close to passing for a standard, tech-free pair of sunglasses as I’ve seen so far. (That doesn’t mean they hide the tech: The cameras and indicator light are still clear. It’s more about the shape of the glasses and how they frame your face.)

Though you might assume they’re fashion-first, these glasses have all the tech you’ll find in any second-gen Meta Glasses, plus a little extra: The ability to make Kylie the voice of your AI assistant, which is a nice touch if you’re a fan. For me, though, I’m into these specifically for the look. It’s a style that goes with basically everything.

There’s a reason why the Ray-Ban Wayfarers have been considered a classic for decades. Their iconic shape is recognizable, versatile, and easy to style. While the Meta-equipped frame is a little chunkier than the standard Wayfarer, you don’t have to think too hard to make these look good.

I’d personally lean more toward the clear-lens version for year-round wear, although the sunglasses are a good choice for travel and outdoor adventures. These have open-ear audio and a 12-megapixel camera, but the Gen 3 model comes with a few upgrades, too, like an extra hour of battery life and a six-microphone array instead of five.

These upswept cat-eye glasses have much more character than many of the “standard” square Wayfarer-style shades. The gently lifted outer corners draw the eyes upward, creating a subtle lengthening effect that can flatter rounder faces, while the curved shape of the frames can also balance a more angular jawline.

The Zena doesn’t look overly bulky or oversized, and the cat-eye shape gives the glasses a fashion-forward appeal, rather than feeling like the frames were just built around the technology. These are a great choice if you want something bold. I’d pick these first if I were looking for bright colors like the Milky Pink Blush or Milky Gray Lilac. But they would also work great with a classic black or tortoiseshell pattern, if you prefer something more subdued for everyday wear.

Meta’s new, camera-free Ray-Ban smart glasses are based on another of the brand’s well-known designs, the Clubmaster, a browline-style frame with bold upper frames, slim lower rims, and square lenses, but this time with smart tech built inside. The Audio Clubmaster mostly embodies that classic design, though the browline and arms are extra thick to account for the added hardware.

The Clubmaster is an audio-focused pair of smart glasses, without a built-in camera. You still get open-ear speakers, microphones, voice controls, and Meta AI, making it a good option for listening to music, taking calls, interacting with an assistant, or if you’re just still not fully on board with the “creep glasses.” With these, you get the wearable tech without the implications of having a camera on your face.

The Meta Adventurer is effectively the tech giant’s own riff on the Wayfarer style, so just about everything I said about the Ray-Bans applies here as well... But also, they aren’t actual Wayfarers, which is why they’re near the bottom of the list.

These frames lean a little bit more rugged than the original Wayfarers, which meshes well with the mandatory bulk in the stems. Despite the name, are these going to completely revolutionize your hiking wardrobe? Probably not. But if you’re the type to spend your weekends on trails, exploring somewhere new.

And, practically speaking, the Adventurer is a great deal. you get the same camera, speakers, and microphones as most of the newer Ray-Ban Meta designs, but they cost $150 less than the Wayfarers.

The chunky, oversize Meta Fury kind of reminds me of the sunglasses that people wear over their glasses at the beach. The stems are thick; The lenses, oversized. If you’re looking for subtlety, you won’t find it here. These glasses make sense for folks with larger faces who want to wear bright, bold colors. (You may notice the Fury has some of the most out-there colorways.) But smaller features may get overwhelmed by their blockiness, which makes them a niche choice.

These aren’t for me, honestly, but I can totally see this being someone’s go-to pair. In some ways, these actually feel more like aviators than the actual Ray-Ban aviators.

Ray-Ban’s aviators are, like the Wayfarers, a timeless staple. The smart version is among Meta’s newest additions, though, and they don’t look exactly like the original. They’re reportedly lighter and thinner than other smart Ray-Bans (I haven’t tested them myself, so take that with a grain of salt), but the frames are very chunky. Without the wire rims that made the style famous in the first place, they don’t look a whole lot like their namesake. Despite that, they have a fun retro aesthetic that feels surprisingly understated, especially in neutral colors.

Given that, don’t expect them to fit the same way as a pair of aviators. They won’t hang off your nose in quite the same way. And, like the Meta Fury, their oversize lenses and a pronounced bridge make them more fitting for larger faces. I’d pay attention to the sizing and definitely try them on before buying.

I can definitely see the appeal of the Oakley Meta Vanguard for runners, hikers, snowboarders, and cyclists who want to document their adventures without fumbling for their phone and also need the benefits of the high-contrast Prizm lenses, which make details sharper, colors more vibrant, and filter light to reduce eye strain when in outdoor environments.

The wraparound shield is very sporty, with a secure fit and open-ear audio, and there’s even Garmin integration for real-time workout stats, so these are made for more than just snapping pics. Some people like to wear these kinds of glasses all the time, but to me, you look silly wearing them if you aren’t on a jog, on a hike, or playing sports. And that’s why they’re near the bottom. With this style, your smart glasses are more of a task-oriented tool than a fashion statement.

Like I said, I ranked these based on my taste and my face. In general, finding glasses that flatter your features comes down to a mix of shape, proportion, and personal style. Frames that contrast with your features can create a nice balance, so think angular designs for rounder faces, and round designs for angular faces. I’d pay attention to scale, too. Bigger frames can overpower smaller faces, while narrower styles can get lost on wider ones. But honestly, these are just guidelines and not strict rules to follow. If you love how a pair looks on you, that’s what’s most important.

So before you buy a pair of smart glasses (or any glasses), it’s usually a good idea to try them on in person. You can use the Ray-Ban store locator to find your nearest retailer that carries the glasses, or go to a Best Buy with a Best Buy Meta Lab station. Both offer hands-on demos of Meta’s A.I. glasses, interactive displays, and personalized fittings with help from specialists.

#817. The McRib Is Returning To McDonald’s Again—Despite Its 2022 Farewell Tour

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

The McRib is back. Again. And at this point, I’m not sure why McDonald’s keeps pretending it’s leaving.

McDonald’s is officially bringing back its beloved (and equally controversial) McRib sandwich for a limited time beginning October 27. But before you head to your nearest Golden Arches, there’s a catch: The sandwich won’t be returning nationwide.

According to McDonald’s, the McRib will be available at select restaurants in cities including Los Angeles, Chicago, Miami, Atlanta, Philadelphia, and Dallas, with additional locations potentially joining the lineup. Customers can check the McDonald’s app to see whether their local restaurant is participating.

For anyone unfamiliar with the sandwich that’s managed to inspire this much devotion, the McRib features a seasoned, boneless pork patty shaped to resemble a rack of ribs, covered in tangy barbecue sauce and topped with slivered onions and dill pickles on a toasted bun. It’s sweet, savory, and saucy, and it's become one of the chain’s most recognizable limited-time offerings since its 1981 debut.

Of course, we need to talk about the McRib’s rather interesting relationship with retirement.

Back in 2022, McDonald’s announced that the sandwich was embarking on a “Farewell Tour,” giving customers what the chain suggested could be their last chance to order one. There was even commemorative merchandise to mark the occasion. Except the goodbye didn’t exactly stick.

The McRib returned to select restaurants in 2023, made another appearance in 2024, and came back yet again in 2025. Now, with its fourth consecutive post-announcement appearance approaching, I’m starting to think McDonald’s and the McRib have very different definitions of the word farewell.

Unsurprisingly, fans are already having some strong reactions to the latest return.

“I need this more than I need air,” one Instagram commenter wrote. Another fan, who said they had recently eaten a McRib, wrote, “It was so damn delicious…and I always ask for extra onions and extra sauce!”

One person who had never tried the sandwich seemed genuinely curious about the hype, asking, “I’ve never had it but I know people go crazy for it. Is it THAT good?”

Another commenter responded to someone asking about the sandwich’s taste with, “Absolutely Delicious 🤤 IMO 🔥🔥🔥.”

And naturally, the sandwich’s repeated returns haven’t gone unnoticed. One commenter questioned what happened to the supposedly final McRib comeback from a few years ago, pointing out the rather obvious contradiction.

Still, love it or hate it, the McRib clearly knows how to get people talking. Between its devoted fans, its critics, and its apparent inability to stay retired, few fast-food sandwiches have managed to turn their return into this much of an event.

McDonald’s hasn’t announced how long the McRib will stick around this time, so if you’re hoping to grab one, check your local restaurant’s availability. And maybe don’t take the next farewell tour too seriously.

#822. Walmart Is Facing A Lawsuit For Overcharging Its Customers

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

The suit claims Great Value shrimp bags include ice in the net weight.

Walmart has faced its fair share of lawsuits since the start of 2026, and another has been added to the list.

In January, the retailer was accused of participating in a price-fixing scheme with PepsiCo; in February, it was accused of overcharging on meat; and in June, it was alleged that Walmart was among many brands selling baby food contaminated with toxic heavy metals. A proposed class action lawsuit filed earlier this month now alleges that the chain is deceiving customers and violating state consumer laws with its Great Value product packaging.

According to the lawsuit, Walmart’s Great Value frozen shrimp products contain less shrimp than the packaging advertises. The suit claims that the bags are often underweight, sometimes offering 11% less shrimp than the label indicates.

Many shrimp products are coated with an ice glaze to protect them from freezer burn; however, FDA guidelines say the weight of the frozen water cannot be included in the net weight of the actual product. The suit argues that Walmart deceives customers by charging them for the weight of the ice as well as the shrimp.

According to the lawsuit, once the shrimp is thawed and drained, it does not weigh the same as the packaging claims. The lawsuit seeks damages, restitution, and injunctive relief, or an official legal order, requiring Walmart to stop mislabeling its Great Value frozen shrimp; however, it has not yet named a specific dollar amount.

Since filing, Walmart has 30 calendar days to submit a formal response or 60 days to formalize a waiver-of-service agreement.

#2337. Unusual Foliage Plants for Shady Containers

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Shady containers don’t have to rely on flowers for color and interest. Foliage plants can bring unexpected colors, textures, and growth habits to the mix, creating combinations that are anything but ordinary. These four favorites have earned a place in my containers, and as a bonus, they can also thrive indoors, extending their appeal well beyond the growing season.

When I first spotted blue star fern (Phlebodium aureum, Zones 9–11) in a holiday display, I just had to track it down. Initially, each emerging blade (the leafy part of the frond) has a powder-blue interior with a faint margin of glowing lime and then matures to a handsome steel blue. Each blade is about 2 feet long by a foot wide and is held on a thin, wiry stipe (the stalk holding the blade).

This upright form makes it an easy companion as a container plant, with plenty of space for flowering or trailing plants at the base. Another selling point is this fern’s tolerance for a variety of shade levels, holding its color well even in deep shade. Like the more familiar rabbit’s-foot fern (Davallia fejeensis, Zones 10–12), blue star fern has thick, fuzzy rhizomes, which lend vigor and drought tolerance and are easy to divide. And you will definitely want more!

If you’ve ever visited the Pacific Northwest, you’re probably familiar with the adorable native piggyback plant (Tolmiea menziesii, Zones 6–9), which carpets soil, rocks, and stumps in moist forests. The slightly rippled and fuzzy apple green leaves look like mini coral bells (Heuchera cvs., Zones 4–9) leaves.

A vigorous grower, piggyback plant earns its name from the baby “plantlets” that form where the leaf blade meets the petiole. The plantlets are easy to pluck off and root in new containers, so each plant is a gift that keeps on giving. In a container, piggyback plant is somewhere between mounding and trailing and looks especially great dangling from hanging baskets. The foliage also has a pleasant cucumber smell when handled, so I always enjoy working with this plant.

Leopard plant (Farfugium japonicum, Zones 7–10) first grabbed my attention in a container at the magical public garden Chanticleer in Wayne, Pennsylvania. This striking beauty has all the characteristics of a catwalk model—long limbs and high gloss from the shoulders up.

Each succulent 12- to 18-inch-long petiole bears a round, dinner-plate-size leaf that is so shiny it almost looks fake—in a good way!

A closer look reveals leopard plant’s kinship with the familiar shade perennial, ligularia (Ligularia cvs., Zones 4–8), but leopard plant is not reliably winter hardy north of Zone 7. Both prefer soil that remains consistently moist. In a container, leopard plant looks great with just a skirt of trailing plants around the base so the plant’s full architecture can be appreciated.

Is it bad to admit I’m not really a begonia lover? Between problems with rotting off, too much or too little sun, or getting too leggy, they typically don’t top my list. Not so with the effortlessly adaptable ‘Sinbad’ begonia (Begonia ‘Sinbad’, Zones 10–11), which has proven to be a hardworking contributor in a container, with stunning unusual foliage.

This begonia is a hybrid between a sturdy cane-type begonia and two-petal begonia (Begonia dipetala, Zones 10–12), with silvery, spotted foliage inherited from its parentage. Though the plant also has nice, soft-pink flowers, the foliage is the main attraction.

The silvery-speckled leaves have color notes including soft bronze, pink, and powder blue, all highlighted by darker green veins. This intriguing color scheme lends itself to imaginative combinations; my favorite container recipe this year contrasts the silvery blues of ‘Sinbad’ begonia and blue star fern with the peach and rust tones of ‘Sweet Tea’ foamy bells (Heucherella ‘Sweet Tea’, Zones 4–9) and ‘Campfire’ coleus (Coleus scutellarioides ‘Campfire’, Zones 10–11).

#1407. Survey: Organizations Report Positive ROI on AI Investments, Yet Data Readiness and Oversight Lag Behind

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

According to a survey by data and AI services provider Further, enterprises are reporting benefits from artificial intelligence even as their data preparation and oversight processes remain incomplete.

The company's recent State of Enterprise AI Maturity research surveyed 515 U.S.-based AI decision-makers. Just 5% believed their organizations' data was largely ready for AI.

The findings suggest that gaining value from individual AI applications and preparing to operate them more broadly are separate challenges. Reported returns alone do not establish that an organization has the foundations to sustain or expand those results.

Further found that 37% of respondents' organizations had paused, scaled back, or abandoned an underperforming AI initiative in the previous 12 months. Among that group, data quality or availability was the most frequently cited main reason, at 24%.

Meanwhile, 29% reported completing a formal data audit and remediation plan. The research also placed 69% of organizations in the exploring, experimenting, or implementing stages of adoption.

Further distinguishes data prepared for conventional reporting from data prepared for AI. Its explanation emphasizes data origins and documentation, along with access controls that support ongoing evaluation and governance.

For readers assessing their own readiness, the practical issue is whether data supports the requirements of a particular application. A collection suitable for a dashboard may need additional preparation before it can reliably support an AI system.

Seventy-seven percent reported a positive return on AI investment. However, only 3% said their organizations had a documented AI-audit process.

The findings measure different things: perceived financial benefit and the existence of a repeatable oversight process. Neither substitutes for the other.

"In reality, confidence without a documented process is exposure," said Kierra Dotson, associate director of AI strategy and governance at Further. "Closing that gap doesn't take new technology; it requires an auditable governance process."

#829. Apple Butter Snack Cake

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Recipe by Margaret Knoebel, Milwaukee, Wisconsin

Made with apples and apple butter, this easy fall cake is just the thing when you need a sweet snack.

I know I’ve fallen into the autumnal magic of a fall farmer’s market when I walk away with too many apples, a jar of apple butter and no real plan for either. Luckily, our recipe tester Maggie Knoebel figured out how to combine both spoils into one super easy bake: an apple butter snack cake, complete with a brown butter glaze and cinnamon-sugar topping.

Preheat the oven to 350°F. In a large bowl, use a hand mixer or stand mixer to beat the eggs, sugar, apple butter, canola oil and vanilla extract.

In a separate bowl, whisk together the all-purpose flour, cinnamon, baking soda and salt. Add the flour mixture to the egg mixture and stir just until no streaks of flour remain. Fold in the apples and pecans.

Pour the batter into a greased 13×9-inch baking dish. Bake the cake until a toothpick inserted in the center comes out clean, 50 to 55 minutes. Cool the cake on a wire rack to room temperature.

Meanwhile, in a small, heavy saucepan, melt the butter over medium heat. Cook the butter until the milk solids are golden brown, stirring constantly, about 5 to 7 minutes. Immediately pour the brown butter into a mixing bowl to stop the cooking process or the butter will burn. Let the brown butter cool to room temperature.

Editor’s Tip: At some point during cooking, the butter will start to foam up. Don’t panic, it’s completely normal! Continue to stir while cooking, making sure to keep an eye on the butter’s color so you know when to take it off the heat.

Use a hand mixer or stand mixer to beat in the confectioners’ sugar until it’s fully combined with the brown butter. Spread the brown butter over the cake, then sprinkle the cinnamon-sugar mixture over the top before serving, if desired. Enjoy!

Store your apple butter snack cake in an airtight container in the fridge for up to four days. You really want to keep it well covered, so the glaze doesn’t absorb lingering fridge smells. If you want to keep the cake in the pan, cover the pan with storage wrap.

Yes, you can freeze apple butter snack cake. If you’re freezing the cake unfrosted, allow it to cool completely to room temperature, then cover the cake in storage wrap followed by aluminum foil. To freeze the glazed cake, place it in the freezer, uncovered, until the glaze is hard, about 30 minutes. Cover the cake with storage wrap and aluminum foil. Freeze the cake for up to two months and thaw overnight in the fridge.

Yes, you can bake an apple butter snack cake in a different-sized pan. Instead of a 13×9-inch baking dish, divide the batter evenly between two 8-inch square or two 9-inch cake pans. The baking time and temperature should be the same.

Yes, you can absolutely make this apple butter snack cake gluten- and/or dairy-free! Replace the all-purpose flour with a good gluten-free flour blend—one that’s designed as a cup-for-cup (or measure-for-measure) replacement. The cake is naturally dairy-free, but you’ll need to replace the butter in the glaze with vegan butter. You won’t be able to brown the vegan butter, so take 2 tablespoons off the measurement. That’s about how much liquid evaporates from butter when it’s browned.

#1616. Louisiana’s public colleges face $4B deferred maintenance backlog

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The Louisiana Board of Regents plans to request over $700 million in state funding in fiscal 2028 for more than 70 capital projects.

The regents are seeking the most money for the Louisiana State University system, for which regents are requesting $279.8 million for 23 ongoing projects and one new one in fiscal 2028.

Across the state’s four systems and 33 public institutions, 30% of facilities were between 25 and 50 years old. Another 30% were over 50 years old, putting them at higher risk of maintenance issues or even failures, according to an analysis prepared by Gordian for Louisiana’s board of regents.

The state’s public colleges aren’t alone in their deferred maintenance backlog. Nationwide, colleges’ deferred capital projects reached $156 per gross square foot in 2025. #4045. The Brain Anchors Others’ Experiences to Our Own Mental Maps

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers at the Netherlands Institute for Neuroscience’s Social Brain Lab have discovered how the brain converts watching another individual’s emotional experience into long-term spatial memories. By recording single-neuron activity in the hippocampus of observing rats, the team found that place cells combine spatial location with observed emotional distress in others. During subsequent rest, these neural patterns replay to consolidate the memory, providing a neurobiological mechanism for observational learning and offering clues into vicarious trauma and PTSD.

Source: Netherlands Institute for Neuroscience

A vast proportion of what social species know about the hazards of the world is acquired second-hand. A child watches a parent recoil from a hot stove and learns caution; a pedestrian sees another person slip on an icy sidewalk and chooses an alternate path; a soldier witnesses a comrade injured by an explosive and approaches that roadway with heightened alertness.

Observational learning allows individuals to adopt adaptive, life-saving avoidance behaviors without enduring physical harm. However, while neuroscientists have long understood that witnessing another’s distress triggers an empathetic emotional response, the neural mechanism that binds that empathy to a specific physical location on an internal spatial map has remained elusive.

Now, a study led by Frédéric Michon Linde at the Social Brain Lab, directed by Valeria Gazzola and Christian Keysers at the Netherlands Institute for Neuroscience, reveals how the mammalian brain converts the observed experiences of others into enduring, location-tagged memories.

To investigate the single-neuron dynamics underlying observational learning, the researchers monitored neural activity in rodents as they observed another animal undergo salient emotional experiences within a shared environment.

The team focused on the hippocampus, the brain’s master coordinator of episodic memory and spatial navigation. Within this structure reside “place cells”, specialized neurons that fire when an individual occupies a particular coordinate in physical space.

The recordings revealed a dual function: these place cells fired specifically when the observing animal was in a designated location and simultaneously witnessing another individual experience an emotionally salient event there. The hippocampal network essentially tagged the spatial coordinate with a biological label of social danger.

“If you see someone slip and fall at a particular street-corner in the winter, you learn to avoid that corner. We already knew that seeing someone else in pain triggers a feeling of pain in the observer, but in humans we couldn’t understand what in the brain transformed this empathic sharing into a spatial map with a label ‘danger’ in a particular location,” explains Christian Keysers. “For that you need to know what individual neurons do. We need animal studies for that.”

“For me, that is one of the most important findings,” adds Frédéric Michon Linde. “In a brain structure that is central to space and memory, we find neurons that combine where we are with what we perceive happening to another individual.”

Memories do not persist as static snapshots; the brain must actively stabilize new representations through offline processing.

During the rest period immediately following the observation, the researchers recorded spontaneous neural reactivation in the observer’s hippocampus. The exact ensemble patterns that fired during the observational event replayed preferentially, driving memory consolidation.

When the observer rats were later reintroduced to the environment, they exhibited marked behavioral wariness, confirming that vicarious learning had taken place, alongside significantly more stable, sharp hippocampal representations of the space.

Under healthy physiological conditions, anchoring an observed danger to a mental map is an evolutionary advantage. However, when the link between another’s distress and internal spatial maps becomes excessively strong or fails to update after a threat resolves, the same circuit may drive pathology.

“An important question, which we have not tested here, is whether related mechanisms might become exaggerated in conditions such as PTSD — for example, if a memory of danger becomes too persistent or spreads to places that are actually safe and results in persistent anxiety,” Michon Linde notes.

Witnessing severe trauma happen to others is a recognized clinical diagnostic trigger for post-traumatic stress disorder. By demonstrating how observed distress is physically integrated into hippocampal place maps and reinforced during sleep-state replay, the study provides a cellular baseline for investigating how vicarious trauma generalizes across safe environments.

The Social Brain Lab is now tracing the upstream pathways that deliver emotional sensory information from others into the hippocampus, aiming to map the complete route from social perception to permanent spatial memory.

The intermediate hippocampus integrates shock-observation and spatial information during observational fear memory in male rats

Learning where danger lurks by observing others requires linking another individual’s distress to one’s own representation of space. How the hippocampus implements this mapping is unknown. We recorded Neuropixels activity across the dorsal, intermediate and ventral hippocampus of male rats during contextual fear learning by observation, subsequent rest and recall.

Observers showed increased immobility in the context where they had witnessed a conspecific receive footshocks, with substantial inter-individual variability. Pyramidal neurons were recruited by shock observation, with enriched shock-excited cells in the intermediate hippocampus.

In the dorsal and intermediate hippocampus, shock-observation responses scaled with baseline place-field firing, revealing a gain-like conjunctive code between observed distress and observer’s position.

Following learning, shock-context spatial representations were selectively stabilized in the intermediate hippocampus, and post-learning sharp-wave ripples preferentially reactivated shock-context ensembles, especially in animals expressing contextual freezing.

These findings identify candidate hippocampal mechanisms by which socially acquired threat information are mapped onto the observer’s spatial representation and consolidated into memory.

#2229. How Does Google Maps Know There’s Traffic Before You Hit It?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A map app can spot a traffic jam before drivers reach it. Here’s how phones detect slowdowns, predict delays, and sometimes get it wrong.

Most drivers know this experience. They’re en route somewhere when their smartphone map app starts pushing back their ETA. Soon, brake lights appear ahead as traffic slows to a crawl.

But how did the navigation app know about the traffic before the driver encountered it? Popular navigation apps like Google Maps, Apple Maps, and Waze use real-time location data from drivers’ phones to detect slowdowns and estimate traffic conditions.

Learn what’s happening and why the estimates aren’t always right.

Map apps combine location data from many phones to understand traffic conditions on the road. Google explains that it uses combined location data for this, and Waze says drivers share real-time information just by driving with the app open. When enough drivers slow down in the same area, the app can show a backup on the map.

Location information plays a big role in this process, although apps also take steps to protect users’ privacy. Apple Maps’s privacy statement reads, “Use of your location is required for most Maps features to function optimally. Because your location can give away your identity, we convert precise locations to less-exact locations within 24 hours. When you use Maps for navigation, we convert precise locations from the beginning of your route to a less exact area.”

Apps also use past traffic information to predict delays before drivers encounter them. “To predict what traffic will look like in the near future, Google Maps analyzes historical traffic patterns for roads over time,” Google’s wevsite states.

These predictions help apps estimate arrival times and suggest alternate routes when traffic starts building.

“Our predictive traffic models are also a key part of how Google Maps determines driving routes,” the Google website continues. “If we predict that traffic is likely to become heavy in one direction, we’ll automatically find you a lower-traffic alternative. We also look at a number of other factors, like road quality. Is the road paved or unpaved, or covered in gravel, dirt or mud?”

Drivers can also report problems such as accidents and hazards through Google Maps, Apple Maps, and Waze, though the options vary by app and region. These reports help explain why traffic has slowed down. Google says it also uses data from local governments, which can flag road restrictions such as construction.

The colored lines on the map show where traffic is building. In most navigational systems, green means no traffic delays, orange means medium traffic, and red means delays, with darker red indicating slower speeds.

Traffic updates are generally useful, but they aren’t always accurate. Apple, for example, says it displays incident markers only when it has a high level of confidence in the reports it receives, so a new incident may take time to appear. Coverage also varies depending on how much information the apps receive. On less-traveled roads, fewer drivers may contribute location data, making it harder for apps to detect slowdowns in real time.

Arrival times are estimates, too. Google says its predictions combine historical patterns with live conditions, but it also notes that historical patterns aren’t always reliable indicators of what the road will look like. Conditions can also change unexpectedly because of events like snowstorms.

Map apps can help drivers avoid delays, but their estimates aren’t guaranteed. Drivers still need to follow road signs and pay attention to the conditions around them.
