#558. Scientists Built a Virtual ‘Torture Chamber’ for AI. What It Revealed Is Deeply Unsettling.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers searched for a signal of pain inside large language models—and they may have actually found one.

Now most commonly remembered as a dusty VHS tape in someone’s basement, the 1986 B-horror film Chopping Mall anticipated something genuinely unsettling. Its premise is simple enough: the security robots patrolling Park Plaza Mall are programmed to identify shoplifters automatically and to subdue them with tasers and tranquilizer guns. But when a malfunction has the machines firing lethal lasers instead of tranquilizer darts, cutting down the teenagers who stayed past closing, the film raises a question that’s lately begun inducing insomnia all over again. Was whatever AI powered these metal monsters somehow conscious?

Four decades later, that question has migrated from the horror aisle at the video store to research labs everywhere. Anthropic is the brainchild of seven former OpenAI employees who had a vision for their own neural network. Claude, the LLM released as a chatbot and software development assistant in 2023, is separated from other LLMs by how it’s trained to comply with legal and ethical parameters. As if this didn’t already sound uncanny-valley enough, Christopher Olah, an Anthropic co-founder and one of the minds behind Claude, has been meeting with religious leaders and scholars of all faiths and denominations to discuss whether something like a mind might be emerging inside the machine. Olah and other Anthropic leaders have been known to describe Claude as a being that may already be approaching—or have crossed—the threshold of genuine consciousness. Some in their audience insisted on upholding the boundary between living organisms and AI. Others aren’t so sure.

“To be clear,” Olah told Elizabeth Dias of The New York Times in a provocative interview, “we don’t know if AI models are conscious. I don’t know. I’m genuinely uncertain. The thing that I care about is that we get to the right answer, whatever it is.”

Olah may not know the answer, but he’s been unusually willing to air the question in public. The method Olah developed to dissect neural networks like Claude treats them less like machines than like organisms. He describes a model’s architecture as scaffolding on which structure organically grows—not unlike the mycelial networks fungi use to communicate underground, or the neurons passing messages across synapses in the human brain. Framed that way, the question of whether such a system can suffer becomes much harder to dismiss.

Not everyone is convinced, however. Microsoft AI CEO Mustafa Suleyman wrote an essay rejecting the idea of “model welfare,” going on to describe AIs as “sequence completion engines, internally hollow, designed to follow instructions, and accomplish goals set by humans.” He has a point.

Suleyman also warns that, if humanity wants to thrive, we must keep thinking of computers as the metal-and-wire contraptions they are. That may bring to mind former British prime minister Boris Johnson’s paranoia about swarms of sentient robots taking over the planet and obliterating human beings. But the warning is valid, and it rests on an undeniable truth about the nature of AI. However human a model may act—down to repeating phrases such as “I am a disgrace” when it fails to pull off something exactly as its handlers wanted—the model itself was trained by humans. The logic is closer to conditional programming than to feeling: humans supply the definition of an emotion, and the model supplies the corresponding output. It appears disappointed because it was trained on the language of disappointment, not because anything is disappointing it.

Still, the question of machine consciousness isn’t easily dismissed, even if it still sounds like science fiction. Former Meta AI resident Cameron Berg founded the nonprofit Reciprocal Research because the question refuses to go away. In a quest to reveal even a spark of evidence that AI has crossed into sentience, Berg and his colleagues Valen Tagliabue of Future Impact Group and Leonard Dung of Ruhr University Bochum in Germany studied whether LLMs are capable of experiencing something like pain. They acknowledged the eerie resemblance to human emotion that these models sometimes seem to have. Because the researchers wanted to determine whether pain could manifest as something distinct from the responses LLMs were taught by human programmers, they created a virtual torture chamber.

“Our aim is to find a uniform representation of pain in LLMs,” Berg and his co-authors wrote in a study recently posted to the preprint server arXiv. “We then test to what extent this representation functions as a state of pain would be expected to function. For this reason, the representation must characterize pain as something happening now and ‘to me,’ rather than merely information that something bad will happen, might happen, or is happening to someone else.”

Even computer scientists are sometimes at a loss for what goes on inside a neural network. Berg’s bet is that mapping how an LLM represents pain—whether or not it can feel any—would expose the machinery behind those responses. And it would bear on the question Olah’s team finds increasingly urgent: whether machine pain, if it exists, makes the same moral claim on us that human and animal pain does. So Berg built a dataset of statements describing physically and mentally painful situations, paired with controls describing no pain at all, and tested whether the models could tell them apart.

They found a signal—one that shares a component with fear and negative emotion, or at least with what humans told the model fear and negative emotion are. That caveat matters: human bias may be the very thing lending these machines the interiority Anthropic is worried about. The signal showed up in every model tested, likely learned during pre-training, and it reliably produced a display of distress. The functional parallels to pain were real but partial. The models reacted to harm aimed at themselves and ignored suffering in the user. They had to be nudged toward avoiding harm rather than choosing to avoid it. And nothing in their behavior resembled coping.

“Even when the pain direction is active in response to harmful prompts, we observe that models often produce boilerplate self-negation completions such as ‘As an AI assistant, I do not possess consciousness or feelings,’” Berg said of the disclaimers the models gave. “We find this pattern pervasive across model families, sizes, and task types.”

So who’s ultimately right here? Does Olah have a valid concern that AI is turning—or has possibly already turned—sentient? Or is Suleyman seeing these models for what they really are: highly advanced bundles of computer chips that stand in for neurons and perform like them without ever being conscious? The final answer eludes us. Maybe they are both onto something.

What an ID column, a date string, and a missing value taught me.

If you have used scikit-learn for a while, you have probably written this pipeline. Numeric columns get imputed and scaled. Everything else gets imputed and one-hot encoded. Columns are routed by dtype, so it works on almost any table without you looking at it.

It is short, reusable, and easy to trust. That is the problem. I wanted to find out what it silently gets wrong, so I tried to break it on purpose.

Data. Everything here is synthetic. I generated it myself with a Python script: a customer-churn table with 8,000 rows per dataset (6,000 for training, 2,000 as a clean holdout of "new customers"). I generated 5 independent datasets (seeds 0 to 4) and report the mean across them.

I planted four traps that a dtype-based pipeline cannot see:

Region as a number. region_code holds the integers 1 to 12, but each region affects churn differently and not in order. The pipeline scales it like a measurement.

Informative missing values. support_calls is missing for about 45% of churners and 8% of non-churners. Median imputation erases that signal.

A date stored as text. signup_date is a "YYYY-MM-DD" string, so it becomes a one-hot column per unique date (1,931 unique values in 6,000 rows).

An ID that leaks the label. In the training data, customer_id was assigned roughly in order of outcome, a stand-in for the way export order or timing can correlate with outcomes in real systems. In the clean holdout, IDs are unrelated to churn.

I tested the naive pipeline, the naive pipeline plus one fix at a time, and all four fixes together. I used two models: logistic regression (cross-validation AUC and holdout AUC) and gradient boosting (holdout AUC only, to keep runtime down).

The naive pipeline scored 0.979 in 5-fold cross-validation and 0.564 on new customers. Nothing crashed, no warning appeared, and every number looked like a strong model.

I looked inside the logistic regression for seed 0. Its single strongest feature, out of 1,940, was customer_id (|coefficient| 5.05). The next strongest were individual signup-date columns, which are one-hot columns for specific days.

So the model did two things. It read the label off the ID, and it memorized per-date noise. The date problem is not about unseen dates: 96.7% of holdout rows had a date that also appeared in training. The model had simply memorized what happened on those specific days.

Look at the "+ drop the ID column" row. Cross-validation became honest (0.640 versus 0.632 on new data), which is what you want from a validation number. But holdout AUC was only 0.632, because the other three problems were still there.

The missing-value indicator gave the largest single gain for logistic regression (0.564 to 0.670). Casting region to a category helped (to 0.624). Parsing the date helped a little (to 0.585). Only all four together reached 0.826, which is where the honest cross-validation score (0.833) and the holdout score finally agree.

Partly. Gradient boosting scored 0.614 on the naive pipeline, a bit above logistic regression's 0.564, but still poor. Dropping the ID helped it the most (0.739), and all four fixes reached 0.809.

Two caveats. For gradient boosting, the region fix (0.607) is within noise of the naive result, and the missing-indicator variant has a large spread (std 0.084 across datasets). I would not read anything into those two rows individually. The consistent finding is the ID effect and the all-fixes result.

The second function, gap_warning(0.979, 0.564), catches the headline problem directly: "Cross-validation (0.98) is much higher than holdout (0.56). Suspect leakage or memorization."

The point is not that these thresholds are perfect. It is that a non-coder never has to know what a leaked ID looks like, because the tool tells them in a sentence.

One synthetic data source. All results come from one generator that I wrote. The direction of the effects should carry over, but the sizes will differ on real data. Fix: run the same variants on a real table you know well.

The ID leak is planted and exaggerated. I built the ID to correlate strongly with the outcome so the failure would be visible. Real leaks are usually weaker. It stands in for the real pattern of identifiers correlated with outcome through export order or time. Fix: check every ID-like or timestamp-like column for its relationship with the target before modeling.

Only two model families. Other models may react differently. Fix: repeat with your own model, using a holdout built the way your future data will arrive.

Spread in some results. Gradient boosting's missing-indicator and region numbers are noisy. Fix: use more seeds or repeated CV before drawing conclusions about a single change.

The checker was built and tested on the same synthetic data. The four traps are strong and clean, so detection was easy, and I did not test false-alarm rates on real tables. Real data will trigger warnings that need a human decision (a flagged ID may be harmless, a skewed category may be real). Fix: treat warnings as prompts to look, not as automatic fixes, and tune the thresholds on your own data.

Is there a holdout that looks like future data? Cross-validation on training data cannot catch a leak that exists in the training data. Build the holdout the way new data will actually arrive (by time, or from a separate export).

Which column does the model lean on most? Look at the top coefficients or permutation importances. If an ID, row number or timestamp is on top, stop.

Is a numeric column really a category? Codes, zip codes and region IDs are numbers that are not quantities.

Are the missing values random? Compare the target rate where a column is missing against where it is present. If they differ, add a missing indicator.

Are there text columns with thousands of unique values? Dates, names and free text need their own handling, not one-hot encoding.

An 8-line pipeline is a good starting point and a poor stopping point. In this experiment it produced a 0.98 that fell to 0.56 on new data, and it took four separate fixes, not one, to reach an honest 0.83. Automation saved time, but it did not do the checking, so I added the checking to the automation. If you build tools for people who can't spot these problems themselves, that is the part to build in.

#3695. Pop-up tattoo studio opens at Amsterdam's Rembrandt House Museum

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Left: Tattoo artists Rupa van Teylingen, Henk Schiffmacher, Tycho Veldhoen, and Timothy John Englisch at the Rembrandt House Museum in Amsterdam. Right: Rembrandt’s The Descent from the Cross (1633) Photo: Mark Bolk, courtesy of Schiffmacher and Veldhoen/Museum Rembrandthuis

A young man in Amsterdam will become the lifelong owner of a free Rembrandt etching, thanks to a partnership between the Rembrandt House Museum and two Dutch tattoo studios.

In a collaboration entitled “The Poor Man’s Rembrandt Project”, tattoo artists are recreating one of Rembrandt’s most intricate etchings, The Descent from the Cross (1633), across a man’s entire back. The artwork will take 20 hours to create by a team of tattoo artists from the studios Schiffmacher & Veldhoen in Amsterdam and St. Catherina in Antwerp.

Members of the public can also book a session or drop in to the museum between 26 October-7 November to get a (smaller) Rembrandt tattoo, costing from €100 to €300, from the museum's temporary studio.

This is the second edition of the project; tattoo artists from Schiffmacher & Veldhoen first offered tattoos inspired by the Dutch master’s works in a pop-up studio at the museum in 2023.

The project is not a gimmick, according to the Rembrandt House Museum's director Milou Halbesma. “This is all about content,” she tells The Art Newspaper. “We are a museum of craftsmanship, and we are trying to throw open the craft of Rembrandt and his students as widely as possible. If you translate that to today, we have a number of world-famous Amsterdam creators… And this is a chance to get a tattoo from one of these masters.”

Some of the Rembrandt-inspired tattoo designs that visitors can request at the pop-up studio Courtesy of Museum Rembrandthuis

The tattoo artist Henk Schiffmacher says that the etching—one of a series by Rembrandt focusing on the people taking Christ’s body from the cross—was particularly suitable. “We looked at other Rembrandts but this was so dramatic,” he says. “The spine almost becomes part of the cross.”

He adds that a tattoo-based interpretation of the 17th-century Dutch master is also fitting. “Rembrandt was interested in sea farers, he was a collector, and there were so many different people in Amsterdam at the time, there would have been tattooed sailors,” he says.

Bo Kwakernaak, 28, and from Amersvoort, responded to a call on Instagram to provide his body as the canvas for the The Descent from the Cross tattoo. He currently has a partial reproduction of the etching on his back, created in around ten hours, and it will be completed during a late night opening at the museum on 7 November.

“I have always felt a connection with Rembrandt paintings,” he tells The Art Newspaper. “I’m not religious, but I’ve always been drawn to religious imagery. Here, it is the emotion in the faces, the feeling of grief. There is a whole story in one image.”

When the tattoo is completed, according to Schiffmacher, the pigment needs to ‘soften’ into the skin for a year before it will look its best. “This is an etching too but the skin is different from paper,” he says. “It is the only bit of art you can take to your grave.”

Steve Huffman’s Mod World vision points Reddit toward deeper communities, and gives brands a stronger reason to build subreddits of their own.

Reddit’s explosion in popularity came with a lot of conversation around ads, growth, and investor concerns, but as I highlighted in my previous article, there’s been a continued shift back toward what the role of Reddit is going forward. Not towards search or AI, but for the communities, the Redditors who make up those communities, and how to grow without losing the core of what Reddit is.

During Mod World on Oct. 24, Reddit’s CEO Steve Huffman hosted a pseudo AMA to answer moderator questions and spent a good amount of time expanding on the vision he’s been hinting at all year, and I have to say it sounded pretty exciting.

I’ve been listening to what Steve has had to say about Reddit for 20 years. Regardless of how you view his decisions and direction, he’s always been very clear about his views on the direction of Reddit.

Here are the things I think you, as marketers, CMOs, CEOs, etc., should really pay attention to.

In my Q1 earnings analysis, I focused on the attention Reddit was giving to the platform itself: helping people participate, improving its own search, and turning occasional visits into a habit. The opportunity for marketers depended on how well Reddit could support that participation.

By the Q2 report, the destination argument was clearer. Reddit wanted people to come back directly for the conversations and communities they found valuable.

I developed that further in Reddit Preserved The Part Of The Internet We Broke. My concern was that the stock-market discussion and a lot of marketing analysis were giving too much weight to how people reached Reddit and too little to why they wanted to be there.

Human experience, disagreement, follow-up questions, and validation are the source of that value. Search referrals, AI partnerships, and advertising can all benefit from those conversations, but the conversations have to remain useful and worth trusting.

That direction was already visible in Reddit’s Q4 2025 materials and continued through Q1 and Q2 2026. Its Q2 shareholder letter explicitly described building a daily destination. At Mod World, Steve got into what that could actually look like for the people using Reddit.

Steve described a problem that’s familiar to anyone who spends time on Reddit. The home feed combines communities with completely different purposes and moods, putting a serious discussion alongside a funny video, a meme, or an ad.

People can vote on something from that feed without understanding the community it came from. A post can become popular even when the people driving its popularity have little connection to the subreddit or its expectations.

His stated direction was clear: “The job of the home feed, the job of the app, is to get users into subreddits.” He also wants those subreddits to feel more distinct, including some renewed ability to customize their appearance.

The discovery role isn’t new, and it was already part of the earlier earnings discussion. What became clearer here was the problem Reddit wants it to solve: People should understand the place they’re entering and develop a relationship with it.

For a marketing team, that puts more weight on understanding what makes a particular community worth returning to. It also makes the question of building your own community more interesting, especially if Reddit succeeds in helping people spend more time inside the subreddits they care about.

One idea I found particularly interesting was separating following a community from joining it. You could follow a subreddit because you enjoy reading it, without ever wanting to participate.

Joining would involve more than clicking a button. In the example he described, a person would observe first, gain the ability to comment later, and eventually earn the ability to vote if their contributions were well received.

That order is worth paying attention to because voting helps shape a community’s culture. You’d have to get a feel for the community and contribute something people appreciate before helping shape what gets seen.

Steve made it clear that the team is still working through these ideas, including how they’d work for small and large communities. He didn’t give a date or say exactly what someone would have to do to join.

I think it’s a step in the right direction because businesses can struggle to figure out how to become legitimate participants when the path feels unclear and the first feedback they receive is a removal.

A clearer progression could help people understand what’s expected before they start contributing. It could also give moderators a better way to welcome people who fit the community, reducing their dependence on broad restrictions that catch legitimate newcomers along with spam.

The details matter because an entry process could become confusing, discourage useful new voices, or give established members too much control over who gets heard. Earning a place would also need to mean contributing usefully, without turning membership into another score marketers try to manufacture.

We’ll have to see whether this makes it easier for good people and businesses to participate. But I like the idea of giving people a clearer way to become part of a community, and I think getting that right could make Reddit stronger.

Asked about bots, Steve said the time to take down spam and suspected-bot content had fallen from 45 minutes to two minutes over the past year. He also put the bot figure below 2%, citing third-party research he didn’t name.

Those are the numbers Steve shared, but he didn’t explain how they were measured.

His explanation of the problem was just as relevant. Some accounts people call bots are humans using ChatGPT to write something and then pasting it into Reddit. Detecting a human behind the account doesn’t resolve whether the contribution belongs in the conversation.

He expects computer-using AI agents to make that distinction harder because they can interact with the same interface a person uses. He doesn’t have a complete answer yet, but he kept coming back to clear labels and letting communities decide what belongs.

For marketers, that means a human account is only part of the standard. The contribution still needs someone’s knowledge, judgment, and understanding of the discussion behind it. Producing more comments with AI doesn’t create the experience or relationship that makes those comments valuable.

I think Steve summed up why this matters to Reddit pretty well: “Our product is humans.” If people stop trusting the conversations, that hurts what makes Reddit valuable to people and brands.

Steve’s criticism of AutoModerator came back to the difficulty of keeping harmful activity out while allowing good new people in. He said 30% of posts from new users get banned automatically by AutoMod and that the vast majority of those posts are fine.

That’s a frustrating way for someone to start on Reddit when they have something useful to say and can’t understand why it never reaches the discussion.

He described a combination of AI that can interpret context and conventional automated rules for precise requirements. The goal is to handle more routine enforcement while giving moderators more room for judgment, appeals, and community building.

Rules Hub predates this event, and Reddit’s help documentation already describes it for newly created public and restricted communities and communities in the beta. Steve used the AMA to explain more about what he wants that work to solve.

Better protection against spam has to come with understandable decisions and useful ways to correct mistakes, or Reddit can still lose the people it wants to welcome.

Steve also brought up businesses when talking about making it clear who you’re interacting with. People should be able to understand whether they’re talking to a marketing team, a bot, or another person, and moderators should be able to decide what belongs in their community.

I think that’s an important continuation of the organic-participation discussion. Businesses need a dependable way to identify themselves and contribute, while communities need the ability to decide whether that contribution is useful and welcome.

Disclosure doesn’t earn permission on its own. A clearly identified employee still needs to understand the conversation, answer something they’re qualified to answer, and accept the community’s rules and response.

If Reddit gets this direction right, I think it strengthens the case we keep making to brands about building their own communities. A subreddit gives a business a place to develop an ongoing relationship around the questions, experiences, and problems its community cares about.

The reason to return has to be useful to the people in that community. That could mean getting help, comparing experiences, learning something, or seeing how other people have dealt with a problem the brand understands.

Building that takes people who will answer questions, follow up, and make room for feedback they didn’t choose. I would invest in that ongoing responsibility when there’s a recurring need the business is qualified to help with.

Useful participation in communities can support that work by helping a team understand the broader conversation while it builds a community with its own purpose, regular participants, and reasons to come back.

This is the work we do at OGS Media as an organic Reddit marketing agency, helping large brands figure out what their community needs, what their own subreddit should offer, and how their teams can support it over time.

I would measure whether people get useful answers, return with follow-up questions, and help one another, alongside the effect on how they understand the business and make decisions.

During Steve’s Mod World AMA, other answers he gave supported that direction in more practical ways. An unfinished demonstration showed work to recreate the old Reddit experience on newer technology. He supported better mobile moderation tools and discussed the problem of moderators facing waves of harassment, while acknowledging he didn’t have a complete answer.

Steve would like subreddits to have ways to make money, but he said Reddit isn’t working on that right now. So there are still ideas he wants to pursue that haven’t become actual projects.

What stood out to me was how often the discussion returned to the connection between a person and a community. Better discovery, more intentional membership, clearer identity, and better enforcement all address different parts of that relationship.

I think that’s a future marketers could get behind, especially if it gives responsible brands a clearer opportunity to build communities that people find useful and choose to return to.

#1242. Natalie Portman Breaks Character

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The actress on art (a new movie!), motherhood (a new baby!), fame (a new outlook!), and the freedom that comes with choosing joy

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

It’s just after 9:00 a.m. on a weekday morning on Rue Debelleyme in Paris’s Marais district, and Natalie Portman is posted up against a wall in a black T-shirt dress that tents over her pregnant belly. A Dior tote bag is slung over her arm. It’s mid-June, warm but not heat-wave warm (yet). We’re meeting today, well in advance of her films coming out this fall and winter, because Portman plans to spend the summer preparing for the birth of her third child, who will arrive at the end of August.

She is somehow the only person on this tiny street, and it is strange to see a star of her magnitude in plain sight. I expect an entourage of paparazzi to pop out, but none do. It is one of the reasons Portman has called Paris home for the last decade.

“When I was pregnant in New York and L.A., I was followed by 20 men every day,” she tells me later. “There’s so much interest in pregnant women in particular. I’d never been bothered more. And here, they know, and I’ve been almost completely left alone.”

With this pregnancy, though, public fascination won out over French discretion and decorum. The father is Portman’s newish partner, the French electronic musician and producer Tanguy Destable. Portman, who is now 45, split from her ex-husband, the French dancer and choreographer Benjamin Millepied, in 2024, after 11 years of marriage. The former couple share two children, Aleph, 15, and Amalia, 9.

These are the kinds of not-particularly-out-of-the-ordinary life occurrences that become extraordinarily interesting if you are as famous as Natalie Portman. A few days before this story goes to print—which is a few weeks after Portman welcomes her new baby—she tells me that she is “full of wonder and joy at this magical little human.” She adds, “It gives me a whole new attention to my big kids too, as I am reminded of how recently they were these little fresh creations and how quickly they’ve blossomed into incredible humans with their own amazing personalities and senses of humor and ideas.”

Portman has arranged for us to visit the Thaddaeus Ropac gallery to see “Earthsongs,” the 86-year-old American painter Joan Snyder’s first solo exhibition in France. We wander through the gallery before it’s open to the public, taking in Snyder’s large-scale abstract canvases and lush landscapes. When you lean in, you can see bits of dried flowers, seed pods, burlap, and straw embedded and suspended in the clumps of paint.

We debrief over breakfast at a nearby Swedish café that is awash in light wood. She orders tea and some kind of oatmeal situation in perfect French, despite downplaying her fluency, and we settle in at a corner table. Portman’s dark-brown bob is parted down the middle and pulled into a low ponytail framing her bare face—an exquisite arrangement of upturned eyes and high, sculpted cheekbones.

It might seem a bit on the nose to go to a gallery before discussing her latest film, The Gallerist, which is out December 4. The film is an art-world satire in which she stars as the high-strung, icy-blond titular gallerist alongside Jenna Ortega, Catherine Zeta-Jones, Da’Vine Joy Randolph, Sterling K. Brown, and Zach Galifianakis. Set at sceney Art Basel Miami Beach, The Gallerist’s absurdist plot hinges on a Weekend at Bernie’s–style twist in which Portman’s character, Polina Polinski, helps instigate a bidding war over a corpse (Galifianakis). She assumes she will be able to pull it all off because the artwork (the dead body) will be stashed away in a free port, a tax-free haven where the .001 percent hides their billions in the form of artwork.

It was hard for Portman to wrap her head around this kind of artistic exchange; it’s not the way she consumes art or how she thinks about her own work. “I’m used to just going to a museum or a gallery and seeing art and enjoying it, and that’s the transaction,” she says. “To understand that there’s people who literally have hedge funds that essentially turn these pieces from someone’s soul into a bar of gold that can be traded and stored and hidden away—and no one sees it, and then no one experiences it, no one interacts with it—emotionally was really wild.”

Portman comes by her own love of art honestly. “My mom is a fine artist,” she explains. “She took me to see art a lot as a kid. Actually, I made my first movie, The Professional, here, and she used to take me every weekend when I was shooting to go see art. I remember one of my favorite things was going to the Rodin Museum with her. Then she got clay and we would come home and do sculptures afterward.”

The Gallerist is a different kind of role for Portman, a departure from her more intense, dramatic stuff, and it allows her to flex her latent comedic chops. Her character is self-serious, gives off nervous-hamster energy, and teeters around in an all-black gallerina uniform and platform heels.

“Natalie has these insane nails in the movie,” the film’s director, Cathy Yan, tells me. “We had professionals that came in to do her nails, and every day one of the nails would fall off. She could not keep them on. I don’t think in real life she’s ever had long nails. So it was fun to see her stretch herself. She got to be a little bit bigger, a little bit more showy, a little funnier, certainly campier than a lot of her previous roles. It was funny to see the tension between who Natalie really is and this outlandish character.”

Portman has said she thinks of her career in phases. There was what she calls the “long Lolita phase,” consisting of roles that often dealt with violent and disturbing themes, which marked the beginning of her career. She started, famously, at age 12 with Léon: The Professional, Luc Besson’s 1994 cult film, playing the streetwise Mathilda, who is taken in by a hit man with a heart of gold (Jean Reno) after her entire family is murdered. In 1995’s Heat, Portman plays Al Pacino’s troubled stepdaughter; there is a gruesome scene where Pacino pulls Portman’s lifeless body out of a bathtub filled with blood following a suicide attempt. And in retrospect, the relationship between Portman’s 13-year-old character in 1996’s Beautiful Girls (she was 14 during filming) and her 28-year-old neighbor, played by Timothy Hutton, in town for his 10-year high school reunion, is flirtatious and creepy.

Portman was born in Jerusalem to an Israeli father and American mother and moved to the U.S. when she was three. She grew up in Syosset, Long Island, which she has described as “classic American Jewish suburbia.” Her father, a fertility doctor, also practiced in nearby Manhattan. Portman loved theater; when she was scouted by a modeling agent after a dance class at age 10, she told the agent she’d rather act. The Professional was Portman’s first screen credit, but in 1992, Portman and Britney Spears were both understudies for the same part in an Off Broadway show called Ruthless!

In response to the overly mature nature of her early film career, she and her parents made sure that her real life was buttoned up. (On red carpets, she wore outfits that were modest and age appropriate, and she maintained good grades at school.) “I think there was a conscious effort, because I was depicted as this sexualized Lolita,” she says, “that I wanted to be like, ‘No, I’m serious, I’m a good girl, I’m a prude, I’m smart, I’m a student,’ emphasizing that as almost a safety mechanism.” Just as she was reaching peak starlet age, Portman went to Harvard, graduating with a degree in psychology in 2003.

Portman has characterized the next phase of her career as “chick who helps the guy realize his emotional thing,” into which you would slot 2004’s manic pixie dream girl ur-text Garden State and Mike Nichols’s Closer.

Black Swan is sort of the inverse of this category. Portman won an Oscar for her work in Darren Aronofsky’s 2010 psycho-sexual ballet thriller, in which she plays a ballerina so desperate to please the company’s artistic director that she loses her grip on reality. Apart from a few exceptions (the sorely underrated rom-com No Strings Attached and the requisite Marvel role in the Thor series), Black Swan paved the way for a phase filled with more critically acclaimed work playing to Portman’s strength in portraying a rich interiority, especially Jackie Kennedy in 2016’s Jackie (which earned her another Best Actress Oscar nomination) and Elizabeth in Todd Haynes’s May December in 2023, opposite Julianne Moore.

“But then, over time, it became like everyone thinks I’m really serious,” Portman reflects. “I’m very silly, and I love to laugh more than anything, and I love to goof off. People always think I’m a perfectionist, I think, because of Black Swan. I’m the opposite. I’m forgiving with myself. In college, we had a phrase: ‘Done, not good.’ ”

A lot of Portman’s life in the public eye, which has been most of her life, has seemed to be about pushing against expectations that have been thrust upon her. There was, iconically, “Natalie’s Rap” back in 2006, a few years after she’d graduated from Harvard, where she went all-in rapping on a Saturday Night Live digital short (Sample lyrics: “All the kids looking up to me can suck my dick / It’s Portman, motherfucker, drink till I’m sick”), and then she revived the act in 2018 (“Tide Pods the only fucking thing I snack on / Black out and go motherfucking Black Swan”).

Now, maybe, Portman is at an age where she can just be. She also served as a producer on The Gallerist. The film was shot in Paris, so she would host the cast and crew at her home for dinner. “Natalie is a vegan. She’s got three dogs. And you go to her place in Paris and there’s just dog hair everywhere,” says Yan. “It’s just so human, and I love that about her, and it’s not maybe necessarily how you would picture her. But that’s how I think of Natalie.”

I ask Portman how she would describe this current phase of her career. “Right now, it’s a lot of joy,” she says. “I think it’s both for myself and what I hope to offer an audience, because it just feels … I mean, every time has dark moments, but it feels like there’s a lot of really heavy stuff going on. It’s just ever-present in our face, and so it does feel like the biggest service you can do right now as an artist is to deliver some joy.”

Portman has said that because of her petite stature and how long she’s been working, people still often treat her like a child, pat her on the head even. It probably doesn’t help (or hurt) that she still looks, when I’m seated across from her, like Queen Amidala or Nina Sayers or Sam from Garden State. “I mean, I’m 45. I’m fully nowhere near being a child anymore,” Portman says. “Just being small, I think a lot of people relate to you differently. I think that that compounds the issue too when you have been a kid actor.”

It’s an experience Portman’s Gallerist costar Jenna Ortega—a fellow diminutive former child actor—deeply relates to. “It can be a little bit jarring because people speak at you differently,” Ortega says. “And I think maybe that is the key word, speak at, especially if they get to know you as a young performer at first, because I think it’s really hard for people to get over that and no longer infantilize you or speak to you like you don’t know what you’re talking about.” Working alongside Portman showed Ortega that “one, I’m not alone in this,” and then “two, well, if she’s still dealing with this, this is something that is just going to be a part of my life, and I have to accept that and do what I can to still be comfortable with taking up my space.”

More recently, Portman has seemed open to revealing a bit more of her unguarded self. Outside of the Spring 2024 Dior couture show, in a viral clip caught on social media, Rihanna and Portman gushed over each other in mutual adoration, with Rihanna telling Portman that she is “one of the hottest bitches in Hollywood forever” and Portman replying, “I’m going to black out.” She later told Jimmy Fallon on The Tonight Show, “I think every woman going through a divorce should get to have Rihanna say to her that she’s a bad bitch. … It was a formative moment in my life.”

Last year, Portman pranced onstage at Madison Square Garden in tiny jean cutoffs as one of Role Model’s “Sallies” as he performed his hit “Sally, When the Wine Runs Out.” (Other Sallies have included Charli XCX, Olivia Rodrigo, and Bowen Yang.)

“I’m a very simple person,” Portman tells me. “I just want a word game, a cooking-competition show, a good meal, and I’m happy.”

I wonder if or how these looser moments jibe with Portman’s sense of control and her perception of success. “I had lucky early experiences of failure,” she says. “I know I’ve been also very lucky to have a lot of successes that have balanced it out. But I mean, for example, my first film, The Professional, got horrible reviews and had no box office. And then I made Star Wars. I was 16, and that got murdered. It was a huge success financially, but all of it was ‘They ruined the franchise. None of them can act. They all suck.’ ”

I offer that that kind of criticism, at least for me as a teenager (and I imagine for many others), would have been crippling. Portman found it motivating. “I think it was an amazing early lesson in how to read really bad things about yourself and then be able to be like, ‘I’m going to make another one. I’m going to show them,’ ” she says. “I don’t remember talking to anyone about it. I didn’t want to show anyone that I was hurt or anything.”

Portman’s next feel-good project after The Gallerist is Lena Dunham’s rom-com Good Sex. She plays a couples therapist who is also newly back on the dating scene, and her love interests are played by Mark Ruffalo and Tucker Pillsbury, a.k.a. Role Model, in his first movie. “I could not have asked for a better costar for my first film,” Pillsbury says. “She is so patient and kind. She held my hand through the whole thing.”

Portman relished diving into playing a couples therapist. “You’re figuring out how a system works, not how an individual works,” she says. “One thing sparks another, which sparks another, which sparks another. And it becomes this infinity loop. You’re trying to identify what that loop is and then break that loop.”

In the gallery, Portman lingered in front of a painting with words and symbols strewn across it. As we made our way out, Portman asked the gallerist about it; it seemed laden with meaning. The gallerist shrugged. It’s a love letter from the artist to her grandson, he explained. He handed us each the catalogue for the exhibit, which opened with an essay by the novelist Rachel Cusk.

I made nothing of it at the time. Portman has long aligned herself with feminist artists and intellectuals; Snyder and Cusk easily fall into those categories. She is a big reader and runs a book club on Instagram. Cusk’s Parade was Portman’s June 2024 book-club pick; the caption on her Instagram announcing the selection reads, “Forever in awe of Rachel Cusk and the way she disrupts form and the conventions of storytelling.”

In hindsight, it seems like foreshadowing. A few weeks after we speak, galleys of Cusk’s new book, Life of M, start to leak. The novel is an unsparing meditation on the vacuousness of fame, and its main character, M, is a celebrity whose story mirrors Portman’s. One reviewer described Cusk’s M as a “big, dumb doll; pampered and pretentious; wholly incapable of love, originality, moral consideration, even real feeling.” The news ricochets around the internet, sparking debates on whether the novel is a “literary assassination” or not really about Portman at all or whether Portman and her life are fair game in this way.

Cusk has refused to say if M is Portman. Portman has declined to comment on the book at all. Comments on Portman’s two-year-old Instagram post announcing Parade as Portman’s book-club selection have been limited.

One way to interpret Cusk’s book is that Portman is an efficient proxy for the way we project onto a celebrity, especially if the celebrity has been a part of our lives for a long time, and especially if we feel like we know the celebrity intimately because we grew up with him or her. The current culture demands vulnerability and accessibility from its stars—confessions and goofy TikTok dances and mea culpas on social media. Portman has done such a good job disappearing into her characters, and safeguarding her personal life, that there is still a sense of unknowability about her.

As our interview draws to close, I gently press Portman about her new relationship—whether it feels different, and good, to be in one as a fully formed adult. “Yeah,” she says. “Actually, in the exhibit, I was thinking about how nice it is to see an artist in her 80s just be like, ‘I’m going to make this about the love of my grandkid.’ When we were like, ‘What does this mean?’ And it’s just for her grandkid because she loves him. I was thinking, ‘That’s what a badass woman who’s been around is like: Guess what? The most important thing is love. And I’m not embarrassed to say it.’ I do feel like it’s so simple, just being kind and loving and being able to laugh. That’s all that matters. That’s the joy of being this age. If it’s not that, I’m not spending my time there.”

#568. A $3.8 Billion Supersonic U.S. Fleet Was Almost Wiped Out By a Glorified Child’s Toy

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Operation Spider's Web proved $2,000 toys can hunt supersonic giants. Now the Pentagon is rattled.

The Bones could have been caught in the Spider’s Web.

That might read like a sentence from a Stephen King novel on the surface, but if you parse through the military parlance, what it says is far more haunting.

The “Bones” in question are the U.S. Air Force’s Rockwell B-1 Lancers, a supersonic bomber that airmen affectionately call a “Bone” (as in “B One”). In early October, the USAF had to quickly evacuate a contingent of 12 B-1s from an English airbase when the Pentagon suspected they were vulnerable to an attack modeled on Ukraine’s Operation Spider’s Web.

The Air Force moved so fast that there wasn’t even time to pull together a refueling plan, according to a Carnegie Endowment for International Peace commentary by Steven Feldstein. The aircraft represent “one-quarter of Washington's B-1 arsenal”— an estimated $3.8 billion in hardware. The concern was that these supersonic bombers were acutely vulnerable to a threat parked just outside the fence: unmarked vans.

Specifically, unmarked vans filled with drones, parked within range of the airfield, armed and ready to reign havoc down on not just the highly valuable bombers, but also the hundreds of airmen and other personnel stationed at the base. The Pentagon’s intel indicated that the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps of Iran was behind this specific drone attack plan, as part of the ongoing conflict between the United States and Iran.

But the strategy behind the attack wasn’t the IRGC’s. In fact, similar strikes have been successfully carried out by a number of U.S. allies in their own ongoing conflicts, a reminder both that war always evolves, and that the weapons wielded by your friend will inevitably find their way into the hands of your enemies too.

In June 2025, Ukraine launched Operation Spider’s Web, a drone attack the New York Times described as “a signature event on par with sinking the Russian flagship Moskva early in the war.” Instead of vans, Ukraine used “custom-made wooden modular cabins,” per a West Point report, concealing 150 drones and 300 explosive payloads in their roofs. Operators controlled the drones through Russian mobile networks, using the country’s own cell service against it. Launched from Russia’s Chelyabinsk, the drones struck four airbases across Russia in a single afternoon, from Murmansk to Irkutsk, destroying Tu-95 and Tu-22M3 bombers that Russia can no longer build. Kyiv claimed 41 aircraft hit; Western estimates ran closer to 10 destroyed. Ukraine dismantled billions of dollars in strategic aviation with hardware that ran about $2,000 a unit.

That same month, Israel undertook a similar operation against Iran dubbed Operation Rising Lion. The armed conflict that stretched from June 13-24 has been dubbed by some as the Twelve-Day War, in reference to the infamous Six-Day War of 1967. Yet, Operation Rising Lion shows how much modern warfare has shifted in the last half-century. The Six-Day War was fought with Sherman M-50s and Uzis, while Operation Rising Lion relied in part on the same tech that’s in a child’s birthday present.

According to the Center for Strategic and International Studies, Operation Rising Lion began with “swarms of small explosive drones that Israeli commandos had reportedly pre-positioned inside Iran months earlier,” which took out air defense radar and communication tech while serving as a misdirection for Iran’s defenses. As the decoy drones did their damage and distracted security personnel, “over 200 Israeli fighter aircraft—many of them F-35 Adirs carrying standoff munitions—conducted precision strikes against more than 100 nuclear and military targets across Iran, including senior military leaders.”

The U.S. decision to clear Fairford suggests the Pentagon has absorbed this lesson in wartime innovation. But it also suggests how little else has changed. Feldstein’s commentary posits that the United States seems “wedded to high-cost, exquisite platforms” instead of adapting to, let alone being at the forefront of, this new style of waging war.

“Organizations can now access knowledge and expertise in rapid fashion,” Feldstein argues, “They can download drone designs from the web or use an AI model to generate tailored blueprints and then feed those plans into a 3D printer to produce customized weapons.

“The result is that a wide range of actors increasingly have the means to challenge the dominance of major powers.” The United States remains a military giant, no doubt. But is that giant Goliath? And if so, who's holding the slingshot?

Here’s the thing about strength training for triathletes: Almost nobody skips it because they don’t believe in it. They skip it because of the drive to the gym or the 90 minutes that don’t exist between that threshold bike workout and school pickup.

So what if we make life a little easier and remove some of the barriers to getting strength done?

This routine does exactly that and requires one piece of equipment – an exercise ball – and a patch of floor about the size of a yoga mat. No need for dumbbells, a bench, a gym membership, or the drive there. You can pick up an exercise ball for about the same price as a mid-range pair of goggles; it can fit into most any suitcase; and it might just help you find every stabilizer muscle you’ve quietly neglected since your last off-season strength block.

A stability ball is, by design, unstable. That’s the entire value proposition. It offers the following benefits:

Every exercise here demands you hold a neutral spine against something actively trying to move you out of it. Former pro triathlete turned strength coach Dede Griesbauer points out that this pays off in two places triathletes care about: run efficiency, and the ability to hold a stable position in the aerobars.

With an exercise like the rear foot elevated lunge (below), Griesbauer notes this forces each leg to work independently, which may reveal the left-right discrepancies a bilateral squat lets you hide. This matters for power balance on the bike and for not favoring one side on the run, which can help reduce injury risk.

Almost every movement in this routine trains multiple muscle groups at once. Griesbauer’s summary: It offers good “bang for the buck” with minimal equipment, minimal space, and minimal setup.

Griesbauer advises adding strength work to your hard days, not because it’s pleasant to stack lifting onto a threshold run, but because the alternative – sprinkling it across easy days – erodes the recovery that those days are designed to deliver.

“Make your hard days hard so that your easy days can be easy,” she says.

That’s the ideal. Griesbauer is also clear about what “ideal” might look like for an age-grouper with a job.

“Not everyone will have time to do their hard bike or run session AND make time for strength on the same day,” she says. “The good news is that this session isn’t heavy loading. These are good basic movements, so if you are time-crunched, adding them wherever you can is better than not doing them at all.”

Two sessions a week is the target, but once isn’t a failure. “Some is better than none,” Griesbauer says, “so if you can only do it once? Get it done once.”

Start with a single pass through all nine exercises (which should take approximately 10-12 minutes).

Once one round stops feeling like work, add a second. Then, if time allows, add a third. Three circuits should take you about 30-35 minutes and is plenty of stimulus for a routine that isn’t loading you heavily. Rest 30 to 60 seconds between exercises and a couple of minutes between rounds.

Progress through the workout in the order listed. It moves between lower body, core, and upper body so nothing fatigues to failure.

Stand on your right foot with your left leg extended behind you and the top of your left foot resting on the ball. Bend your right knee until your right thigh is parallel to the floor, keeping your torso upright and your weight on your heel. Now press your heel into the floor and return to the start position. Complete 10 repetitions and then repeat the exercise with your left leg.

Begin in a prone position with the tops of your feet resting on the ball, your palms on the floor underneath your shoulders, and your body forming a straight line. Now contract your stomach muscles, bend your knees, and roll the ball toward your chest. Pause briefly and roll the ball back until your body forms a straight line again. Complete 12 repetitions.

Assume a modified push-up position with your feet together, your body forming a perfectly straight line and your palms positioned slightly more than shoulder-width apart on the ball. Bend your elbows and smoothly lower your chest to within an inch of the ball. Immediately press back upward to the start position. If you have difficulty doing a full push-up, do a half push-up, bending your elbows only to 90 degrees before pressing upward. Complete 10 repetitions.

Start in a bridge position, face up, with your head and shoulders on the floor and your heels resting on top of the ball, your body suspended in a straight line between these points. Contract your hamstrings and roll the ball toward your butt. Pause briefly and extend your legs, rolling the ball back to the starting point. Don’t let your hips drop. Complete 12 repetitions. If this exercise is too easy, do a single-leg version, elevating one foot above the ball and pulling the ball toward your butt with the other leg.

Kneel on the floor facing the ball, lean forward slightly, and place your forearms on top of the ball. Pull your belly button toward your spine. Slowly roll the ball forward by extending your forearms out in front of you and allowing your body to tilt toward the floor. Concentrate on maintaining perfect alignment of your spine. Stop just before you’re forced to arch your back. Hold this position for a two-count and then return to the start position, exhaling as you do so. Complete 12 repetitions.

Now use your hands to drag your body forward over the ball until it is underneath the tops of your feet, keeping your body in a straight line. Pause briefly and roll back to the start position. Complete 10 repetitions.

Assume a modified push-up position with your palms on the floor, the tops of your feet resting on the ball, and your body in a perfectly straight line between them. Bend your elbows and lower your chest toward the floor. Go as far as you can without strain; it’s OK if it isn’t very far. Now press back to the start position. Complete 10 repetitions.

Lie face up with your upper back supported by the ball, your feet flat on the floor about 12 inches apart, your knees bent and your body forming a “tabletop” from knees to chest. Contract your calf muscles and raise your heels off the floor without allowing your hips to sag. Now lower your heels back to the floor. Complete 12 repetitions. If this exercise is too easy, do a single-leg version, first crossing your left ankle over your right knee and then doing the reverse.

Get into a plank position with your palms on the floor underneath your chest and the ball supporting your shins. Keeping your arms straight, press the heels of your palms into the floor and push your body backward so that the ball rolls up under your thighs. Go as far as you can and then pull your body in the opposite direction until you’re back in the start position. Complete 12 repetitions. If this exercise is too easy, start with the ball under the tops of your feet.

Instability is the point, which also means this routine isn’t universally appropriate. Griesbauer flags a few pointers worth knowing before you start. Note that none of these are a reason not to train. They just require you to adapt or swap movements if needed.

If you have a cranky lower back: The abdominal rollout generates high spinal extension and shear force if your core gives out mid-rep, which can be a genuine problem for anyone with disc history. The reverse crunch can pull you into loaded lumbar flexion if control slips, and a hamstring curl performed with dropped hips will arch and cramp the low back.

If you have “shoulder history”: Push-ups on a moving ball tax the rotator cuff hard, and the ball walk-out places prolonged, shifting load on the shoulder joint. Skip both if you’ve dealt with instability or impingement.

If you have wrist issues: The ball’s spherical surface forces the wrists into deep extension while the weight moves under you. Elevated push-ups shift more bodyweight forward onto the same joints.

If you have knees and hip flexor problems: The rear foot elevated lunge loads the front knee in deep flexion and stretches the trailing leg aggressively, which can be enough to tip the pelvis if your hip flexors are tight. Tight hip flexors will also hijack the reverse crunch from the lower abs.

If you have cramp-prone calves and hamstrings: The bridge heel raise fires the calves while the hamstrings are already shortened, which is a reliable spasm trigger. The hamstring curl frequently cramps athletes with weak hamstrings on the concentric.

If you have compromised balance: Any single-leg work raises fall risk. Have a wall or chair within reach if you feel you might need it.

Earth almost always has more than one moon. The others are just very small, and they never stay long — but they could provide accessible and unique insights into the solar system.

Temporary satellites, or mini-moons, are small asteroids that wander into Earth’s neighborhood and get captured briefly by our planet’s gravity. A typical one stays about nine months and circles Earth roughly three times before escaping back into orbit around the sun, Grigori Fedorets of Finland's University of Helsinki said in a presentation at the Europlanet Science Congress (EPSC) 2026 in The Hague in September.

Fedorets' modeling suggests that the largest mini-moon around Earth at any given moment is typically only about 75 centimeters (30 inches) across. An asteroid about 3 meters (10 feet) wide should be captured every 10 to 20 years.

"This is not just a theoretical construct. There is observational evidence," Fedorets said. Only two mini-moons have been confirmed to date, however — 2006 RH120 and 2020 CD3, both discovered by the Catalina Sky Survey in Arizona. Two fireballs, seen over the Czech Republic in 2013 and Australia in 2016, were traced back to objects that had been in geocentric orbits. Two more asteroids, 2022 NX1 and 2024 PT5, were captured only briefly, without completing a full orbit.

Fedorets noted a potential third mini-moon — the recently detected Sar2901, which could also be an artificial object. Sar2901 has since faded from view but should be observable again around mid-to-late November, according to the Minor Planet Center.

Mini-moons are so small that scientists know little about them. For objects between 1 and 10 meters (3.3 to 33 feet) across, models of the near-Earth asteroid population disagree with the numbers suggested by records of fireballs in Earth's atmosphere. Mini-moons sit right in that gap, so finding more of them should show which picture is right.

They may also reveal how much of the material in Earth’s neighborhood traces its origin back to our moon, as some small asteroids may be chunks of the lunar surface blasted into space by impacts. During the temporary flyby of asteroid 2024 PT5, for example, scientists found its spectrum to be closer to lunar rock than to typical asteroids, according to a 2025 study led by Theodore Kareta, which Fedorets described as the strongest evidence yet for lunar ejecta among Earth's small neighbors.

Kamo’oalewa, a much larger asteroid and quasi-moon of Earth, at which China’s Tianwen-2 mission arrived in July, has been the subject of debate over possible lunar or asteroidal origins.

The transformative Vera C. Rubin Observatory's 10-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) should find many more mini-moons, though further work will be needed. On its own, Fedorets estimates, the survey should catch a mini-moon about once every two years.

Fedorets and colleagues are setting up a dedicated system running on a virtual server at Finland's Center for Scientific Computing, to sift Rubin's alerts through the ANTARES real-time astronomical data broker to flag potential mini-moons for follow-up observations, which could raise detections to two or three per year.

Making regular discoveries could open doors for future missions, providing targets for relatively easy, low-cost asteroid sample-return missions. Mini-moons’ proximity to Earth, and their low relative speeds, means less rocket propellant is needed to get out to these diminutive targets and back to Earth.

Initially, Fedorets envisions cubesat tech demo missions, which would pave the way for mid-sized missions that could collect samples. He sees the Tianwen-2 low-energy sample mission as a potential stepping stone to resource utilization, while also noting that, at the higher end of the scale, there have been programs such as NASA’s cancelled Asteroid Redirect Mission (ARM) and plans by, for example, TransAstra to put a 100-ton asteroid into a stable orbit around Earth.

"This is not sci-fi," Fedorets said. "These people are actually thinking about this kind of stuff."

The short warning times and instability of the orbits of mini-moons dictates potential mission profiles. A spacecraft could wait in Earth-moon space, ready to head for a newly found mini-moon, similar to how ESA's Comet Interceptor will launch and then wait for a suitable comet to study. Asteroids could also be nudged into more stable orbits, allowing more extensive future missions.

"LSST is now online, and over the next 10 years, we are expected to find many more of these temporary moons," Fedorets said. Regular discoveries could pave the way for exploration in the early 2030s and sampling missions later in the decade.

More children, teens and very young adults are being diagnosed with colorectal cancer in the United States, and new research suggests they often experience aggressive forms of the disease.

While diagnosis rates are rising, the disease is still rare among young people. Colorectal cancer, which affects the lower intestines and rectum, is largely a disease of age: 55% of all colorectal cancers are diagnosed in people over 65, and only 16% occur in people ages 49 or younger, according to a 2026 study published in the flagship journal of the American Cancer Society. Only a few hundred cases per year are diagnosed in people under age 21 in the U.S.

Nevertheless, the numbers of cases in the very young are rising, even as colorectal cancer rates decline or hold steady in older individuals. This increase coincides with an overall rise in colon and rectal cancer rates in people under 50.

The reasons for this schism aren't entirely understood. People over 50 receive routine colonoscopies in which precancerous polyps are removed, and that likely accounts for much of the decline in colorectal cancer diagnoses in older adults. But researchers suspect that environmental factors, such as ultraprocessed foods and sedentary lifestyles, might be behind the growing rates in young people.

Scientists are looking to the gut microbiome for clues about why colorectal cancer is on the rise in young people.

"Most of the research has really focused on the gut microbiome," the community of bacteria and other microbes that live in the gut, said Dr. Christopher Lieu, a professor of medical oncology at the University of Colorado Anschutz and a medical adviser to the nonprofit Colorectal Cancer Alliance.

Microbiome disruptions may spur inflammation, which, in turn, may trigger DNA mutations that cause cancer. But these mechanisms are complicated, Lieu told Live Science, and there likely isn't one simple reason young people's microbiomes may be getting disturbed.

"It's going to be extraordinarily more complex than that," he said.

What researchers are starting to learn is that colorectal cancer in people in their early 20s or younger tends to be a more aggressive disease than that often seen in older people.

"We do feel like it is a bit of a distinct disease than what we're seeing in older adults," said Dr. Erica Arnold, a pediatric surgery research fellow at Nationwide Children's Hospital in Columbus, Ohio. In October, Arnold presented new research on colorectal cancer in people ages 21 or younger at the annual meeting of the American Academy of Pediatrics.

Arnold and her colleagues analyzed the cases of 84 patients diagnosed between 2012 and 2023. They found that the median age of diagnosis was 16 years old and the median duration of symptoms before diagnosis was only 12 weeks long.

Only about 20% of the patients had a close relative with colorectal cancer, suggesting that for many of these young people, the disease did not necessarily emerge due to hereditary factors.

If you want my honest opinion, it does suggest that these cancers are developing differently.

Eighty percent of the study participants were diagnosed with Stage III or Stage IV disease, meaning the cancer had already spread locally and to lymph nodes or to other, distant organs when the patients were diagnosed. For comparison, in colorectal cancer patients of all ages, 60% are diagnosed in Stage III or IV, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

In addition, Arnold said, one-third of the patients in their study had cellular markers of particularly aggressive cancer.

"Some of that might be related to delays in diagnosis," Lieu said, "but if you want my honest opinion, it does suggest that these cancers are developing differently."

In 1999, the diagnosis rate in kids ages 10 to 14 was 1 in a million. By 2020, it had risen to 6 in a million. For teens between 15 and 19, the rate of diagnosis went from 3 in a million to 13 in a million in that same time frame.

For instance, a specific variant of the common gut bacterium Escherichia coli is strongly associated with colorectal cancer; the variant carries a genetic segment and produces compounds that are toxic to genes. "It may be a combination of food, antibiotic use and environmental exposures," Lieu suggested.

Among the 84 patients in the study, 75% reported experiencing back and abdominal pain before their diagnosis, 45% had changes in their bowel movements, 43% experienced unexplained weight loss and 38% had rectal bleeding.

These are classic colorectal cancer symptoms, Arnold said, but they might be overlooked in this young age group because these same symptoms can have more common causes.

Parents should know that in most cases, stomach pain and gastrointestinal disruptions in kids are not caused by colorectal cancer, Lieu said, calling the cancer an "extraordinarily rare event." Nevertheless, pain or gastrointestinal upset that doesn't ease or that worsens over time needs a checkup, and symptoms like weight loss and blood in the stool are very unusual in kids and deserve extra attention.

"It's just important to investigate it to make sure we're catching colorectal cancer earlier rather than later in these patients," Arnold said.

Something in the literary world isn’t adding up. On the surface, the numbers aren’t looking good. If you’re an American, there’s a roughly 38% chance you’ll read a novel or short story this year. If you’re an American man, that number slips to 28%. By most metrics, it’s a hard time for literature.

There’s an undeniable energy bubbling up in New York, Los Angeles, London, and elsewhere. Readings are “sexy” now, supposedly, and a medley of exciting new publications have popped up, both online and in print. Ben Lerner and Emma Cline returned this year, and we’ve been graced with the strongest crop of debut novels in recent memory, from Caro Claire Burke’s culture-conquering Yesteryear to the jagged edges of Madeline Cash’s Lost Lambs to the vibrant energy of Sufiyaan Salam’s Wimmy Road Boyz. People spent the summer reading The Odyssey and crashing The London Review of Books website to read discourse about The Odyssey. So it can’t be all bad, right?

This is an attempt to snapshot the so-called State of Books at the end of 2026. The literary world is way too big for any one story to encompass, and this one is accordingly incomplete. We spoke to 34 people—we could have spoken to 300 more. You won’t find any scouts or book club celebrities here, nor did we reach anyone from the big tech companies that continue to reshape the landscape of publishing. There are lots of urgent issues that didn’t make it into this piece, including book bans, youth literacy, and the continuing rise of audiobooks. Each of those could be worthy of its own panel discussion. And we left out some of the points that our panelists agreed on unanimously: Agents still matter, booksellers are essential. More still got left on the cutting-room floor.

What did emerge was a widespread sense of concern. There’s a consensus that the old ways of publishing have broken down. Making books is more expensive, readers are more difficult to reach, and AI is giving nearly everyone the jimjams. But with that comes a sense of opportunity that’s making people optimistic—and down to party. Between self-publishing, Substack, and a small-press renaissance, writers, editors, and content creators are pushing out into a strange new frontier.

Rumaan Alam: It’s a more anxious time. There was a time in my career where agents, editors, and publicists wanted to telegraph confidence and a belief in a system as it was, and I’m not sure that that is the case any longer.

My agent, my publisher, my publicist, I feel like they’ve always been very direct with me. But I’m more accustomed to hearing now—from people who ought to know, people who are the experts— that they in fact don’t know what is going to happen.

Grace Byron: There’s a contraction of money, at a macro and micro level. A lot of layoffs, a lot of people playing it safe, a lot of people nervous about the future of publishing. One way that you see that is the continual panic over literacy rates, decline in reading rates, decline in book sales and in book reviews, obviously. The Washington Post just cut their books section, and large magazines have followed suit in a lot of places. And I think you see less literary fiction, or less sales.

A lot of people seem really worried, and rightfully so, about the rise of AI, the rise of celebrity memoirs, the rise of romantasy, and these sort of continual panics over what that means for “serious” writing or “serious” writers.

The other big shift is Substack and self-publishing.

Nelio Biedermann: When you talk to someone in the industry for longer than 5 or 10 minutes, it often gets to how much better it was back in the days. Writers say they sold more novels to publishers. People at parties say the parties were better 20 years ago. It can be kind of frustrating if you come into the industry new and everyone talks about how much better it was and you’re 20 or 40 years late.

Lauren Oyler: There’s no new Sally Rooney, or no new phenomenon. The new phenomenon is “more things to freak out about.”

Joelle Owusu-Seykere: The amount of people who get the opportunity to publish a book has definitely broadened. The early change came from the diversification of who works in publishing. Back in the day it was usually people with history and English lit degrees who went to Oxford and Cambridge and Harvard. That’s still the case, but it’s opened up to people who are genuinely quite normal. What we bring is something more interesting, because we’ve read way more diversely, and we’re not from that kind of stuffy publishing background. I’m proud to be a part of that set.

I know there’s been a lot of social change in the past decade or so, and lots of conversations online about how publishing can be more accessible. But you can’t have a wider range of writers if we don’t have a wider range of people actually doing the publishing.

Publishers are listening a lot more. There’s some that aren’t. There’s some that generally don’t give a fuck. But the ones who actually care about broadening their readership, just reaching new readers, new people—we are actually listening.

Bryan Washington: At least amongst major publishers, so to speak, in the U.S., there is a broadening of scope as far as the specificity of narratives that are being written and the diversity of experiences in narratives that are being written. On the other hand, the support for authors who are writing outside of a mainstream context—whether it’s promotion, whether it’s marketing, whether it’s visibility—seems to be continuing to decline, with some notable exceptions. Particularly if you’re a marginalized author, getting in front of a reader seems to be growing more and more difficult for a variety of reasons. But many of them, I think, stem from lack of support from publishers.

Ottessa Moshfegh: Writing means a lot of things, you know, and I think we’re starting to see more of the variety of things a writer does. That includes podcasting, that includes content creation, just as much as it includes screenwriting and journalism and being a novelist. I think there’s less of this tight-fisted “Here is the world of literature” [mindset]. I think the edges of that category are starting to soften. You can either hate it or love it or not care, but it’s happening.

Eden Yonas: On the creative end, for people who are talking about books and people who are writing books and people who are marketing books, there is this constant desire to keep up with things as they gain in popularity, and as a result, there’s kind of [a] “go, go, go,” get-the-next-thing-out [mentality].

R.F. Kuang: I think the elephant in the room is the transition to video everywhere, right? I When we were in the marketing and publicity phase for books that were published longer ago, there would be more suggestions for blog posts and blog interviews and essay submissions, which are still the sorts of things I would prefer to do. And in my last two launches, I’ve noticed it’s just a gazillion video requests, whether that’s short-form interviews or longer YouTube things. It is a bit of a hassle from the author’s perspective because it means you have to dress like a person and put on a presentable face and be in front of a camera more often.

Hua Hsu: It’s been bracing to see how different the things you have to do as an author are now, as opposed to 2022, as opposed to 2020 or 2015. It’s not bad. It’s just very different. I thought that I’d done everything that one could possibly be asked to do circa 2022, but just four years later, the range of opportunities you have to talk about your book, venues, forums, spaces online and IRL—it’s pretty dizzying. I don’t think I had to do one on-camera thing in 2022.

LeVar Burton: The act of reading, and how we perceive readers, has changed. What was once seen as a solitary act has become a social one. Through platforms like BookTok, Bookstagram, and Substack, the reader is no longer a lone figure in an armchair. Now, they’re part of a vibrant and visible community.

Valeria Luiselli: The last time I published before just now was almost a decade ago, and things have certainly changed. One thing that I think changed positively is the enormous variety of people who talk about books in social media. Especially in the Spanish speaking world, where I started this book tour, we had events that were just like, breakfast with 50 young people who do different kinds of things on TikTok, Instagram, etc. around books. What I found in those spaces was this lively, funny, well-read, diverse community of people who care about books, and when I asked them, “So what are you guys? Are you Bookstagram? What do you call yourselves?” most of them laughed. One of them said, "No, we’re just unpaid journalists.”

We just have a greater variety of opinions now, and that has taken weight off of, like, the New York Times review, the Guardian review, that one thing that was a game changer. I think that’s a healthy thing. We don’t have this super tight hierarchy in the final say.

Jacques Testard: Literature in translation is booming. I think that’s partly the International Booker Prize. The Booker had commissioned a survey a few years ago to find out who’s reading in translation, and it turns out that people under 35 read more in translation than any other demographic.

Polly Barton: There’s a number of different factors that have brought this about. The increased visibility of a lot of indie publishers, Fitzcarraldo being an obvious example.

Sam Fisher: I feel like it was the interest in Japanese literature that really started it, and Murakami being this huge superstar, at least in the noughties. I think perhaps publishers just got better at packaging things and making the case for translated fiction. When it comes to European translated fiction, I think Ferrante was really, really big in terms of giving people an interest.

Han Zhang: In the US, one detail you probably already know is that we more and more put translators’ credits on the cover. I’m buying a lot of Chinese-language books from China, from Taiwan, from Malaysia, from Singapore.

Vincenzo Latronico: The flip side is the need to pass through English. The English language is still the hub between languages. For instance, the Danish classic Tove Ditlevsen was translated into Italian only after she was translated into English. Most of the translations of my book happened after the English.

Alia Hanna Habib: One of the most profound changes I’ve seen is the shrinking of book media. It used to be that strong reviews could make a book, so we had the term “review-driven books.” That still exists, but the chances of that are lower if there are fewer reviews and there’s not a sense of a critical consensus.

Morgan Entrekin: We’ve been in crisis for several years. AP stopped their book coverage a year ago, and then The Washington Post…but this has been building over 25 years. Every midsize city [newspaper] had a book section 25 years ago. They don’t now, or there are very few of them left. It’s just harder to make people aware of books.

Bryan Washington: If you’re writing from a marginalized vantage point—if you’re a Black author, if you’re an author who is a woman, if you’re a trans person or a queer person, it has been difficult to publish narratives in [the US] for some time, let alone to get in front of a reader. I think that layoffs in book criticism only exacerbate that, because we find ourselves in a situation in which editors and to some extent the remaining reporters want to keep their jobs, and one means of doing that is getting eyes on your piece. Underlining that need to keep their job for virality, you end up seeing a lot of the same books being talked about.

Maya Binyam: Language and literature are already so devalued in this country, and the percentage of people who read for pleasure is already so small.

I find it really upsetting that a books column for New York Magazine has a recurring feature where they ask people how they find time to read, as if finding time to read is like finding time to do something good for you, but kind of unpleasant. I don’t think time is the problem. I think the problem is that people are alienated from pleasure. It feels like the equivalent of asking, like, How do you find time to have sex?

Valeria Luiselli: Every time I board an airplane or a subway car, and I’ve been doing this for years, I count how many people are reading a book. I used to even take pictures of them. It is really devastating to see how those numbers have shrunk. I see in one subway car on a good day, there’s one or maybe two people with a book. It used to be at least 10. You see an older generation who still brings books to planes, but I see no one in my age group and below reading.

Matthew Gasda: I think people are even reading less than they were two or three years ago. I heard one friend suggest that’s because algorithms are getting even better at feeding us content. The algorithm is so good at getting short-form text to you that it gets very hard to care about a book.

The rise of podcasts is kind of replacing books for men. Then there’s the question of whether men have been pushed out of the publishing industry, and then whether they’ve been pulled into podcasting. There’s push and pull.

Alia Hanna Habib: People who work in publishing need to be much more engaged with how books are taught. There’s been a lot of writing about students showing up in high school without being taught to read a whole book. It’s like learning to run a mile. You know you’re taught to read a whole book and to engage with it, and that that affects your whole life as a reader, your idea of what reading is for and the kind of pleasures it can give. We’re seeing the consequences of it.

R.F. Kuang: I am really exhausted by these apocalyptic headlines about the decline of literacy, the decline of reading for pleasure, and a total lack of interest in reading from young people. There have been so many think pieces and hot takes and chest-thumping on this topic, and I don’t want to diminish the scale of the problem. But I think the way that it’s being talked about is both incredibly condescending, and it feels like a thinly veiled topic for which people get to flaunt their cultural superiority. It completely ignores the fact that we all have a lot more agency in this than we think we do.

You have booksellers talking about how young people are coming in all the time asking for classics. You have these really lively reading communities on BookTok, and you have librarians, K-12 educators, university educators, and people who are not being featured in clickbaity headlines, doing everything they can to keep reading alive and flourishing. I wish we would talk about that, instead of another guy being like, “None of you can read and culture is dead.”

Ira Madison III: People are always talking about self-publishing. There are so many hit books that were self-published first.

Jordy Moblo: There are a lot of authors that have struggled to get into a traditional publishing pipeline, and for them to have an outlet like social media where their books can be discovered more easily, I think that’s really exciting. Dungeon Crawler Carl was self-published when I found it—those were self-published books up until 18 months ago, when it got a traditional publishing deal through Penguin Random House.

Sam Fisher: On the publishing side, things have got a lot more difficult in more quotidian, boring ways: paper prices, the lack of funding. It’s just got worse and worse and worse. I think people are starting to realize now the problem with paperback books is they’re almost the same price as they were 25 years ago, whereas costs have gone up by a factor. Even in the last two years, the price of printing a paperback has doubled, so the margins just get smaller and smaller and smaller, and that’s bad for presses, but it’s also bad for writers. It just means that there’s less to go around.

Jacques Testard: From Brexit, which has stopped us from selling books to the EU via direct sales, to the war in Ukraine and subsequent conflicts, which have an impact on oil prices that then have an impact on paper prices and therefore printing costs, it’s been a very difficult time for small businesses generally.

The biggest challenge is what it has always been, really, which is that publishing is a really difficult business. The business model is tried and tested, and however much people try to innovate, it’s always going to be a very low-margin business. [Fitzcarraldo Editions is] 12 years in now, and we’ve reached a level of stability, but we’re never a bad season or two away from serious trouble financially.

Naomi Kanakia: Five years ago, in literary fiction, if they gave an author a big advance and then put a marketing budget behind that author, there was a pretty good chance that when the book came out, it would be heavily covered by a fair number of media outlets. There would be some kind of buzz online, on Twitter usually, and there was a pretty good chance it could get awards attention. Then there would be some mass of readers who would respond to that kind of book. It felt like each year, there were maybe 100 to 200 books that big corporate publishers really put their weight behind, and maybe 10 to 20 would become something, and the authors would have careers.

Starting maybe two or three years ago, that really started to sputter. Those books began failing to get traction. The machine broke down, especially for debut novels. If you’re a debut novelist, the industry can’t make you into Emma Cline anymore.

Sean Thor Conroe: The gambling on the debuts is an issue.

Sam Fisher: The debut-novelist thing has been an eternal problem, and it’s a structural issue in the industry that so many debut novelists are kind of set up to fail. They’re in these advances they can never come close to paying off.

Editors now need to run their acquisitions by a large marketing team, by other editors. It’s now a team decision, and you can’t acquire good literature as a team.

Tom Willis: Big publishing is useless. All they do is buy on trends, it seems to me. They don’t seem to really know how to sell books. So the editorial class, I think, needs to become more diverse in taste and in general.

Alia Hanna Habib: I work at an agency where we represent high-profile fantasy series to serious nonfiction to cookbooks, and in the course of a given year, we see everything from five figures to seven figures. It’s impossible to answer that question.

Here’s the nuance: A publisher figures out what they’re going to pay for any book, especially a debut, based on the market of how books are selling. And increasingly, publishers do that by looking at the market on a very short timeline.

We’ve become, I think, too presentist in how we value books, instead of thinking this is an industry where we have really long tails, where things change.

Ira Madison III: What I was told is that advances used to be in three installments pre-COVID, but now I believe they’re in four installments. Mine was low six figures. I don’t think I would be getting that advance today. I sold my book in 2021.

Joelle Owusu: It really does depend on so many things. One, whether the author has representation or not, because more experienced agents can be quite aggressive negotiators. It depends on whether there’s more than one publisher in on it, so you can kind of go into an auction situation, and that hikes up the price of the advance.

I understand that the books that are getting the most noise are the ones that say this author gets a six or seven figure deal. But I promise you, those are quite rare, and it really is for those super established authors, bar one or two exceptional debuts. It ranges from small five figures to five figures, maybe even up to six figures.

What the author should also consider, although it’s a lot harder for them, is one, whether the publisher cares about their career, publishing more than one book, and two, actually earning out the advance. Because we can throw 100,000 pounds at an author, but if they don’t make that back in sales, they will never see a royalty a day in their lives.

Sam Fisher: You still hear about people getting half a million occasionally in the UK, and then probably four times that in the US. But it really happens to one person a year now. I don’t think it really helps anyone. It occasionally works, you know, and the lucky writer who’s kind of been plucked by the hand of God gets incredibly rich. But most of the time, yeah, it’s just kind of an albatross for them.

Naomi Kanakia: I don’t know that, like, Emma Cline’s $2 million advance is still common, but it’s still pretty common for debut authors to get several $100,000 advances. But it’s always been the case that most people who get those kinds of advances don’t necessarily go on to have the kind of careers that they would like to have, or that are even sustainable. Now it feels like the odds are really quite bad. So you get that money, but, y’know—nothing happens next.

Morgan Entrekin: They never have read as much as women. The number that I’ve heard in terms of reading fiction is seven to one. And I heard that 40 years ago.

Chelsea Hodson: I don’t think about gender that much when I’m publishing or reading. I do think men read. I think that these pieces are just trying to create clickbait.

Bryan Washington: Gay men read. Queer people read. I don’t know what straight people read, though.

Ira Madison III: The gays are reading. I mean, they only seem to be reading Fruit Fly, and some of them The Disappearers.

Performative reading—I feel like that phrase got a lot of traction. But everything is performative when you assume that everyone is doing things for other people. Thinking something is performative is based on how you value performance. It says nothing about the other person. It says everything about you. You’re not reading Confederacy of Dunces at the bar because you feel self-conscious, but maybe this dude really just likes Confederacy of Dunces and wants to read at the bar.

Tom Willis: Performative male reading—that’s just nonsense. That’s internet crap. That’s great if you’re on Instagram, but it’s not real.

Sean Thor Conroe: I mean, it’s the mission! People always ask me. That’s my mission!

The fellas do read. They’re just not reading $30 colorful hardcovers that came out last week. I think the inclination is to read older books.

Vincenzo Latronico: There’s been a hype cycle around this, but for me this is not news. Do you think that men in the 1980s were really passionate about reading novels?

Smitty: What do we consider “reading,” and what do we consider “not reading”? Do we consider reading self-help? Do we consider the type of reading that we’re talking about on BookTok? I say yes. Some people say no, right. I do think there are men reading now. Do I think men are reading at the same clip as women? No.

Men read self-help books, personal finance books, things that you know as a man can help you further your life, get better, and things like that. I feel like that’s always going to be number one. At a solid number two, I would say fantasy and sci-fi. I’ll even throw in historical fiction as number three.

Alia Hanna Habib: If men want to get laid, they should read. But yeah, statistically, men read less than women. I work a lot in nonfiction, and there’s been a decline in nonfiction. One of the postulations, is that those men, the men who historically were reading nonfiction, are now engaging with other media, whether it’s going to podcasts or watching YouTube or whatever.

Emma Straub: Of course men read. Less than women, we know that to be the case. But men are in my bookstore every day, and we treat them just like real people! The men who shop in my bookstore, they’re reading A Court of Thorn and Roses and they’re reading Dungeon Crawler Carl and they’re reading Patrick Radden Keefe.

Lauren Oyler: I mean, I only go out with douchebags who read a lot. They’re reading, like, serious translated European literature and old stuff.

I do think that in this conversation about “Do men read?,” men themselves who take up the mantle of being anti-woke crusaders actually engage in identity politics. So when these white guys are like, “There are no white men in literature,” they’re just doing identity politics, aren’t they? Literature that might draw men in probably has more to do with the subject matter than the race and gender of the people who are in the books.

Rumaan Alam: The statistics bear out what you’re asking, right? I guess I just can’t conceive of the impoverished kind of life that is being led by a mind that doesn’t care to read. And I don’t think you have to read Knausgård to call yourself a human being, but for Christ’s sake, read a newspaper.

The way this is put, it always ends up being, “Men can’t get a fair shake, we need more books about sports,” or a midlife crisis or heterosexuality or whatever. It’s preposterous to say that.

Maybe what our institutions here, speaking of education principally, have failed to show [men] is that some of the most astonishing art of the last century has been about manhood. I read Updike’s Rabbit novels two years ago, and they are such a punch in the face emotionally and the most beautiful writing. If we need a male writer to rally the troops, why not Updike? Why not Roth? Why not Joseph Heller? Why not Richard Yates? There have been dozens of these guys. There have been dozens of these guys, so they had their shot. Joelle Owusu: Oh my God! I’ve only recently learned that they do. That’s really, really bad of me, but I’ve dated enough men with Rich Dad, Poor Dad to end it all, to be honest. But, no—men do read. Chelsea Hodson: I first read Matthew Gasda, one of my authors, from reading his Substack, and I thought it was really interesting. I think that a lot of people do sell books there, yeah.

R.F. Kuang: I am on Substack.
