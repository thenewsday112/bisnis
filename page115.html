#3682. Ste Marie imbues Monos Vancouver office with "residential quality"

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Canadian interiors studio Ste Marie has designed the Vancouver headquarters of luggage brand Monos to emphasise simplicity and shift mood throughout the day.

Located in the city’s Mount Pleasant neighbourhood, the Monos office was created to reflect the brand’s development to date and provide space for continued growth.

Ste Marie was presented with an open-ended brief, allowing the studio to develop a “flexible framework” for a company that is “still exploring what it could become” and would allow its team to collaborate, experiment and host.

“Monos is a brand grounded in design, mindfulness, human connection, and we understood this as an opportunity to create a place where those values could be put into practice,” said Ste Marie.

“A place where their people could come together to work, create, exchange ideas, test new things, host events and gather as an extended community.”

The studio, which previously designed the interiors for a Monos store in Toronto, approached the workplace like a residential project and organised the spaces into three overlapping areas.

“Like an apartment, it accommodates everyday activity as naturally as moments of hosting,” said the studio. “The residential quality also supports Monos’ culture of openness.”

The first area features low, soft modular sofas for lounging and conversation, and enclosed banquettes below conical pendant lights that facilitate personal exchanges, small meetings or shared meals.

An elongated communal table at the centre of the open plan provides a flexible surface for meetings, product development, testing, dining and gathering.

Here, the custom millwork is arranged in perpendicular planes that form vertical supports and horizontal overhead light boxes.

At the back is a kitchen and tea room with solid-surface counters and handleless cabinets, continuing the minimalist schema that runs throughout.

“These areas can expand and contract as needed: a meeting can become a meal, a work surface can become a display, and a lounge can transform into an event space,” Ste Marie said.

The hardness of the polished concrete flooring and rift-cut timber millwork is offset by soft furnishings, natural-fibre rugs and sheer curtains.

Dimmable lighting enables the mood to shift from early morning focused work to more casual afternoon meetings and then evening events.

“The result is a workplace that feels considered but not overly designed, with the warmth and informality of a personal space and the flexibility of a creative studio,” said Ste Marie.

Ste Marie was founded by Craig Stanghetta and has developed a reputation for its cinematic hospitality spaces across North America.

Other recent projects by the studio include a trio of venues in Edmonton, a series of amenity spaces within a renovated 1960s tower in Chicago and a Vancouver restaurant influenced by Hong Kong’s “love motels”.

#2357. Some Communications by Rabbi Shmuley with AI Platforms May Be Discovered in Order to Determine Punitive Damages

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From Wednesday's decision in Fontainebleau Florida Hotel, LLC v. Botach [Rabbi Shmuley], decided by Magistrate Judge Marty Fulgueira Elfenbein:

Plaintiff's four subpoenas to the AI Providers and its Seventh RFP seek the same information, specifically Defendant's communications with AI platforms since December 3, 2024, concerning various categories of subjects:

In addition, to these six categories, Plaintiff seeks one more category of documents in the subpoenas to the AI providers (but not in the Seventh RFP), consisting of:

Plaintiff [argued] that Defendant's AI communications may reveal the extent of his alleged malice toward Plaintiff, the intentionality underlying his conduct, and evidence relevant to the determination of punitive damages, including whether an exception to Florida's statutory cap [on punitive damages] applies….

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(b)(1) permits discovery of nonprivileged matters relevant to a party's claims or defenses and proportional to the needs of the case. The Rule expressly recognizes that information within the permissible scope of discovery need not be admissible in evidence to be discoverable…. The District Court's Order at ECF No. [261], which authorized the Parties to conduct "discovery limited to damages," also governs the scope of discovery at this juncture…. Thus, the relevant inquiry is whether the particular AI communications sought by Plaintiff bear a sufficient relationship to the damages issues remaining in this action….

The degree of reprehensibility, the defendant's intent, and the circumstances surrounding the wrongful conduct are … relevant to determining an appropriate punitive-damages award…. To the extent Defendant used AI platforms to generate, revise, or refine statements concerning Plaintiff, its counsel, or its representatives, those communications may reveal not merely the final content of Defendant's statements but also the purpose for which the statements were created, the instructions Defendant supplied, and the effects he sought to achieve. Such information may provide evidence of the intent underlying Defendant's conduct that cannot necessarily be discerned from the resulting public statements alone. The Court does not determine that those communications will establish malice or specific intent to harm; it determines only that they bear a sufficient relationship to those disputed damages issues to warrant discovery….

According[] to Defendant, his alleged hostility does not necessarily prove constitutional actual malice, and it therefore should not be considered when deciding whether punitive damages are appropriate. Constitutional actual malice in a defamation action concerns knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for truth. By contrast, the reprehensibility inquiry and the specific-intent exception under section 768.73(1)(c) require consideration of the defendant's culpability and intent to harm the claimant. Evidence of hostility, planning, or deliberate efforts to inflict or amplify injury to Plaintiff may be relevant to those questions when sufficiently connected to the alleged wrongful conduct here.

Plaintiff seeks evidence not simply to establish that Defendant disliked the Hotel, but to determine whether Defendant deliberately engaged in conduct intended to cause injury and whether that intent bears on the reprehensibility and statutory treatment of the punitive damages sought. The Court therefore rejects Defendant's argument that because hostility does not necessarily prove constitutional actual malice, it should not be considered when deciding whether punitive damages are appropriate.

Nor does the fact that some requested communications postdate the original publications render them categorically undiscoverable. Subsequent statements and conduct may, depending on their content and context, provide circumstantial evidence of a defendant's earlier intent or reveal a continuing course of conduct relevant to the injury. The temporal relationship between a communication and the alleged injury may affect its probative value, but it does not create an absolute bar to discovery….

The Court does not hold that every subsequent statement by Defendant may support an award of punitive damages. The Supreme Court has cautioned that punitive damages may not be used to punish a defendant for unrelated or dissimilar conduct merely because that conduct reflects poorly on the defendant's character. But the permissible scope of discovery is broader than the scope of evidence that may be ultimately admitted at trial. The present inquiry is whether the requested information is relevant and proportional under Rule 26(b)(1) to the damages issues remaining in this case.

Here, Plaintiff alleges a continuing course of conduct directed toward the Hotel, its personnel, and its representatives, including conduct involving AI-generated content. Plaintiff has also identified testimony from its damages expert concerning the alleged cumulative effect of Defendant's continuing online activity on the Hotel's reputation and business. As such, Defendant's communications with AI platforms concerning the Hotel and the Hotel's employees, owners, or representatives, including Plaintiff's counsel, may reveal information relevant to Defendant's alleged intent to harm Plaintiff, the nature and persistence of the challenged conduct, and the extent to which that conduct allegedly amplified Plaintiff's injuries. The Court therefore concludes that Request Nos. 1 (seeking AI communications about the Hotel), 3 (seeking AI communications about Plaintiff's counsel), and 4 (seeking AI communications about any employee, owner, or representative of the Hotel) in the subpoenas to the AI providers and the Seventh RFP fall within the scope of damages discovery authorized by the District Court….

Further, to the extent Request No. 7 in the subpoenas to the AI providers … seeks Defendant's AI communications regarding Requests Nos. 1 through [6] that have been deleted since their creation, the Court concludes that this Request is authorized by the District Court's Order only to the extent it seeks any deleted AI communications regarding Request Nos. 1, 3, and 4….

But the court rejected the other requests, which dealt with AI communications "concerning or relating to this litigation" (2), "concerning or relating to any Court Order entered during this litigation" (5), and "concerning or relating to Judge K. Michael Moore or the [Magistrate Judge]" (6):

Starting with Request No. 2 in the subpoenas and the Seventh RFP, it seeks all AI communications concerning or relating to "this litigation." That formulation is exceptionally broad. It encompasses potentially every AI exchange on matters having no meaningful connection to the amount of damages or Defendant's alleged intent to harm Plaintiff. Plaintiff has not established that every communication relating to this litigation bears on an issue remaining for the damages trial….

Turning to Request No. 5 in the subpoenas and the Seventh RFP, it seeks all AI communications concerning or relating to any Court Order entered during this litigation. Such communications may include Defendant's attempts to understand an Order, research its consequences, consider procedural options, or evaluate the litigation. The fact that Defendant may have expressed disagreement with, frustration about, or hostility toward a Court Order does not, without a sufficient connection to the alleged injury, make the communication relevant to Plaintiff's damages. Although Plaintiff contends that Defendant's reactions to Court Orders reflect the depth of his hostility and the reprehensibility of his conduct, that theory is too attenuated to justify discovery of every AI exchange concerning every Order entered in this case. The Court therefore finds that Request No. 5 exceeds the permissible scope of damages discovery.

Finally, Request No. 6 in the subpoenas and the Seventh RFP seeks all AI communications concerning or relating to District Judge K. Michael Moore or the undersigned Magistrate Judge. The Court likewise finds that Defendant's private communications with AI platforms concerning judicial officers, without more, do not bear a sufficient relationship to the damages Plaintiff seeks for the alleged harm to the Hotel. The discovery inquiry is not whether Defendant has criticized the judiciary; it is whether the requested information is relevant and proportional to the damages issues remaining in this action. A request encompassing every AI communication about the judges presiding over this litigation exceeds that scope. The Court thus finds that Request No. 6 exceeds the permissible scope of damages discovery.

The Court recognizes Plaintiff's argument that Defendant's criticism of the judicial process may demonstrate the persistence of his alleged hostility. But the Supreme Court has cautioned against using dissimilar conduct as a basis for punishing a defendant for being an objectionable person rather than for the conduct that injured the plaintiff….

And the court rejected the argument that the communications were categorically "protected work product":

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(b)(3)(A) generally protects "documents and tangible things that are prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial by or for another party or its representative." The doctrine is designed to protect the adversarial process by preserving the privacy necessary for litigation preparation….

The Eleventh Circuit's interpretation of Rule 26(b)(3)(A) confirms that work-product protection is not limited to materials prepared by counsel but extends to materials prepared by or for a party or its representative in anticipation of litigation….

When determining whether a document is protected under the work-product doctrine, "litigation need not necessarily be imminent … as long as the primary motivating purpose behind the creation of the document was to aid in possible litigation." The document must have been "created principally or exclusively to assist in anticipated or ongoing litigation." "Just because a document may have been used later to assist in litigation … does not mean it was principally prepared for such purpose." …

Here, communications independently generated by Defendant to create, revise, or disseminate content concerning the Hotel, its personnel, or Plaintiff's counsel are not transformed into attorney work product merely because Defendant is a litigant or because the resulting content may bear on this action. To the extent such communications were created for the purpose of producing public-facing statements or pursuing Defendant's independent activities, rather than preparing the defense of this litigation, they ordinarily would not satisfy Rule 26(b)(3)(A). The Court therefore rejects Defendant's blanket work-product objection to Request Nos. 1, 3, 4, and 7.

However, that conclusion does not foreclose a properly supported, document-specific claim of protection. If a particular communication was prepared because of this litigation and otherwise satisfies Rule 26(b)(3), Defendant may assert the protection in accordance with the Federal Rules. Defendant must, however, identify the withheld material and describe the nature of the asserted protection sufficiently to permit Plaintiff to assess the claim without revealing the protected information itself….

[I]f Plaintiff serves the subpoenas on the AI providers and requires them to produce the document directly to Plaintiff's counsel, Defendant will be deprived of the opportunity to determine whether there are any work-product protected documents within the production that should otherwise be withheld and identified on a privilege log. For that reason, the Parties are DIRECTED to meet and confer to discuss the logistics of how the AI providers, to the extent they have responsive materials, will be instructed to produce such materials….

#4030. The Everyday Nutrient Linked To 32% Lower Dementia Risk

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Your brain uses this nutrient for memory, but its relationship with dementia risk is more complicated than “more is better”.

Your brain uses this nutrient for memory, but its relationship with dementia risk is more complicated than “more is better”.

Moderate intake of choline, a nutrient found in eggs, fish and legumes, is linked to a lower risk of dementia.

Older adults consuming moderately high amounts had a 32 percent lower dementia risk than those consuming the least.

However, the highest intake was not associated with a clear benefit, suggesting that more is not necessarily better.

The research followed over 5,000 American adults, whose average age was almost 69, for around eight years.

Participants were initially free of dementia but around 500 developed the condition during follow-up.

After dividing participants into five groups according to choline intake, the researchers found a 32 percent lower dementia risk in the second-highest group than in the lowest, after accounting for other factors.

However, there was no statistically clear reduction in the highest-intake group.

The result therefore points towards a potentially beneficial range, rather than a steady fall in risk with every increase in consumption.

The researchers then combined their results with four previous studies to assess the broader pattern.

Together, these followed almost 138,000 people and recorded around 2,500 cases of dementia.

In the combined analysis, dementia risk fell as choline intake rose to approximately 465 milligrams per day, with no further reduction beyond that level.

Choline helps the brain produce acetylcholine, a chemical messenger involved in learning and memory.

It also supplies building blocks for the membranes surrounding brain cells, helping maintain their structure and communication.

These roles could help explain a link between choline and cognitive ageing, although they do not prove that consuming more prevents dementia.

The nutrient also participates in processes that regulate homocysteine, a substance linked to problems with blood vessels and cognitive function.

Eggs, meat, poultry and nuts were among the main sources of choline in the American participants’ diets.

People consuming more choline obtained a greater proportion of it from eggs.

Crucially, the analysis assessed choline from food and did not include intake from supplements.

The findings therefore cannot establish whether choline supplements would lower dementia risk.

The study was published in the Nutrition Journal (Chen et al., 2026).

#1309. 'My Workouts Stopped Working in Midlife. These 8 Lessons Helped Me Get Back on Track'

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Coach Michaela Bentley shares these hard-earned lessons transformed her approach to strength, nutrition, and healthy aging.

At 60, Michaela Bentley knows exactly how to train, eat and recover. But that wasn't always the case.

“As someone who's always maintained a pretty high level of fitness, that all came to a crashing halt in my late 40s,” the personal trainer and nutrition coach, who specializes in women's midlife fitness, wrote on Instagram.

“I started packing on the pounds, and all the things I had done my entire life to stay fit stopped working,” she recalled. “I was putting on weight and couldn't figure out why since I was doing the same things I'd always done.”

Here, Bentley shares what she learned from navigating those changes, while Sarah Campus, personal trainer, instructor, nutrition coach, and founder of LDN MUMS FITNESS, adds her expert perspective.

Effective training can be pretty boring—and that's okay. You don't need a completely different workout every time you train.

Repeating key movements allows you to practice technique, build strength, and track progression, both experts say.

“If you're doing completely different workouts every week, you're not building strength,” says Bentley.

“Constantly changing everything makes it much harder to see whether you're actually getting stronger,” agrees Campus.

There is no magic number of reps that suddenly makes a workout effective, whether that's eight, 10, or 12.

“What matters more is that you are challenging your muscles and working with enough effort to create a stimulus,” says Campus. Your final few reps should feel difficult and bring you close to failure while still allowing you to maintain good technique.

Make sure your training also becomes more challenging over time, Bentley adds, by gradually increasing the weight, reps or training volume—otherwise known as progressive overload.

Doing random workouts can keep you active, but they'll usually deliver random results.

“A structured program gives your training purpose and allows for progressive overload,” Campus says.

In midlife, consistency and progression are far more valuable than constantly chasing the latest fitness trend, which may even increase your risk of injury, she adds.

Bentley recommends sticking with a program for at least six months to a year.

Particularly if fat loss is your goal, increasing your Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis—or NEAT—can help reduce body fat.

NEAT includes everyday movement such as housework, walking, carrying shopping, gardening, taking the stairs instead of the elevator, and even fidgeting.

Walking, in particular, is one of the “most underrated tools for fat loss,” says Bentley.

“It increases daily energy expenditure without adding a ton of fatigue or interfering with recovery.”

“You don't have to spend hours in the gym to increase your daily energy expenditure,” Campus adds. “Taking the stairs, playing with your children or simply breaking up long periods of sitting all count.”

Your training only works if your body has the opportunity to recover and adapt.

“More training isn't always better, especially when life is already demanding,” says Campus. “Prioritize sleep, rest days, nutrition and lighter sessions when needed.”

Bentley also recommends resting for at least two minutes between challenging sets so you can perform at your best.

Consistent habits over weeks and months matter far more than one meal or one day.

“A meal out, birthday cake, or a weekend treat isn't going to undo weeks of regular training and healthy eating habits,” Campus says. “The goal is balance, not perfection.”

Bentley agrees, urging women to “eat the pizza” and “enjoy dinner with your friends.” Have fun, she says, then simply “get back to your usual routine” without dwelling on it.

It's rare to feel motivated all the time, but you don't need to, say both experts.

Building habits and routines that fit around your life—and sticking with them even when motivation dips—is a much more reliable approach.

“For fat loss, your overall calorie intake matters more than obsessing over whether those calories came from carbs or fat,” says Bentley.

When considering macros, she says to focus on food in this order: calories first, followed by protein and fiber.

Campus agrees. “Energy balance is important, but that doesn't mean calories are the only thing worth considering,” she says. “Protein, fiber, food quality, portion sizes, sleep, and activity all influence how sustainable your diet feels and how well you can stick to it.”

Early progress on a weight-loss plan can give way to a stretch where the same effort no longer produces the same results. That shift reflects a set of predictable physiological and behavioral forces that push back against restrictive eating.

Below are nine of those forces and how each one slows progress.

As body weight drops, the body burns fewer calories at rest, partly because it starts conserving energy.

Researchers call this adaptive thermogenesis. A 2026 randomized trial published in the Journal of Human Nutrition and Dietetics detected it within the first week of reduced energy intake. Over time, the same plan produces less progress, even when effort stays consistent.

Weight loss pushes appetite in the wrong direction.

As the body’s energy stores shrink, hunger increases and meals feel less filling. Steeper cuts in food intake intensify both signals, and that pressure makes aggressive restriction hard to sustain.

Banning a food intensifies the desire for it. Eliminating a food works against established habits and social life at the same time, so the plan ends and the banned foods return.

Restrictive weight loss draws energy from muscle as well as fat. Muscle burns energy even at rest, so losing it lowers metabolism further.

A 2025 paper from Reviews in Endocrine & Metabolic Disorders reports that regained weight disproportionately returns as fat rather than as the lean tissue that was lost. The method of weight loss influences how much muscle the body keeps.

In a peer-reviewed randomized controlled trial published in Obesity Science & Practice, participants on the Trilivy™ Reset 5 & 1 Plan retained 98% of lean mass and reduced visceral fat by 14% over 16 weeks.*

“A weight management program that protects lean mass, reduces visceral fat, and builds habit infrastructure is designed for the years after the active weight loss phase and addresses the conditions that drive regain at the source,” said Satya Jonnalagadda, PhD, MBA, RD, Vice President, Scientific & Clinical Affairs at Trilivy.

Constant restriction demands steady mental effort, and a single stressful event can be enough to derail a plan.

Coaching gives people support to continue their plan through stressful periods.

In the same trial from Obesity Science & Practice, participants with coach support lost up to 10 times more weight and 17 times more fat than those in a self-directed group. Trilivy coaches provide accountability and real-time support that help keep a plan on track.

“In a world of automation and AI, human connection is our superpower,” Jonnalagadda said. “A coach can meet a person in the exact moment a routine breaks down, and that is something a notification has never been able to do.”

A pass-fail approach to eating leaves no room for an imperfect meal. Under that framework, deviation from a meal plan feels like a reason to stop, and each restart interrupts the consistency that progress builds on.

Small, untracked additions, like a slightly larger serving or a few bites while cooking, add energy without registering as a meal. Weight loss also prompts the body to reduce spontaneous movement, which lowers daily energy use.

Both shifts happen gradually, and together they slow progress.

In a 2026 study from Annals of Internal Medicine, adults who slept about 80 minutes less per night gained roughly a pound and became more sedentary over six weeks. Their weight gain tracked closely with the drop in daily movement.

The finding connects short sleep to reduced activity, one of the everyday inputs a weight-loss plan relies on.

Restaurant meals, birthdays, work events, and holidays bring menus, timing and traditions that differ from an established meal plan.

Rules designed around a home kitchen do not offer guidance for those moments. A plan that accounts for eating out and celebrations gives people a way to navigate them.

Each of these nine factors traces back to two pressures: a body that works to protect its energy stores and daily routines that strict rules don’t account for.

A sustainable approach to weight management addresses both. It protects lean muscle, pairs balanced nutrition and physical activity with healthy daily habits, and builds in coaching and accountability that continue past the initial results, supporting long-term metabolic health.

On October 24, 1946, a V-2 rocket captured the first-ever photo of Earth from space.

While these grainy, black-and-white images might not look like much today, they were a huge deal at the time, because no one had ever seen Earth from space before.

The V-2 rocket launched from the White Sands Missile Range in New Mexico. It carried a 35-millimeter motion picture camera that captured a new frame every second and a half. The rocket soared to an altitude of about 65 miles before falling back to Earth.

Both the rocket and the camera were destroyed after crashing into the Earth at a speed of about 340 miles per hour. But the film survived because it was protected inside a steel case. Scientists had to drive out into the New Mexico desert to retrieve the film. When they saw the images for the first time, the scientists were literally jumping up and down with joy.

The ability to photograph Earth from space drove both an expanded interest to continue pushing into space and led to unexpected societal changes with regards to how humanity considered its home planet.

For military and intelligence agencies, the potential now existed for reconnaissance at a far larger scale than what was possible with high-altitude aircraft. That capability expanded to agriculture and meteorological purposes, eventually making Earth observation one of the most common reasons satellites and space stations are deployed into orbit today.

Earth photography from space also gave birth to the environmental movement, as people began to see the planet differently. The fragility of this orb became more apparent, as now Earth appeared a "blue marble" set against an endless black void.

#2846. These Are the 4 Best Big Cities for Fishing

The hottest fishing news, tips, and tactics

Solitude in nature. It’s one of the many reasons we fish. We’ll hike a little farther, climb a little higher, and burn a little more boat fuel to get as far away from other people and the sights and sounds of everyday life. Even if the best we can do is a (mildly) quieter park in the busy suburbs, we’ll take it. Fishing is so often our reason to “get out” that it’s easy to overlook opportunities for great action — and yes, even solitude — in some of the most urban environments in the country.

I was recently invited to fish with Captain Zach Flake in the shadow of Manhattan on the New Jersey side of the Hudson River. For 12 years of my life, I commuted to the city for work, and every day on the train I looked out at the vast marshes, flowing tidal rivers, and creeks in what is the most densely populated part of my home state. The New Jersey Meadowlands are not pretty. They are chock full of industrial buildings, railroad bridges, seas of shipping containers, and intricate networks of highway interchanges. But I still always wondered what was swimming in those waters. I found reaffirmation that sometimes it’s worth running toward the rust and concrete instead of away.

What struck me immediately when I met Flake at a very well-maintained public boat ramp in Secaucus, New Jersey, was that there was only one other boat launching. Mind you, this was a Saturday during peak fall striped bass season — jog 30 miles east to the coast and every ramp would be a zoo. What we tend to forget is that while there are more people in cities than out in the mountains, fewer of the people in the heart of concrete jungles are anglers. Flake had a milk run of spots in the Hackensack River and wasn’t concerned about being beaten to any of them.

Read Next: Fishing for Giant Blue Catfish in the Shadow of Capitol Hill

I’d witnessed the same lack of pressure years earlier fishing for peacock bass in Miami. The cacophony of commercial planes taking off over our heads, hip-hop and Cuban music blaring from open car windows, and the drone of the clogged highways were all present, though they seemed to melt away when you focused on the fishing. Not once throughout the day did we see another boat in the same waters. Of course, what good is a lack of pressure if there are no fish to catch? This brings us to the biggest myth about urban angling.

Thanks to so many changes in environmental law, a lot of waterways that cut through major cities are cleaner than they’ve ever been. Still, the notion persists that they’re largely void of life compared to surrounding “cleaner” water. The Hackensack River where I fished with Flake is a prime example of why that’s wrong.

Historically it was very polluted. It’s still not perfect, but it’s far better than it was years ago. It’s also a tidal system, drawing clean saltwater in and flushing it back out several times a day. All that manmade structure — the bridge pilings, bulkheads, piers, and rock walls — creates current breaks and depth variation. It is not only attractive to large migrating stripers, but a great rearing ground and over-winter habitat for smaller ones. All morning, we cast flies against concrete rubble, old, collapsed wooden docks, and banks strewn with everything from office chairs to soggy mattresses. And on cast after cast, we got hammered by stripers. In an odd way, I found the merging of the natural world with the manmade peaceful, but this was certainly not the only place you can find this different style of solitude.

I’ve never shied away from fishing big cities, and over the last 20 years, I’ve met some great anglers who spend most of their time in these less-than-natural settings. If you’re looking to give urban angling a try, consider some of my favorite locations.

He made a surprise appearance on The Graham Norton Show

Prince William, it turned out, celebrated his 42nd at the concert with his two eldest children, Prince George and Princess Charlotte. #1271. This Designer’s Long Island Family Home Has Been 40 Years in the Making

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE Decor editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Phillip Thomas’s Bellport retreat is a laboratory for color, collecting, and the occasional improbable antique.

Phillip Thomas’s first shop had a captive clientele. As a child, he commandeered the mudroom of his family’s house in Bellport, New York, filled it with yard-sale finds he had repainted or refinished, and ushered his parents’ friends inside. He called the enterprise Phillip & Co. (“There was no Co.,” he says. It was just him.) His customers, he recalls, were more or less obligated to buy something.

The Phillip & Co. space is now a pantry for extra tableware, but Thomas is still rearranging the house around it. His family has owned the late-19th-century home for 40 years, and the New York–based interior designer returns almost every weekend. The four-bedroom house includes two porches, a sunny sitting room overlooking the bay, and a living room anchored by a large fireplace. Thomas still sleeps in his childhood bedroom when there, and his parents, siblings, nieces, and friends gather at the property regularly, too.

Thomas’s Manhattan apartment is white and contemporary, filled with Latin American art. Bellport called for something different: “an American cottage by the sea,” he says. “I really just want to feel comfortable and I want everyone around me to feel at ease.” Getting there has involved decades of experimentation, a few unlikely purchases, and regular consultation with a formidable client committee: his family. “It’s a democracy,” he says. “It’s not a dictatorship.”

His love of Bellport extends well beyond the borders of his family’s property. The South Shore village has long been a mecca for creatives. The South Bay Art Association held its first official meeting at the village’s community center in 1957. Thomas, who has been coming to Bellport since he was four, remembers spending time in that same building as a child. In 2019, he led the renovation of its basement bowling alley and recreation space through the Bellport Village Program Fund.

There, he took Long Island’s Great South Bay as a starting point, introducing blue floors punctuated with red circles that evoked buoys, which village workers helped paint. The project restored a childhood gathering place for another generation, with color doing much of the work on a limited budget. It is a public expression of the instincts that have shaped his family’s house: keep what matters, reconsider what no longer works, and make the result inviting enough that people will use it.

At home, those decisions are rarely final. The dining room has gone from lacquered hunter green to red to its current painted pattern, where undulating lines surround a large, exuberantly colored painting. Elsewhere, Thomas used stencils to give walls the irregularity he seeks out in handmade things. “Having something painted onto the walls gives the room soul,” he says. “There is perfection in imperfection.”

The collections have accumulated on similar terms. Antique tools, gathered with his father over decades, line a hallway in carefully ordered arrangements. Thomas admires them as objects made by hand to facilitate other handmade things; displayed together, their worn wooden forms are sculptural. In the powder room, a coal-mining cart found a second career as a basin, paired with a vintage mirror frame fitted with glass cut to accommodate the plumbing. “You’d be surprised what I’ll put in the back of my car,” he says.

Other pieces traveled even farther. A campy painting of dogs playing poker came with his mother’s family from Yugoslavia to northern Chile, then to Santiago. After his grandmother died, the family agreed it belonged in Bellport, where it hangs in Thomas’s studio. The family’s routines shift with the seasons, which in turn shape how they use the house. Winter draws everyone toward the living-room fireplace, where the sofa is covered in a Schumacher fabric reminiscent of an American rag rug and throw pillows are made from textiles Thomas found in Turkey. From May through October, life moves out to the porch, where varied flexible seating facilitates long dinners and several conversations at once.

For the porch fireplace, Thomas collaborated with local artist Jay Sylvester on a sculpture of foraged branches surrounding a bull’s-eye mirror. The reflection ensures that even someone seated with their back to the bay gets a view.

Even the garden has benefited from his instinct to collect. Thomas acquired Chilean sculptures, including a Corten-steel piece inspired by wind-gnarled trees. For the latest overhaul, he collaborated with John Beitel of Landspace Inc., who has tended the gardens for 35 years. Together, they shaped a landscape where formal beds give way to looser planting along paths that frame views of the water, with red dogwood branches providing color through winter.

For all its evolution, the house’s greater purpose has remained remarkably consistent. Thomas’s nieces now invite their own friends; bedrooms are invariably full on summer weekends; and everyone helps cook or set the table. His mother, Sava, spends time on the porch before the household wakes, with a cup of coffee and the breeze coming off the water. After 40 years, she is still photographing the view. “I take photos and photos and they’re all the same,” she says. “But it’s beautiful.”
