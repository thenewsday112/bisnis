President of the European Central Bank, Christine Lagarde. File | Photo Credit: AP

European Central Bank President Christine Lagarde will resign nine months ahead of the end of her term, a move that opens the way for European leaders to choose a successor ahead of France’s presidential election next year.

Also read | Why have Central banks around the world started hiking rates? | Explained

Ms. Lagarde will leave February 7, the ECB said Thursday (October 29, 2026). Her eight-year term was to end October 31, 2027.

The resignation opens the way for complex political horse trading over her successor and to fill the posts of two other top officials who are leaving next year.

The announcement was not a surprise since there has been speculation that Lagarde would leave early to enable EU leaders to choose a successor while pro-EU French President Emmanuel Macron is still in office. That would guard against the possibility that right-wing presidential candidate Marine Le Pen might win the election and influence the choice.

“That she leaves early is no surprise and does not change the outlook for ECB policies,” said Holger Schmieding, chief economist at Berenberg bank.

European Council President Antonio Costa said that the selection process “will be initiated today and will proceed without delay.”

The term of another top ECB official — chief economist Philip Lane — is to end May 31, while another member of the ECB’s executive board, Isabel Schnabel, is resigning at year end to move to the International Monetary Fund. European leaders are expected to work out a package deal to apportion the three vacancies among member countries in a complex round of political bargaining.

EU leaders would likely seek to reach agreement on all three posts before a summit meeting on December 17-18, said Holger Schmieding, chief economist at Berenberg bank.

He said that leading candidates for Lagarde’s job appeared to be former Netherlands central bank head Klaas Knot and Spain’s Pablo Hernandez de Cos, the general manager of the Bank for International Settlements.

“The European Union is trying to guard itself at least somewhat against the risk that right-wing Marine Le Pen may win the French presidential election in April-May 2027,” Mr. Schmieding wrote in an email. “Lagarde’s successor will still be appointed while the staunchly pro-European centrist Emmanuel Macron still resides in the Elysee Palace.”

MS. Lagarde, the former head of the International Monetary Fund, was appointed in 2011 to succeed Mario Draghi.

#979. Africa seeks expanded Russian investment, industrial partnerships — AU Chairman

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

MOSCOW, October 29. /TASS/. African states are interested in expanding investments by Russian companies and establishing joint industrial production facilities on the continent, African Union (AU) Chairperson and President of Burundi Evariste Ndayishimiye announced at the plenary session of the third Russia-Africa Summit.

"We invite Russian enterprises to invest more in Africa, form partnerships within the industrial sector, develop projects with our local partners, and facilitate the transfer of expertise," he said.

According to the AU Chairperson, African countries seek to move away from an economic model where the continent primarily exports raw materials and imports finished goods.

"We need to develop our own industrial capacity, modernize our agriculture, strengthen our energy infrastructure and logistics networks, and invest more in research and technological development," Ndayishimiye stressed.

He noted that Africa possesses significant natural resources, a growing market, and a young population, while Russia can offer technologies, industrial capabilities, and expert knowledge--a combination that opens up broad prospects for mutually beneficial cooperation.

The AU Chairperson also underscored the need to establish domestic value-added production chains on the continent.

"Our continent must become a genuine hub for production and sales, rather than remaining merely a consumer market," he declared.

Ndayishimiye added that developing productive investments and industrial partnerships, alongside facilitating mutual market access for Russian and African enterprises, should be a priority for the Russia-Africa Partnership Forum’s 2027-2029 action plan.

Haiti’s government has announced new election dates as the armed gangs who terrorize the country continue to fight against Haitian police and soldiers, who are now backed by the recent arrival of more than 2,000 troops from El Salvador.

The Provisional Electoral Council said late Wednesday that the first round of general elections will be held next July 18 and the second round on Oct. 4.

Haiti has not held elections in more than a decade and is run by an unelected prime minister. The presidency has been vacant since President Jovenel Moïse was killed in 2021.

The council said it expects to publish the final candidate list, including those running for the presidency or the Senate, by mid-January, with May 20 marking the end of voter registration. Among the presidential hopefuls are former presidents Michel Martelly, Claude Joseph and Jocelerme Privert, along with a former president of Haiti's Chamber of Deputies, Gary Bodeau, and Public Prosecutor Jean Ernest Muscadin. Martelly and Bodeau have been sanctioned by the U.S. following allegations of corruption, while Muscadin has been accused of summary executions of suspected criminals.

Among those seeking to become mayor of Port-au-Prince, Haiti's capital, is Samuel Joisil, leader of the civilian brigade of Canape-Vert, whose members include vigilantes known for successfully pushing back the violent gangs seeking to take over their community.

The first round of elections was initially scheduled for Dec. 13, but was delayed by gang violence in Haiti’s central region and in Port-au-Prince. Four aviation technicians from El Salvador working with a U.N.-backed gang suppression force went missing on Oct. 18 in the capital after coming under attack.

In response, Salvadoran President Nayib Bukele sent more than 2,000 troops to Haiti to help rescue the four technicians, who the Haitian government says were abducted.

Scrutiny of Salvadoran troops in Haiti intensifies

El Salvador’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs said in a statement late Wednesday that Bukele had spoken with Haitian Prime Minister Alix Didier Fils-Aimé about the security operation to rescue the technicians.

Meanwhile, Haiti’s government issued a statement saying the two leaders are reinforcing cooperation within the framework of the U.N.-backed gang suppression force.

Experts have questioned exactly what role the Salvadoran troops are playing in Haiti.

Romain Le Cour, head of the Haiti Observatory at the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime, said their rules of engagement, chain of command, objectives and duration “remain opaque.” He questioned what kind of coordination they might have with the gang suppression force, Haitian police and Vectus Global, the private security firm founded by former U.S. Navy SEAL and Blackwater founder Erik Prince.

“Are we witnessing yet another model of deployment, parallel to the multilateral framework? The role of the United States, which supported and facilitated this deployment, also deserves scrutiny,” he said.

Le Cour said the number of Salvadoran troops in Haiti will surpass that of personnel in the gang suppression force, raising questions about the future of the U.N.-backed force.

“What does that mean for its authority, its coherence and its future?”

He added: “the most basic question remains unanswered: why were the Salvadoran technicians in a gang-controlled neighborhood in the first place?”

Outcry grows over deaths and injuries of civilians

Le Cour and others also raised concerns about the safety of Haitian civilians caught in a gun battle between Haitian gangs and the international forces.

On Wednesday, Doctors Without Borders issued a statement calling on “the authorities and all parties involved to spare civilians and ensure access to health care facilities for all the wounded and sick, without distinction.”

The aid agency said it treated 30 patients injured by gunfire or drone explosions from Oct. 18-26, and that it took some of the wounded several hours to reach hospitals run by Doctors Without Borders.

“Roads and bridges are blocked, and any travel is extremely risky,” said Davina Hayles, Doctors Without Borders head of mission in Haiti. “In a capital city where the health care system has collapsed and many facilities are closed, it is essential to ensure there is access to functioning health care facilities like ours.”

More than 5,700 people have been killed or wounded in Haiti from January to mid-September, according to the U.N. Those recently killed include two Haitians who worked for the Norwegian Refugee Council.

“We call on all parties concerned, including Haitian authorities as well as the international community, to do everything in their power to protect civilians, uphold international law, and prevent further violence,” the council said in a statement Thursday. “Neither the Haitian society nor the outside world can tolerate that civilians, including aid workers, are killed with impunity.”

#653. Make no bones about it, the giant skeleton has become an American Halloween fixture

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

It reared its bony head in 2020, during that first, chaotic pandemic year that was rife with grief and fear but also toilet paper hoarding and questionable lockdown-inspired purchases. Behold Skelly, the 12-foot, 90-pound skeleton, on sale for $299 from Home Depot, just in time for Halloween — at least, if you can get your hands on one.

Many did, because this is America. And when we get sad, stressed or bored, we shop, drowning our collective anxieties in things that we definitely don't need but most certainly want.

So, beginning in 2020, Skelly showed up on suburban lawns from California to New Jersey and on brownstone stoops in Brooklyn, naked or dressed in a costume, towering over jack-o’-lanterns and plastic gravestones with spooky puns like “Rest In Pieces.” It didn't take long for people to realize that keeping Skelly out year-round meant new opportunities to dress him for every season: Christmas, Fourth of July, or even just a bikini for summer.

In 2024, Skelly got glowing LCD eyeballs. And in 2026, a Bluetooth connection lets you record sounds or speak into it live, entertaining (or unsettling) your whole block. He also has a dog, a cat, and a smaller, 6½-foot version for those who can't quite commit to a giant. It has sold out before Halloween every year.

“It is the ultimate American decoration,” says Aubrey Horowitz, merchant of Decorative Holiday at Home Depot. “We really love this sense of nostalgia and having some form of connection with our communities and the people that live around us. And frankly, you know, a lot of times bigger is better.”

I did not celebrate Halloween until I arrived in the U.S. from Eastern Europe at age 13. I was shocked by the sheer size of everything: giant cars, highways with all those lanes, enormous gallons of milk instead of our polite liters, skyscrapers so tall they were licking the clouds.

We also weren't used to whimsy. Gaudy yard decor, whether for Halloween or Christmas, was as foreign a concept as warehouse shopping or smiling at strangers.

Skelly would have certainly stopped us in our tracks. It still does. The uniquely American delight it brings to me, an immigrant still often in awe of Americans' seasonal joy and over-the-top ornamentations, is contagious.

The U.S. did not invent Halloween. But Halloween excess, with its pillowcases full of candy, elaborate costumes for everyone from babies to adults to dogs (and a few unlucky cats), could not be more American.

And why have a 5′8″ skeleton when you can have a 12-foot one?

The brave captain who fought off his co-pilot during the attempted hijacking of a passenger jet last month has revealed his injuries from the savage axe attack.

Smit Machchhar was awarded the UAE’s First Class Order of Zayed II on Wednesday for his bravery in helping to thwart what authorities believe was an attempted suicide attack targeting Israel’s Ben Gurion airport on September 30.

Flydubai Flight 1073 from Dubai to Tel Aviv plunged 16,000ft in 30 seconds before the attacker was overwhelmed and two other pilots who were on board were able to safely land in Saudi Arabia.

Capt Machchhar, still wearing a sling and with stitches in the back of his head, told UAE president Sheikh Mohammed during yesterday’s ceremony that it would still be another two months before he heals fully.

“I have an idea. When you heal, I want to have a session with you to train you how to fight,” the Emirati president told him, suggesting he would benefit from learning jiu-jitsu.

“It’s my biggest honour,” replied the captain.

The aircraft was in Saudi airspace when the co-pilot attacked Machchhar with a crash axe, typically kept in the cockpit for emergency use.

UAE public prosecutors have said the plan was inspired by the September 11, 2001, attacks on the United States.

Speaking from a hospital bed in Abu Dhabi earlier this month, Machchhar said that when the first blow landed, he thought something had gone “horribly wrong the aircraft structure”, before quickly realising he was being attacked.

“The next thought was I need to get help in here. My immediate action was to free myself from the seat belts and I tried to just block the blows as much as I could,” he said, pointing to his arm that was wounded as he defended himself.

“The blows were coming on the head. Immediately my hand went to the knob which opens the door and screaming for my colleagues, screaming his name. I believe that was the first time when I lost my consciousness.”

Machchhar was covered with blood and on the floor when he managed to open the cockpit door, a spokesperson for Israeli prime minister Benjamin Netanyahu said.

Yaniv Hayun, one of the men who incapacitated the attacker, described running to the cockpit as the plane nosedived.

“We pulled the assailant out, I pulled him out. After I pulled him out we took control of the plane, I pulled up the yoke (steering control of the plane), I straightened the plane out a bit,” he said.

Netanyahu earlier said that the co-pilot, whom he identified as an Omani national, had undergone radical Islamist indoctrination and was clearly suicidal, but that so far a motive for his actions had not been established.

Israel does not have official diplomatic relations with Oman, and Netanyahu has said pilots from such countries were not permitted to operate flights to Israel. It was not clear how the co-pilot came to be assigned to the flight to Tel Aviv.

#494. Iranian media debate new war amid Hormuz attacks, military buildup

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The prospect of another US-Iran war has fuelled debate over whether Washington is preparing for a prolonged campaign of military and economic pressure or whether diplomacy can still avert a wider conflict.

The debate comes amid continuing attacks on shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, warnings from Tehran that it is better prepared for another confrontation, and fresh revelations about Iran's efforts to circumvent sanctions and the US naval blockade to import military equipment.

Iran's Revolutionary Guards said Thursday that three tankers had struck naval mines in the strait over the previous 24 hours. Vice President Mohammadreza Aref said Tehran was managing the strategic waterway and that its defensive readiness had improved since the 12-day and 40-day wars.

Analyses published in Iranian newspapers in recent days offer differing assessments of the likelihood of another confrontation and the prospects for a negotiated settlement.

The economic daily Donya-ye Eghtesad said war was not inevitable, pointing to continuing backchannel contacts despite deep disagreements between the two sides.

Political analyst Rahman Ghahramanpour told the paper that another conflict would be unlikely to end after only a few days, warning that even limited US military action could become part of a sustained campaign combining strikes and sanctions.

Ghahramanpour described a possible eight-to-twelve-month strategy aimed at weakening Iran's ability to resist US demands.

Reuters investigation published Thursday revealed how Iran has used a network of more than 5,000 military-linked agencies to import at least $2 billion worth of equipment, explosives and heavy-duty trucks since January 2025, circumventing Western sanctions and the US naval blockade.

The findings shed light on Tehran's efforts to sustain its military capabilities despite the pressure campaign.

Former diplomat Kourosh Ahmadi challenged predictions that another war was imminent, arguing that the remaining disagreements between Tehran and Washington might not be substantial enough to justify the costs of a new conflict.

Writing in the reformist daily Shargh, Ahmadi said President Donald Trump's statements should not be treated as evidence that he had decided to attack Iran, while warning that another war could have devastating consequences.

He identified three broad options available to Washington: pursue a diplomatic settlement, continue economic and military pressure to weaken Iran, or undertake another military operation.

Diplomacy remained possible, Ahmadi argued, because the dispute concerned the sequencing of concessions more than fundamentally incompatible objectives.

The moderate news website Fararu examined the damage already being inflicted by the prolonged stalemate.

Former diplomat Fereydoun Majlesi argued that Iran's state of “neither war nor peace” was itself becoming a major economic burden, with uncertainty prompting businesses to hold on to cash and avoid investment.

Such decisions weaken production, employment and economic growth over time, he said, even without another military confrontation.

Both sides, Majlesi said, continued to define an acceptable agreement primarily in terms of their own interests while resisting the concessions necessary to bridge the gap.

He warned that prolonged uncertainty could make an eventual settlement more difficult as economic pressure and mutual distrust intensified.

For Ghahramanpour, the question was not simply whether Washington might strike again, but whether military pressure could secure political concessions without triggering a wider war.

The departure of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu could benefit Iran even if his successor maintained the same policies toward Tehran, Khorasan, a conservative Iranian newspaper close to Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, said on Thursday.

The newspaper said a change in Israeli leadership would not necessarily reduce military threats against Iran but could weaken the political and diplomatic networks Netanyahu has built during his years in power.

"Ultimately, Netanyahu's departure is in Iran's interest, not because his successor would necessarily adopt a friendly approach, but because removing one of the main architects of the confrontation with Iran would reduce the accumulated political and diplomatic capacity behind it," Khorasan wrote.

The assessment came after Israel's parliamentary election on Tuesday produced no clear winner, leaving Netanyahu and his main rival, former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, seeking support to form a government.

With nearly 86% of votes counted on Wednesday, Israeli broadcasters projected Netanyahu's right-wing bloc would secure 53 seats in the 120-member Knesset, compared with 48 for Eisenkot's opposition bloc and 19 for Arab-led parties.

Neither main bloc reached the 61 seats needed for a majority, leaving the outcome dependent on coalition negotiations.

Netanyahu's experience seen as key factor

Khorasan said Israeli political groups shared many positions on security matters despite their differences and that Eisenkot, a former military chief, was not opposed to confrontation with Iran.

It argued, however, that Netanyahu had built political and diplomatic networks over many years that a successor could find difficult to replace.

"Netanyahu is not merely a prime minister," the newspaper wrote, adding that he had developed extensive political, regional and international connections and considerable experience influencing US decision-making.

The newspaper said Netanyahu knew how to make Iran a central issue in Washington's foreign policy and connect security developments with political opportunities.

"Removing such a figure from the top of power, even if the basic structure of Israeli policy remains unchanged, means losing part of this accumulated capacity," it said.

"Netanyahu and his allies have built ties over the years between the Israeli government and some opponents of the Islamic Republic, particularly the monarchist movement," the newspaper wrote.

"Netanyahu's departure could weaken part of this political image, although one should not expect all these relationships to disappear with a change in leadership," it added.

It said political divisions within a future Israeli coalition could make decision-making more difficult, although such disagreements would not automatically reduce the risk of military action.

Election leaves Netanyahu and Eisenkot seeking coalition partners

Netanyahu's Likud-led bloc emerged ahead of Eisenkot's opposition in the preliminary results but remained short of a parliamentary majority.

Parties representing Arab Israelis were projected to secure an unprecedented 19 seats following high voter turnout, giving them a potentially important role in determining the next government.

Eisenkot could seek support from Arab-led parties to secure a majority, although several parties within his opposition bloc have opposed such cooperation.

Khorasan said the high turnout among Arab voters was one of the election's most important developments but questioned whether Eisenkot could reach an agreement with Arab parties while maintaining support from his existing allies.

Reuters reported that Netanyahu's electoral performance had strengthened his position in coalition negotiations despite the loss of his previous parliamentary majority.

The prime minister will remain in office in a caretaker capacity while the process of forming a new government continues.

Iran remains central to Israeli political debate

The election took place during the US-Israeli war on Iran, which began in February, with the possibility of further military action remaining an issue in Israeli politics.

Netanyahu has repeatedly emphasized the Iranian threat during the campaign and has received public support from US President Donald Trump.

Eisenkot has criticized Netanyahu's handling of security matters and warned against the political use of military decisions.

Khorasan said Netanyahu's departure would not necessarily bring a change in Israel's approach toward Iran, noting that different political groups shared positions on key security issues.

"Internal disagreements do not necessarily lead to a reduction in military threats," the newspaper wrote, adding that political competition could sometimes result in more aggressive behavior.

It said the removal of Netanyahu would not end the confrontation between Israel and Iran but could reduce the ability of Israeli leaders to coordinate political and diplomatic efforts against Tehran.

"Sometimes removing one player does not, by itself, end the game, but it can make the game more difficult for the opposing side," Khorasan concluded.

A reported order by Iran’s supreme leader to end the state of “neither war nor peace” may signal a turn towards diplomacy. But a closer reading suggests Tehran is offering no concessions, only restating its longstanding demands for ending the confrontation with Washington.

On Tuesday, Iranian state media announced that Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei had received Maj. Gen. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, and ordered an end to “the war and the ‘neither war, nor peace’ conditions.”

Abroad, that was read as Tehran wanting out. It wants nothing of the sort. Read the whole sentence, and the “order” is the same demand the Islamic Republic has made since the shooting stopped in April: the stalemate ends when Washington capitulates.

The news cycle started with a readout released by Mojtaba’s official website, which was reproduced word for word across the domestic press. The item is a summary, not a transcript. Every line attributed to Mojtaba is in reported speech, without a single direct quotation.

He is said to have called for “exerting control over the Strait of Hormuz,” declared the enemy “desperate,” bound Iran’s “interests” to those of its regional allies in the “Axis of Resistance,” and called for an end to the war “through realization of the conditions set out by the country’s officials.”

That last clause is the whole message, and the conditions it refers to are no secret. Rezaei himself set them out in September: an end to the war on all fronts, Lebanon included; the release of Iran’s frozen funds; an end to the US naval blockade of Iranian ports; and guarantees against renewed strikes.

Earlier versions added compensation for war damages, the lifting of sanctions and recognition of Tehran’s control over the strait. In short: Tehran keeps its grip on Hormuz, its regional allies stay in place, and Washington concedes. Then, and only then, the stalemate ends. Not one condition softened, not one position moved.

Now consider what the readout is not. It is not independent evidence that a meeting took place. It carries no date and no place. Under Ali Khamenei, the leader’s office opened such items with “this morning” or “this afternoon” and named the venue, usually the Imam Khomeini Hussainiya in Tehran. That convention has quietly disappeared.

The readout carries no image, either. No independently verified new photograph, audio or video of Mojtaba Khamenei has surfaced since the February strike that killed his father. The same was true of the four earlier meetings state media has reported for him, twice with the president, once with the parliament speaker and once with the chief justice.

Eight months in, the authorities have offered only written accounts of these meetings, without independently verifiable visual or audio evidence. When and where they took place, and whether the official accounts are accurate, remain unverified.

Some have nonetheless read the announcement as significant, on the grounds that Mojtaba called for an end to the war. The record says otherwise: the conditional clause simply restates Tehran’s standing terms.

A call to end the stalemate with no change in terms is the oldest tactic in the Islamic Republic’s repertoire: stall, buy time, rebuild; when the pressure rises, flirt with peace; and when an opening appears, strike.

The readout did not specify when or where the meeting took place. Although it was published Tuesday, a day after Saudi, Turkish and Pakistani envoys visited Tehran, the text does not establish that the meeting occurred that day or that it was connected to the diplomatic visit.

What “neither war, nor peace” means in the mouth of a Khamenei, the family’s own usage helps explain.

In August 2018, Ali Khamenei told a public audience that there would be no war and no negotiation with the Trump administration, because talks would end to Tehran’s detriment. The government daily Iran bannered it the next morning: “there will be no war, and we will not negotiate.” In May 2019, he called negotiation “poison” and declared the nation’s choice to be “resistance.” The limbo was Tehran’s own posture, and a boast.

After the twelve-day war of June 2025, he flipped the phrase. Addressing the cabinet on September 7, he said hope and productivity had to prevail over the “neither war, nor peace state that the enemy wants to impose on us” as part of its “psychological war.” His remedy was self-reliance: “don’t wait for external developments.” The limbo was now the enemy’s doing; the answer was still not to talk.

President Masoud Pezeshkian has leaned on that speech ever since, most recently on October 24, when he said, “Many are struggling for their evening bread” because of the war and the stalemate, adding, “One cannot plan in limbo... The martyred Leader ordered us to go and finish the job.”

The IRGC’s press corps answered within a day. Javan headlined that exiting the stalemate “will not come through negotiation” but through “changing the enemy’s calculations.” Fars News presented the phrase as the doctrine of the “martyred Imam,” with negotiation a “strategic trap.”

Tuesday’s readout appears to settle that argument in the IRGC’s favor. The son inherits the father’s phrase and makes it a condition for ending the confrontation: the stalemate is over when the other side accepts Tehran’s terms.

Three uses of one phrase, one underlying position. Refuse talks in 2018. Blame the enemy for the stalemate in 2025. Demand an end to the stalemate on Tehran’s terms in 2026. At no point has “ending neither war nor peace” meant Tehran moving an inch.

Meanwhile, the authorities continue to announce meetings involving a leader who has not appeared publicly in independently verified images or recordings. This is not an order to end the stalemate. It is the stalemate, restated as a demand.

The IRGC-linked Tasnim news agency has set out the case for Iran to withdraw from the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), adding to a renewed debate over Tehran's nuclear strategy as the region remains caught between diplomacy and war.

In a commentary published Wednesday, Tasnim argued that the treaty had failed to protect Iran's interests and questioned the benefits of remaining a member after successive wars with Israel and the United States.

The intervention comes as Qatar promotes a diplomatic framework reportedly linking the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, sanctions relief and efforts to reduce tensions involving Yemen's Houthis.

President Masoud Pezeshkian said Wednesday that his government would do everything it could to fulfil the leader's wishes.

Tasnim's commentary, titled “Why Should Iran Leave the NPT?”, highlights divisions over how Tehran should respond to international pressure on its nuclear program.

Hardline politicians have repeatedly raised the prospect of leaving the NPT during periods of nuclear negotiations, arguing that the treaty has failed to protect Iran's interests.

Tasnim identified three central objectives of the treaty: preventing the spread of nuclear weapons, encouraging nuclear-armed states to disarm and protecting members' right to use nuclear technology for peaceful purposes.

The agency argued that the treaty had failed on all three counts.

It accused the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) of abandoning its role as an impartial technical body and becoming politically aligned with the United States and Israel.

The agency had failed to defend Iran's rights when its nuclear sites were attacked, so withdrawal would be a legitimate option for Tehran, Tasnim argued.

Tasnim maintained that withdrawal would not necessarily mean developing nuclear weapons. Some lawmakers, however, have explicitly linked the two.

On Tuesday, MP Abolfazl Aboutorabi called for Iran to withdraw from the NPT and begin building a nuclear bomb.

Other hardline figures, including members of the ultraconservative Paydari Front, have advocated withdrawal, arguing that continued membership has failed to prevent attacks on Iranian nuclear facilities despite their being subject to international safeguards.

The calls have met resistance from other establishment-aligned media, reflecting disagreements over the potential costs of withdrawal.

Khabar Online, a pragmatic-conservative outlet, sharply criticized the campaign in an October 25 report, singling out the Paydari Front and questioning the political judgment of those advocating departure from the treaty.

Its headline described the proposal as a radical demand dating back two decades, with “neither head nor tail.”

The reformist Arman Melli newspaper has also taken a position against withdrawal, broadly reflecting the Pezeshkian administration's preference for maintaining Iran's existing treaty commitments.

Nour News, which is close to the SNSC, adopted a more cautious approach in its own October 25 analysis.

It argued that neither withdrawal nor continued membership could guarantee Iran's security and that dissatisfaction with the IAEA did not, by itself, justify abandoning the treaty.

The outlet also warned that withdrawal from the NPT would create further political and diplomatic complications, while remaining in the treaty would still allow Tehran to restrict aspects of its cooperation with the IAEA.

Withdrawal, it concluded, should not be considered without a clear plan for what would follow.

Leaving the NPT would have implications beyond Iran's relations with the IAEA.

Under Article X of the treaty, a state party may exercise its right to withdraw if it determines that extraordinary events have jeopardized its supreme interests. It must give three months' notice to other treaty parties and the UN Security Council, explaining the events behind its decision.

Withdrawal could further complicate Tehran's relations with Western governments and raise questions about the future of international monitoring of its nuclear activities.

Despite the calls from hardline lawmakers and media outlets, Iran has announced no decision to leave the treaty.

The government continues to maintain that its nuclear program is peaceful, and neither the SNSC nor Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei has publicly endorsed withdrawal.

The competing arguments underscore a debate within Iran's political establishment: whether abandoning the treaty would strengthen Tehran's position after repeated military attacks or leave it more isolated and vulnerable as regional powers seek a diplomatic settlement.

The Supreme Council of Cyberspace, established by former Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei in 2012, already plays a central role in internet policymaking.

For now, lawmakers remain divided over the proposal's prospects, while the refusal to release its official text has left its scope, legal implications and potential impact on internet access unclear.

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Sign up now: Get ST's newsletters delivered to your inbox

TAIZ, Oct 29 - In a hospital in Yemen's Taiz, a 25-year-old pregnant mother of two lay immobilised after a bullet lodged in her spine while she was trapped inside her home without food or water for two days.

The UN and aid agencies say health needs in the besieged city of Taiz are critical, amid acute shortages of medicine, oxygen, surgical gloves, anesthetic and fuel. Hospitals near Yemen's front lines are facing large influxes of patients, treating up to 700 war wounded in a single week, according to the Red Cross.

Amal al-Sabri said she was injured by a sniper bullet in the village of al-Shaqb — perched on the slopes of Mount Sabir overlooking Taiz — as an escalation in fighting between the Saudi-backed government and the Iran-aligned Houthi movement takes a devastating toll on civilians.

Medics at Al-Thawra hospital in Taiz said they could not perform a scan because she was over four months pregnant.

"The bullet entered from the side and is now lodged near the spine. They (the doctors) couldn't remove it because of the fetus,” Sabri told Reuters. "Now I am giving up the fetus."

Taiz governorate, which hosts about half of the 281,000 people who have been newly displaced in Yemen since the start of August, has been largely isolated since October 3, with fighting preventing humanitarian supplies from entering and civilians from leaving.

Taiz, Marib and the Red Sea coast are seeing some of the highest casualties, with patients arriving at hospitals with gunshot wounds and complex blast injuries, according to the Red Cross.

“The healthcare system across Yemen is at a breaking point,” said Red Cross spokesperson Hachem Osseiran.

Even before the latest fighting, more than 40% of hospitals and healthcare facilities across the country were already either closed or partially functioning following years of protracted conflict, he said.

There are also concerns about the outbreak of disease as water runs short and rubbish accumulates on the street.

Throughout Yemen, primary care for pregnant women is disrupted. In some hospitals near the front lines, maternity wards are being converted into emergency rooms, Osseiran said.

“Some of them (doctors) have to make a very difficult and painful choice: Do I help this woman deliver her baby, or do I rush to a person who is bleeding out because of a gunshot wound?” he said.

In Taiz city, the UN says community midwives have stopped working due to restrictions on movement.

Not only are health services stretched, they are also under attack. A hospital in Taiz's Sabir Al-Mawadim district was hit by mortar fragments for the second time this month, while a strike on an ambulance killed its driver and a paramedic, according to the UN.

On Monday, another strike that hit near Mocha General Hospital — where medical charity Médecins Sans Frontières supports the maternity and pediatric wards — damaged the building and triggered panic among patients.

“Such incidents are preventing patients from coming to the hospital for fear of their lives,” MSF said. REUTERS

#690. What was the best and worst news for small firms in the Budget?

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Chancellor John Healey gave with one hand and took with the other

The Independent SME Channel is brought to you by Hostinger.

The 2026 Budget is in the books and it will not go down as one of the most notable ever seen.

Chancellor John Healey was clearly keen to tread lightly and steer away from anything that would rock the boat for an already struggling economy.

There was still some substance there from the perspective of small businesses, some of which was positive – but some that was bad news.

Raising small business rates relief thresholds to £18,000 and £21,000 is a decent helping hand for some businesses.

Phil Vernon, head of specialist business rates projects at PwC UK, described it as a welcome boost for the high street.

“It will take around 70,000 more shops, cafés and pubs out of business rates entirely, and a two-year real-terms freeze gives the rest some certainty,” he said.

“But small business rates relief is generally limited to businesses with a single property, so a local baker or café owner with a second shop will often get none of it, though they will still benefit from the freeze. That also sharpens the cliff edge: opening a second site could now cost a growing business more in lost relief than ever before.”

Another helpful move is the trade allowance being tripled. While it has only moved from £1,000 to £3,000, it will take some people with small side hustles out of paying tax.

Lee Murphy, managing director of the Accountancy Partnership, said: “Raising the trading allowance is probably the most useful measure announced in the Budget for anyone with a side hustle. From selling on Vinted to tutoring in the evenings, anyone with a side income can earn up to £3,000 per year and have nothing to file.

“In a practical sense, this takes a lot of people out of self-assessment altogether. My warning to those who still need to submit a return is that the allowance can still be used to reduce your tax bill, but only instead of claiming expenses, and does not sit on top of them.

“If your costs are more than £3,000, claiming them could be better. You’d also still need to keep records to prove your income.”

Other measures that provide a small boost included changes to mileage cost rules and paternity leave entitlement.

Josh Toovey, head of policy and research at IPSE, said: “Some of these tweaks are genuinely helpful. Reversing the cash ISA cut, better mileage rates, parental pay and an increased trading allowance will all make a difference, and business rates reform is a welcome boost for our smallest high street businesses.

“But taken together, they don’t go anywhere near far enough.”

The minimum wage has been raised by 3.9 per cent, which will add to the cost of running many businesses.

For workers aged 21 and over, it will rise from £12.71 an hour to £13.21.

The rate for workers aged 18-20 will go from £10.85 an hour to £11.27.

Large corporations may not feel the squeeze too much and have the market power to pass the cost through to their customers, but many SME businesses could feel a real hit to their books.

Mr Healey also made some ominous comments about changes to filing requirements for self-assessment taxpayers.

He said HMRC will “reform” self-assessment in 2029. He wants to bring these taxpayers “into line with the majority” by replacing large annual or semi-annual bills with smaller, more regular payments.

This is already in motion to some degree, with self-assessment payers above staggered thresholds having to start filing quarterly returns. The government has badged it up as Making Tax Digital. The first threshold was £50,000 and came in this past April.

The aim from the government’s point of view is not to make life easier or more profitable for businesses, but to get more money into HMRC, more quickly.

This is an attack on small businesses, as it creates a much bigger admin burden. Filing a tax return quarterly is essentially four times the work as filing annually, however you dress it up.

The other aspect is that it will cost people money in a couple of ways. If you are unable to do your own returns, it means you will need to pay an accountant. It is highly unlikely accountants will take care of four returns for a similar price to one.

Another way it will cost people money is that a pay as you earn system will move money from a small business bank account to the government’s pocket earlier.

Small businesses will therefore lose all the interest that would have been earned from that money being in their accounts during the year.

#699. Fact check: Disposable incomes set to rise each year after 2026

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

Responding to the Budget on Wednesday, Opposition leader Kemi Badenoch claimed the Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) has forecast “real household disposable income: lower every year”.

Robert Jenrick, Reform UK’s Treasury spokesman, made a similar claim on GB News on Thursday. Speaking of the Chancellor, Mr Jenrick said: “By his own figures he’s saying that living standards for people in this country will decline every single year for the next five years.”

The OBR’s forecast sees real household disposable income (RHDI) – which is likely the measure both politicians are referring to – fall this year, but rise in each subsequent year until 2031.

Compared with its previous forecast in March, RHDI annual growth has been downgraded in some years by the OBR and upgraded in others. However, thanks to the fall in 2026, RHDI is lower in each year of the forecast than the OBR had previously estimated. It appears this is what Mrs Badenoch was referring to.

It is unclear what Mr Jenrick was referring to as there does not appear to be any measure in the OBR forecast by which living standards decline every single year.

The OBR calls real household disposable income “a useful though incomplete measure of living standards”.

According to the OBR’s report, RHDI is forecast to fall this year, but then rise in each of the following years in the forecast. It falls by 0.6% in 2026, then rises by 0.2% in 2027, 0.9% in 2028, 0.7% in 2029, 1.1% in 2030, and 1.0% in 2031.

Compared with its March 2026 forecast, RHDI annual growth was downgraded by 1.3 percentage points this year and 0.5 points in 2027. It was upgraded by 0.2 percentage points in 2028 and 2030, while there was no change to the 2029 forecast. There was no previous forecast for 2031.

Chart 2.6 in the OBR report shows that compared with March’s forecast, RHDI in this forecast is lower each fiscal year than what was previously expected. This is because of the falls early on in the forecast. This is what the Conservative Party pointed to when asked to clarify Mrs Badenoch’s comment.

RHDI falls in 2026, but rises each of the next five years – growing by £766 to 2031. However, compared with the March forecast, RHDI will be £356 lower in 2026 than previously thought, and £250 lower in 2030. These figures are all in constant 2023 prices.

What other forecasts could measure living standards?

The OBR calls real GDP per person “an indicator of average living standards”. However, this increases in every year of the forecast. The lowest rise over the period is 0.9% in 2027, while the highest is 1.6% in 2029.

Average real earnings – both weekly and hourly – could also be seen as a way of measuring how well off people in employment are. However, these also rise in every year of the OBR’s forecast, apart from a dip in 2027.

Reform UK has been contacted to clarify Mr Jenrick’s comments.

A family have paid tribute to a “truly special” teenager who was killed in a two-car crash.

Suffolk Constabulary were called by the East of England Ambulance Service at 10.15am on Sunday after reports of the crash on Debenham Road, Little Stonham.

The vehicles involved were a red Peugeot 107 and a silver Volkswagen Passat.

Two teenagers travelling in the Peugeot died in the collision while the occupants of the Volkswagen sustained minor injuries, police said.

The driver of the Peugeot, now named as Amelia Miller, 19, of Stowmarket, was taken to Addenbrooke’s Hospital in Cambridge where she died of her injuries.

Her family paid tribute to her on Thursday in a statement issued through police.

They said: “Amelia was a daughter, sister, aunty and cousin.
