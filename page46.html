#1207. In Brooklyn Decker and Andy Roddick’s North Carolina Home, Southern Hospitality Meets Modern Charm

The home was, decidedly, not love at first sight for Brooklyn Decker and Andy Roddick. “One of the rooms had caved in; there was water pouring in through the ceiling,” Decker says of the first time the couple saw the Charlotte, North Carolina, Georgian-style house.

But even in its dilapidated state, the 1960s-era home was still intriguing: It was located in the favored neighborhood for the young family, and its traditional, elegant proportions were still evident through its disrepair. At first it was Roddick, the former number-one men’s singles tennis player, who urged Decker to keep the house on their short list. Soon model and actor Decker, who grew up in nearby Matthews, warmed at the chance to rehabilitate a proper Carolina home with details like dental crown molding and classic brick. “We’d moved so many times [before this house],” she says. “And we were excited to try something new. For us, that meant a historic house with some old Southern bones.”

In the entry, Roddick is seated on a vintage sofa covered in a Zak and Fox fabric. Decker stands next to the walnut staircase built by Masterpiece Staircase and finished by Tony Montognese. The custom chandelier is by Kalin Asenov, the rug is vintage, and the artwork is by Kieran Brennan Hinton.

The family’s living room, clad in mahogany paneling, is Decker and Roddick’s favorite space in the whole home. The fireplace was designed by Decker, fabricated by Tyler Ernsberger, and stained by Tony Montognese, with animal carvings done by Stan Dunham. The 1930s coffee table is by Paul László, and the light fixture is a vintage Longobard for Poliarte. The chairs, clockwise from top right, are by BDDW, vintage Swedish sourced from PRB Collection, and a vintage Guglielmo Ulrich.

The home’s foyer received the biggest glow-up, with the staircase being entirely redesigned from a curving one. Chandelier and flush-mount light designed by Decker and Kalin Asenov, sideboard by BDDW, the mirror and sofa are vintage, millwork by Bill Truitt Woodworks (with finishes by Tony Montognese), and the walnut flooring is by Richard Marshall Fine Flooring.

Decker and Roddick were no strangers to home renovation, but as evidenced just from their first walkthrough, this latest undertaking would be on a scale they had not yet explored. The home had been little touched since its construction in 1964, and the couple would be only the second owners. Aside from obvious fixes like leaking ceilings and sagging floors, the layout, which was chopped up into a warren of tight quarters, needed a full gut to allow the space to breathe and gain more light. “We were parents of two young kids,” Decker says. “So the question was, How do we respect the grandeur of the house but also make it feel hospitable to a young family and one that can live up to the demands of everyday life?”

The dining room shows off many of the home’s thematic colors, including red, yellow, and green. Drapery fabric by Kravet, the pink chairs are vintage Kerstin Hörlin Holmquist, dining chairs by Studio Valle de Valle, antique farm table sourced from Obsolete, painting by Tyler Buck Guinn, and the pendants are by Of Enso, made with a vintage silk gingham from Decker’s personal collection.

The first-floor front room continues the nature theme thanks to its hand-painted custom mural. The sofa is a vintage Carl Malmsten upholstered in a Rose Uniacke fabric; the blue club chairs are vintage and sourced from Eneby Home; the leather chair is a vintage Hans Wegner; coffee table designed by Decker and fabricated by Dunham and Ernsberger Custom Cabinetry; and the armoire is a vintage walnut wet bar sourced from 1stDibs.

The kitchen hall’s allover paneling was fabricated by Bill Woodworks and installed by Blockhaus. Flush mounts by The Urban Electric Co.; the painting is a vintage William Skilling; sideboard by Bicyclette; and the rug is vintage found on Etsy.

The kitchen is painted in a muted shade of Decker’s favorite color: green. The double islands were designed by Decker and Dunham and fabricated by Ernsberger Custom Cabinetry; the pendants are by Roman and Williams Guild; the fittings by Waterworks; and the painting is an antique signed by Adelaide Briggs.

The breakfast nook brings the garden inside with its collection of plants and green accents. The table was custom-designed by Decker and Dunham and fabricated by Ernsberger Custom Cabinetry. The chairs are by Studio Ashby and upholstered with a Lee Jofa fabric, and the light fixture is a vintage Hans-Agne Jackobsson.

To help with the transformation, the couple engaged both Ruard Veltman Architecture for the architectural design and Stanwick Dunham for contracting. The group transformed the nearly 7,000-square-foot structure into a home that celebrates its lineage without being mired in the past. Nowhere is this effort more evident than in the gracious entryway, whose original carpeted staircase curved up from stark black and white tiles. And while that design wasn’t in poor taste, its severity read as both formal and cold. The solution was twofold: both to square-off the staircase and to clad the entire area in rich walnut paneling. The result not only allows sunlight to flow down from the second story, but showcases warm millwork. “We wanted it to look like it predated our arrival, and also be a bit more humble in its materials,” Decker says.

The primary bedroom’s wood floors and cream walls are softened up with the addition of a full wall of drapery, with fabric from William Morris. The chandelier is a vintage Carlo Scarpa sourced from 1stDibs, the bed from The Expert, linens from Nordic Knots, chair by Commune for George Smith, and the painting is by Alexandra Valenti.

Another view of the bedroom spotlights the family’s dog finding his light. Art by Tyler Hays Studio, nightstands by BDDW, vintage daybed from 1stDibs, and the vintage chair is sourced from Evan Reid.

The couple’s closet displays shoes almost in a museum-like setting, inside an antique French shoe cabinet sourced from 1stDibs. Ottoman from John Derian, the paint color is Farrow & Ball White Tie, and the chandelier is from RH.

The home’s primary bath was completely gut-renovated, but Decker wanted the design to appear original to the house. Tile from Waterworks, tub from Water Monopoly, side table by BDDW, painting by George Buckner of The Florida Highwaymen, and the pendant is an antique French copper lantern sourced from 1stDibs.

For the home’s interior design, Decker had no need of hiring a professional: She’s become a decorator herself. Over the last six or so years and mostly by word of mouth, the actor has spearheaded a variety of design projects (her latest, along with her design partners at By Idiots, being the interiors of Intown Golf Club’s new properties in Houston and Raleigh). For her family home, the brief was to keep the Georgian influence while updating the aesthetic. And it remains an evolving one: “Whenever I see that furniture dolly out, I know I’ll be involved at some point,” Roddick says.

Decker achieved the cozy yet elevated mix through color, using muted shades of green (her color), red, blue, and yellow; wallpaper with natural images like leaves or rivers; and plenty of vintage patterned rugs to soften up the wood floors. “It was originally old English meets early Georgian meets Shaker,” she says. “But when our kids became older, we really wanted to incorporate and take seriously what they wanted. Because, how cool to be eight and actually have some say in the space around you? So now the house is a hodgepodge of explosive color, a lot of which is totally inspired and dictated by them.”

#2455. 10 Make-Ahead Thanksgiving Sides That Genuinely Reheat Well

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

Assemble them ahead of time and Turkey Day will be a breeze.

Maybe you’re trying to avoid a spicy mother-in-law and want to be sequestered in the kitchen all day on Thanksgiving (and more power to you). But for most people, the idea of having your house full of family and friends and being trapped standing at the stove the whole time isn’t ideal. That’s where these make-ahead sides that actually reheat well come into play. You see, no one wants clumped-up, days-old, dried-out mashed potatoes on Turkey Day, nor do they want to be hand-mashing while everyone laughs and drinks in the living room. But with recipes like these, a happy medium is possible.

Sweet potato casserole is a must at every Thanksgiving feast. When else is it socially acceptable to serve a vegetable topped with marshmallows? All The Healthy Things’ recipe includes special notes of orange and brown butter, as well as a few notes about different ways to prep the recipe ahead of time and save yourself some work the day-of.

Just go ahead and whip up All The Healthy Things’ slow cooker mashed potatoes in advance; they’ll microwave beautifully, or fit right back into the slow cooker on “keep warm.” This way you can focus all your efforts on the turkey gravy.

Make Simply LaKita’s corn pudding ahead of time so you can use that oven space for other things on Thanksgiving. Her reheating instructions say you can put the whole dish back into the oven at 300 degrees, or just put individual portions in the microwave.

Who knew you could make stuffing ahead of time? Just follow Damn Delicious’ recipe up to step five (which is when you combine all the ingredients in the baking dish). Instead of putting it straight into the oven, let it cool and pop it in the fridge overnight, then bake when you’re ready to serve the following day.

Just like the stuffing, you can make this green bean casserole from Damn Delicious nearly to completion — just hold off on popping the breadcrumbs and fried shallots on top until you’re ready to bake it. Then, they’ll come out crispy and perfect and right on time.

Princess Pinky Girl’s scalloped potato recipe is perfect for planning ahead. You can bake it completely and reheat individual portions, assemble but wait to bake, or even bake it halfway and finish it off on the day of Thanksgiving dinner.

Damn Delicious’ make-ahead yeast rolls are perfectly plump and fluffy, ideal for spreading with butter or mopping up gravy (mmm). Her post explains that you can refrigerate the rolls overnight so they double in size before baking, or simply bake, freeze, and reheat, so you can make them much farther in advance.

Fried apples may not be a staple on every Thanksgiving table, but in the South, they often make an appearance at holiday meals. Simply LaKita’s recipe is simple and easy, and includes instructions for reheating the apples in the microwave or a skillet.

This mac and cheese recipe from Averie Cooks is incredibly easy and will definitely reheat beautifully. It’s so creamy and saucy, it won’t dry out like a premade baked mac tends to.

Whether you choose to reheat them in the microwave or the oven, Simply LaKita promises her candied yams come out tender and flavorful every time. You can make them up to two days in advance, so this could be one of your Tuesday evening preps.

#1181. This Is the Right Way to Do a Gallery Wall, According to a Framing Expert

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

It’s the artful update your space has been waiting for.

Framed art never goes out of style, and gallery walls are one of the go-to ways to display cherished photos and prints. For Tara Gavin, the founder of On the Wall Framing in Toronto, the secret to doing a gallery wall right is to make sure it has a “design direction.”

“My team and I love playing with scale, unexpected mat colors, different frame finishes, and the way photographs are positioned within a frame in order to keep them fresh,” Gavin says.

Gavin’s approach proves that gallery walls can look unexpectedly artful instead of off-kilter. Here’s how she intentionally designs gallery walls to make them a standout feature, plus, her three rules to follow so that you can do the same.

The easiest solution for any gallery wall is to stick to white backdrops and wood frames. And while a classic approach will always look great, Gavin wanted to give the above space an edge. She kept the familiar oak and black frames for some pieces, and then distinguished others with burgundy-hued mats and frames.

“The burgundy introduces warmth and richness, while the oak keeps everything from feeling too formal,” she says. “The black frames provide a little contrast and help tie the composition together.”

You can follow suit with the same palette, or you can use this pop of color to coordinate with a prominent shade in the surrounding furnishings—the results will be eye-catching, while still allowing the gallery wall to blend in with the rest of the room. “We tend to think of white as the default, but a rich burgundy, soft blue, olive green, or even a warm neutral can completely transform the look of a photograph,” Gavin adds.

One of the most interesting aspects of Gavin’s gallery wall approach is the use of negative space: the size of the mat around the image. She notes that it’s one of her team’s favorite tools for making a photo feel special, since it inadvertently makes you want to lean in for a better look at the shot. And given that the smaller size of the images naturally creates more room, Gavin also recommends using this as an opportunity to stick more than one photo into a frame. Then she’d play around with a composition that feels right.

“Rather than always centering a single photo, we often use multiple openings within one frame or place photos slightly off-center,” she says. “It's a small detail, but it makes the whole gallery wall feel more interesting and a little less predictable.”

Although it isn’t always necessary, Gavin recommends printing images in black and white for a uniform look, as was the case with the above project.

“One of the biggest challenges with family photographs is that they're often taken at different times, in different lighting conditions, and with completely different color palettes,” she says. “Converting them to black and white can bring a sense of consistency to the collection.”

Since you’ll already be planning out the positioning of your gallery wall, devote the same consideration to the photos that will go in it. The black-and-white composition will make the pictures look cohesive as a display, but the memories they stir up will be much more dynamic.

“People sometimes forget that the photographs sitting on their phones or tucked away in boxes can become some of the most meaningful art in their homes,” Gavin says. “You don't need an expensive art collection to create a beautiful wall. Sometimes the most compelling gallery walls are built around the memories and moments that matter most to you.”

PALO ALTO, Calif. — Cowboy Space just showed us how it plans to become a big player in the final frontier.

The California-based company, which is run by Robinhood co-founder Baiju Bhatt, intends to operate a constellation of 20,000 AI data centers in low Earth orbit (LEO). "It's a fundamentally novel approach to rocketry in the modern era, in our opinion," Bhatt said during the event. "The idea is that you have a two-stage rocket where the upper stage of the rocket itself transforms into the satellite."

#598. Raw milk dangers go far beyond bird flu, experts warn

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Pediatricians warn that raw milk carries serious health risks, including dangerous bacterial infections and new concerns about bird flu.

Parents who buy raw milk have usually heard that it is healthier or more natural than the pasteurized kind.

The pediatricians who wrote the newest guidance looked for the evidence behind that claim and couldn’t find any.

What they did find is a new entry on the list of things that can be living in that milk: the bird flu virus.

The pediatricians’ advice is the same as before, only firmer. No raw milk or raw-milk cheese for children or for anyone who is pregnant or breastfeeding, and no legal sale of it anywhere in the United States.

The guidance is a policy statement from the American Academy of Pediatrics, written by its infectious diseases and nutrition committees.

Adam J. Ratner of NYU Grossman School of Medicine led the writing with Anne Sardi, also at NYU, and Mark R. Corkins of the University of Tennessee Health Science Center.

The statement updates the academy’s 2014 guidance, and it is a review of published evidence rather than a new experiment.

“Parents may choose raw milk because they have heard it is healthier or more natural, but the scientific evidence tells a different story,” Corkins said.

“Pasteurized dairy products provide the same essential nutrients without exposing children and families to preventable infections from dangerous bacteria and viruses.”

The new risk is the strain of bird flu, H5N1, that wild birds passed to dairy cows and that cows now pass to each other.

In an infected cow, the virus concentrates in the udder, so it ends up in the milk at high levels.

In refrigerated raw milk, the virus stays alive for up to five weeks.

Among the states hit by the 2024 outbreak in dairy cattle, infectious virus turned up in nearly one-quarter of raw milk samples.

Pasteurization kills it, so properly pasteurized milk does not pose the same known H5N1 risk.

No human H5N1 infections have been confirmed from drinking raw milk.

Dairy workers have, though, from handling infected cows, and they came down with pink eye, fever, muscle aches and breathing symptoms.

Animals that drank the milk developed breathing problems and weak limbs, and some died.

The committees’ advice to doctors is practical. A person who drank raw milk and then developed flu symptoms, especially pink eye, should be tested for H5N1 and treated with antivirals if positive.

Bird flu is the newcomer. The bacteria that have contaminated milk for a century are still doing most of the damage.

From 2013 to 2018, raw milk caused 75 outbreaks in the United States, with 675 illnesses, 98 hospitalizations and two deaths.

Campylobacter was behind 49% of the outbreaks, followed by Salmonella at 17%, toxin-producing E. coli at 15% and the parasite Cryptosporidium at 11%.

Every person who caught Listeria in those outbreaks ended up in the hospital.

Nearly half of the people who got sick, 48%, were under 20, and 14% were under five.

Ten patients developed hemolytic uremic syndrome, a kidney failure that can follow an E. coli infection in young children. Two developed Guillain-Barré syndrome, a nerve disorder that can cause paralysis.

Of the outbreaks confined to one state, 78% happened in states that permit retail sales of raw milk.

The guidance emphasizes the serious risks of raw milk during pregnancy.

Pregnant people are 12 to 14 times as likely as the general population to develop listeriosis, and the infection reaches the fetus.

In pregnancies with listeriosis, 20% end in miscarriage or stillbirth.

Of the babies born alive to infected mothers, 68% are infected themselves, with pneumonia, sepsis or meningitis, and 24.5% of those infected newborns die.

The parasite Toxoplasma raises similar risks. Most Americans aged 15 to 44 have never been exposed to it, so they have no immunity during pregnancy.

Drinking unpasteurized goat milk raises the chance of catching it in pregnancy sevenfold.

Aged raw cheese can legally cross state lines if it was aged at least 60 days and labeled, and the authors wrote that the exemption still carries risk.

E. coli can survive that long, because aging a cheese is not the same as pasteurizing it.

A multistate outbreak traced to aged raw Gouda sickened 38 people and put 15 in the hospital. A Salmonella outbreak from raw milk and aged cheese reached 171 cases, 70% of them children.

Raw milk’s supporters say heating destroys fats, proteins, vitamins and enzymes. The committees reviewed the evidence and found little support for these claims.

Pasteurization doesn’t change the fats in milk, including its triglycerides and free fatty acids, and it doesn’t change the lactose. Calcium, magnesium and zinc come out the same.

A 2011 review of vitamins found that, among the vitamins milk is an important source of, pasteurization lowered only vitamin B2.

Heating does unfold milk’s proteins, but stomach acid does the same thing during digestion. Some active cow proteins, such as lactoferrin, are lost.

The two main milk allergens, whey and casein, are present in raw and pasteurized milk alike, so raw milk is no safer for a child with a milk allergy.

Ratner put the trade-off in one sentence.

“Pasteurization – a safe, effective process of heating and cooling milk before consumption that has been used for more than a century – dramatically reduces the risks without taking away the nutritional value families rely on,” Ratner said.

Raw milk is a small market. In surveys in 2016 and 2019, 4.4% of American adults said they had drunk it in the past year.

Young adults and rural residents were the likeliest to say so.

How often the less common organisms in raw milk, from Brucella to the rabies virus, actually make someone sick is largely unknown, the authors wrote. Whether H5N1 can pass through human breast milk is also unknown.

What the academy wants next is a ban on the sale and distribution of raw milk and raw-milk cheese nationwide.

It asks pediatricians to lobby their state legislators, to oppose bills that would legalize raw milk where it is banned, and to push for warning labels where it isn’t.

Until the laws change, the counsel to families is to buy only dairy labeled pasteurized.

So you can imagine my surprise when, three miles into the hike, we came across a sign pointing toward the Confluence.

“Six miles. ONE WAY.” All caps. Just like that.

Confused, I checked my GPS again. It showed only a few miles remaining, so we pressed on.

Past Beaver Falls, the crowds thinned out dramatically. The canyon narrowed, the trail got rougher, and the red walls rose higher around us. We skirted cliffs, scrambled over rocks, and crossed the creek repeatedly. A few sections made me wish I hadn’t worn water shoes. Other sections of the trail were in the creek itself. We got wet, but with temperatures pushing 100 degrees, that wasn’t necessarily a bad thing.

And the scenery? Havasu Canyon is every bit as stunning as the waterfalls. At one point, the canyon narrowed into what looked like a sea of green. Thick vines and foliage covered practically every inch of the canyon walls, emerald leaves spilling across the deep-red sandstone. It looked like someone had planted a vineyard in the middle of the desert. I’d never seen a photograph of it—never even heard someone mention it. And yet, there it was: a place every bit as beautiful as the waterfalls we’d come all this way to see.

The farther we walked, the more I realized how much of Havasu I’d been missing in all those photographs.

And the farther we walked, the more I questioned my GPS. Eventually, my watch beeped at 10 miles, and we still weren’t at the Confluence. We were way out there. Several bends later, we rounded a corner and saw a huge group of people wearing swim trunks and life jackets.

“Where the hell did they come from?” my wife asked. They certainly didn’t look like they’d just hiked 10 miles through the desert. A few minutes later, we had our answer.

Five large river rafts were moored at the canyon’s narrowest point. A leathery-skinned, deeply tanned river captain sipped a beer while his crew guided rafters toward a cool pool. The Colorado River ran brown, carrying its sediment downstream. Havasu Creek was still that impossible shade of blue. The two met and mixed, though not quite as dramatically as I’d expected. But that was it: The bottom of the Grand Canyon—the Confluence. We’d made it.

The captain raised a fist. “You coming from Havasu? That’s a helluva hike!”

We chatted for a while. Then he reached into a cooler and handed us a beer. Ice cold. There we were, deep in the Grand Canyon, 11 miles from our campground, drinking a cold beer with a group of people who’d arrived by raft. It wasn’t quite the Havasu experience I’d seen on Instagram—this was much better.

The return trip from the confluence to camp brought our total to 22.11 miles, according to my GPS, with roughly 2,500 feet or more of elevation change over the day. And just when we thought the canyon had thrown everything it could at us, we encountered a herd of surly bighorn sheep. They’d apparently decided the trail was a fine place to stop for dinner and weren’t particularly interested in sharing it. We weren’t in much of a position to argue, so we waited until they finished feasting to finally get past.

A word of advice for anyone considering the Confluence hike from Havasu Campground: Don’t underestimate it. Bring plenty of water, plan for the heat, and give yourself an entire day. This is a serious desert hike, not a quick detour from the waterfalls. But it’s also extraordinary.

I came to Havasu for the waterfalls, and they were every bit as beautiful as I’d hoped—maybe even more. You could spend your entire visit swimming beneath them and leave perfectly satisfied.

However, beyond those famous blue pools is another Havasu. One with towering canyon walls, hidden green oases, river crossings, grumpy wild sheep, and miles of wild desert scenery that rarely make it into the photographs.

Scientists have sequenced the genomes of three Late Stone Age people who lived in Europe 30,000 years ago and discovered that they had closer biological ties than researchers previously thought. The new finding suggests that places like southwestern France may have been critical cultural hubs during the last ice age.

Shortly after Neanderthals disappeared from Europe, a Stone Age culture known as the Gravettian spread across the continent. Artifacts found during archaeological excavations ‪—‬ including portable art, such as "Venus figurines" ‪—‬ have shown that the Gravettian culture lasted from about 34,000 to 25,000 years ago. But ancient DNA has been recovered from only two dozen human skeletons from the Gravettian period, so questions about these people's ancestry remain unanswered.

Based on the small number of genomes from Gravettian people that were previously sequenced, experts believed that groups in Western Europe were largely separate from groups in Eastern Europe. But in a study published Oct. 23 in the journal Science Advances, researchers sequenced the genomes of three people who lived in what are now Spain, France and Italy during the Gravettian period. They discovered that one person had mixed genetic ancestry that revealed East-West exchange at least 30,000 years ago.

"Our results show that eastern and western European populations were not as genetically isolated as previously assumed," study first author Pere Gelabert, an evolutionary anthropologist at the University of Vienna, said in a statement.

The new information about mixed ancestry comes from a woman who died around 30,000 years ago and whose bones were found in Isturitz Cave in southwestern France. Originally used by Neanderthals, Isturitz Cave was occupied off and on from about 80,000 to 10,000 years ago. Cups made out of human skulls dated to roughly 15,000 to 12,000 years ago have also been found in Isturitz Cave.

Researchers found that the DNA from the Gravettian woman in Isturitz Cave was most similar to the previously known Western European genomes, but with an intriguing connection to Eastern and Central European groups.

This discovery of gene flow from the East is uncommon for the Late Stone Age, also known as the Upper Paleolithic, the researchers wrote in the study. It "could be explained by movements of people from Eastern Europe during the Gravettian period, which eventually contributed to this ancestry in Isturitz." Specifically, the researchers dated this "pulse" of gene flow to about 31,500 to 32,500 years ago.

The DNA evidence supports the archaeological theory that Isturitz Cave was an important cultural and biological hub for tens of thousands of years during the Late Stone Age, as the cave was located at a critical passage between continental Europe and the Iberian Peninsula, the researchers wrote.

All three newly sequenced Gravettian genomes revealed close biological connections to Western European groups, as well as genetic traits suggesting they had dark skin, dark hair and dark eyes. What's more, all of them had parents who weren't closely related to each other, suggesting that those populations may have actively avoided inbreeding.

"Overall, our results suggest that population structure and gene flow in western Europe during the Gravettian were more complex and dynamic than previously assumed," the researchers wrote.
