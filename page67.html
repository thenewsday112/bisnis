#1139. 2026 Is the Year of Berry Tones in Fashion

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Juicy purple and pink tones are ubiquitous; here’s why and how to style them.

Everything is coming up berry-toned in fashion suddenly. Juicy shades of grape, bright raspberry, and deep cranberry have defined the fall/winter 2026 season and continue to punctuate the spring/summer 2027 collections. At Eckhaus Latta, simple ’90s-inspired skirt and dress silhouettes popped with purply red hues accented by luminous layers, intentional wrinkles, and luminous sheen. Tory Burch gave us deep grape-colored suits and brocade statement coats. Likewise, Kiko Kostadinov gave us neon pinks spliced with bright cherry reds and the most royal shades of purplish blueberry blue.

It’s easy to see why these darker shades of red, purple, and blue are trending now. As we shift into winter fashion, the color palette naturally gets darker. Bright berry tones are a fashionable alternative to neutral black or dark gray. “Dark hues are gaining importance across apparel, accessories, interiors, and digital experiences, reflecting a broader cultural shift towards depth, resilience, escapism, and emotional grounding in uncertain times,” says Sara Maggioni, head of womenswear at WGSN. “This is closely linked to WGSN’s Embracing Darkness trend. In fashion, the definition of a dark color is expanding beyond black to include richer, tinted shades of brown, red, purple, blue, and green, with berry tones at the centre of this shift. We expect the direction to continue and evolve.” The forecasting agency named bloodstone, a deep, multidimensional red, a key color for the fall 2028 forecast.

The easiest way to wear the trend is by replacing your normal go-to black staples with a bright berry hue instead. Trade your classic black puffer for a statement coat, your everyday work bag for a cool, colorful clutch, or, alternatively, try purple and red accessories like a silk scarf or pendant necklace. “The appeal of berry tones is that they bring color into the wardrobe while retaining the versatility and investment value of black,” adds Maggioni. “Shades such as dark red and burgundy are sophisticated, seasonless, and easy to wear, making them particularly relevant for high-ticket pieces traditionally designed in black, including outerwear, tailoring, leather accessories, and footwear.”

If you’re looking for more ways to integrate the berry trend into your wardrobe, look no further than the runways for ample inspiration.

Choose a biker jacket, longer coat, or blazer in a deep purple tone instead of your typical neutral. A skirt, especially in a dark shade of black or gray, helps balance the color and keep the proportions experimental but still wearable.

Try a maxi dress in knitted material or crochet in cherry red or deep blueberry for instant impact. Extra little details like fringe give it great movement.

Purple is at the center of the berry tone trend, and it has never looked better in all different shades. Choose various saturations (lilac, lavender, royal purple) and combine them for an opulent look.

When in doubt, combine all the shades of the berry rainbow (purple, blue, red, and pink) for a striking look that is somehow harmonious. Geometric prints and lines keep it streamlined.

Bright colors are easy to wear when you think in terms of complementary colors. It’s okay to get a little technical, as in the case of the above Zankov look: yellow is the complementary color of purple, which acts as a brilliant pop of personality.

What's in the photo? Hurricane Madeline (left) and Hurricane Lester (right)

Which satellite took the photo? Suomi NPP

This eye-catching satellite snap from a decade ago shows a pair of hurricanes barreling toward Hawaii. The clouds swirling around the twin cyclones make them look eerily like the eyes of a pale face smiling up at space. The sight appears even spookier when viewed on a weather map (see below).

The surprisingly symmetrical storms — Hurricane Madeline (left) and Hurricane Lester (right) — were the 14th and 13th named storms, respectively, during 2016's Pacific hurricane season, which is tied for the sixth-most-active year on record, with 22 total named storms.

Madeline peaked as a Category 4 storm on Aug. 30, reaching maximum wind speeds of around 130 mph (210 km/h). Lester also briefly became a Category 4 storm on Sept. 1, reaching a maximum speed just below Madeline's, but it quickly weakened to Category 3.

On Aug. 30, the day after the image was taken, Madeline was just under 400 miles (650 kilometers) from Hawaii's Big Island, while Lester was around 900 miles (1,450 km) behind on the same trajectory, according to NASA's Earth Observatory. At the time, meteorologists were uncertain whether the pair would hit the island.

The Central Pacific Hurricane Center later issued a hurricane warning for the state of Hawaii, saying that Madeline, in particular, would pass "dangerously close to the Big Island," according to the Earth Observatory. However, neither storm ended up making landfall. (In the photo, the Big Island is obscured by reflected sunlight, known as a sunglint, on the left side of the image.)

When viewed on a radar-based weather map, the hurricanes look like they are staring up into space even more.

Madeline eventually passed south of Hawaii on Aug. 31. However, by then, it had been downgraded to Category 1. Lester passed north of the Big Island on Sept. 2 and came within around 130 miles (210 km) of Honolulu, before sharply turning northward, where it petered out.

Although neither hurricane made landfall — meaning the eye of the storm did not pass over a landmass — they still caused high winds, heavy rainfall and strong surf across the state.

Despite being in the middle of the corridor where most Pacific hurricanes form, Hawaii's islands have surprising good luck in avoiding the supercharged storms.

For example, in 2025, Hurricane Kiko was similarly spotted from space bearing down on the island state. But like Madeline and Lester, it suddenly changed course and weakened at the last minute.

Hawaii usually manages to dodge the worst effects of hurricanes for two reasons. First, the islands are surrounded by a near-constant area of high pressure on their eastern side, where most hurricanes come from. Second, strong winds often blow eastward across the islands, according to FOX Weather. These factors create a sort of invisible shield around the islands, causing storms to skirt around them.

While hurricanes almost never make landfall in Hawaii, they often come close enough to trigger strong gusts of wind on the islands.

However, given the recent rise in extreme weather events across the globe — caused by human-caused climate change — as well as changes to regional weather patterns, the islands may not stay so lucky forever.

For example, in August and September, two hurricanes — Lowell and Lala — skimmed unusually closely past the island of Kauai, causing a significant amount of damage to power lines, boats and other coastal infrastructure. Researchers think the ongoing "super" El Niño, which officially began in May and has already caused record-breaking sea surface temperatures in the Pacific Ocean, may be partially to blame for these storms, according to The Guardian.

The man who portrayed the U.S.S. Enterprise’s Pavel Chekov has left us after a 60-year stage and screen career

The "Star Trek" community lost one of its most beloved icons on Sunday, October 25, with the sad passing of Walter Koenig at the age of 90 in his North Hollywood home.

Koenig was best known as the U.S.S. Enterprise's Russian navigator Pavel Andreievich Chekov for two seasons on "Star Trek: The Original Series." The popular American actor continued in that role for Paramount's first seven "Star Trek" films, which began in 1979's "Star Trek: The Motion Picture" and ended with 1994's "Star Trek: Generations."

A native of Chicago, Illinois, Marvin Walter Koenig was born on Sept. 14, 1936, to Lithuanian immigrant parents and spent his formative years in New York City. After a short college stint at UCLA in California, Koenig was accepted into the prestigious Neighborhood Playhouse School of the Theatre in New York in 1958, joining future stars like James Caan and Christopher Lloyd.

TV guest spots in the early '60s on "Mr. Novak" and "Ben Casey" led Koenig to join the intrepid crew of the U.S.S. Enterprise for Gene Roddenberry’s sophomore series of "Star Trek." He co-starred in 36 of the 50 installments airing on the NBC Network in 1967-68 and 1968-69, premiering in famed sci-fi author Theodore Sturgeon's excellent episode, "Amok Time," broadcast on Sept. 15, 1967.

Although Koenig's Ensign Chekov character was not resurrected for the 1973-74 Saturday morning cartoon spinoff, "Star Trek: The Animated Series," due to budget issues, he did write a screenplay for that show’s first season titled "The Infinite Vulcan."

In 1982's "Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan," he was promoted to Commander aboard the U.S.S. Reliant when he and Captain Terrell (Paul Winfield) beamed down to Seti Alpha V and ran into the genetically enhanced Khan Noonien Singh (Ricardo Montalban).

Following his work in the "Star Trek" movies, Koenig scored an ongoing TV role on creator J. Michael Straczynski's "Babylon 5" starting in 1993, where he portrayed the former Psi Ops commander and war criminal, Alfred Bester, through the 1998 season.

A core member of the original "Star Trek" and its Hollywood features alongside William Shatner, Leonard Nimoy, DeForest Kelley, George Takei, Nichelle Nichols, and James Doohan, Koenig was often utilized as comic relief.

His thick accent, where he pronounced his “Vs” as “Ws,” was derived from hearing his Russian-Jewish father's particular manner of speech. Koenig’s undeniable contributions to the legacy of "Star Trek" and the chemistry of its cast cannot be overestimated, and he'll be sorely missed.

His bestselling 1998 autobiography, "Warped Factors: A Neurotic’s Guide to the Universe," was later followed by a detailed memoir titled "Beaming Up and Getting Off," released back in 2020.

Koenig is survived by his sole daughter, screenwriter and actress Danielle Koenig.

#1229. This Is What Weed Really Did to My Body

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

How a medical marijuana card works for three real guys.

In November 1996, California became the first state to legalize medical cannabis. (Many) politics and (some) physicians promised us breakthroughs in better health. Thirty years later, how are we really doing? Men’s Health decided to find out in this special report package. Read more here.

THREE REAL GUYS. Three dramatically different mental health conditions. Here’s how a weed card works for them.

THE GUY: TJ, a 47-year-old data insights professional

THE CONDITION: Multiple sclerosis, a chronic autoimmune disease that affects the central nervous system

THE EXPERIENCE: TJ was diagnosed with MS at the age of 25. Doctors prescribed him Rebif, a drug that helped his symptoms but made him depressed. “I was immediately suicidal on that medicine,” he says. Years before medical marijuana was legalized in his state, a friend suggested pot. TJ was desperate, tried weed, and says he felt immediate relief. In 2022, he obtained his medical card. Today, TJ struggles with nerve pain and migraines but says cannabis helps him play volleyball, travel, and stay active. “I don’t know that I would have a productive and happy life if I were not treating myself that way.”

THE GUY: CJ, a 32-year-old postdoctoral researcher of addiction neurobiology

THE CONDITION: Sarcoidosis, an inflammatory disease that causes collections of immune cells, called granulomas, to form in the body. Depending on the location, granulomas can result in fatigue, joint pain, nausea, and swelling. CJ has a rare form located in his brain stem.

THE EXPERIENCE: CJ’s symptoms began in his early 20s, starting with skin issues and leading, eventually, to debilitating nausea. After doctors diagnosed him with sarcoidosis in 2021, he went from using cannabis occasionally to using it daily for nausea control. Through self-experimentation, he learned the combination of cannabinoids that worked the best. Because the ratios were harder to find in OTC products, CJ got his medical card in 2023. “This brought down my overall symptom load a huge amount,” CJ says. Along with an off-label Crohn’s disease medication, medical marijuana has brought him back to 90 percent of his old self, he says.

THE GUY: Jonah, a 41-year-old bookstore manager

THE CONDITION: Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), a mental health condition that causes excessive fear and worry and interferes with day-to-day functioning.

THE EXPERIENCE: Jonah says he was always labeled “the weird kid.” He ran away at 16 and experienced problems with drugs, alcohol, and homelessness in his early 20s, until his mother convinced him to talk to a therapist, who diagnosed him with anxiety and recommended medical marijuana. After using cannabis for a few weeks, Jonah says, the dark fog that had made him want to act out or isolate himself began to lift. “Conversations with people became a lot easier; decision-making, especially good decisions, became a lot easier.” Sixteen years after that therapy appointment, he’s happily married and life is good, he says.

HIGH-CONCENTRATION SYNTHETIC THC products are producing wild reactions in some people. Kevin Gray, MD, a practicing physician and director of addiction sciences at Medical University of South Carolina, helps break them down.

“You experience hallucinations, paranoia, delusions,” says Dr. Gray. “It’s become undeniable looking at the association between teen cannabis use and psychosis that if somebody has some kind of already innate risk for developing a psychotic disorder, there’s something about use of cannabis during this stage of development that [may trigger the onset of] a lifelong disorder.” (The risk of someone developing the condition is less than 1 percent.)

“Paranoia is a symptom of psychosis. Instead of feeling the usual sort of high and calming feeling with cannabis, people feel more detached and more suspicious. Sometimes there will be a very high concentration or some unusual strain that may be more likely to lead to paranoia. The effect can be intense, but it’s transient and wears off with time.”

That’s screaming and vomiting at the same time. “Cyclic vomiting, cannabis-related emesis—I don’t see it that often among my clients, but it happens,” says Dr. Gray. “THC can reduce nausea, but there’s something about the interaction between THC and the systems that control our nausea that goes haywire in some people. We don’t know even how to treat it symptomatically, other than taking showers.” So if you totally misread a gummy label, just call 911—no judgment.

#1202. 30 Years of Legal Weed: Has It Made Us Any Healthier?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Our product picks are editor-tested, expert-approved. We may earn a commission through links on our site. Why Trust Us?

The very highs and bizarre lows of America on medicinal marijuana.

EARLIER THIS YEAR, during a brief and mercifully abandoned period when I was considering doing stand-up as a hobby, I went to a comedy show in downtown Austin. I was legitimately shocked to find, as part of the two-drink minimum bill of fare, the option for several different flavors of $5 THC seltzers. This wasn’t “take a pull off your vape or smoke a roach in the alley before the show” territory. No, marijuana drinks were on the menu.

In the past few years, my only social activities have been poker, competitive trivia, and going to the movies, all activities people do stoned, but not activities where you can literally order drugs from the bar alongside your chicken wings. The comedy club, on the other hand, considered marijuana products as a profit center.

This was one of those lightly attended weeknight shows where the comedians banter with the audience because they haven’t prepared much material. So, during a Q&A, I brought up the fact that there were THC sodas available to order, in laminate. “Sir, this is 2026,” the host said.

This is where we are now, a full three decades after California legalized medical weed. But is that good for us?

As I write this, it’s been eight-and-a-half years since I quit marijuana, a drug that at one point made pretty much anything—listening to music, shopping for groceries, cleaning the house—fun for me.

But really, I’ve been sober since I stopped using THC, since I was hardly just smoking there at the end. I was vaping, eating, drinking, dabbing, and otherwise gorging myself on what has become an extremely potent, psychosis-inducing drug. And I wasn’t having fun.

My breaking point, which I recounted in my 2020 memoir Pothead, came at Game 2 of the 2017 World Series at Dodger Stadium, where I was so wasted on weed that I had a screaming meltdown in front of security when I found out the ticket I’d bought was fraudulent.

It was a low point, a bottom so deep and so pathetic that I should have quit right there. But I went on for another three weeks before I finally crawled to a Marijuana Anonymous meeting and asked for help.

Now, nearly a decade later, I’ve emerged from my cocoon to find that THC is everywhere. I live in a state where it’s still technically illegal, but you can find some sort of THC variant in most convenience stores and in most bars and nightclubs.

My friend is an attorney who represents the Texas Alcoholic Beverage Commission. He says his client is in a near panic. Sales are down for every beverage except for nonalcoholic mixers and those THC seltzers. THC is the beer, wine, and vodka of the present day, combined.

And so we find ourselves in a bizarre situation where people are drinking way less alcohol because it’s unhealthy for you, but they are instead ingesting THC products—either through soda, gummies, or any number of other concoctions—from unknown manufacturers, with unknown formulas and malleable dosages.

They’re also vaping, which emerging research is showing is kind of awful for our lungs. A University of Michigan study recently showed that teens who vaped cannabis were twice as likely to experience respiratory symptoms like “wheezing or whistling” in the chest than those who vaped nicotine or smoked traditional cigarettes.

Unlike traditional marijuana, this stuff isn’t “from the earth.” Who knows where it’s really from—or where it’s going?

When I quit weed in 2017, and somewhat loudly and publicly announced myself as an addict, I got the requisite amount of sympathy, but also a tremendous amount of pushback. Marijuana isn’t dangerous, people said. It isn’t addictive. It’s actually good for you.

In 2016, Gavin Newsom, then California’s lieutenant governor, spearheaded the legalization push for recreational marijuana. Cannabis sales have produced more than $8 billion in tax revenue for California since retail sales began in 2018, with a decent proportion of that funneled into teen substance-abuse prevention programs. But the state has also seen an increase in children suffering from cannabis poisoning.

It’s been a massive shift, moving from medically administered weed for veterans and cancer patients to powerful THC beverages you can buy at your bodega.

Legalization has created a new frontier of intoxicants without a centralized warning system for users. Data from a 2025 national study of U.S. adults stated that 15 percent of respondents reported daily or near-daily cannabis use.

Among those daily users, 18 percent reported experiencing symptoms consistent with cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome, a disorder characterized by cyclic, severe nausea, intractable vomiting, and gut pain alleviated temporarily by hot baths or showers.

This translates to an estimated 7.2 million people in the United States alone, many of them teenagers, experiencing these symptoms. That number is probably vastly underestimated, given that fewer than 12 percent of sufferers have received a formal diagnosis.

Welcome to the darker side of legalized weed, a world of cannabis-induced psychosis, “scromiting” (yes, screaming while vomiting), and concoctions like “shatter” or “wax,” which contain up to 90 percent THC, as opposed to the 3 to 5 percent in joints from previous decades.

So I find myself in a somewhat odd situation. I was a relatively early victim of modern weed’s potency, as well as a relatively early unadopter, and a very early voice in the wilderness warning the world that marijuana might not be the answer to all our health concerns. But now I find myself, like a patient waking up from a long coma, in a world where THC is as prevalent as, and cheaper than, whiskey. Yet still, I find myself thinking it should be legal. So which is it? Maybe we still don’t have enough data.

Thirty years since the start of legalization might feel like a long time, but it’s really not. In terms of scientific analysis, we’re still in relatively undiscovered country. If you’re suffering from chronic pain, or dying of cancer, marijuana is a healthier option than, say, oxycodone. But for the rest of us? No one really knows.

The only thing I can be truly sure of is that it’s no longer for me.

#1194. Weed Addiction Is a Real Thing. The World's Leading Cannabis Researcher Explains Why.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

In November 1996, California became the first state to legalize medical cannabis. (Many) politics and (some) physicians promised us breakthroughs in better health. Thirty years later, how are we really doing? Men’s Health decided to find out in this special report package. Read more here.

KEVIN HILL, MD, the guy when it comes to this stuff. He’s the chief of the Division of Addiction Psychiatry at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. We talked to him about all the questions you probably have about weed and addiction.

Dr. Hill: Absolutely. Most people who use cannabis don’t develop a cannabis use disorder or cannabis addiction. But there has always been a subset of individuals who do. Five years ago, the data indicated about 10 to 30 percent of adults develop a cannabis use disorder. Now the published data says 30 to 35 percent. With the rise in potency, we’re seeing a rise in the number of users who are developing problems.

MH: Does cannabis use disorder differ from other addictions?

Dr. Hill: It affects a lot of the same neurotransmitters that other substances of abuse might affect. If you use cannabis, you’re going to have a surge in dopamine. Similarly, if you use opioids, you’re going to have a surge in dopamine, but the scale of it is different. It’s why a lot of medications we use to treat addictions operate on common pathways.

MH: Is there a genetic risk of developing a weed addiction?

#1220. People Think They Drive Better High. These Scientists Are Testing the Theory.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Inside the lab that’s decoding the dangers of driving stoned.

Our product picks are editor-tested, expert-approved. We may earn a commission through links on our site. Why Trust Us?

In November 1996, California became the first state to legalize medical cannabis. (Many) politics and (some) physicians promised us breakthroughs in better health. Thirty years later, how are we really doing? Men’s Health decided to find out in this special report package. Read more here.

A HIT OR two from a friend’s vape at a party, a low-dose gummy at a concert, a THC-infused drink at a comedy show—the average guy would be fine to drive home a few hours after doing any of this, right?

Michael Stevens, PhD, director of the Clinical Neuroscience & Development Laboratory at the Olin Neuropsychiatry Research Center, has spent roughly a decade trying to answer that question. When I asked Stevens how long he’d recommend a friend wait before getting into a car after taking THC, he joked, “How much do I like this particular hypothetical friend?” before settling on three to six hours. Yes, that’s a huge range.

That uncertainty is a big issue right now, especially as cannabis legalization spreads across the U.S., and researchers beyond Stevens are raising concerns. One 2021 analysis found a 15 percent increase in fatal car crashes following marijuana legalization, and a separate review linked legalization to increases in car accidents, injuries, or fatalities.

Recently, Stevens and his colleagues conducted a lab study involving a high-tech driving simulator. The “car” looks something like an elite gaming rig, complete with a steering wheel, pedals, and a screen that features virtual roadways.

After tracking 19 performance metrics over several hours and examining brain activity through an MRI, researchers found that six of those metrics (roughly one-third) showed significant impairment following cannabis use. Stevens and his team witnessed drivers passing cars too closely or tailgating, increasing the risk of collision. Some drivers failed to make the necessary steering microadjustments to keep their vehicle centered in its lane.

It’s probably obvious that participants who inhaled higher-potency cannabis tended to drive worse than those who received lower doses of THC. But also, THC levels weren’t a strong predictor of who drove more dangerously—and when. Some participants’ dangerous driving faded gradually, says Stevens, while others’ worsened before improving. “The other thing that jumps out is that about two-thirds of people said they’d be willing to drive, even while admitting they still felt high and after we measured they were objectively driving worse,” Stevens says.

It’s true, 35 percent of people believe they drive better when high, according to a recent AAA survey. There is some truth there, says Stevens: People can be more cautious when they’re stoned. “But at the same time, being high whacks out many cognitive abilities,” he says. In Stevens’s study, people who drove while high tended to drive a little too slow, which can disrupt the flow of traffic, encourage other drivers to make risky passing maneuvers, and cause rear-end collisions.

Given what we know (and don’t) about how cannabis works in the body, it’s unlikely that we’ll find a magic THC number that tells us who is and isn’t impaired, at least in the near future. For now, Stevens says, the safest approach to cannabis and driving is pretty simple: When in doubt, wait it out. Regardless of how high you think you are—or aren’t—just call an Uber, text a buddy, or stay put.

ALL 50 HAVE an impaired-driving law that can apply to cannabis. But determining if a person’s too high to drive is all over the place.

Some states (Illinois, Montana, Nevada, Ohio, Washington) have been trying to apply the blood-alcohol framework to gauge drunkenness to cannabis by adopting a legal threshold, ranging between 2 and 5 ng/mL THC in the blood. These roadside assessments take time and aren’t lock-tight, because THC levels can plummet within 30 to 40 minutes of use. Many states (including California, Massachusetts, New York) use an effects-based approach, which looks for signs of impairment: erratic braking, slowed responses, bloodshot eyes. But that’s subjective. “You need a cut-and-dried kind of a test where there’s little room for error,” says Michael Stevens, PhD, of the Olin Neuropsychiatry Research Center. “Think of it like a mammogram screening—you really want the right answer.” For years, tech firms have been racing to develop a reliable roadside cannabis Breathalyzer. None have solved the core problem: Measuring THC isn’t the same thing as measuring impairment. That’s because THC sticks around in body fat. So if someone uses THC frequently, they have a reserve of the main psychoactive chemical that would throw off the test.

You will need two yoga blocks for your practice. If you don’t have access to blocks or block-like props, tent up on your fingertips to give yourself more height.

Now, reach for your blocks. Step your right foot forward in Low Lunge. Tuck your back toes and lift your torso a bit higher by placing your hands onto blocks or coming onto your fingertips.

Take one inhalation here, lengthening through your spine.

As you exhale, shift your hips back, straightening your right leg in Half Splits.

Repeat this pattern of movement 5 times with the pace of your breath.

The next time you come back through Low Lunge, lift your back knee into Runner’s Lunge.

Finish back in Child’s Pose. This time, take your hands off of your blocks and bend your elbows, stack your fists to create a pillow, and use them to support your forehead.
