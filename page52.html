#3719. Ursula von Rydingsvard, Who Sculpted Monuments of Cedar, Dies at 84

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

"She was like the sun. She was this force that pulled people in and kept them orbiting around her," said her studio manager.

Ursula von Rydingsvard, a sculptor whose monumental objects were partly inspired by her childhood in post-World War II refugee camps, died in her Manhattan home this morning, October 27, at 84. The artist's studio manager, Courtney Childress, attributed Von Rydingsvard's death to Alzheimer's disease, according to the New York Times.

During her over five-decade career, Von Rydingsvard chipped away at enormous cedar beams to create a menagerie of massive sculptures, from an assembly of cones to a 17-foot-tall tornado-shaped behemoth made from 15,000 pounds of wood. The abstract sculptor worked with an array of other materials, including bronze, textiles, and animal intestines.

"She was like the sun. She was this force that pulled people in and kept them orbiting around her," Childress told Hyperallergic in a phone interview. "She moved through the studio like her hair was on fire, and could never slow down."

Born in 1942 in Deensen, Germany, in the midst of World War II, to a Polish mother and a Ukrainian father, Von Rydingsvard took significant inspiration from her childhood experiences. When the war ended in 1945, her family of nine navigated eight Nazi slave-labor and postwar refugee camps before taking a military ship to the United States in 1950, as she relayed in an interview with Art 21.

While she resisted autobiographical readings of her oeuvre, her observations of the wooden barracks in camps and pre-industrial wooden Polish homes informed her preferred medium of cedar, she continued in the interview. Other memories, from the feeling of a linen nightgown to the sun warming her body, created a reservoir of inspiration for her works.

She went on to obtain a Bachelor of Arts and a Master of Arts from the University of Miami in 1965, and earned a Master of Fine Arts in studio art from Columbia University in 1975.

Recipient of a spate of honors, including the Guggenheim Fellowship, the Joan Mitchell Fellowship, and grants from the National Endowment for the Arts, Von Rydingsvard gained widespread recognition for translating visual cues of human anatomy, the environment, and household vessels into large-scale pieces, departing from the aloof nature of 1970s minimalism. She was the subject of solo exhibitions at the Philadelphia Museum of Art, the National Museum in Krakow, and the Yorkshire Sculpture Park in the United Kingdom, and her work is in the permanent collections of the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Museum of Modern Art, and the Whitney Museum of American Art in Manhattan.

#1058. An Exclusive Look Inside the 30th Anniversary Celebration of ‘Rent’

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

On Monday night, members of the original Broadway cast of Rent—the iconic rock opera that dropped the plot of La Bohème into Alphabet City during the HIV/AIDS crisis—united with a sprawling company of theater talent to celebrate the show’s 30th birthday before a sold-out crowd at New York’s Lunt-Fontanne Theatre.

Rent played its first preview off-Broadway, at the New York Theatre Workshop, on January 25, 1996—the same day that Jonathan Larson, its 35-year-old composer and librettist, died suddenly at his home in Manhattan. Three months later, on April 29, it opened at the Nederlander Theatre, going on to earn 10 Tony nominations (the most of any production that season) and four wins, including for best musical. After making stars of its young actors, including Anthony Rapp, Adam Pascal, Taye Diggs, and Idina Menzel, Rent’s subsequent iterations across the country, in regional and touring productions, also helped to launch the careers of performers like Aaron Tveit, Brandon Uranowitz, and Norbert Leo Butz. (Its Broadway run ended in 2008, after a dozen years and 5,123 regular performances.)

Astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope have observed a galaxy with a ring of newborn stars as it appeared 2.1 billion years after the Big Bang — hinting that the early universe may have been more chaotic than once thought.

A view of the possible ring galaxy GS18660 as seen by the James Webb Space Telescope. New observations hint that the ancient galaxy may have been created by a collision with an 'intruder' galaxy.

Astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have discovered a galaxy with a glowing ring of newborn stars shining in the early universe. The galaxy, which the researchers observed as it appeared just 2.1 billion years after the Big Bang, is the most distant ring galaxy ever found.

The ring galaxy, known as GS18660, may be the result of a collision between two galaxies, the researchers reported in their study, published Sept. 8 in the journal Astronomy & Astrophysics.

It's rare to find the possible snapshot of a merger like this, given the timescales involved in these systems, Christopher Conselice, a professor of extragalactic astronomy at the University of Manchester in the U.K. who was not involved in the study, told Live Science. Although interactions between galaxies may have been common in the early universe, the creation of ringed systems like this is thought to be unusual.

"In this sense, the galaxy system is quite unique and may give us important information about early galaxy formation," Conselice said in an email.

Scientists selected GS18660 for observation because it looked stretched out in older Hubble Space Telescope images.

"We therefore did not target GS18660 specifically as a ring galaxy," Michele Perna, a postdoctoral researcher at the Spanish Astrobiology Center and the first author of the study, told Live Science in an email. "When we observed it with JWST [Near-Infrared Spectrograph instrument], the much more detailed data revealed the prominent ring structure and allowed us to investigate the galaxy in three dimensions."

Using JWST, the team looked at the galaxy's unique shape, tracked the motions of its hot gas, analyzed the gas's chemical properties, and studied the galaxy's star formation history.

When a small galaxy plunges through the center of a larger galaxy, the impact creates a wave of dense gas that ripples outward, similar to how water moves when a stone is dropped into a pond. As a result, a collisional galaxy is formed. Gas piles up along the wave and forms stars, producing a ring. These galaxies are rare in the local universe, and the few candidates found in the distant universe have been hard to confirm.

Several clues support the case for GS18660 as a collisional ring galaxy. For one, the researchers spotted a small companion galaxy, moving away from GS18660 at about 264 miles per second (425 kilometers per second). They also detected a bridge of gas linking the two, and the velocity along it changed, Perna noted. The change in velocity along this bridge-like structure indicates that the companion and GS18660 may be interacting.

Its position is also important, Perna added ‪—‬ the companion is located roughly along the main galaxy's minor axis, consistent with where an intruder would be expected to appear after a head-on plunge through the galaxy's disk.

Using the companion's speed and distance, the team estimated a rough timeline for the impact. It revealed that the collision happened about 17 million to 42 million years earlier in the galaxy's history. This time scale seems reasonable, Conselice said.

He explained that it is difficult to determine the age of a past interaction directly, but that if one did occur, it must have happened around the time a rapid star-formation episode began and its age should be consistent with the average ages of the star clusters.

Looking at star formation rates with different instruments, the team estimates that 10% to 20% of the stellar mass in the ring formed in the past few tens of millions of years. This suggests that the collision and the ring's burst of new stars might have happened at the same time, so they were most likely caused by the same event, Perna explained.

"It is not, by itself, definitive proof of a collision, but together with the bridge and the ring morphology, it strengthens the case for a recent impact," Perna noted.

The discovery may also shed light on how common these kinds of collisions used to be. Whereas earlier estimates suggested that collisional rings in the early universe might be about as rare as they are today ‪—‬ around 1 in 10,000 galaxies ‪—‬ the new findings hint that head-on collisions that form ring galaxies may be more common in the early universe.

"If GS18660 is confirmed as a collisional ring, it would therefore suggest that we need to revisit our estimates of how frequently these systems occur in the distant universe," Perna said

The team expressed caution about how much the data can prove. "It is really the combination of several pieces of evidence rather than one single observation," Perna said. "So we regard the collisional interpretation as our preferred scenario rather than a definitive conclusion."

Conselice thinks the evidence presented in the paper is consistent with a genuine collisional origin for the ring. "It looks to me like the merger hypothesis is more likely, given the nearby galaxy intruder and the stability of the intruder galaxy and the main galaxy itself," Conselice said. “Overall, it is convincing evidence that a past interaction and/or merger has occurred for this galaxy system based on the light distribution."

The other competing explanation involves a stellar bar. It proposes that the ring was not made by an intruder at all. GS18660 also shows a bar-like structure running north to south, and bars can funnel gas and stars into rings at specific distances from the center. Perna said the team cannot yet tell whether this is a true stellar bar or a result of the interaction.

A bar would be an exciting finding, too. JWST has already discovered barred galaxies in the early universe, including CEERS-2112. But researchers have not yet found a clear example of a bar-driven ring so early in the universe.

"Whether the ring was triggered by another galaxy or by the internal dynamics of GS18660," Perna said, "the result would tell us something important about how galaxies were already becoming structurally organised when the universe was only a few billion years old."

Almost every deer on the farm is on its feet. Bucks and does of all ages are hammering food sources. They’re hungry after the rut, but there’s also a snowstorm incoming. Temps are dropping, and there’s a flurry of snowfall — and activity.

Even though the entire herd appears to be on its feet, one buck is still holed up in his fortress. It’s broad daylight, frigid, and snowing. The 5-year-old you want to kill refuses to leave his bedding sanctuary. He’ll move in daylight — all deer must move eventually — but not until it gets closer to dark.

It’s been said many times, but a deer and a mature buck are two different animals.  Older bucks don’t just have more inches of antler on average — they have more wits, senses, and experience. You don’t get to be a five-year-old deer without learning how to survive in the wild, and that’s why these deer not only look different but behave in ways that don’t often align with the does and younger bucks we’re used to seeing. Here’s how.

Hunters can use many physical signs to estimate a buck’s age, but the key I often look for is a buck’s muscle definition. Older whitetails look vastly different than their younger cohorts. Mature bucks have a muscled-up body structure that younger deer just don’t have. As deer progress through each age bracket, they exhibit different body structures.

For example, yearling bucks look like small does with antlers. Two-year-old bucks look like large does with antlers. Three-year-old bucks exhibit a more muscular frame but aren’t filled out just yet. Four-year-old bucks show significant swelling, with heavy shoulders and an enlarged brisket and neck. Five-year-old bucks and older exhibit even more body weight with heavy swelling of the neck, shoulders, and brisket. They might even begin displaying a pot belly and swayed back. Practice judging age in the field, and you’ll be able to better recognize an old buck when he shows up.

Every set of antlers is unique, regardless of the buck’s age. Still, as bucks reach older age classes, they express their genetic code and capabilities. Often, this leads to antler character traits that are usually present in older deer, regardless of the overall score or size of a deer. Examples include kickers, stickers, drop tines, and other unique antler points. It can even lead to greater numbers of points and other interesting non-typical characteristics. Even as they age and their antler size may decrease, older deer still tend to have more mass than younger bucks, particularly around the bases of their antlers.

Deer don’t have personalities in the human sense, but researchers have identified behavioral preferences that we can think of as personalities — particularly when it comes to their movements across the landscape. Some bucks are homebodies or wanderers. (One study found that 33 percent of bucks had a mobile personality.) And as bucks reach older age classes, these personalities can become even more apparent. For example, bucks might be more aggressive or passive. They might be more or less engaged in the rut. Perhaps they even express preferences for specific food sources. If you can start to identify those tendencies and preferences, you have a better shot of patterning your buck.

A common myth suggests that bucks increase their home ranges as they age. For the vast majority of whitetails, this isn’t true. Numerous studies have determined that whitetail buck home ranges and core areas actually decrease in size as they get older. As they become more familiar with their surroundings, they learn the safest areas, best bedding, premier food sources, and more. This leads to a more distinct and deliberate use of the landscape. Hunting older bucks requires finding these smaller areas and home ranges and hunting them accordingly.

As bucks get older, they become more in tune with their environment. They learn where hunters focus their efforts (we humans can become patternable and predictable) and how to avoid us. Older bucks use the landscape to their advantage, especially from a safety perspective.

For instance, bucks use thermal hubs, the low points in the topography where scent collects from the surrounding area. In other words, they don’t necessarily have to walk just downwind of your stand to know there’s a hunter in the area.

Another example includes bucks bedding in ways that give them a security advantage. For instance, mature bucks often bed on leeward ridges, ridge endings, bench edges, and more. Bedding in certain ways allows them to see danger coming from a distance while also smelling any threats upwind that could approach from behind them.

Bucks are also good at finding trails and travel routes that elude stand locations, blinds, and other hunting setups.

Let’s dig even deeper into a buck’s bedding habits, all of which are proven methods that help mature deer detect danger before it detects them. From a scenting perspective, deer find many ways to benefit from prevailing winds and daily thermals.

Using Advantageous Terrain: Bucks tend to bed in areas with advantageous terrain. These locations give them better visibility but also provide winding advantages. As noted above, hilly and mountainous terrain allows deer to use topography to their advantage, but even flatland whitetails find ways to do this. Often, deer that bed in flatter creek or river bottomlands bed close to the water.

For example, deer routinely bed in oxbows. A location with water on three sides helps diminish the odds of a predator approaching from those directions. And if they do, deer can hear them coming through the water. Meanwhile, they can see danger that comes by way of land. If something does approach, deer can bolt out the back door and across the water to safety.

As mentioned above, bucks tend to express a unique personality type. Within those individual preferences comes varied responses to hunting pressure. Human intrusion impacts every deer differently, but most mature bucks don’t tolerate much of it. If they do, they’ve likely been conditioned to certain activities on the property. In other words, some mature bucks may tolerate more pressure than others, but in general, mature bucks won’t tolerate all of it.

Though all bucks are biologically motivated to participate in the rut, some individual bucks can be more active than others. Depending on an individual buck’s tendencies, they might be very active and move a lot during daylight, or they might be reclusive, searching for does at night and bedding back down before daylight.

Numerous expert hunters I’ve talked to agree on one particular thing — bucks are hardest to hunt from ages four to six. Often when a buck hits seven, eight, or older, they seem to become more active during the daylight. Perhaps they become overconfident, get delusional and senile, or maybe they just care less. Regardless, they move more and get easier to kill again, almost as a 3-year-old buck would.

Hunting old bucks requires different approaches and tactics than hunting deer in general (does and younger bucks). So, put together a good plan this season, and tag that old buck that’s been haunting your dreams.

#1159. 4 Workout Strategies This Over-50 Trainer Uses to Keep Building Muscle

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Use his guidance so you can train hard and hurt less.

THE MUSCLE-BUILDING NARRATIVE after age 50 isn’t pretty. According to doomers online, your testosterone levels are falling, your energy levels are in massive decline, and everything seems to throw your back out.

But contrary to popular belief, you can still muscle up. And Bryan Krahn, 53 years old and as strong as he’s ever been, is proof. The trainer, who created MH’s Max Muscle at 50 workout program, still works out four or five days a week and has maintained a muscular 210-pound physique..

That doesn’t mean he trains exactly like he did when he was in his 20s. Instead, over the last three decades, he’s made a series of adjustments, shifting his fitness priorities (much less bench pressing) and experimenting with new gym ideas. All that trial-and-error helped Krahn develop these four critical post-50 training principles, which will allow you to push your muscle-building limits for decades to come without crushing your body. He discussed these ideas (and much more) on MH’s Strong Talk podcast.

LIKE MOST GUYS, Krahn relied on dumbbells and barbells for years. But about a decade ago, those exercises started to hurt his joints. He has since shifted his focus to training that minimizes his risk of getting hurt. He is quick to note this isn’t about being overly cautious—avoiding injury is not the same as training scared.

Part of the fix for Krahn involved taking a little bit of time off. The other part: He headed to a section of the gym that guys often forget about: the machine area. Because yes, dumbbells and barbells and kettlebells seem to build more “functional” strength (so the narrative goes). But as you get older, those tools don’t always work as well with your joints. “Within four or five months, I have no pain, and I’m strong on these machines,” Krahn says. “Cables are indispensable as you get older.”

Machines allow you to focus on doing movements along very fixed tracks, and that lets you concentrate on generating the maximum amount of force with your muscles. That’s a key mechanic for growth. “And if you’re a beginner, generating that force isn’t always easy with free weights,” says MH fitness director Ebenezer Samuel, C.S.C.S. “Inserting one machine exercise in your routines near the end can insure you get a chance to push your limits just a bit.”

Two machines to consider: A cable row machine, which blasts your back, and the leg press machine, which ensures you can challenge hamstrings, glutes, and quads with relatively heavy load.

A bonus: Experimenting with machines just may reignite your passion for the gym, too. Krahn says the machines allow him to enjoy his workouts—much like he did in his 20s. “I love going to the gym again,” he says.

FROM BARBELL BENCH presses to back squats to barbell deadlifts, some lifts in the gym are so popular and widespread, they might feel mandatory. But no—Krahn says you don't have to commit to any of them.

Krahn learned that when training arms. Yes, he loved skull crushers, a triceps exercise that can be notoriously tough on the elbows. But he swapped in triceps pressdowns and still built the muscle he was chasing. He did the same with his biceps work, swapping out supinated dumbbell biceps curls for hammer curls. "As soon as something feels off," he says, "let's try a variation."

Exercise physiologist and strength coach Dr. Pat Davidson agrees. Most lifts that guys think are mandatory can easily be replaced. "So many people just get emotionally attached to an exercise," Davidson says. "Don't marry an exercise."

Krahn says machines are especially valuable here, too. "It's so difficult to get hurt (on machines)," he says. "You can kind of unleash that side of yourself and really push into failure."

Major lesson: The moment you feel pain in your joints, consider finding a substitute lift for the exercise that's caused you pain. Don't wait.

THESE DAYS, KRAHN thinks about building more than muscle. He also wants to maintain his body’s ability to be powerful. What does it mean to be powerful? Muscles produce force—but they also need the ability to produce that force very quickly, which happens whenever you jump, hop, or begin to run.

And sure, plyometric exercises can train power. But once you’re in your 50s, your body can’t always absorb that pounding. That’s why Krahn loves sled pushes. He uses the move as a warmup, especially on upper body days when he’s focused on a variety of push activities. Pushing a weighted sled is a move that can build glutes, quads, and hamstrings, and light up your core. It also quietly trains something called triple-extension, the act of straightening your knee, hip, and ankle joints all at once—which is a key to running and jumping.

The best part of a sled push, Davidson says: You get to build power without much injury risk. “Anytime you’re in a forward lean acceleration posture, it's much safer,” Davidson says. “It’s almost a Smith machine of running.” Consider doing two to three sets of 30-second sled pushes to start your next workout.

SHORT ON TIME? If Krahn has only 15 minutes in a day, he’ll walk. It’s a non-negotiable in his life. “I get a lot done in that 15 minutes,” he says. “I don’t lift weights every day, but I walk every day.”

There are a lot of reasons to add walking into your daily routine. For Krahn, walking gives him the mental reprieve to step away from his responsibilities and keep his mind refreshed. Walking also keeps your body moving, limiting your sedentary time.

At 50, it’s not always about going hard or pushing or limits. Walking keeps your body moving in a joint-friendly manner, even when you're fatigued (while sneaking in some light calorie-burn, too). Daily walking also helps to lower the risk of heart disease, a concern at any age but especially for older adults.

“Right now, that’s the number one killer in the US,” Mario Mejia, PT, DPT, physical therapist at Next Play Physical Therapy & Performance, previously told MH. “Heart disease can happen anytime but as we age, physiologically there are differences in the heart, lungs, and bone structure.

Above all, Krahn's tips provide a template for how to keep building muscle and lose fat into your 50s and beyond. And Krahn's full Max Muscle at 50 program delivers even more guidance. Check it out here.
