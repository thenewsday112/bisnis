#1374. A Complete Timeline of Blake Lively and Ryan Reynolds’s Relationship

📅 Feb 24, 2023  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Blake Lively and Ryan Reynolds have been together for over a decade. The pair began dating in 2011 and got married about a year later. That might seem fast, but they’d been friends for a while and could simply no longer deny the chemistry between them.

Today, Lively and Reynolds remain happily married and share three daughters—James, Inez, and Betty—and their fourth child, son Olin.

Here, the complete history of Lively and Reynolds’s love story.

Lively and Reynolds met through work. The two starred in the superhero movie Green Lantern but didn’t date at the time. They both were actually in other relationships. Lively was dating her Gossip Girl co-star Penn Badgley, while Reynolds was married to Scarlett Johansson, a future on-screen superhero herself. They did appear together for the first time during Comic-Con in San Diego in 2010, preceding the film’s release.

A few months later, Badgley and Lively split after three years together but supposedly remained friends.

They did not publicize the news of their breakup right away, however.

At the end of that same year, Reynolds and Johansson released a joint statement to People confirming they were separating, which read, “After long and careful consideration on both our parts, we’ve decided to end our marriage. We entered our relationship with love, and it’s with love and kindness we leave it. While privacy isn’t expected, it’s certainly appreciated.”

Reynolds filed for divorce a few weeks later in December.

Lively and Reynolds walked the red carpet premiere of Green Lantern in Hollywood on June 15, 2011. There were still no rumors of a romance, and Lively was actually romantically linked to Leonardo DiCaprio. Lively and DiCaprio were first photographed together in Cannes, France, that May, and then spent the summer together. But it was a brief fling, and E! reported they ended things when the season changed.

But with fall came new love. People reported that Lively and Reynolds were dating shortly before Halloween, when she was seen exiting his apartment in Boston at the crack of dawn after his birthday weekend. A source told People at the time that the two are “very much a couple,” and “they’re really happy together.”

By Christmas time, Lively was already familiar with Reynolds’s family, visiting his hometown of Vancouver in late December. Paparazzi caught a shot of them holding hands while walking in the streets between family time.

In the new year, PopSugar reported the pair went to New Orleans to Reynolds’s private home for a romantic getaway.

The couple were back in Vancouver in the spring, PopSugar reported. They also ferried to Nanaimo, British Columbia, to meet with Reynolds’s brother.

On Sept. 9, 2012, Lively and Reynolds got married during a small secret ceremony with friends and family. The bride wore a couture gown designed by Marchesa, and Reynolds was in a classic Burberry suit.

“When Ryan first saw her, his face broke into a huge smile that crinkled his eyes,” a source told People. “They just stared at each other with absolute love. Their faces were lit up with the candlelight, and it looked like something out of a romance movie.”

The couple have since apologized for holding their event at Boone Hall in Mount Pleasant, South Carolina. Boone Hall is a former plantation, and a wedding on one is generally viewed as distasteful and offensive given the site’s history.

Reynolds said in an August 2020 interview with Fast Company, “It’s something we’ll always be deeply and unreservedly sorry for. It’s impossible to reconcile. What we saw at the time was a wedding venue on Pinterest. What we saw after was a place built upon devastating tragedy. Years ago, we got married again at home—but shame works in weird ways. A giant fucking mistake like that can either cause you to shut down or it can reframe things and move you into action. It doesn’t mean you won’t fuck up again. But re-patterning and challenging lifelong social conditioning is a job that doesn’t end.”

In 2019, the couple donated $2 million to the NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund and the Young Center for Immigrant Children’s Rights.

Just after their marriage, Lively told Allure, “Right now, I’m so peaceful. I’ve never been happier in my life,” she said. It was also her first mention of a family since her marriage.

“I’ve always wanted a big family,” she told the outlet, answering a question about how many kids she wants with, “Oh, I’d love 30 if I could.”

The pair was photographed out in Paris showing a little PDA.

The pair appeared on the red carpet together for the first time since getting married, attending The Sound of Change Live concert at Twickenham Stadium in London.

For Lucky magazine’s September cover, Lively opened up about how marriage was influencing her personal style. Not at all!

“I was recently asked how my style has changed since I got married, as if I was living under some sort of dictatorship,” she claimed. “I should hope I dress differently at 25 than I did when I graduated high school. I hope I never stop changing."

But she admitted that Reynolds does influence her in some ways.

For years, travelers have become accustomed to meticulously packing liquids ranging from shampoo to sunscreen in their carry-on bags in order to meet the TSA liquid limit—and avoid getting that expensive perfume confiscated.

Internationally, this rule is known as “3-1-1." Liquids, gels, and creams packed in a carry-on must be in containers no larger than 3.4 ounces (100 mL). All containers must fit in a single, clear, quart-sized bag. (Larger liquids can still be brought along on your travels but must be packed in checked luggage.)

While commonly referred to as “liquids,” these regulated substances are technically known as LAGs, an abbreviation for liquids, aerosols, and gels. This category also encompasses creams or pastes—essentially anything that can be cut through or spread with a butter knife—from common travel necessities like shampoo, face wash, and sunscreen, to toothpaste, hairspray, and even peanut butter.

The Transportation Security Administration (TSA) first introduced the rule in August 2006, after British police foiled a terrorist plot to detonate explosive liquid in soda cans on flights from London to the US and Canada—prompting countries around the world to adopt the new airport security measure.

Today, most countries abide by the rule, though it has loosened in recent years, as more airports install advanced CT scanners with the capability to identify hazardous liquids. Some UK airports, including London Heathrow (LHR), now allow liquids of up to 2 liters (about 67.6 ounces).

In the US, a TSA spokesperson tells Condé Nast Traveler they “anticipate the limit on liquids will be lifted in the coming years." However, with the roll-out of the new scanners varying by terminal, the limit still remains in effect nationwide.

In this transitional period, the decisions are ultimately up to the individual officers, but it turns out there are certain exceptions to the 3-1-1 rule. The TSA currently allows travelers to take 10 common full-size liquids through airport security in the US.

To bring approved oversized liquids through security, travelers need to declare these items to a security officer prior to screening and place them in a separate bin. Anything that triggers the alarm while going through security is subject to further inspection. Here's what you need to know.

In the expansive world of vitamins and nutrients, you’ve probably heard of magnesium, but you may be fuzzy on the details. Because many Americans don’t get enough magnesium in their diet, it is a micronutrient worth paying attention to.

Here’s the thing: Magnesium supports more than 300 reactions in your body, says Sonya Angelone, RD, a spokeswoman for the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics. That includes things like your body’s ability to break down protein, help your muscles function, and produce energy. “Magnesium also helps transport calcium and potassium across cell membranes,” Angelone says.

Magnesium shows up in a slew of foods (more on those in a sec), but you can also get more of it from taking a supplement. So, what benefits of magnesium do you need to know about? Are there any risks to taking a magnesium supplement? Here’s the deal.

Meet the experts: Sonya Angelone, RD, is a spokeswoman for the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics. Jessica Cording, RD, is a dietitian and author of The Little Book of Game-Changers. Scott Keatley, RD, is co-owner of Keatley Medical Nutrition Therapy.

“Magnesium is an essential micronutrient, meaning that we have to get relatively small amounts of it from our diet or via supplementation,” explains Scott Keatley, RD, co-owner of Keatley Medical Nutrition Therapy. Magnesium is a “co-factor” in many systems in your body, “which means without magnesium things just won't function properly,” Keatley reiterates.

Most adult women should get 310 to 360 milligrams of magnesium a day, depending on age and whether you’re pregnant or breastfeeding. If you get less than that, your body may not function as efficiently as it should.

Unfortunately, an analysis of data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) found that 48 percent of Americans of all ages have less magnesium than they should.

Magnesium is “crucial for proper muscle contractions,” Angelone says, noting that it helps prevent muscle cramps and has even been linked with helping people work out more. A 2024 review found that adequate magnesium intake benefits muscle mass, power, and performance. Moreover, this micronutrient may improve muscle stem cells and counteract muscle atrophy.

“Magnesium helps maintain a regular heart rhythm,” Angelone says. “It has also been found to help with atrial fibrillation and high blood pressure since it is a smooth muscle relaxant.”

In fact, magnesium supplements positively affected risk factors for heart disease, including LDL (bad) cholesterol, HDL (good) cholesterol, and blood pressure levels—especially in people who were deficient in magnesium, according to a 2017 scientific review.

“Magnesium helps block certain receptors in the brain, which can lead to less excitement and damage to the cell,” Angelone says. Low magnesium intake was linked with depression in study participants who were under 65, one 2015 data analysis of more than 8,800 people found. In fact, those with the lowest levels of magnesium intake had a 22 percent greater risk of depression.

Still, it’s hard to say that low magnesium levels actually cause depression. “What is more likely is that individuals with depression have eating patterns that are not conducive to maintaining food magnesium status and that magnesium supplementation or increased intake of magnesium helps get everything back running to 100 percent,” Keatley explains. “But the underlying depression has to be dealt with.” So make sure to work with a mental health professional if you are experiencing symptoms of depression.

People who had higher levels of magnesium had a lower risk of anxiety and depression, one study of nearly 3,200 people found. Magnesium deficiency “is associated with high stress levels which may contribute to anxiety,” Angelone says. “Magnesium may also help lower cortisol and increase GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid), which, in turn, can help lower anxiety,” she adds.

Bear with us here for a sec: Magnesium competes with calcium in your body, and high levels of calcium are linked with a decrease in production of insulin, which helps move glucose (aka sugar) into your cells where it’s used for energy. That “means more sugar stays in the blood, causing damage,” Keatley says.

On the flip side, magnesium can help your body use insulin and keep those levels in balance, Angelone says. Higher magnesium levels have been linked with a lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes, which is when your body can’t make or use insulin properly, per 2017 research in Diabetes Care.

Bodily inflammation has been linked with everything from pain to the development of serious health conditions like diabetes and cancer. As a result, controlling the inflammation can be beneficial.

But magnesium’s role in tamping down inflammation is indirect. “Magnesium is a major player in glutathione, the body's master antioxidant, but magnesium by itself does not have any anti-inflammatory benefits,” Keatley says. Magnesium also impacts the production of the active form of vitamin D in the body, “which is related to a healthy immune system,” Angelone says.

PMS can cause intense symptoms like pain and cramping, and magnesium may help. “The role of magnesium as a smooth muscle relaxant may help alleviate cramps,” Angelone says. Research backs this up, showing it may help with cramps, menstrual migraine, and other symptoms, a 2017 scientific literature review determined.

“Magnesium can help you relax and sleep longer,” Angelone says. “It can also help regulate melatonin secretion, which helps a person fall asleep.” She also points out that magnesium helps maintain healthy levels of GABA, a neurotransmitter that promotes sleep.

Worth noting: People who took magnesium supplements were able to fall asleep about 17 minutes faster in a 2021 scientific review of 151 older adults with insomnia.

Increasing your magnesium intake may help lower the number of headaches you get, Keatley says. #833. How Long to Cook a Turkey by Weight

Several factors contribute to a perfect Thanksgiving, but the holiday hinges on one question in particular: How long will it take to cook that turkey? Notoriously prone to overcooking, turkey meat has been ridiculed for its perceived dryness time and again. On top of the meat’s naturally lean characteristics, these very large birds have sections that vary widely in shape and thickness, making it tough (pun intended) to achieve a juicy interior and crispy golden brown skin. Sadly, not even the richest turkey gravy can save a bird that isn’t cooked properly.

So, how long does it take to cook a turkey? For a whole, unstuffed turkey, plan on roughly 13 minutes per pound at 350°F. That works out to about 3 hours for a 12–14-pound bird, though the precise roasting time depends on its size, your oven temperature, and how you prepare it.

But that doesn’t mean it has to take up all of your prep time. Armed with the turkey cooking time chart below and a few essential roasting tips, you’ll have ample time to focus on arguably everyone’s favorite part of the Thanksgiving dinner spread (the side dishes) without stressing too much about the main event.

The simplest approach is to roast a whole, unstuffed turkey, breast-side up, at 350°F. Ideal for first-time turkey cooks, this method requires relatively little attention and results in an excellent bird. As a general rule, allow about 13 minutes per pound, but keep in mind that cooking times are only estimates.

These times assume your turkey is fully thawed and unstuffed. Start checking for doneness about three quarters of the way through the estimated roasting time, then check more frequently as the turkey approaches its target temperature. Remember, the thermometer—not the clock—gets the final say.

One more thing: Stuffed turkeys take much longer to cook through, making the breast meat prone to drying out while you wait for the center of the stuffing to reach a safe temperature. We prefer stuffing recipes (a.k.a. dressing recipes) that bake separately.

A perfectly browned bird isn’t necessarily a fully cooked one. The most reliable way to check for doneness is with an instant-read thermometer, inserted into the thickest part of the breast and the innermost portions of the thigh, without touching bone.

The USDA recommends cooking turkey to an internal temperature of 165°F in all three spots. Food director Chris Morocco recommends cooking dark meat to an even higher final temperature—around 175°F–190°F—so its connective tissue has more time to break down. That’s part of what makes cooking a whole turkey tricky: The breast and legs don’t reach their ideal temperatures at the same time.

You’ll also want to account for carryover cooking. A turkey’s internal temperature keeps rising after it comes out of the oven because of residual heat. Some of our favorite roast turkey recipes use that residual heat to finish cooking the meat, but the temperature increase varies with the size of the bird and the roasting method; don’t assume it will rise by a specific number of degrees.

The Bon Appétit Test Kitchen recommends removing your turkey from the oven when the breast has reached an internal temperature of 150°F. Check the temperature periodically while the bird rests to ensure it reaches 165°F.

Before you start carving, give your turkey a rest. For a whole bird, 30–60 minutes allows the juices to redistribute, helping keep the meat moist when you cut into it.

Set the turkey on a cutting board with a trough or a platter to collect any runoff juices (save them for gravy). You don’t need to tent it with aluminum foil: The bird’s insulation will keep it warm surprisingly long. Plus, trapping steam beneath the foil can cause crispy skin to go limp.

Whether you roast your turkey whole, spatchcocked, or cut into parts, take these preliminary steps. They won’t necessarily shorten the cooking time, but they’ll help you get that succulent-on-the-inside, crackly-on-the-outside result.

Unless you look forward to eating leftover turkey for days, shop for a bird that’s just big enough to feed your Thanksgiving crew. A smaller turkey will cut roasting time, leaving the oven free for sides and pies.

A frozen turkey needs time to thaw—and not just a few hours on the counter. Plan on about 24 hours in the refrigerator for every 4–5 pounds of turkey. That means a 12–14-pound bird will need roughly three to four days to thaw completely.

A partially frozen turkey can seriously throw off your roasting time, and without thawing, you may not be able to remove the giblets. If Thanksgiving is approaching faster than your bird is defrosting, there are other safe ways to speed things along.

Dry brining is key to moist, succulent meat. Whether you choose plain kosher salt, a salt-and-pepper mix, or a custom spice blend, salt draws moisture to the turkey’s surface. The salty juices are then reabsorbed into the meat, carrying the seasoning with them. This helps the turkey retain moisture as it cooks, tenderizes the meat, and encourages skin browning—all hallmarks of a properly cooked bird.

What about a wet brine, you ask? We’re firmly in the dry-brine camp. It’s easier, less messy, and doesn’t require finding space in an already crowded refrigerator for a giant container of salt water. (Fridge space is at a premium leading up to Thanksgiving.)

Give your dry-brined turkey at least 12 hours and up to three days in the refrigerator, uncovered, before roasting. That might sound lengthy, but the salt needs time to work its way through the turkey’s big muscles. If you’re running behind, you can start dry-brining before the bird has completely thawed. Once the exterior is pliable enough to season, apply the brine and return the turkey to the refrigerator to finish thawing.

Invest in an oven thermometer before the big day arrives. Oven temperatures can vary from what the dial shows, and even a small discrepancy can make a difference when you’re roasting something for several hours.

Once the turkey is in the oven, resist the urge to keep checking on it. Opening the door lets heat escape, which can lengthen cooking time. A thermometer and a little patience will serve you better than peeking at the bird repeatedly.

This method comes from Andy Baraghani’s dry-brined roast turkey recipe (a.k.a. the no-fail turkey method we come back to every Thanksgiving). Andy had two main goals for his perfect turkey: Brown the skin so it’s crackly and delicious, and roast the meat so it’s tender and moist.

The secret to accomplishing both? Two oven temperatures. Andy starts his turkey at relatively high heat—425°F–450°F for 30–45 minutes—to encourage browning, then reduces the temperature to 300°F–350°F for another 65–85 minutes, following the recipe’s internal-temperature guidance. As a bonus, he brushes the outside of the bird with a tangy honey glaze to build a glossy, bronzed shellac.

This two-temperature method can get a 12–16-pound turkey out of the oven in around two hours, considerably faster than the standard approach. You’ll have to pay closer attention, though: Keep an eye on the skin, and follow the recipe’s instructions for adjusting the heat if it starts to get too dark.

For the full technique, including the dry brine and glaze, follow Andy’s recipe.

Roasting a turkey in parts is another way to address uneven cooking. With the pieces arranged separately, each one gets optimal exposure to the oven’s heat, giving you more control over when the breast and dark meat are done. And yes, even turkey pieces benefit from a dry brine, so the preparation tips above still apply.

You can break down the raw turkey yourself if you feel confident (and have a sharp enough knife), but there’s no shame in asking your butcher to handle the job. Request five pieces: two legs, two wings, and one whole turkey breast, with the backbone removed. Save that backbone for turkey stock.

Instead of a typical roasting pan, this method uses a wire rack set inside a rimmed baking sheet. The setup lets hot air circulate around the meat, giving the skin its best chance to crisp while catching the umami-packed drippings.

In another favorite Thanksgiving turkey recipe, the dry-brined pieces roast at 425°F for 20–25 minutes, then 300°F for another 50–70 minutes. Cooking in parts lets you can monitor each piece individually, so you can pull the breast when it reaches the right temperature and give the legs more time if needed. Follow the recipe’s instructions for assessing doneness, keeping in mind the USDA’s 165°F minimum temperature recommendation.

The only downside to roasting a turkey in pieces? You’ll lose some of the drama that comes with ushering a whole roasted bird to the table—but who needs oohs and aahs when a chorus of mmms awaits?

Musky. The name alone elicits a variety of reactions from anglers, some good, and some painful. However you feel about musky, there’s no denying their appeal as a top-tier freshwater sportfish that puts tackle, especially musky reels, to the test. The work to land these freshwater beasts often has anglers screaming in agony more than joy, and even with the proper gear, fishing for musky is a challenging enough pursuit on its own. The last thing you need is to hook into the fish of your dreams and find your reel isn’t up to the task. To help you avoid that fate, I’ve used my musky fishing experience as well as input from trusted guides to compile a list of the best musky reels.

To make your musky reel decision ask yourself two questions:

There’s no denying musky gear can get expensive quickly, so there’s no need to spend a ton of money if you’re only going to fish for them a few times a year. You’ll probably be fine with one of the less durable but still extremely capable reel options. But, if musky are your new favorite species then it’s worthwhile to invest in the best musky reel you can afford.

If you’re throwing larger lures and casting all day, something lighter and smoother is probably the best choice. If you are trolling and not casting as much, weight and castability are less of a factor.

If you’re a serious musky angler who throws a variety of lures and spends a lot of time and effort chasing these fish, this is the reel for you. While pricey, it’s a step above all others in quality and functionality and well worth it for the dedicated musky angler.

The Shimano TranX line has been around for over a decade, and when it first appeared, it immediately made a statement about the ability of low-profile baitcasters. While they had previously been in the realm of bass fishing, the TranX helped pioneer the new generation of purpose-built low-profile reels capable of handling big gamefish.

The 300 and 400 models are ideal for throwing smaller finesse glide baits and spinners, as well as capable of handling some of the best musky lures like larger swimbaits and plugs that are notorious for making musky fishing so painful. The 500 is a reel designed to handle big saltwater gamefish. Still, its indestructible design and robust frame make it ideal for heaving the heaviest musky baits on the market while still allowing for a smooth retrieve and delicate touch during the fight.

The only downside to the TranX series is the drag for the 300 and 400 sizes. While the reels are significantly lighter and easier to use than the 500 (around 12 ounces), they range from 18 to 22 pounds of drag, which is less than some competing reels in the category. The 500 tops out at 25 pounds of drag, which is solid but still not enough to target anything that swims. The TranX is also a great reel for throwing the best swimbaits for bass.

Honestly, it was a toss-up between the Daiwa Lexa HD and the Shimano Tranx for best overall. Many anglers and guides are split on which they prefer, with the slight edge going to the Shimano. With a proven design and high-end capability, the Daiwa Lexa HD is no slouch.

The light aluminum frame and side plate are complemented by stainless steel gearing, which helps maintain strength and enhance durability. It comes stock with a counterbalanced power handle and EVA foam grip, which both enhance comfort and help with cranking ability.

Most impressive, the 400 size alone comes in five different models and gear ratios, allowing the Lexa HD to be tailored to any task the angler needs.

The bail and inner mechanics of spinning gear are designed for finesse, putting all the dirty work and heavy lifting on the rod. On the other hand, a baitcaster is essentially a glorified winch with mechanics that allow for a significantly higher workload. That said, modern spinning reels have come a long way, and many of the issues seen as inherent deal breakers for musky fishing have been alleviated or outright eliminated.
