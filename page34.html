#563. Light pollution is reaching deep into the sea, putting marine life at risk

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Artificial light from coastal towns and offshore installations is penetrating Europe's seas, raising concerns about its effects on marine ecosystems.

Light pollution is often discussed as a problem for people trying to see the Milky Way. But artificial light also reaches beneath the ocean’s surface, where it can disrupt marine life.

The glow of a seaside town doesn’t stop at the water’s surface. It penetrates the sea, and in clear water, it can reach deeper than a three-story building is tall.

A new risk map reveals which of Europe’s seas face the greatest exposure to artificial light and where marine life may be most vulnerable.

The Adriatic, the Baltic, and the western Mediterranean come out worst, while the open waters around Iceland, the Barents Sea, and the islands of Macaronesia come out cleanest.

The map is built from satellite data and a brightness threshold, not from surveys of harmed animals.

Thomas Davies, a marine conservation researcher at the University of Plymouth, led the work with Tim Smyth of Plymouth Marine Laboratory and colleagues in Britain and Italy.

The goal was to develop a way to measure underwater light pollution risk, something European regulators have lacked until now.

In an interview with Earth.com, Davies explained that the most striking finding was how differently the risk was distributed across Europe’s seas.

“What is interesting is that most European Seas at high or moderate risk are small and bounded by developed coastlines around their periphery, the Adriatic for example,” he said.

“In those regions the maximum exposure risk is concentrated near the coast.”

The team needed a level of light at which marine animals begin responding. They used measurements from a copepod, one of the most light-sensitive marine animals studied.

These tiny crustaceans swim toward the surface at night as part of the largest daily migration of living organisms on Earth.

A tenth of a microwatt of white light per square meter is enough to move those copepods, and they can respond to moonlight more than 164 feet (50 meters) down.

The team used that tiny amount of light as its threshold, choosing a cautious approach to assessing risk.

Only about two dozen such measurements exist, and all involve open-water animals or larvae rather than seabed dwellers.

From there, a global atlas of undersea light gave the depth at which artificial light fades below that line.

Where artificial light fails to reach the threshold at the surface, the area is classified as having no exposure risk under the study’s criteria.

If artificial light reaches 3 feet (1 meter) below the surface, the risk is considered low. At depths of up to 33 feet (10 meters), the risk is moderate, and beyond that, it is high.

The atlas was sliced into 15 European sea regions and into bands by distance from shore.

Within 6 miles (10 kilometers) of the Adriatic coast, 56% of the water was classified as high risk, while another 43% was considered moderate risk.

The western Mediterranean faced even greater exposure near the coast, with 73% of its coastal waters classified as high risk.

The Baltic’s coastal band was mostly medium. Light pollution generally decreases with distance from shore, leaving most open waters at little or no risk.

Beyond 62 miles (100 kilometers) from shore, nearly all European seas were classified as having no risk from artificial light exposure. Only one region was an exception.

In the Greater North Sea, a fifth of the far-offshore water rated medium and 5% high, because oil and gas platforms light the middle of the sea.

Davies told Earth.com that this changes who is affected.

“In the North Sea, the maximum exposure risk is concentrated around these offshore installations in the middle of the sea,” he said.

“This means the marine species and ecosystems most impacted will be very different.”

The team ran a second map for life on the seafloor, using the light that actually lands on the bottom.

Water soaks up light on the way down, so the seabed is less exposed everywhere, and almost every sea region rated nil beyond 6 miles out.

The exception was close to shore. Within the first 0.6 miles (1 kilometer) of the Bay of Biscay and the Iberian coast, 49% of the seabed rated medium and 12% high.

The team put that down to dense coastal development. The Adriatic, Baltic, North Sea, and central Mediterranean followed, with low risk reaching 3 miles (5 kilometers) out.

The one-kilometer grid misses a lot. Ports and harbors produce more artificial light than the atlas can accurately capture, so the researchers emphasized the need for direct measurements in the brightest coastal areas.

The European Union’s marine strategy law of 2008 asks member states to keep “introduction of energy” at levels that do no harm. In practice that has meant underwater noise.

No country has set a target, threshold or test for light, and the law is now under review.

The paper adapts the approach used for underwater noise, demonstrating how exposure thresholds could help regulators assess artificial light pollution.

The authors stop short of saying what counts as good. That, they wrote, needs political agreement and consultation.

When Earth.com asked what he hopes the EU will do next, Davies pointed to the ongoing review as an opportunity for action.

“This paper demonstrates that the knowledge and tools are available to develop useful criteria,” he said.

“We believe we have clear evidence of risk and harm that urgently needs to be translated into meaningful action.”

Davies recommended that coastal communities follow the five lighting principles established by DarkSky International.

These include removing unnecessary lights, shielding fixtures to direct light where it’s needed, reducing brightness, using lights only when necessary, and choosing warmer bulbs that emit less blue light.

Blue light poses a particular concern because it penetrates seawater more deeply than many other wavelengths.

Most white LEDs emit substantial blue light, and their growing popularity could increase underwater light pollution.

According to the researchers, LED lamps grew from 2% of the world’s lighting market in 2012 to 48% in 2019.

The authors also suggest dark zones around islands, where tourists could see a sea without a lit shore.

Although the map identifies areas of potential exposure, it cannot determine how much harm artificial light is actually causing marine life.

The threshold is based on measurements from a single copepod species, and little is known about how sensitive seabed animals are to artificial light.

The atlas also relies on data from just one month, June 2019.

The team expects both the thresholds and the rankings to change as researchers gather more data on other species.

The map is online for any manager who wants to look at their own coastline.

The full study was published in the journal Ocean & Coastal Management.

#1083. Should We Really Date in Constant Fear of Getting Ghosted?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ghosting, unfortunately, is nothing new. The term has been around since the early 2000s, and even before then, people were being randomly cut off by romantic interests before there was a word for it. And yet, of course, no matter how commonplace it’s become, it still stings the same.

So in today’s particularly awful dating landscape, people are trying to prepare themselves ahead of time. Actually, they’re going into dating expecting to be ghosted.

In a viral TikTok video, user @kiwiproductions.tx says that this is how she moves in her relationships. “Nowadays you have to pretend that every time you see the person you’re talking to, it’s potentially the last time you’re ever going to see them,” she says in the video. “It’s happened to me too many times to count.”

As a response, people in the comments are sharing their own ghosting stories—and warnings to be aware of this happening to others. “You have to be prepared to be ghosted at any moment, even if you’re fully in a relationship,” one user wrote. “That’s why I no longer get excited when I meet someone,” another commented.

“This can often be a protective mechanism, especially for people who have experienced rejection or hurt in past relationships,” psychotherapist Mandy Lam tells Cosmopolitan. “It makes sense why someone might feel this way, particularly if they've been in situations where things seemed to be going well, only for the other person to suddenly disappear. For some people, this can also be a trauma response, especially if they've experienced significant relational hurt in the past.”

And while I feel for everyone who has been ghosted at one point (or more) in their life, perhaps we’ve gone from being cautiously guarded to a flat out cynical.

Part of dating is knowing that you’re putting yourself out there, allowing yourself to be vulnerable, and yes, taking the risk of being hurt. And although it feels like mentally preparing yourself for that hurt is a way to protect yourself, you actually may be doing more damage than you think. Lam explains that confirmation bias might happen in these situations.

Confirmation bias is the unconscious tendency to search for information that aligns with your pre-existing beliefs or expectations while ignoring anything contradictory.

“When we go into dating already expecting that things won't work out, we may be more likely to look for information that reinforces that belief,” she says. For example, if someone takes longer to respond to a text, we might automatically assume it’s because they’re losing interesting—because we convinced ourselves that was bound to happen before we even started texting them in the first place.

Plus, when you make it your mantra that any date you go on will likely be the last time you see that person, it affects the way you show up on dates—which then can influence how the other person reacts and responds.

If you walk into your next date already expecting to be rejected, it may make you hold back from sharing as much as you’d usually do or even try to appear more nonchalant. While you may think you’re just protecting yourself, your date is seeing a closed-off, detached person who really isn’t interested in them. Wouldn’t that make you uninterested in another date?

“In some ways, this can become a self-fulfilling prophecy, where our fear of rejection influences how we behave, which can then contribute to the very outcome we were afraid of,” Lam says.

I’m not saying that approaching dating with caution is a bad thing—quite the opposite. “Being guarded in dating, especially in the early stages, can be a healthy form of self-protection,” Lam says. “After all, you're still getting to know someone and figuring out whether they're someone you can trust and feel safe opening up to.”

This might mean making a conscious effort to take things slowly and setting boundaries around physical and emotional intimacy. This can actually be great for intentionally dating, as it allows you to develop the relationship at a pace that feels comfortable and appropriate.

But living in a state of constant distrust with someone you want to build a connection with isn’t going to benefit you or the potential relationship you want to create. Now, that’s a hard line to tow. “I think there's a difference between acknowledging that dating comes with uncertainty and going into every date expecting to be ghosted,” Lam says.

And guess what? Whether you lower your guard or put up 50 more walls, you still might get ghosted. You can’t control what another person does. But does that mean you should live your romantic life constantly preparing for the worst? I don’t think so. Self-preservation is one thing, but that just doesn’t sound like a fun experience at all. And shouldn’t dating, at the very least, be fun?

“It's understandable to want to protect ourselves, especially when we've been hurt before, but I also think it's important to recognize when those protective mechanisms might be getting in the way of allowing ourselves to connect with someone,” Lam says.

Ghosting absolutely sucks. But not everyone is going to ghost you. So maybe it’s time to try a new technique: give everyone the benefit of the doubt, because I’m sure you’d want them to do that for you.

#3560. what can looking at earth from space teach us about how we live on it?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

bringing together more than 50 artists, astronauts, scientists, and cultural thinkers, 'inploration: art exploring space to understand earth' by richelle ellis and lawrence azerrad explores how art can turn the perspective of space exploration into a deeper understanding of our planet.

‘We are not observers of this world. We are participants in it. We always were.’ In an interview with designboom, artist Richelle Ellis and creative director Lawrence Azerrad discuss the thinking behind Inploration: Art Exploring Space to Understand Earth, a book published by DelMonico Books. Bringing together more than 50 artists, scientists, astronauts, ecologists, and cultural thinkers, the publication examines how exploring space can change our understanding of Earth, and how art can turn a sense of planetary wonder into a more active form of environmental responsibility.

The book takes its starting point from Earthrise, the photograph captured by Apollo 8 astronaut Bill Anders on Christmas Eve 1968. Not included on the shot list of the mission, the image was captured spontaneously, revealing Earth as a blue and white sphere suspended against the darkness of space, without visible borders. Its impact extended beyond the space program, offering a new perspective on the planet as a shared and vulnerable home. Nearly six decades later, Ellis and Azerrad return to that image asking how the Overview Effect can resonate with people who may never leave Earth. In a culture saturated with photographs, videos, and digital feeds, the challenge is not simply to reproduce the sense of wonder that Earthrise inspired, but to connect that perspective to the ways people live, make decisions, and care for the world around them.

For Richelle Ellis, the relationship between space and Earth extends beyond the astronaut’s view. Her artistic practice moves between deep space, the deep ocean, remote terrestrial environments, and analog space missions, including projects that send artworks beyond Earth. These different settings share a central question: what happens to our understanding of home when we leave the environments we know? The farther we travel from familiar surroundings, Ellis argues, the more apparent our dependence on Earth becomes. Space makes borders seem arbitrary, while the deep ocean reveals worlds that remain largely invisible despite their importance to planetary health. Both environments challenge the assumption that what cannot be seen or directly experienced is somehow separate from everyday life.

Art can bring distant or unfamiliar environments closer, helping people connect with ideas that might otherwise remain abstract. In Inploration, this is where art and science meet. The book brings together people from both fields to explore how we understand the world, what remains unknown, and how looking at things differently can change our perspective. While their methods may differ, artists and scientists share a willingness to ask questions, look more closely, and explore the unfamiliar. Among the contributors is Amber Straughn, deputy project scientist for the James Webb Space Telescope, whose work helps communicate the significance of images made by the observatory. As she puts it, ‘Astronomy can lead us to think about bigger things rather than just the facts’. The telescope’s images allow viewers to reconsider time, scale, and humanity’s place in the universe. As Straughn explains, ‘we’re literally seeing them as they were further back in time.’ The publication also responds to changing ways of seeing. Its cover and back cover were updated at press time to include images from the Artemis II mission, many taken by crew members using their iPhones. Placed alongside the Hasselblad film photography associated with the Apollo era, these contemporary images point to how space exploration is documented and shared across generations. The tools change, but the challenge remains: how can an image of somewhere remote alter our understanding of where we are?

Azerrad brings another perspective to the project through his work as a creative director and his involvement in the 40th-anniversary edition of the Voyager Golden Record. Launched aboard NASA’s Voyager spacecraft, the record was designed as a message to potential extraterrestrial life, assembling images, sounds, music, and information intended to communicate something about humanity. Its audience may be unknown, but its message also turns back toward Earth. The act of selecting what to send into the cosmos requires a decision about what matters enough to represent us. The Golden Record is as much an exercise in collective self-understanding as it is an attempt at interstellar communication. Azerrad approaches Inploration in a similar way, as an attempt to prompt new ways of seeing Earth. His creative process begins with listening and asking questions, allowing different perspectives to sit alongside one another.

The work of artist Cai Guo-Qiang offers another perspective on humanity’s relationship with time and the planet. His pyrotechnic works exist only in the moment of their creation, demanding the viewer’s attention before disappearing. Reflecting on his practice, Ellis and Azerrad share that it reminds them that ‘our only true relationship with the planet exists in the finite moment we are in. The past and the future are really just ideas.’ The observation connects Cai’s ephemeral works to the book’s broader argument: planetary awareness begins with paying attention to the world as we experience it now.

The section on Indigenous knowledge and cultural cosmologies expands the book’s exploration of how different ways of understanding the universe shape our relationship with Earth. Rather than treating the cosmos solely as something to observe and measure, these perspectives connect knowledge to relationships among people, land, other living beings, and future generations. The difference is not simply between scientific and spiritual understandings, but between the ways knowledge informs our responsibilities to the world. Where some Western approaches have treated nature as a resource to study and extract, many Indigenous cosmologies emphasize reciprocity, care, and the obligations that come with being part of a living world.

Artist Cannupa Hanska Luger expresses the relationship between cultural knowledge and scientific understanding directly. ‘The stories are the cosmology. The cosmology is the science,’ he notes. Here, storytelling is not an alternative to knowledge but a way of transmitting an understanding of how the world works and how people should live within it. Other contributors bring the discussion back to the scale of everyday responsibility. Artist Tavares Strachan asks us to reconsider the geography of care: does responsibility extend to someone twenty miles away, or only to the person next door? Artist Rebeca Méndez poses a more fundamental question: ‘What does the Earth need from us?’ These questions complicate the familiar image of Earth as a single, unified home. Seeing the planet as a whole can produce a sense of collective belonging, but that feeling must extend beyond the photograph. It requires attention to the unequal ways environmental harm is experienced, to the knowledge held by communities whose relationships with land have endured across generations, and to the consequences of decisions made in the present. Physicist and systems thinker Fritjof Capra also highlights the importance of slowing down enough to recognize relationships that are otherwise easy to miss.

Inploration: Art Exploring Space to Understand Earth also asks how we move from understanding environmental crises to feeling able to respond to them. Climate change, biodiversity loss, and the complexity of planetary systems can be difficult to grasp on a personal level. Faced with a constant stream of alarming information, it is easy to feel overwhelmed rather than empowered to act. Art and design offer another way to engage with these challenges by translating complex ideas into images, sounds, and experiences. As musician and artist Beatie Wolfe observes, data alone cannot always convey the emotional weight of an issue. Art can help people connect with information that might otherwise feel distant. Astronomer Janna Levin captures this role succinctly: ‘The point is to move people with the knowledge.’

For artist Lita Albuquerque, understanding our place in the universe begins with locating herself within it. She describes her cosmic address, tracing her position from a specific date and time through the Solar System to the Laniakea Supercluster. ‘I don’t feel overwhelmed,’ she says, adding, ‘I feel connected.’ Her perspective suggests that looking beyond Earth can reveal the connections that make life possible. Design offers another way to consider our relationship with the future. Designer Forest Young notes that ‘any brief is an exercise in futuring,’ describing the act of creating as an exercise in imagining what comes next. He follows this with a question: ‘What is the thing we’re steering away from?’ His approach asks us to consider the choices shaping the future now. Activist Van Jones makes a similar point: ‘The future is not something we enter. The future is something we create together.’ The book carries this thinking beyond its contributors through a series of self-directed exercises that invite readers to reflect on their own relationship with the planet. A related program is planned for MASS MoCA in November 2027, alongside a companion LP to be released through Magnolia Record Store.

For Richelle Ellis and Lawrence Azerrad, the Overview Effect is not limited to astronauts looking back at Earth from space. It can start with looking up at the night sky or noticing details of the world we often take for granted. As they suggest throughout Inploration, we do not need to leave the planet to see it differently. The harder part is carrying that perspective into how we live. How do we account for the generations that come after us? Who do we include when we talk about humanity, and what do we owe the planet we share? The Earthrise photograph changed how millions of people saw Earth, even though most would never experience that view for themselves. With Inploration, Ellis and Azerrad hope art can prompt that same sense of recognition today, by paying closer attention to the world we already call home.

#923. Artist Jane Benson Has Some Strong Ideas About ‘Frankenstein’

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Popular culture would have you believe that Frankenstein, Mary Shelley’s seminal 1818 novel, is a horror story of male hubris, about a mad scientist contending with the dangers of his own creation. And it is, at least in part. But within Shelley’s critique of patriarchal power, there is another voice that is often overlooked: her own. About a third of the way into the book, the narration switches to the perspective of the so-called monster as he tells Victor Frankenstein what has happened to him during the nearly two years he and his creator have been apart.

The six chapters written in the creature’s voice contain some of the most achingly beautiful prose in the whole novel. He details how he learned to understand language and emotion. How he marveled at the warmth of the sun, the blooming of flowers, the sweetness of fruit. How he grew to love the family he tenderly observed from his hiding place, and how devastating their rejection was. He explains how he came to know anger, loneliness, loss, rage—and the vengeful acts they inspired.

“Reading those chapters, I find it really hard to think of this as a man’s voice,” the British-born, New York–based conceptual artist Jane Benson tells me during a recent visit to her Chelsea studio. “This sounds like a woman’s voice crying for justice, and to be heard. It’s bathed in feelings of isolation and grief and desertion and betrayal—all things that in everyday life, that was the woman’s lot.” She came to think of the creature’s voice as not only Mary Shelley’s, but also a general evocation of the struggles women face in a misogynistic world.

Benson is not the first to find feminist undertones in Shelley’s Gothic classic—scholars like Barbara Johnson, Ellen Moers, and Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar have written about themes of motherhood and female authorship that they found embedded within Frankenstein. (When Shelley first published the book, anonymously, many readers assumed it was written by her husband, Percy Shelley.) But as an artist, Benson wondered how she might visually represent this idea of the monster’s voice as a woman’s. What would that look like? What would it sound like?

She tracked down a limited-edition facsimile of Shelley’s original Frankenstein manuscript, released to mark the book’s 200th anniversary, and began her delightfully obsessive deep dive into the text. The resulting artworks make up “her her,” Benson’s new gallery exhibition that opens at Sargent’s Daughters in Tribeca on October 30. (More of the series will go on view at Art Basel Miami in December.) The show contains more than two dozen works, mostly text-based drawings that use the author’s own handwriting as their main material—a decision that allows the focus to stay on the devastating phrases an 18-year-old Shelley used to illustrate the creature’s tragic arc.

“I haven’t altered anything,” Benson says of the drawings. “These are just blow-ups of her pages.… These sentences, that’s exactly how they appear on the page.”

But these are not mere re-creations of the manuscript. Instead, Benson has come up with myriad ways of excavating the text, something that has become a signature in her politically engaged art practice over the past three decades. (Benson, who is dyslexic, finds that this process gives her a way into a text that focuses not so much on the linear process of reading as on the ideas the text contains.) Some works in “her her” are as tall as six feet, and many of them are layered on iridescent yellow film with reflective borders. They seem to vibrate off the wall, as if Mary Shelley herself were knocking around the frame, begging us to listen.

Take the piece her her (2026), which lends its name to the exhibition and features a tight zoom on those two crossed-out words. In highlighting such an instance within the manuscript, Benson “aims to reiterate and emphasize the negation of women throughout history.”

For her “solfège” pieces, Benson has excised all words but the seven syllables of solfège (do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti), with the resulting score performed by mezzo-soprano Hai-Ting Chinn, a frequent collaborator of Benson’s. (In a bit of cheeky fun, the recordings will be played on a loop throughout the gallery.) As she cut out all the non-solfège words and letters, Benson found herself surrounded by “a sea of sentences.” Out of these cast-offs, she made little lyric poems befitting her themes. Like this one, missing its initial do: ubtless, / on I wept, without / my limbs / I sat.

And for the visually striking “counter works,” as she calls them, Benson filled in the enclosed space of letters like g and d and o (called “counters” in typography) to create a rhythmic pattern of ovals and dots—a kind of literary animal print. In most of the sentences, only the letters’ filled-in counter space remains, with the rest erased. But some choice Shelley sentences—I waited with forced patience until the moon had sunk, for one example—are left intact. “Those are the ones that drove me to being so struck by this being a woman’s voice,” Benson explains.

There are even more Easter eggs—like the varying shades of yellow, which come from tone notes that match up to the solfège scores—but at a certain point this body of work becomes less about how she’s doing it and more about the dedication to the idea. “I was just compelled to keep going and make the work so that these words can be seen.”

Benson first read Frankenstein alongside other classics at school (she was born in Thornbury, a town in Gloucestershire, in 1973, and grew up in the tiny village of Gawsworth in northwest England). Like many people who first encountered the book at a young age, and like countless others who have seen its various adaptations for the screen, she was swept up in the flattened narrative of a violent creature and the regrets of its maker. “You have the sort of similar arguments, like, Who’s the monster?” she says.

Then, a few years ago—before Guillermo del Toro’s Frankenstein and looser interpretations like Yorgos Lanthimos’s Poor Things and Maggie Gyllenhaal’s The Bride! dragged the story back into the zeitgeist, and after she became a mother—Benson reread the book, and those 40 or so pages from the creature’s perspective triggered a whole new set of subversive ideas for her. “Is a monster the person in society that nobody understands, and that’s why we’re afraid of it?” she wondered. “I’m like, That’s a woman. No one understood Mary Shelley; people were afraid of her.”

Frankenstein is often overshadowed by the lore of its author (the daughter of two famed English intellectuals, women’s rights advocate Mary Wollstonecraft and philosopher William Godwin); her relationships to men (at 16 she ran away to France with the poet Percy Shelley, a pupil of her father’s who was already married); and the fantastical circumstances under which she wrote the book (holed up during the stormy summer of 1816 in a Swiss villa rented by Lord Byron, who one night challenged Mary, Percy, Mary’s stepsister, and his personal physician to a ghost-story contest).

But perhaps more consequential to the writing of Frankenstein was the grief that Mary Shelley carried. “It’s hard to separate any of it from what she was going through at that time,” Benson says. Shelley never knew her mother, who died 11 days after Shelley was born. Seventeen years later, Shelley lost her first baby at less than two weeks old. She wrote in her diary, “Dream that my little baby came to life again; that it had only been cold, and that we rubbed it before the fire, and it lived.” She began writing Frankenstein the following year. (Shelley would lose two more young children, including the baby she nursed while writing Frankenstein, all before her 22nd birthday. Only her fourth child, Percy Florence Shelley, born in 1819, would live to adulthood.)

At Sargent’s Daughters, three tender graphite drawings of elm trees hang on the back gallery wall. Benson made them as an homage to both Shelley’s grief and her own, over the recent passing of her father. The margins of Shelley’s manuscript were full of doodles of what look like English elms, which covered the lush landscape in the part of England where Benson was raised. Benson loved these trees, and was devastated when many were lost to Dutch elm disease. “My first heartbreak,” she says. Her father photographed them back in the 1970s, and after his death Benson looked everywhere for the photos, to no avail.

Seeing them there in Shelley’s manuscript felt like this personal connection between author and artist, a reassuring echo across time and space. Keep going, the trees seem to say. Keep looking.

“Jane Benson: her her” is on view at Sargent’s Daughters in New York City from October 30 to December 5, 2026.

#408. Unlike Most Combo Robot Cleaning Devices, the Shark Navigator Mops Just as Well as It Vacuums—Maybe Better

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The 2-in-1 robot vacuum and mop also features a tank that empties itself into a larger storage bin whenever it’s full.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. Why Trust Us?

I live in a home that’s almost evenly split between hard floors and rugs. The bathroom is tile, and the majority of the kitchen and living room is hardwood, but large area rugs in my bedroom and by the living room couches cover huge swaths of floor space. Cleaning every surface has traditionally required two devices and about an hour of labor. The near-perfect split of surfaces always kept me from committing to a robot cleaning device.

Then the Shark Navigator entered the chat.

This is a two-in-one robot cleaner that functions equally well as both a mop and a vacuum. At Popular Mechanics, we haven’t always been huge fans of combo robot cleaning devices, usually because the mop functionality leaves something to be desired. But the Navigator easily vacuumed dirt from my carpets, and after filling the tank and adding a mop pad, it transitioned into an impressive wet mop for my hard floors.

Beyond cleaning power, I also liked the Shark Navigator’s hands-free automatic emptying base. Whenever the tank is full, it will immediately go back to its charging station and empty itself out into the larger storage bin. That meant I didn’t have to empty debris into the trash more than once every two months or so.

There are only a few pieces that need to be installed on the Navigator, and doing so takes little to no time at all. That said, I had a bit of trouble when it came to connecting it to my smartphone. After downloading the app, it took a few tries to get it connected.

It’s worth noting that you must connect the robot vacuum to your home WiFi in order to operate it via your smartphone. That’s not uncommon with an appliance like this considering even my air purifier requires an app and WiFi to access the full range of features. However, it can be a bit annoying—especially if your WiFi goes out. There is a manual mode you can use by pressing the buttons on the robot in order to operate it, though it isn’t possible to enact all of the possible commands this way.

At the end of the day, the most important factor for any robot vacuum is how it cleans. On this front, the Shark Navigator is impressive. As a mop, the cleaning process is straightforward and thorough. With the help of its 360-degree LiDAR navigation, it basically doesn’t miss a spot. Even near the lip of a door that extends slightly beyond the wall, the robot would adjust itself to get as close to the wall as possible. I was able to see the moisture from the mop within millimeters of the wall.

One of my favorite features was the spot cleaning. Essentially, you can set the Navigator to continuously clean the same 5-by-5 square of floor over and over again. This worked well for cleaning up spills and light stains that needed some extra love to clean up.

On the vacuuming side, I was impressed by the roller brush’s power. It was able to genuinely disrupt some set-in dirt from the carpet. While the suction power numbers aren’t available to the public, I was pleased with how well it worked. Even transitioning from hard flooring to carpet, the cleaning power stayed consistent.

The Shark Navigator comes with a charging base that also functions as an auto-emptying storage bin. For those that like to clean daily (or the supremely lazy, like myself), this is a huge difference-maker over a robot vacuum without one. Because of the storage on the base, you only have to actually empty it into the trash about once every two months.

So, not only can I cede all cleaning responsibilities, I have now reduced even the bare minimum care to just once every couple of months.

As far as robot vacuums and mops go, the Shark Navigator gets the job done well. The LiDAR navigates well and ensures that it reaches tight corners and hugs walls, the mopping and vacuuming are powerful, and the self-emptying base reduces the labor I have to do by an even larger amount. Especially for homes with a healthy mix of hard flooring and carpet, this is well worth your time and attention.
