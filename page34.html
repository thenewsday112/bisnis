#848. Hackers expose US immigration surveillance network

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Personal and operational details of more than 41,000 US Department of Homeland Security (DHS) employees and contractors, including undercover immigration agents, have been leaked online, reportedly exposing the department’s internal structure and surveillance operations.

The disclosure comes amid growing public anger over President Donald Trump’s mass deportation campaign, with the department’s Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) facing growing criticism over aggressive raids, detention conditions, and the use of force against migrants and protesters.

Released on Wednesday by transparency nonprofit Distributed Denial of Secrets (DDoSecrets), the files contain names, contact details, job titles, and internal system access groups across ICE, Customs and Border Protection (CBP), and other DHS agencies.

DHS confirmed to the media that it is “aware of a potential breach” and is working to address it, adding that it will protect employees and update the public “when more information is available.”

Dubbed ‘Icepick’, the archive was supplied to DDoSecrets by a hacktivist group called ‘Department of Peace’. It contains around 21,300 CBP records, 6,600 linked to ICE, and 3,400 to Homeland Security Investigations, along with records from immigration services, cybersecurity, and other DHS divisions. Some records match employees’ social media profiles, but others exposed internal organizational charts and previously undisclosed programs and operations.

According to Wired, the files reference undercover investigations, wiretapping, and immigration databases, with internal access groups linked to surveillance tools including Palantir’s Falcon software and Mobile Fortify, a facial recognition system used by immigration agents. Other groups reference AI services including OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Google’s Gemini, and Elon Musk’s xAI chatbot Grok. Access groups are lists of employees authorized to use particular systems, which means the records could help identify which employees have access to surveillance tools and how DHS operations are organized.

“This dataset is massive in terms of both size and impact,” investigative journalist and hacktivist maia arson crimew, who developed a searchable version of the archive, said in a statement accompanying the release. She called it “a complete game changer for anyone investigating DHS,” arguing that it could help identify agents and their chains of command.

DDoSecrets co-founder Emma Best said the files go beyond an ordinary employee directory.“Rather than an indiscriminate directory of every DHS employee, Icepick identifies many of the architects and drivers of DHS policy and operations,” she said, pointing to officials overseeing covert investigations, efforts to identify government leakers, and deportation programs.

The hackers claim to have obtained the records through DHS’s Okta employee login system, though Okta denied that its systems were compromised.

The hackers linked the leak to Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin’s pledge to intensify immigration enforcement. Speaking after two fatal ICE shootings in Texas and Maine, Mullin said federal agents are “turning up the heat on the streets,” despite growing criticism of their tactics. “On July 17th, Markwayne Mullin said he was ‘turning up the heat.’ This is what happens to ICE when you do that,” a Department of Peace spokesperson said in a statement published by DDoSecrets.

The disclosure comes amid growing backlash over Trump’s immigration crackdown, a central priority since his return to the White House in January 2025. His push for mass deportations, expanded detention, and tighter border controls has sparked protests over aggressive raids, masked agents, and alleged racial profiling.

Anger intensified after federal agents fatally shot US citizens Renee Good and Alex Pretti in Minnesota in January, followed by other violent incidents involving ICE officers. Just this month, federal agents shot and wounded a Dominican migrant during an arrest in New York, prompting further demonstrations.

ICE has also faced scrutiny over detention conditions, with at least 52 deaths in its custody during Trump’s first 500 days back in office, according to Human Rights Watch and Physicians for Human Rights.

The administration maintains that its operations are necessary to enforce immigration law and remove dangerous criminals. The Icepick publishers say the records can help journalists, lawmakers, and civil liberties groups investigate DHS activities. Emma Best argued that the disclosure can enable investigations, congressional inquiries, and legal challenges by replacing the administration’s “anonymity with accountability.”

The disclosure adds to a series of US government data security incidents.

In September, hacking group ShinyHunters claimed that it stole personal data on nearly all FBI agents and their spouses, including sensitive job details and medical information. The group said the attack was “not financially motivated” and demanded that the FBI withdraw a cybersecurity advisory published in May that it claimed contained false allegations about it.

Separately, a Pentagon personnel system breach reportedly exposed Social Security numbers, personal details, and military job records belonging to more than 3 million people, including service members and their families. The incident prompted a class-action lawsuit last week.

The departure of Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu could benefit Iran even if his successor maintained the same policies toward Tehran, Khorasan, a conservative Iranian newspaper close to Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, said on Thursday.

The newspaper said a change in Israeli leadership would not necessarily reduce military threats against Iran but could weaken the political and diplomatic networks Netanyahu has built during his years in power.

"Ultimately, Netanyahu's departure is in Iran's interest, not because his successor would necessarily adopt a friendly approach, but because removing one of the main architects of the confrontation with Iran would reduce the accumulated political and diplomatic capacity behind it," Khorasan wrote.

The assessment came after Israel's parliamentary election on Tuesday produced no clear winner, leaving Netanyahu and his main rival, former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, seeking support to form a government.

With nearly 86% of votes counted on Wednesday, Israeli broadcasters projected Netanyahu's right-wing bloc would secure 53 seats in the 120-member Knesset, compared with 48 for Eisenkot's opposition bloc and 19 for Arab-led parties.

Neither main bloc reached the 61 seats needed for a majority, leaving the outcome dependent on coalition negotiations.

Netanyahu's experience seen as key factor

Khorasan said Israeli political groups shared many positions on security matters despite their differences and that Eisenkot, a former military chief, was not opposed to confrontation with Iran.

It argued, however, that Netanyahu had built political and diplomatic networks over many years that a successor could find difficult to replace.

"Netanyahu is not merely a prime minister," the newspaper wrote, adding that he had developed extensive political, regional and international connections and considerable experience influencing US decision-making.

The newspaper said Netanyahu knew how to make Iran a central issue in Washington's foreign policy and connect security developments with political opportunities.

"Removing such a figure from the top of power, even if the basic structure of Israeli policy remains unchanged, means losing part of this accumulated capacity," it said.

"Netanyahu and his allies have built ties over the years between the Israeli government and some opponents of the Islamic Republic, particularly the monarchist movement," the newspaper wrote.

"Netanyahu's departure could weaken part of this political image, although one should not expect all these relationships to disappear with a change in leadership," it added.

It said political divisions within a future Israeli coalition could make decision-making more difficult, although such disagreements would not automatically reduce the risk of military action.

Election leaves Netanyahu and Eisenkot seeking coalition partners

Netanyahu's Likud-led bloc emerged ahead of Eisenkot's opposition in the preliminary results but remained short of a parliamentary majority.

Parties representing Arab Israelis were projected to secure an unprecedented 19 seats following high voter turnout, giving them a potentially important role in determining the next government.

Eisenkot could seek support from Arab-led parties to secure a majority, although several parties within his opposition bloc have opposed such cooperation.

Khorasan said the high turnout among Arab voters was one of the election's most important developments but questioned whether Eisenkot could reach an agreement with Arab parties while maintaining support from his existing allies.

Reuters reported that Netanyahu's electoral performance had strengthened his position in coalition negotiations despite the loss of his previous parliamentary majority.

The prime minister will remain in office in a caretaker capacity while the process of forming a new government continues.

Iran remains central to Israeli political debate

Iran could face an economic contraction comparable in scale to the Great Depression in the United States, economists say, as the loss of oil revenue and wartime disruption threaten to drive an ailing economy into a deeper crisis.

Their assessments follow a prediction by US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who told Iran International that Iran's economy would shrink by more than 25% between March 2026 and March 2027.

Iranian-American economist Ali Dadpay told Iran International that he found Bessent's forecast plausible, particularly as Tehran attempts to finance wartime spending by expanding the money supply.

That risks accelerating inflation and weakening the rial further, he said, creating a cycle of rising prices, falling purchasing power and declining economic activity.

Mohamad Machine-Chian, an Iran International economist, offered a stark comparison.

"The United States lost about 26% of its output in the Great Depression, over four years, from 1929 to 1933. Secretary Bessent is describing a loss of Great Depression size in twelve months," Machine-Chian said.

Iran's economy contracted by 21.6% in 1980, the first year of the Iran-Iraq War, World Bank data shows.

Even the severe sanctions of 2018 and 2019 produced a smaller cumulative contraction of approximately 12%, despite oil exports plunging from about 2.7 million barrels per day to roughly 650,000.

Economic output was already approximately 10% lower in spring 2026 than a year earlier, according to figures from Iran's Statistical Center.

For the economy to contract by more than 25% over the full year, the decline would have to accelerate considerably.

Machine-Chian estimated that even with an 8% contraction over the summer, output would have to fall far more sharply during autumn and winter for the annual decline to approach Bessent's forecast.

The disruption of oil exports is central to that outlook. Energy intelligence firm Kpler reported no new Iranian crude loadings since late August, while revenue from earlier shipments is running out.

But the damage extends beyond the oil industry. Factories depend on imported materials, transportation companies require replacement parts, and businesses need financing to sustain operations.

As supplies and revenue dwindle, manufacturing, construction and other industries face further disruption.

The economic deterioration is already visible in the price of everyday necessities.

Food prices in September were more than double their level a year earlier, according to Iran's Statistical Center. Eggs cost three and a half times as much, rice nearly twice as much, and official bread prices in Tehran have doubled.

“The minimum wage increased 60% this year while inflation approached 90%,” Machine-Chian said. “In dollar terms, a minimum-wage worker now earns approximately $61 a month, compared with $96 a year earlier.”

He also warned about savings being eroded. With the dollar costing two and a half times as much as a year ago, money held in rials has lost approximately 60% of its value against the US currency.

Further disruption to imports could bring shortages of medicines, industrial materials and vehicle parts, potentially forcing factories to reduce production or close and putting more jobs at risk.

Dadpay described a possible future resembling aspects of the economic crises in Venezuela and Zimbabwe, where severe currency depreciation and inflation pushed people toward dollars, gold, secondhand markets and barter.

As production declines and essential goods become harder to obtain, even those who remain employed could struggle to afford basic necessities.

Sanctions policy analyst Max Meizlish expects pension payments and civilian services to come under pressure as revenues decline, while military and security personnel could continue receiving salaries.

That could allow the Islamic Republic to preserve institutions central to its political survival even as living standards deteriorate.

Economic devastation does not necessarily translate into political collapse, particularly if Tehran can preserve its security institutions and access to financial channels abroad.

Astronomers have discovered a large icy object beyond Pluto that could qualify as a dwarf planet, potentially adding a new member to the solar system’s officially recognized group.

The discovery was announced on Thursday by the US-funded Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, which houses the world’s largest 3.2-gigapixel digital camera, built specifically for astronomy.

Researchers estimate that the object, provisionally named 2026 ED138, is between 600 and 1,500 km in diameter and may be one of the largest dwarf planet candidates ever found. It currently sits roughly 46 times farther from the Sun than Earth. Pluto’s average distance, for comparison, is about 40 times Earth’s.

The object likely escaped detection until now because it appeared against a crowded backdrop of millions of stars near the plane of the Milky Way. However, Rubin’s repeated images and tracking software has now allowed scientists to pick out its movement.

If confirmed, ED138 would join five dwarf planets recognized by the International Astronomical Union, which include Eris, Haumea, Makemake, Ceres, and Pluto, which was considered the solar system’s ninth planet until it was reclassified in 2006. Dwarf planets orbit the Sun and have enough gravity to become roughly round, but share their orbital neighborhoods with other bodies.

ED138 may be large enough to meet the roundness requirement, but astronomers still need more research and better measurements before determining its status.

“The main thing we need to do is to establish the physical size of 2026 ED138,” discoverer Matt Holman, an astrophysicist at the Center for Astrophysics, Harvard and Smithsonian, told Reuters. He explained that ED138’s size is currently estimated from the sunlight it reflects. However, a small shiny object can appear just as bright as a large, dark one, Holman said.

To narrow down the true size of the object, researchers could use Chile’s ALMA telescope which can detect radiation emitted by ED138 itself, helping get a more accurate measurement.

The last dwarf planet officially recognized by the International Astronomical Union was Haumea in 2008. Other candidates have since emerged, including 2017 OF201, whose discovery was announced in May 2025, but none have yet been formally added to the list.

"Her seven years at the ECB have been, on the whole, pretty good," said Willem Buiter, an economist and former Bank of England official, noting that the ECB managed to bring inflation back down faster than peers in Britain and the United States.

Her exit, however, threatens to be as political as her arrival.

By walking away before her eight-year term ends, Lagarde gives European Union leaders a chance to pick her successor before a French election next spring that opinion polls suggest far-right candidate Marine Le Pen will win, replacing President Emmanuel Macron.

For an institution that fiercely guards its independence from political meddling, the timing has drawn sharp criticism from central bank watchers.

"This is debasing the institution and just following the purely political calculus of the French president," said Paul De Grauwe, a professor at the London School of Economics.

Lagarde and Macron have not addressed such criticism directly. Lagarde has said she would not be a candidate for the French presidency but would advocate for pro-European values in her home country "in whichever capacity" she deemed most efficient.

Throughout her career, Lagarde has stood apart from more technocratic colleagues through a mix of charm, consensus-building and folksy pragmatism.

Favouring plain-speaking homilies over dense economic theory, the then-IMF chief famously declared that "everything is better with butter" when discussing growth and told governments to "fix the roof while the sun is shining".

Unorthodoxy was a hallmark of Lagarde's career, and the subject of her upcoming autobiography "Lady First".

A former corporate lawyer who spent years in Chicago, she entered French politics in 2005 as an outsider. Early missteps earned her the domestic moniker "Madame Lagaffe," but she soon became one of then-President Nicolas Sarkozy's sharpest operators, steering French banks through the 2008 crash.

She led the IMF through Europe's debt crisis, navigating a messy Greek rescue that imposed harsh austerity, and sparked outrage in Athens when she bluntly urged suffering citizens to "pay their taxes".

Her appetite for high-stakes bets showed again in 2018 with a record $57-billion lifeline for Argentina to back then-President Mauricio Macri. The gamble unravelled, failing to halt a currency collapse or avert a deep recession, and left the IMF bound to its most troubled debtor.

Her occasional unfamiliarity with the nuances of central banking still peeked through.

In March 2020, as the pandemic rattled sovereign debt markets, Lagarde told a press conference the ECB was "not here to close spreads" between Italian and German borrowing costs.

The comment triggered an immediate selloff in bond markets and forced her to swiftly walk it back.

Under Lagarde's watch, the ECB swiftly rolled out emergency bond-buying that stabilised markets as COVID-19 spread. It launched its fastest series of interest-rate hikes to bring inflation under control following Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, although it later acknowledged it had been slow to recognise the full impact of spiking energy prices.

As ECB president, she once described economists as a "tribal clique" — a provocative jab at the profession's establishment, though she later softened the blow by saying she hoped to join the tribe.

Lagarde used her political skills to repair rifts. Unlike her predecessor Mario Draghi, whose top-down style embittered some hawkish northern European peers, she worked to build consensus among the euro zone's 21 national central bank chiefs.

Her willingness to wade into politics remained evident: in January, Lagarde walked out of a World Economic Forum dinner in Davos during a speech by US Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick that criticised Europe, an episode that underscored fraught transatlantic relations.

Whoever succeeds her when she leaves the ECB on February 7 will face fresh challenges including strains in French bond markets ahead of next year's election and a Middle East conflict that threatens a new bout of inflation.

Ultimately, Lagarde's legacy may be a paradox: an outsider who showed that political diplomacy could steer a central bank through historic shocks, but whose early departure served as a final reminder of her political instincts.

"She was put there for a reason: being more political than her predecessor and, on that mission, I think she largely succeeded," Frederik Ducrozet, head of strategy and macro research at Pictet Wealth Management, said. REUTERS
