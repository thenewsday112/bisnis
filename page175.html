#1421. The New <i><em>Sense and Sensibility</em></i> Makes Jane Austen’s World Moody and Wild

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In creating the sets, production designer Francesca Di Mottola treated the film “as if it were an original screenplay.”

Before filming started on the new Sense and Sensibility movie, director Georgia Oakley and stars Daisy Edgar-Jones and Esmé Creed-Miles had a sleepover. The trio spent the night in the Dorset house that would soon play Norland Park, the estate belonging to the Dashwood sisters and their widowed mother at the beginning of the Jane Austen novel.

It was so the actors “had a feel for the space,” says Francesca Di Mottola, the film’s production designer. They played games in the house’s entrance hall, where a badminton game takes place in the film. “They lived the spaces a little bit,” she notes.

This adaptation of Sense and Sensibility, out in theaters this weekend, is a more wild, rustic take on the world of Jane Austen. As compared to the 1995 film, starring Emma Thompson and Kate Winslet, this version is a bit less polished. The estate’s rooms are somewhat damp and worn; the grass on the grounds is tall and unkempt; the colors are muted and moody, and Barton Cottage, where the Dashwoods move later in the story, seems nearly crumbling. All of that was intentional, as Oakley wanted to create a universe that was “earthy and tonal,” according to Di Mottola, and entirely its own.

Di Mottola read the book many years ago and has seen the previous film adaptations, but she didn’t return to them in preparation for this film. “I wanted to treat this as if it were an original screenplay,” Di Mottola says.

When she first connected with Oakley, Di Mottola, whose past work includes The Great and Mother Mary, was struck by her vision of the film as “almost a coming of age story.” She then put together a lookbook, which mostly featured paintings of nature from Austen’s era. “There’s a lot of gloominess and tonal paintings that’s all reflected in the movie,” she says.

It was paramount to Oakley that they film on location, not on soundstages. “The actors immediately feel like they believe in where they are placed. For Georgia, it was just so important, that approach,” Di Mottola says.

The film’s grandest location is Norland Park, the estate owned by the Dashwood family that they eventually have to vacate due to their patriarch’s death. It’s where the three sisters grew up, played, and spent hours wandering the vast grounds. The movie’s location team found a private house in Dorset, owned and lived in by a real family with three children, that had never been used in a film before. They loved the bones of it: a Jacobean house that had evolved over time, with a Georgian facade added on later.

“We had quite early on made the decision that we wanted it to feel really Georgian rather than other adaptations leaned more into the Regency style of interior decoration,” Di Mottola says. “We wanted to avoid brickwork and red brick.”

Di Mottola and her team redecorated almost the entire interior with antiques and pieces that she had professionally aged. They found and reupholstered Chippendale sofas, rented and hung up vintage tapestries, and even built a library and a courtyard outside. But they left most of the old, worn interior walls alone, as they were a major part of the estate’s appeal to Di Mottola and Oakley.

“When there was damp coming through, we were so happy to show that. We wanted it to feel like the family was wealthy, but not that wealthy that they were always restoring and keeping it perfect,” Di Mottola says. “The fact that the house had a real lived-in and aged feel to it really helped tell the story.”

Di Mottola similarly wanted the grounds to feel natural and unkempt: “We didn't want spaces or gardens or woods that felt manicured or planted. We wanted them to feel like they were wild beings surrounded by nature that they couldn't control.” She needed to be sure to tell the homeowners that, despite the cameras coming, she did not actually want them to touch or clean up any element of the estate.

“It happens to me in every single movie when I choose a location because it's wild and stunning. Suddenly, someone might think, ‘Oh, a film crew is coming. Let me cut the grass entirely,’” Di Mottola says, laughing.

After Norland was settled, the team turned to finding Barton Cottage, the small, dilapidated house where the Dashwood women move after getting booted out of their home. It took a long time to find the right spot: someplace close to the sea but still remote and rustic. They eventually stumbled upon a semi-abandoned cottage in the moors of Dartmoor in Devon. It was certainly untouched—but almost to an extreme. The team had to work extensively to make it safe for filming.

“We had to get rid of mold, and then at one point we almost lost the location because there was a threat of there being a bat living in the roof,” Di Mottola says. But, in the end, it was all worthwhile.

She continues, “It just felt absolutely perfect to tell the story that this family actually leaves this beautiful family house and finds themselves in the middle of absolutely nowhere.”

And it indeed did help the actors transport themselves into Austen’s era. So much so that they may not have wanted to leave it. As Edgar-Jones tells ELLE Decor, “I was obsessed with the interiors of this film.”

#1657. See Inside Coastal Nantucket Home Designed by One of Jackie O.'s Favorite Boutiques

She could often be found rummaging through the iconic store’s shelves.

Some stores are grab-and-go spots. Others are inspiration hubs, enchanting customers with curated goods and sparking a full-on design movement. Nantucket Looms is one such spot. Set on the iconic island off the coast of Massachusetts, the weaving studio founded by Bill Euler and Andrew Oates in 1968 evolved into a bustling boutique (Jackie Kennedy Onassis could be found rummaging its shelves) and launchpad for artisans making the island’s famous heirloom blankets, baskets, and rugs.

When Euler and Oates retired, they handed over the reins to their most trusted employee, shop manager and in-house tastemaker Liz Winship, who had worked side-by-side with the duo for more than 40 years. Winship further expanded the business to include an acclaimed interior design studio.

When it was her turn to retire, Winship left her daughter Bess Clarke—responsible for bringing the shop into the digital world—and two longtime employees, principal designer Stephanie Hall and master weaver Rebecca Peraner, to steer the ship. You can read all about the legacy and transformation of this American treasure in their book, Nantucket Looms.

“It’s such a great tribute to all who’ve been a part of our story,” says Clarke. “I really hope people see a side of Nantucket that may be a little harder to find these days.” Keep reading to explore the third home Hall and the Nantucket Looms team have designed and discover a bit of the store’s magic.

FAST FACTSDesigner: Stephanie Hall of Nantucket LoomsLocation: Nantucket, Massachusetts

For many of Nantucket Looms’ design clients, the island is their second home. “They really want to feel like they’re in Nantucket and not anywhere else,” Hall says. One way the designer delivers the coastal aesthetic in an authentic way is by incorporating different shades of blue. “The reflective light out here on Nantucket, because we have so much water and we’re surrounded by the ocean, is so beautiful,” she says. “On any given day, if the sun’s out and the sky is bright blue, the water can be blue or blue-green; those are the colors that you feel you’re surrounded by. I love to bring that to the inside as well.”

To complement the dramatic scale of the living room, an arrangement of sofas and modern wingback chairs is anchored by two coffee tables styled with books and decorative objects that relate to Nantucket’s seafaring history.

Inspired by blown-glass fishing boats, the sculptural chandelier is made of handblown glass “bubbles” bound together with knotted rope. “I just love that piece so much,” Hall says. “It’s a little nod to nautical without doing a red, white, and blue thing.”

A space for playing games and building puzzles, replete with a marble-topped table surrounded by four upholstered chairs, was created adjacent to the living room. A painting by Adam Umbach hangs in the background.

“Our clients have two teenage daughters, so this is a space for the kids to congregate away from the parents,” Hall explains. To keep things fun and cozy, she brought in pops of red with handwoven blankets from Nantucket Looms. Anchoring the room is a large white sectional sofa with bench-seat cushions and two oversized white swivel chairs, all covered in high-performance fabrics. Silk-screened throw pillows add the finishing touch. A painting by Megan Hinton is displayed on the coffee table.

“To me, a space isn’t finished until art is hung on the walls,” Hall says. For its interior projects, the team mixes the clients’ collection with art and decor sourced locally and from Nantucket Looms. “We represent over 70 local artists,” she says. On the bar are two paintings by Kenneth Layman, and a painting by Julija Mostykanova hangs nearby. The comfortable blue armchairs are upholstered in fabric by Peter Dunham.

Rustic vintage finials, matched by a pair of contemporary rattan chandeliers, add interest to the double-pedestal dining room table. A reproduction of a French handkerchief planter is filled with ferns to brighten up the space.

The homeowners are big on wallpaper, but instead of going wild with prints and patterns, Hall took a subtler approach and played with texture on the walls. “There’s a large sitting room in there, which we use for our client’s office,” she explains. To define each space but maintain the flow, Hall used two different types of textured wallpaper. Another reason Hall and her team used textured wallpaper is to add warmth and coziness. “Some of the rooms were quite cavernous,” she says.

A variety of other textures can be seen in the bedroom as well. Rope handles embellish the chest of drawers, and the lamps are white-washed wicker. The bed features a tufted linen headboard with Euro pillows in a bouclé fabric. A kelp green handwoven cashmere throw is draped across the king-size bed. To keep consistency between the two rooms, she used the same painted rattan light fixtures.

Departing from the mostly white walls of the home, a leaf-patterned wallpaper was chosen to make this dressing room stand out. Covering the walls and ceiling is a clever design trick for a space that gets no natural light. The beautiful Schumacher paper adds dimension and makes the room feel brighter and larger. An oversize upholstered bench in a joyful fabric was selected to bring character to the space.

“The clients love modern, very colorful art, and we tried to make the pieces the star of the show,” Hall says. “We kept the walls very white, and a lot of the furniture very neutral so they can incorporate really strong statements through their artwork.”

Barbara Clarke’s photograph “Seafoam” adds depth to the space above an upholstered white linen bed with navy blue welting in the main house, flanked by navy wooden inlay bedside tables. A white chevron wool rug brings comfort underfoot.

By mid-October, many tomato plants look pretty rough, with yellowing leaves, a few stubborn green fruits, and stems flopping over their cages. The easy move is to yank the whole mess out and drag it to the trash or the compost pile. Before you do, though, there’s some useful work hiding in those tired plants.

Good end-of-season tomato plant care starts with a close look at what you’re pulling. Some vines can feed next year’s garden as compost, and the best fruit can give you free seed. Others may carry diseases you don’t want to spread around the yard or carry over into next summer.

That’s the main thing to keep in mind as you work. Healthy tomato plants can usually go in the compost, while diseased ones should be bagged and disposed of according to your local guidance. Nearly every other job, from saving seeds to cleaning cages, depends on knowing which kind of plants you have.

Walk the row slowly and look at leaves, stems, and fruit before a single plant comes out. A few problems should never go into a typical home compost pile. Late blight (Phytophthora infestans) is one, and the bacterial tomato diseases are others, including bacterial spot (Xanthomonas spp.) and bacterial canker (Clavibacter michiganensis), which can survive in plant debris. Mosaic viruses are worse still – tobacco and tomato mosaic viruses can hang on in dried plant debris for years. Most backyard piles never get hot enough, evenly enough, to kill these organisms. Bag those plants and get rid of them according to your local rules, and do the same with any plant that’s heavily infested with pests.

Seed saving only works with open-pollinated or heirloom varieties – hybrids won’t come true to type, so an F1 tomato’s seeds can give you something quite different next year. Pick fully ripe fruit from your healthiest, best-tasting plants and scoop the seeds and gel into a glass jar with a little water. Stir it twice a day for up to about five days, until it ferments and the good seeds sink. Pour off the floaters, rinse what’s left in something like the Farberware fine mesh strainers from Walmart, and dry the seeds on a coffee filter or paper plate.

Once a fingernail can’t dent them, they’re ready for labeled paper envelopes; the Lotmer seed-saving envelopes from Amazon are printed with seed name, variety, date collected, and growing notes. Kept cool, dry, and dark, tomato seed usually stays good for about four years.

This one’s optional, but if there’s a variety you loved this year, you can carry it through the winter indoors. Pick a few healthy side shoots about 3–4 inches (8–10 cm) long and snip them off with clean pruners – Fiskars' bypass pruning shears, available on Amazon, are a great budget-friendly pick if you need a new pair.

Cuttings will usually root in a plain jar of water, as long as the cut ends sit 1–2 inches (2.5–5 cm) under the surface and the jar stays somewhere cool and bright. Once roots show up, pot them and give them all the light you can. Don’t put this off until after a hard frost has already hit the garden.

Leave out any plant that had disease problems, and viruses in particular, since a cutting can carry them along from the parent.

Healthy vines are worth composting, but toss them on the pile whole, and they’ll take ages to rot. Chop them into short lengths first – 8–12 inches (20–30cm) or so – because smaller pieces break down faster. It also pays to strip off any leftover tomatoes. Seeds can make it through a backyard pile in one piece, and you may find volunteers popping up next summer.

Layer the chopped vines with dry leaves or other brown material. A compost bin like the Redmon 65-gallon compost bin from Walmart keeps it all corralled.

If you’re on the fence about whether a plant was healthy, use that compost away from where tomatoes will grow. Healthy potting mix from containers can go on the pile, too, so dump those pots before you stack them for winter.

For plants that stayed healthy all season, there’s a good case for leaving the roots in the ground. Just cut the stems off at the soil line, and the roots will break down in place, adding organic matter without much soil disturbance. A plant that wilted, stunted, or yellowed for no clear reason is another story. Loosen the root ball with a spading fork, like Home Depot’s Ames 4-tine forged spading fork. You’re looking for knotty swellings on the roots, a sign of root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.).

Remove infested roots soon after harvest and leave them out of the garden. The same goes for plants with Fusarium wilt (Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici), which can persist in soil for many years. In containers, tip the root ball out and check it the same way.

Some disease organisms ride along on tomato supports. Bacterial canker can persist on stakes, and mosaic viruses can last weeks to months on stakes and trellises. Knock off the dirt first, then scrub or hose them down, since soil blocks disinfectants. Then soak them fully in a 10% bleach solution (1 part bleach to 9 parts water) for at least 20 minutes.

Rather skip the bleach? ZeroTol 2.0, an OMRI-listed hydrogen peroxide product sold on Amazon, is labeled for hard, nonporous surfaces like metal cages and tools. Wooden stakes are trickier – bacteria can hide in the wood – so toss any that held canker-infected plants. Plastic pots can get the same bleach soak, followed by a good rinse.

Bare soil over winter is a missed opportunity. Winter rye is very cold-tolerant and grows in cool weather. In mid-October, it’s still an option in many areas, as long as you sow it at least four weeks before steady cold sets in. You’ll find Mountain Valley Seed winter rye on Amazon in a 5-pound bag on Amazon. Mow it in spring before it sets seed, then turn it under two to four weeks before planting.

If it’s too late for a cover crop, spread compost such as Back to the Roots organic compost, available from Home Depot, or a blanket of shredded leaves, instead. Next year’s tomatoes should go somewhere else, too – keep tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, and potatoes out of that spot for three to four years. For containers, reuse potting mix only if the plants stayed healthy, and replace the top 6 inches (15 cm) in big pots with fresh mix.

Keep it Burning: Making History on the Trango Towers follows Edu Marin as, along with his father and brother, he attempts a second free ascent of Pakistan’s Nameless Tower. He’s after the great Eternal Flame line, created by Wolfgang Gullich and Kurt Albert in 1989.

After failing the previous year, Edu Marin has returned to Eternal Flame with what he calls his dream team. That includes his 70-year-old father, Francisco “Novato” Marin, as well as his brother Alex. Neither has much experience with big-wall climbing or high altitude. However, as Edu explains, “I knew that they would stay until the end.”

It’s a strategy that paid off for the first people to complete a free ascent of Eternal Flame. Two decades after Gullich and Albert created the route, brothers Alex and Thomas Huber free climbed it. Both return for this film, sharing their experience from the first climb.

The three Marin climbers. Photo: Screenshot

They get to base camp at 4,200m and begin acclimatizing and preparing the logistical side of the climb. Managing the weather, the altitude, and the 1,200m of rope to fix adds another layer of difficulty. Then, when the team has settled in to begin the climb, an avalanche hits in the middle of the night.

Everyone emerges unharmed, though shaken. Novato recalls his fear that the avalanche would bury his sons, as they survey their dismantled camp. Everything needs to be dug up, but everything essential is still there, so the trio goes ahead and begins the climb.

They are making steady progress. But at the 6,000m Snow Ledge, they learn that a week of bad weather is heading right for them. While Alex and Novato head down to rest and wait out the storm from below, Edu sits tight.

“If you descend from the wall,” he argues, “you have to climb all the pitches again from the bottom.”

So instead he will sit on this 6,000m ledge, at below-freezing temperatures, for eight days. There’s a weather window that far out, and he plans to take it. This, Thomas Huber says, may be the hardest part of the project.

The sun comes out after 10 days of waiting. Reunited with his family, Edu leads the final ascent.

Edu on a technical pitch. Photo: Screenshot

The weather holds, and, encouraging each other as they ascend the last few meters, all three climbers reach the summit, with Edu completing the second-ever free climb. We see them on top, laughing and embracing, while final cutaway interviews with Novato and Edu discuss what it meant to complete the climb as a family.

“It’s a pleasure to share this sport, and to share it with my sons— there’s nothing better!” Novato says, smiling. “As long as my body allows me, I’ll keep going.”

#4014. Scientists discover how deep sleep builds muscle, burns fat, and boosts the brain

📅 Oct 17, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A good night's sleep does much more than leave you feeling refreshed. During deep sleep, the body releases growth hormone, a powerful chemical messenger that helps build muscle, strengthen bones, and regulate fat metabolism. For teenagers, getting enough sleep is especially important because growth hormone plays a major role in reaching their full height potential.

Scientists have long known that insufficient sleep can interfere with growth hormone production. What remained unclear was exactly how the brain controls this process, particularly during non-REM sleep, the deep, restorative stage that typically dominates the earlier part of the night.

Now, researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, have uncovered the brain circuitry behind this relationship. In a study published in the journal Cell, the team identified the neurons that regulate growth hormone release during sleep and discovered a previously unknown feedback system that helps maintain a delicate balance between hormone levels and wakefulness.

The discovery reveals that the relationship works in both directions. Sleep stimulates growth hormone production, but growth hormone also sends signals back to the brain that influence when we wake up.

The findings could eventually help scientists develop new approaches to treating sleep disorders associated with metabolic diseases such as diabetes, as well as neurological conditions including Parkinson's and Alzheimer's.

"People know that growth hormone release is tightly related to sleep, but only through drawing blood and checking growth hormone levels during sleep," said study first author Xinlu Ding, a postdoctoral fellow in UC Berkeley's Department of Neuroscience and the Helen Wills Neuroscience Institute. "We're actually directly recording neural activity in mice to see what's going on. We are providing a basic circuit to work on in the future to develop different treatments."

Why Deep Sleep Matters for Growth and Metabolism

Growth hormone is best known for its role in childhood development, but its importance extends well into adulthood. It supports the maintenance of muscle and bone, helps the body regulate how it uses stored fat, and influences blood sugar levels.

When sleep is consistently disrupted, growth hormone regulation can suffer. Because this hormone helps control glucose and fat metabolism, inadequate sleep may contribute to an increased risk of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.

To understand how the brain coordinates these processes, the Berkeley researchers focused on a region called the hypothalamus. Located deep within the brain, this small but essential control center helps regulate hunger, body temperature, hormone production, and the daily cycle of sleeping and waking. Its basic functions have been preserved throughout mammalian evolution.

Within the hypothalamus, the researchers examined neurons that produce growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH) and two distinct types of neurons that produce somatostatin.

These chemical messengers have opposing functions. GHRH encourages growth hormone secretion, while somatostatin suppresses it. Together, they help control how much growth hormone enters the bloodstream, largely by regulating its release from the pituitary gland, a small hormone-producing structure at the base of the brain.

The scientists also investigated how growth hormone influences the locus coeruleus, a region in the brainstem involved in alertness, attention, thinking, and the drive to explore new or unfamiliar experiences.

Disruptions in the activity of this region have been linked to several psychiatric and neurological disorders.

"Understanding the neural circuit for growth hormone release could eventually point toward new hormonal therapies to improve sleep quality or restore normal growth hormone balance," said Daniel Silverman, a UC Berkeley postdoctoral fellow and study co-author. "There are some experimental gene therapies where you target a specific cell type. This circuit could be a novel handle to try to dial back the excitability of the locus coeruleus, which hasn't been talked about before."

Scientists Map the Brain Circuit That Controls Growth Hormone

The research was conducted in the laboratory of Yang Dan, a professor of neuroscience and of molecular and cell biology at UC Berkeley.

To investigate how the circuitry works, the team studied mice using electrodes implanted in their brains. The scientists recorded neural activity while using light to stimulate specific neurons in the hypothalamus, allowing them to observe how different parts of the system responded.

Mice are particularly useful for studying sleep regulation because their sleep patterns differ significantly from those of humans. Rather than sleeping through one long stretch each night, mice typically sleep in brief episodes lasting only several minutes, scattered throughout the day and night.

This gave the researchers repeated opportunities to examine how neural activity and growth hormone regulation changed as the animals moved between sleeping and waking.

Using advanced techniques to trace connections between brain cells, the team discovered that GHRH and somatostatin behave differently depending on the stage of sleep.

During rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, the stage commonly associated with vivid dreaming, activity involving both GHRH and somatostatin increases. Although these hormones normally exert opposing effects, their coordinated activity during this stage contributes to increased growth hormone release.

During non-REM sleep, the pattern changes. Somatostatin activity decreases, reducing its inhibitory influence, while GHRH activity rises moderately. This combination also promotes growth hormone secretion.

The discovery helps explain how different sleep stages can support the release of the same essential hormone through distinct patterns of brain activity.

Growth Hormone Also Helps Control When We Wake Up

Perhaps the most surprising finding was that growth hormone does not simply respond to signals from the sleeping brain. It also influences the brain circuits that control wakefulness.

As growth hormone gradually builds up during sleep, it stimulates neurons in the locus coeruleus, increasing their activity and helping promote the transition toward waking.

This creates a feedback loop in which sleep encourages growth hormone release, and the resulting hormone activity helps regulate the sleep-wake cycle.

The researchers also identified an unexpected complication. Although greater activity in the locus coeruleus generally promotes alertness, excessive stimulation can have the opposite effect.

In an earlier study published the same year, Silverman demonstrated that when neurons in this region become overexcited, they can paradoxically promote sleepiness rather than wakefulness.

Together, these findings reveal a carefully regulated system in which the brain must balance competing signals to maintain healthy sleep and hormone activity.

Scientists describe this type of self-regulating process as homeostasis, the body's ability to maintain stable internal conditions even as its environment and physiological demands change.

"This suggests that sleep and growth hormone form a tightly balanced system: Too little sleep reduces growth hormone release, and too much growth hormone can in turn push the brain toward wakefulness," Silverman said. "Sleep drives growth hormone release, and growth hormone feeds back to regulate wakefulness, and this balance is essential for growth, repair and metabolic health."

A Surprising Connection Between Sleep and Brain Health

The newly identified feedback system may have implications that extend beyond physical growth and metabolism.

Because the locus coeruleus helps regulate attention, alertness, and cognitive function, growth hormone's influence on this region suggests it could also play a role in how effectively the brain functions after waking.

This raises the possibility that maintaining healthy sleep and growth hormone rhythms may support both physical recovery and mental performance.

"Growth hormone not only helps you build your muscle and bones and reduce your fat tissue, but may also have cognitive benefits, promoting your overall arousal level when you wake up," Ding said.

By identifying the specific brain cells and signaling pathways involved, the researchers have provided a foundation for exploring therapies that target disrupted sleep and hormone regulation.

Such approaches could eventually prove useful for people with sleep disturbances related to metabolic disorders or neurological diseases. However, the experiments were conducted in mice, and additional research will be needed to determine how these mechanisms operate in humans and whether they can be safely targeted with treatments.

The findings underscore a fundamental principle of sleep biology: Rest is not simply a period when the brain switches off. It is an active, carefully coordinated process in which hormones and neural circuits constantly communicate to support growth, repair, metabolism, and wakefulness.

I’ve checked in thousands of bikes for service, and I used to hear this line all the time: “I ride the bike a lot, but it’s in great shape. The shifting just jumps around a little.” Once I would get to the step of putting a chain checker on the chain, that $16 tool might tell the story of hundreds of dollars in upcoming repairs.

A chain checker was one of my most recommended service tools to customers when I ran a bike shop service department. The key is to make sure you are buying the correct one and understand how to use it. The chain checker most shops I worked in was the Park Tool CC2 —the chain checker with the swivel dial.

This option was great because it gave customers and service advisors an easy visual on the wear level of a chain. But the biggest reason my favorite chain checker isn’t this one is because it does not give a clear reading on certain SRAM chains. Read on to find out which chain checker I suggest, why it can save you thousands of dollars across the life span of your bike, and how to use this small tool.

When a chain gets stretched it can cause issues like slow shifting, unexpected chain jumps, and the chain can have a slipping feeling under power. Sometimes these issues can also be mistaken for cable tension being off in a derailleur, but you can easily check the chain stretch at home if you have this chain checker.

It is extremely difficult to tell how worn a drivetrain is with the naked eye. By checking how worn a chain is, you can tell what other parts will need to be replaced. My favorite chain checker is the Pedro’s Chain Checker because it is compatible with most chain makes and models. It also retails for only $16 and it is small enough to fit in almost anyone’s tool roll or kit.

Many shops will recommend replacing an 11, 12, or 13 speed chain when a chain checker reads over 50 percent worn. If the chain is over 75 percent worn that recommendation changes to replacing a chain and cassette or freewheel (gears on the back wheel) and replacing chain, cassette or freewheel, and chainrings or crank (gears in the front near the pedals) when it measures over 100 percent worn. Typically 10-speed chains and lower have a bigger tolerance to stretch, with singlespeeds having the biggest acceptable stretch.

Many times a customer would ask if the shop could just throw a new chain onto a drivetrain that is past that 100 percent mark. If you do this, the shifting can actually get worse because the wear pattern in the new chain no longer lines up with the wear pattern on the existing cassette and chainrings. An even worse case scenario of putting a new chain on a worn drivetrain is chain suck. This is when a worn chainring can get stuck to the chain from the bottom of the pedal stroke. Then the chain gets sucked up and jammed between the chainring and the frame—possibly leading to damage on the frame. Once a drivetrain has a certain level of wear replacing only one part of the system does not work.

The price for some higher-end cassettes can range anywhere from $400 to $660 from brands like Shimano and SRAM. I go by a three-strike rule with chains (spokes too, but that’s a different story). For my own bikes I typically get three chains out of each cassette.

If you are on top of chain wear with a chain checker, this could save you almost $2,000 in cassette costs alone when using high-end SRAM cassettes. Remember, this price doesn’t include labor or the cost of chainrings—which you’ll need to replace if you keep running the chain until it’s above that 100 percent wear mark.

Some higher-end chainrings, like the SRAM Red direct-mount chainring, can run up to almost $300 per ring. If someone runs the whole drivetrain into the ground, then a checkup for shifting issues can quickly turn into a thousand-dollar bill on a high end drivetrain. If you’re running Shimano 105 or SRAM Rival, then being on top of chain wear might not save you thousands as quickly as running the highest end group set. Rest assured, overtime you’ll get there too. The better news is that Pedro's chain checker is so easy to use.

Start by dropping the tooth with the number one above it into the chain. Once the number one tooth is dropped in, then drop the tooth with the number two above it into the chain. Finally, try to drop the third tooth (numbered with a three) into the chain. If this tooth doesn’t drop in through the links in the chain, then good news! Your chain is less than 50 percent worn. If it drops halfway through the chain that means your chain is about 75 percent worn. Finally, if the tooth numbered three on the chain checker drops all the way through the chain that means it is between 75 percent and 100 percent worn. Typically I suggest if you have 11, 12, or 13 speed chains around that 50 percent worn mark change the chain soon to keep the rest drivetrain alive as long as possible.

Riyadh has invested in own post-industrial zone, Jax, which hosts artist studios as well as the Diriyah Biennale spaces, and Abu Dhabi’s cherished Salama bint Hamdan Al Nahyan Foundation has also recently launched Miza, a creative district centred around its Warehouse421 arts space, in the port area of the city.

The name Noma is derived, New York-style, from its location: North of Manarat Al Saadiyat. Manara is the multi-purpose exhibition site that has hosted Abu Dhabi Art since 2011. When Frieze Abu Dhabi sets up shop there next month, it will bring 88 galleries, with a focus on the Swana region (South-west Asia and North Africa).

There will be elements familiar to Frieze aficionados: Frieze Sculpture installs public works along the beach at Mamsha, on Saadiyat’s northern edge; a Focus section; and Frieze Masters, curated by Marika Sardar. The fair is also drawing on decades of artistic activity in the UAE, with the Abu Dhabi-based collective Dirwaza Curatorial Lab putting the work of two Emirati artists into cross-generational dialogue (Mohammed Kazem and Alia Zaal), and by using the Global Futures section to show artists with well-established profiles in the country, such as Abdullah Al Saadi and Ala Younis.

It is unclear whether the current geopolitical unrest will affect the fair. US President Donald Trump continues to make incendiary claims about a resumption of the war with Iran, though it is impossible to know how seriously to take these. And the closure of Saudi Arabia’s airspace this week—due to a conflict that the UAE is currently not a part of—has also made some gallerists nervous. But Frieze has underlined that the organisation and DCT are “fully committed to Frieze Abu Dhabi, and preparations are well advanced for the opening on 19 November”.

“We are in close and regular contact with our exhibitors, and will be as flexible as we sensibly can be for any gallery affected by travel advisories outside their control,” says a spokesperson for Frieze. “As with every Frieze fair, the safety of participants is the primary consideration in all our planning, and we are working closely with DCT and the relevant authorities.”

#4193. ‘Artist-first’ Fair Play launches to strong attendance but slow sales during London Frieze week

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Crowd control: Fair Play's private view on Thursday evening attracted large numbers Donna Ford

Of all the various satellite fairs within walking distance of Frieze London this year, the inaugural Fair Play has the advantage of being the closest. Located across two floors in the repurposed church One Marylebone, the new fair boasts an “artist-first” status, cutting the galleries out of the equation by only hosting individual artist stands.

Rather, the fair acts somewhat as a gallery, with the artist playing dealer. Fair Play provides the exhibition space and white walls, gets the artists’ work in front of collectors and other audiences, and, of course, takes a 50% cut (including VAT) of any sales made. The majority of the 70 artists exhibiting were chosen by a selection committee of curators and creative directors following an open call, with an £84 application fee (including two fair tickets for all applicants) but no cost for the stand itself. For each of the chosen artists, it was up to them to sort transportation, hanging, pricing and curation of their work. Most of the artists do not have gallery representation and, for many of them, this is their first fair.

The energy on Friday morning was upbeat, and the slim corridors between the tightly placed stands were extremely busy. “Busy” was the keyword when speaking to artists at the fair so far, many of whom pointed to the apparently heaving opening the night before. According to Ryan Stanier, the fair’s founder, the private view saw more than 3,000 attendees. “It was the best opening night I’ve ever hosted,” he tells The Art Newspaper, referring to his prior role as the founder and director of The Other Art Fair.

Sales, however, seemed significantly less buzzy. Although affordable—most of the prices lie in the three- to four-figure range—red dots were scarce. The vast majority of the artists The Art Newspaper spoke to had not made any sales, although all of them were optimistic about interest and collectors returning later on. One notable exception was the London-based artist Lea Rose Kara, who sold two wall-based sculptures on the opening night: a smaller work to one of her returning collectors for £300 and a larger piece to a new collector for £3,200. Other artists were positive about alternative opportunities that may come from the fair, such as the Cornwall-based sculptor Eleanor Turnbull, who had several enquiries regarding potential commissions.

Nevertheless, staff remain steadfast about the strength of sales at the first edition. “Our ethos is to buy the piece and walk out the door with it,” said one staff member at the payment desk, with a wrapping and shipping stand located just around the corner.

#4204. An artist’s poetic rumination on the political promise of Opec

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A scene from Sanaz Sohrabi's documentary An Incomplete Calendar (2026) © Sanaz Sohrabi

The new film An Incomplete Calendar, by the Iranian Canadian artist and film-maker Sanaz Sohrabi, thoughtfully employs archival footage and ephemera about the Organisation of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (Opec), reframing the oil cartel as both an agent of anti-imperialist solidarity and an unlikely vehicle for peace through cultural exchange.

Shown at the Vancouver International Film Festival earlier this month after winning a prize for the best debut feature this past March at the Cinéma du Réel festival in Paris, with a plethora of upcoming festival screenings, the film is especially timely in the midst of US and Israel’s war with Iran. In it, Sohrabi subverts the typical talking-heads documentary format by using poetic voiceovers—her own as well as those of the writer and curator Murtaza Vali, scholar Laleh Khalili, journalist Layla Maghribi, former Universidad Central de Venezuela concert choir member Leonardo Montaño Salas and others—as she visually centres archival materials.

“Over the course of six years, I gathered the archives for this film while I was working on my trilogy about the colonial history of extraction in Iran and West Asia,” Sohrabi said in a statement. “I collected a repository of archives without realising I was piecing together remnants and fragments of the Third World’s political calendar of independence and economic sovereignty. I layered rarely seen anticolonial magazines, stamps, newsreels and ephemera—found in flea markets, bookshops, online exchanges and mailed across continents—to shape the film’s narrative and visual structure.”

A scene from Sanaz Sohrabi's documentary An Incomplete Calendar (2026) © Sanaz Sohrabi

The film documents a key moment when political decolonisation in the 1950s empowered newly independent Asian and African nations to pursue economic sovereignty. Its timeline spans Pan-Arabism and the Arab-Israeli wars of 1967 and 1973. Archival footage from British Petroleum conveys both the mid-century optimism that began with the creation of Opec in Baghdad in 1960 and its poignant denouement, when the war between Iran and Iraq (with Ronald Regan’s administration funding both sides) began in 1980.

Footage of the Iranian Prime Minister Mohammed Mossadegh’s historic 1951 visit to Cairo before he was overthrown in a CIA-backed coup after nationalising the oil industry is of particular interest, as is footage of Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser after he nationalised the Suez Canal Company (the joint British-French enterprise that operated the commercial waterway). While relations were never quite the same between Egypt and Iran when the Shah came to power, that mid-century moment (in a voiceover, the Egyptian film-maker Jihan El-Tahri speaks of her relative’s pride at living on the newly named Mossadegh Street in Cairo) serves as a memento of rare Shiah-Sunni solidarity, as war now rages between Yemen and Saudi Arabia, Iran is bombed by the US and Israel, and Iran in turn bombs US allies in the region. Sohrabi began her project six years ago, researching and filming in Tehran, Cairo, Beirut and Caracas; she had no way of knowing just how timely her documentary would be.

A scene from Sanaz Sohrabi's documentary An Incomplete Calendar (2026) © Sanaz Sohrabi

Beyond its archival value, the film—which Sohrabi says was inspired by Mona Damluji’s book Pipeline Cinema: The Cultural Infrastructure of Oil Extraction in Iran and Iraq (2025), on the history of BP films made in the region—offers a unique cultural perspective. Sohrabi rediscovered a little-known 1980 vinyl, Rhymes and Songs for Opec, a recording by the Central University of Venezuela’s concert choir commemorating the organisation’s 20th anniversary, and makes it the film’s starting point. Through interviews with former choir members, we learn that the choirmaster taught songs from Algeria, Iraq and other Opec countries, in Arabic dialects, Farsi and even Yoruba.

Scenes from Tehran’s Museum of Contemporary Art—designed by Kamran Diba under the patronage of Empress Farah and opened in 1977—offer haunting images of art and conditions before and after the 1979 revolution. Unearthed from an open-source archive is an unforgettable clip of a Japanese artist who creates his own petroleum-based installation, featuring a fountain of oil that melds Zen and Persian features.

A scene from Sanaz Sohrabi's documentary An Incomplete Calendar (2026) © Sanaz Sohrabi

Those scenes are juxtaposed with footage from the Aula Magna auditorium at the Universidad Central de Venezuela in Caracas, a Unesco World Heritage site where Alexander Calder’s monumental commission Floating Clouds (1953) still has pride of place. Here, the film-maker appears reading Farsi poems to an empty auditorium, as an anonymous viewer stands opposite with a mirror, reflecting poetry into the suspended sculpture.

This image of cultural exchange in the midst of ongoing war and horror is juxtaposed with footage of a cut-out image of the Iranian Oil Minister Mohammed Javad Tondguyan—who was captured and held hostage in Iraq at the beginning of the war in 1980—speaking at an Opec meeting earlier that year. An Incomplete Calendar ends at the moment in 1980 when “third world” secular solidarity among Opec members was superseded by political Islam, revolution and war. Yet the scenes filmed inside the Caracas auditorium suggest the phantom possibility of another way.

#4213. Frieze London Diary: A dinner dance with a twist, Barbie gets the blues and Warhol’s feline showstoppers

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Mind the china: Holly Blakey and her dancers mesmerise guests at the Frieze Collectors’ Dinner

Art world glitterati descended on the Frieze London tent on Thursday evening for the lavish Collectors’ Dinner attended by gallerists, patrons and (some) journalists. Guests including the director of London’s Delfina Foundation, Aaron Cezar, and Ed Behrens, the editor of Apollo magazine, joined a throng of luminaries who dined on delectable dishes such as chicken, potato and minestra nera (leafy green brassica), along with coffee fudge and chocolate truffle. (Yum—thanks, Sessions Arts Club, for the menu.)

Tongues wagged about hot topics of the day—does anyone actually like the Tate Britain 90s art show?—while quaffing Fortnum’s wines. Attendees lapped up a top-notch performance from the UK choreographer Holly Blakey, whose troupe of brilliant dancers wound in and out of transfixed diners. One guest said he almost choked on his petits fours when Blakey’s crew built a human tower that collapsed—on purpose.

Tschabalala Self waxes lyrical about Bronzino

Always one of the hottest tickets of Frieze week, Unexpected Views at the National Gallery, in which eight leading contemporary artists deliver a ten-minute talk in front of their favourite paintings, celebrated its tenth anniversary with a stellar lineup. Tschabalala Self, whose monumental Lady in Blue currently occupies Trafalgar Square’s Fourth Plinth, chose Bronzino’s An Allegory with Venus and Cupid (around 1545). Yinka Shonibare, meanwhile, talked in front of Thomas Gainsborough’s Mr and Mrs Andrews (around 1750), which shares a gallery with his own subversive life-size sculpture that presents the aristocratic couple decapitated and with their clothes remade in Dutch wax fabric.

Feeling blue: Joy Gregory’s Explosive Barbie at 1-54 Courtesy of the artist and Whitechapel Gallery.

Barbie is shedding her girly pink image for something altogether more subversive at Frieze London this week. Helen Chadwick’s photopiece Birth of Barbie (1993), on Richard Saltoun Gallery’s stand, finds the iconic dolly upside down, coloured deep scarlet and emerging out of labial folds of red meat, an image rendered all the more disconcerting by the fact that Chadwick used this image as her Christmas card for that year. Meanwhile, over at 1-54 on the Whitechapel Editions stand, Joy Gregory’s Explosive Barbie (2025) shows our favourite blonde having a very bad hair day, her splayed locks rendered in silhouette against a cyanotype blue background. Being a Barbie Girl in a Barbie World just got a whole lot less fantastic…

Feline groovy: Grayson Perry joins Andy Warhol’s kitty pals Photo: Silke Lohmann

Cats are all the rage at Frieze Masters; we’re talking mainly about the moggies on show at Shapero Modern gallery by none other than the Pop art maestro Andy Warhol. The 16 offset lithographs from the series 25 Cats Name(d) Sam and One Blue Pussy (around 1954) are stopping fairgoers in their tracks. The lettering was done by Warhol’s mother, Julia Warhola, who apparently is also responsible for the title’s missing “d”. Grayson Perry clearly thought they were purrrfect.

John Banting was one of the most flamboyant and hedonistic figures of the 1920s and 1930s, but died in obscurity. Now, he is being spotlit this week with a solo show, Livid Cosmoramas, at John Swarbrooke Fine Art, as well as a new biography by Hugh St Claire. At the exhibition opening one veteran art journalist, no slouch in the hedonism stakes herself, revealed that she had taken the ultimate fangirl plunge of having his distinctive 1932 woodcut Siamese triplets tattooed into her leg. Devotion indeed.

Maria Kreyn at the Swiss Churchphoto: Gareth Harris

A talking point show on the Frieze art trail this week is at the Swiss Church in Covent Garden. The exhibition of works by US artist Maria Kreyn (The Dream Logic, until 19 October), organised by the Ministry of Nomads Art Foundation, is a swirl of absorbing storm paintings that draw on art historical giants such as JMW Turner. Kreyn tells us what inspired one of the head-spinning pieces. “I grew up doodling under a piano while my mom practised. It’s one of my most vivid childhood memories— that space, and the feeling and the sounds. The second concerto is a reference mainly to all of the Rachmaninoff I’ve heard her play over the years. The piano felt like the whole sky, and the whole sky now feels musical,” she says. Other works by Kreyn hang in a London landmark building—her depictions of Shakespearean scenes are dotted around Theatre Royal Drury Lane (the artist was commissioned by none other than the musicals supremo Andrew Lloyd Webber).

Jim Amaral, Flowered Ears Nº 13, 14 y 15 (1978)photo: Diego Amaral/Artist’s Archive. Courtesy of LAMB

The spotlight falls on the US-born, Bogotá-based artist Jim Amaral at Frieze Masters whose fantastical drawings on show at LAMB gallery explore the body, desire, and the subconscious. The fair presentation centres on Invisible Flowers (1979–80)—ink and watercolour drawings that “merge human reproductive anatomy with imaginary plants”, says the gallery. Amaral’s back story is explored meanwhile in a parallel show at LAMB’s gallery in Mayfair (Jim Amaral: A Study of Five Decades, until 21 November). Jim and his wife Olga met as students at Cranbrook in Michigan around 1954 when their romance apparently began “over a tango”. When she returned to Colombia and he left for naval service in the Philippines, they exchanged letters, doodles, and photographs for two years. The Love Letter drawings feature in the Mayfair show, tracing his works on paper from the 1960s to the 2010s. Oh l’amour.
