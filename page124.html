#2255. What Happened to Cheap, Basic Cars?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

"Feature creep" has left cars over-expensive and stuffed with technology. Is it even possible to go back to basics?

There was a time when buying a cheap new car was relatively simple. The answer might have involved crank windows, manual door locks, an AM/FM radio, and seats upholstered in something that looked suspiciously like industrial carpeting.

Today, the idea of a truly low-budget, no-frills vehicle is getting hard to find. Even the least expensive new cars come with touchscreens, driver-assistance systems, connectivity features, multiple airbags, and enough electronic wizardry to make a 1990s luxury car look like a farm tractor by comparison.

That raises a reasonable question: Can you still buy a genuinely inexpensive, basic vehicle in 2026? The short answer is yes, but “basic” no longer means what it used to.

Before we talk about what no-frills, low-budget vehicles are available, it’s important that we set a baseline for what that kind of car is in today’s market.

Modern safety and fuel economy technology are two of the biggest reasons today’s inexpensive vehicles don’t look anything like their ancestors. A new car sold in the United States must meet extensive federal safety requirements. That means airbags, stability control, rearview backup cameras, crash protection, and a long list of other requirements.

Consumers have also developed expectations. Try selling a new car without air conditioning today and see how quickly the internet catches fire. Nobody wants to return to the days when a buyer had to choose between air conditioning and an AM/FM radio. Budget buyers today expect power windows, remote locks, smartphone connectivity, and decent climate control.

The result is an interesting middle ground. Today’s cheapest cars aren’t truly bare-bones. Automakers have figured out how to put a surprising amount of technology into relatively inexpensive vehicles without necessarily making them luxury cars.

For years, the sub-$20,000 new car was the entry point for budget-conscious buyers. Models like the Mitsubishi Mirage, Nissan Versa, Chevrolet Spark, and various tiny hatchbacks gave shoppers an inexpensive way into a new vehicle.

Many of those cars are gone now. The market has steadily moved toward larger vehicles, more equipment, higher prices, and greater complexity. That doesn’t mean inexpensive cars have disappeared. It means the definition of inexpensive has moved upward. A buyer looking for a new vehicle in the low-$20,000 range can still find options, but those vehicles aren’t exactly stripped-down A-to-B appliances anymore.

Even inexpensive cars now tend to have infotainment screens, Bluetooth, backup cameras, automatic emergency braking, lane-departure warnings, and other features that were once the exclusive territory of expensive luxury models.

The economy in the U.S. has changed significantly. Not just from COVID and various political changes, but also from the general mindset of most Americans. When we compare pricing from 30 or even just 15 years ago, we often mentally skew the results due to our perspective of that change. It’s important to remember that, thanks to inflation, a dollar from 2011 is not a dollar in 2026. That ‘11 dollar is now $1.49. Inflation compounds and that 15 years has meant a 49 percent decrease in value for our money.

Knowing that, we can compare the average new vehicle purchase price (currently about $51,000) today with the average 15 years ago ($30,500). Then we can compare those values to the average household income from both eras: $50,000 in 2011 and $87,500 in 2026. Doing that, we find that things haven’t changed much.

The average new vehicle price seems very high compared to 15 years ago, but it was about 60 percent of the average household income in 2011 and is still that for 2026.

Yes and no. If your definition of low-budget means “I need reliable transportation and I don’t care whether it has the latest technology,” the used market is still where you’ll get a good deal. But a good deal doesn’t always mean good value. Shopping for a used vehicle is a whole discussion unto itself and countless books, videos, and articles have been made detailing the do’s and don’ts.

What’s important and relevant here is to realize that while a three- to seven-year-old compact car can provide much of the transportation value that new economy cars once offered, it will come with some downsides. The trick is to shop based on condition and ownership history rather than simply finding the cheapest four-door vehicle.

What’s more, the used car market has changed significantly since the COVID blip. Up until just before the shutdowns began, the used car market remained relatively steady in pricing versus the new car market, with long-term changes largely following inflation. During COVID, used car prices jumped by about 49 percent as demand for vehicles collided with a lack of new vehicles due to closures. Afterward, as new cars began to once again appear on dealership lots, the used car market eventually saw less demand and adjusted accordingly.

That adjustment, however, wasn’t back down to pre-COVID numbers. It’s still 17 percent higher than it was. And it seems to have decided to hold steady there. The reason used cars are much more expensive is, again, supply and demand. This time between used car availability and demand from buyers wanting them. There are fewer used cars being sold because owners are keeping their used cars longer. This disparity and its higher price result has been a big driver in new car sales for the past few years.

Yes, but you have to adjust your expectations. If your definition of “no-frills” is “nothing I don’t need,” the new-car market still has possibilities. If your definition is “give me an engine, four wheels, a steering wheel, and absolutely nothing else,” you’re probably shopping used or waiting on the upcoming Slate pickup truck.

#801. Saudi Arabia Joins the Search for Alien Civilizations

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is joining in the technological effort to detect signals from other civilizations, called SETI. The SETI (Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence) Institute, a non-profit body, uses the Allen Telescope Array and other ground-based facilities to search for alien signals. They've also used the Hubble and other space telescopes.

LaserSETI is a new network of optical detectors under development since 2024. AlUla, in Saudi Arabia's northwestern region, is the fifth LaserSETI site, and the first one in Asia. The others are in California, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and Arizona.

LaserSETI brings new capabilities to the SETI endeavour. It can watch the entire sky continuously, catching rare transient events. Most of SETI's other efforts are slower, or target specific targets in the sky.

Most of what LaserSETI detects will just be flashes of light from events in the cosmos. But, the hope is, it will one day detect a laser originating from another ETI. The chances of it doing that increase with sky coverage, which is why LaserSETI is expanding. As the first one in Asia, Saudi Arabia's two detectors are important.

They're placed in Alula Manara, which is being developed as Saudi Arabia's first major astronomical observatory. Alula is also the kingdom's first UNESCO World Heritage Site, and has hosted multiple civilizations from the Neolithic through the Roman times and the Ottoman times. Alula is also a Dark Sky Park, and will eventually host a 4-meter telescope and a pair of 2-meter telescopes, if plans are successful.

For most of its history, Saudi Arabia has been known as a closed, conservative society with no separation between church and state. A place where science took a backseat to pretty much everything else. It hosts Islam's two holiest cities, Mecca and Medina, the site of pilgrimages by the Islamic faithful. Its weath, and its influence, comes entirely from oil.

But in recent decades and years, the desert kingdom has made a number of changes. It's sought to host international sporting and cultural events. It's also moved to use some of its extraordinary wealth in scientific endeavours. In 2009, it founded the King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST). Among other things, it hosted the fastest supercomputer in the Middle East. KAUST is also the highest-ranked university in the Arab World.

Hosting LaserSETI is part of a new direction for Saudi Arabia, and the upcoming telescopes that will join it at Alula will be part of its scientific future. As Universe Today readers likely agree, more science can only be good for Saudi Arabia. If, one day, Alula's LaserSETI were to play a role in detecting an ETI, that would be a culture-changing triumph.

#963. 13 Foods Experts Say You Should NEVER Eat After They Expire

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Some expired foods lose flavor—others can carry serious pathogens.

I hate wasting food, and I’m more likely to eat a container of leftovers that’s been sitting in the fridge for a little too long than throw it away. But some foods are riskier to mess with than others, like infant formula, meat, and eggs, and experts say it's better to throw them out if they’re past their prime.

Although I’m a proponent of using your five senses over the expiration date—if it looks OK, smells OK, and ultimately tastes OK, it’s probably safe to eat—some hazards you can’t necessarily suss out on your own. That’s when the label comes in handy.

Here's what you need to know about expiration dates, plus what food experts say you definitely shouldn't eat after that date has passed.

“Generally speaking, expiration dates in the U.S. don’t mean a lot,” says Laurie Beyranevand, director of the Center for Agriculture and Food Systems at Vermont Law School. “What’s even more confusing is that they’re written in a few different ways on a product label.”

You might see a “best before” date on one package and a “sell by” date on another. Typically, these labels indicate when manufacturers believe quality may start to decline, and they rarely address a product’s safety, Beyranevand says.

Expiration dates on food items also tend to be conservative, adds food safety attorney Jory Lange, leading many of us to throw away perfectly safe food out of fear it’s rotten.

As long as you store your groceries properly, you can still eat or use foods after their expiration dates, says Janilyn Hutchings, CP-FS, a food scientist with StateFoodSafety. “Better indicators for whether food has gone bad are ‘off’ smells, textures, and flavors,” she notes.

That said, some foods are more likely to lose quality or carry harmful pathogens after their expiration date. Here are the 13 foods experts say you should skip.

Federal law doesn’t require food manufacturers to provide expiration dates—except for infant formula, says Hutchings. After the expiration date, nutrient levels may drop, which is a problem because the Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) requires the formula's nutrients to match those listed on the label.

Ground beef, steak, and chicken can sometimes be safe to eat after the use-by date, but be extra cautious and look for signs of spoilage, such as unusual smells, discoloration, or visible mold, Hutchings says.

You can also use the fingerprint test for chicken: “If you press down on the chicken and it bounces back, it's still good,” she notes. “If the imprint of your finger stays, it's likely not good and should be thrown away.”

While eggs may be fine past their use-by date, Hutchings recommends performing the float test to be sure. Just fill a bowl with water and drop the egg in: if it sinks, it’s fresh; if it stands up, it’s older but likely OK; if it floats, it’s too old.

A spoiled egg will also smell when cracked, according to FSIS. Because eggs can carry Salmonella, your best bet is to store them in the original carton and aim for use within three weeks, according to the Food and Drug Administration.

Hard cheeses (like a block of cheddar, for instance) can sometimes be salvaged by cutting away mold, but soft cheeses (like cream cheese, ricotta, mozzarella, etc.) and crumbled varieties are different. Mold threads can spread and contaminate the cheese, extending beyond what you can see. Harmful bacteria like Listeria, Brucella, Salmonella, and E. coli can grow with the mold, the Mayo Clinic notes, so once soft cheese is moldy or expired, it’s a no.

Moisture and low salt speed up spoilage in deli meats, while dry foods such as rice and pasta last longer, says Lange. “We wouldn’t recommend eating high-moisture, low-salt items past their expiration dates because of bacteria growth that can lead to food poisoning,” he says. If your cold cuts smell sour or look slimy, toss them.

You probably don't cook with fiddleheads often, but keep this in mind if you do: These tightly coiled, asparagus-like ferns can produce toxins that can make you sick if you eat them past their expiration date, Lange says.

One of summer's biggest bummers is opening your fridge and discovering your strawberries in a furry white coat. If you see a moldy berry, remove it and any fruit it touched. Mold spreads quickly on soft fruit, so check the rest and discard anything that shows signs of spoilage.

Some molds can cause illness or trigger allergic reactions. If your berries are nearing their expiration date, pop them in the freezer to extend their shelf life and use them later in smoothies or cooked desserts, says professional chef Norah Clark.

It’s not a food-safety concern, but expired spices mean your meals aren’t as flavorful as they should be, Clark says. “Some spices that lose flavor after expiration include ground cinnamon, paprika, and ground cumin,” she notes.

Store your spices in airtight containers, away from heat and direct sunlight, to preserve their optimal flavor.

Your restaurant to-go container won’t have a printed expiration date, but timing still matters. Leftovers often sit at room temperature on the way home, giving bacteria a chance to grow and multiply, says dietitian Hannah Byrne, M.S., R.D.N.

“While refrigeration can slow down the bacterial growth, it doesn't stop it completely,” she says. “Plus, some restaurant leftovers have a variety of different food groups in them, and they all have different expiration dates on them, so for safety, it’s best to consume them within three to four days.”

If you pick up raw fish from your local store, make sure it’s stored on plenty of ice. If it feels dry or mushy, it’s likely old and not safe to eat. Once home, refrigerate seafood for one to two days before cooking or freezing it, as recommended by the FDA. If seafood spoils, it’ll smell sour, strongly fishy, or like ammonia, and the smell gets stronger after cooking. When in doubt, throw it out.

That jumbo bag of spinach seemed like a good idea—until it wasn’t. Once past their date, leafy greens like spinach and kale turn bitter, Byrne says, and eating expired greens increases your risk of foodborne illness. If they’re nearing expiration, blend them into a green smoothie.

High in unsaturated fats, nuts tend to go rancid fast, says dietitian Kelsey Kunik, R.D.N., and nutrition adviser for Zenmaster Wellness. Most last about four to six months at room temperature when stored in an airtight container, away from light.

“When the oils oxidize, the nut produces a bitter or sour taste, letting you know it's gone rancid,” Kunik says. “While it's safe to eat nuts that have gone bad in small amounts, you won't want to because of the bad taste.”

Unopened cooking oils generally last about a year; once opened, they last approximately six months, says Tracee Yablon-Brenner, R.D. She recommends storing cooking oils in dark glass away from light and heat. If it tastes bitter, it’s rancid and should be thrown away.

Unprocessed oils, such as extra-virgin olive oil, lose their beneficial polyphenols as they age, Kunik adds, and the flavor suffers.

#1341. ‘You Can See Everything’ Leaves Us Wondering About Elizabeth Holmes—But Do Her Homes Provide Evidence of Her Deception?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

What, exactly, is Elizabeth Holmes’s angle here? It’s one of many questions raised in You Can See Everything, the three-hour documentary directed by Lance Oppenheim and Nathan Fielder that has drawn hordes of theatergoers, to which answers are elusive. Why did Holmes, the fraudster who was sentenced to 11 years in prison for the bald-faced lies that once made her the richest self-made female billionaire in America, invite Fielder and a documentary crew to live with her at a rented beach house near San Diego for the entirety of her last month of freedom? “This is a question that we are still debating to this day,” the comedian recently told Wired.

While Holmes and Evans’s rented beach house is charming and located in an area where real estate is at a premium, it appears more low-key than the place they called home not long before filming began.

Throughout the film, Fielder’s questions are met by Holmes with vague responses, unsettling facial expressions, and insistence on her total innocence—naivety, even—including the claim that she has never, in her life, told a lie. In many of these scenes, the Theranos founder cries or rocks her baby in a bare face and casual garb (not a red lip or a black turtleneck to be seen) alongside her domestic partner, hotel heir Billy Evans, who micromanages Holmes as she whisks eggs, misuses her public image once she’s behind bars, and brusquely intervenes when onscreen interviews don’t go in his preferred direction. All the while, they mill about a modestly furnished two-bedroom home in Del Mar, California (an adjacent two-bedroom guesthouse hosted Fielder). The overall effect makes Holmes surprisingly sympathetic.

Holmes and her partner, Billy Evans, in their rented beach house with Fielder in the days leading up to her prison sentence

If you look past its direct beach access and affluent zip code (Zillow estimates the simple house is worth $9 million), the 1940s dwelling is unassuming. Wood-paneled and a bit outdated, it could be described as rustic. Its interiors are widely relatable. However, Holmes and Evans hadn’t been renting the home for long before Fielder showed up. Less than five months before the cameras started rolling, they lived on the grounds of a massive estate that was then listed for $135 million.

The main house at Green Gables, also known as the Mortimer Fleishhacker House

In September 2021, reports revealed that the couple had moved into a house at Green Gables Estate in Woodside, a well-heeled enclave outside of San Francisco. They resided in one of six houses that share the 74-acre property with the main house: a historic English country-style mansion spanning over 10,000 square feet. The estate was originally owned by businessman Mortimer Fleishhacker, who commissioned architect Charles Sumner Greene to build it in 1911. A tennis court, a 300-foot-long Roman-style pool, and three more normal-sized swimming pools dot the property. Holmes and Evans stayed there throughout Holmes’s trial and at least until November 2022, according to multiple reports. On the 18th of that month, she received her sentencing. (The Fleishhacker family finally sold Green Gables in 2025 for a discounted but still record-breaking $85 million.)

Holmes with one of her two children, who are featured heavily throughout the film. Fielder watches from nearby.

By April 26, 2023, You Can See Everything had begun filming Holmes and her family at their rented beach house in Del Mar, a San Diego suburb. On May 7 a New York Times profile revealed to the public that they had relocated to the area, where Evans’s family and their hotel empire are based. “Elizabeth Holmes blends in with the other moms here,” the story read. “If you are in her presence, it is impossible not to believe her, not to be taken with her and be taken in by her.”

If renting that cozy beach cottage was a calculated choice by Holmes to shape her image, it perhaps wouldn’t be the first time she took this tack. “She pulled this exact same move when Ken Auletta did a piece for The New Yorker on Elizabeth in 2014,” Theranos whistleblower Tyler Shultz recently pointed out. In the profile, Auletta wrote, “Her home is a two-bedroom condo in Palo Alto, and she lives an austere life,” adding that Holmes “doesn’t date” and “doesn’t own a television.” In fact, at the time, Holmes lived in a mansion in tony Atherton, California, with Theranos executive Sunny Balwani, whom she was secretly dating and who was later also convicted of fraud and sentenced to nearly 13 years in prison. The couple jointly purchased the French provincial-style house in 2013 under an LLC for $9 million. Spanning nearly 9,000 square feet, it featured crystal chandeliers, staff quarters, formal gardens, a guesthouse, a pool, and a sprawling pavilion for outdoor entertaining. Balwani bought out Holmes’s ownership of the property in 2018 for $7.9 million, then sold it in 2022 for $15.8 million.

Whatever story Holmes wanted to emerge from You Can See Everything, it seems she at least wanted to rewrite her character—a feat that she may have accomplished, in part, by carefully constructing the setting.

#647. What If We Only Had Weeks to Stop a Killer Asteroid? Our Best Bet Is Straight Out of Hollywood.

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

New simulations reveal the one option left when warning time runs out—and it’s nuclear.

Earth hasn’t seen an impact as intense as the Chicxulub asteroid since the Late Cretaceous. If it had, we might have never existed.

At about 6 to 9 miles across, with explosive force 4.5 billion times greater than the atomic bomb that devastated Hiroshima, this behemoth obliterated 75% of life on the planet, most famously all non-avian dinosaurs. Volcanoes were spewing lava and ash everywhere in its wake. Enormous tsunami waves rose from the oceans. Irreversible climate change followed, and anything that survived in the aftermath barely managed. That was 66 million years ago.

While nothing of that magnitude has happened since, an encore isn’t impossible. Projectiles continue to fly through space at incredible speeds. Some of them follow orbits that may someday bring them far too close to Earth, which is why prevention is being taken seriously. In 2022, NASA’s DART mission successfully deflected the asteroid Didymos by striking its moon Dimorphos, proving that a kinetic impactor could send a space rock out of orbit. Didymos was a viable experimental target because it was small and never a threat. What if something much more menacing came barreling towards us? It would be impractical to launch a swarm of impactors for something the size of Chicxulub. This is why a team of researchers at the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) ran simulations of an asteroid nuke.

Taking out an impactor with a nuclear detonation was never out of the question. While its effectiveness has been debated, early detection of a monster asteroid followed by physical action could mean the difference between survival and extinction. Headed by physicist Isaiah Santisevan, the LLNL team has been experimenting with scenarios in which larger objects come straight at Earth with unnervingly short warning times. Most meteorites that break into the atmosphere usually burn up before ever hitting the ground. Hypothetically, a gargantuan hunk of rock would have enough mass to stay intact, unless something either managed to deflect the invader or shatter it into pieces small enough to avoid any serious damage. Using a powerful nuke could either bump the approaching asteroid off course or blow it apart.

“An alternative approach is nuclear mitigation, where a nuclear explosive device (NED) is used to deflect or robustly disrupt the asteroid,” Santisevan said in a study recently published in The Planetary Science Journal. “The energy output available from an NED is much higher than a kinetic impactor, enabling larger [velocity] for deflection, or energy for intentional robust disruption at shorter warning times and for larger targets.”

When simulating a nuclear approach, the most common model of NED mitigation is known as a standoff burst. Using this model, the device is detonated at a predetermined height above the surface of the asteroid, at enough of a distance from Earth that neither the planet nor its satellites would face the risk of anything worse than a shower of cosmic dust (if even that). Because most NED energy is generated as x-rays, it ends up in a thin surface layer of the asteroid, vaporizing and eroding it. What has been vaporized will expand until it overcomes the asteroid’s gravity field and is blasted out, with the shift in momentum changing its velocity. That massive release of energy also sends a shock wave through the body of the rock—one extreme enough to fragment an asteroid into literal smithereens.

Santisevan ran simulations in Spheral with virtual asteroids made of different materials. Each would determine how high above an asteroid the nuke would have to be detonated, along with the amount of force needed to dismember the incoming projectile depending on its material composition, mass, and velocity. LLNL’s Ares simulation code was used to predict the results of a deflection. After 1,500 one-dimensional simulations, the researchers leveled up to 3D, modeling nuclear disruptions of an asteroid about 525 feet in diameter, changing detonation distance and fracturing potential with every simulation.

In the longest simulation, the asteroid fell apart, with most of its material streaking through space in the opposite direction. Shards of rock were moving faster than the asteroid’s escape velocity, meaning it was defying gravity. Future research will follow the trajectories of these fragments to find out where they end up.

“These results are a step toward the immensely difficult problem of doing comprehensive nuclear mitigation modeling for planetary defense scenarios,” the researchers said. “High-fidelity simulations as the ones presented here allow us to keep pushing the limits of what is possible.

A drop in jet fuel prices will not automatically lead to lower air fares, at least not in the near term, according to American Airlines’ chief financial officer.

In an interview with the Financial Times published on Thursday, Devon May said ticket prices will at best remain stable.

“If fuel comes down, I think air fares probably stay largely where they’re at right now,” May told the outlet. “If fuel prices go up, we need to generate higher yield, we need to generate higher fares to offset that higher fuel price.”

Jet fuel prices have effectively doubled since the start of the U.S.-Iran war in February, and fares have risen along with them, though Airlines For America has said that most U.S. carriers are absorbing the majority of the shock.

The impact of higher spending on fuel became clearer this week as numerous U.S. airlines slashed their full-year financial guidance. Many carriers are performing well, with resilient demand propping up record-breaking revenues, but fuel bills are eroding profits, with no relief in sight. American, United, and Southwest said this week that they expect to spend even more on fuel in the fourth quarter based on projections from mid-October.

May said part of the increase in ticket prices is actually a correction from the COVID-19 pandemic, which temporarily collapsed demand worldwide.

“This ideally should have happened back in 2024, but the industry found itself in an oversupply environment… This was the first year that everything came together in a way that we were able to push yields back up to where they were seven years ago in real terms,” he said.

American said on Thursday that it expects an adjusted loss per diluted share between $1 and $1.60 for all of 2026.

The airline anticipates an average fuel price of about $4.70 per gallon in the fourth quarter, nearly double the price paid in the same period in 2025. At that price, American’s fuel expense would increase by about $2.8 billion compared to a year ago.

The carrier said it will slow network growth in 2027 as result of the headwinds from fuel.

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

You Can See Everything co-director Lance Oppenheim calls him a “very unconventionally hyper-masculine man.” We get what he’s talking about.

IF YOU’RE EVEN somewhat interested in movies, you’ve heard all about You Can See Everything (including from us at Men’s Health). In the week and change since the movie was officially released, the film—which combines unprecedented access to former Theranos CEO and convicted fraudster Elizabeth Holmes and her partner Billy Evans with a meta-narrative threaded by co-directors Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim—has been discussed ad nauseam, sparking discourses about not only the facts realities of Holmes’s case and situation, but about the very nature of documentary films, language, and the truth.

And while the headlines of You Can See Everything are largely about the access to Holmes as a public figure and things she did or didn’t say, the reality is that it’s more of a Nathan Fielder movie than an Elizabeth Holmes movie. Fielder, who broke out with the docu-spoof series Nathan For You on Comedy Central before moving on to more surreal takes on similar subjects with HBO’s The Rehearsal and Paramount+ and Showtime’s The Curse, is at the center of the story all along, looking for something, anything, in Holmes and Evans as he talks to them. He’s searching less for the truth of the case—that’s already been investigated by the courts—and more for the truth of who Elizabeth is and why she’s behaved the way she has.

For a film of this structure to work, the person we’re along with for the ride needs to be someone we both understand and don’t mind spending that kind of time with. While You Can See Everything ultimately leaves us questioning everything we’ve just seen, Fielder, to his credit, is someone who makes the three-hour runtime an easy watch. He’s dry, yes—that’s his on-screen persona. But he’s also vulnerable and often humanistic. When he faces down Holmes, for example, in a scene where he shows her Abbott and Costello’s famous “Who’s On First?” routine for the first time, he reacts in a way most viewers could probably see themselves reacting.

Oppenheim did an interview with GQ, and he suggested that Fielder’s nature in the film makes him something of an emblem for modern men. “I feel like he's at his most masculine in You Can See Everything,” he said. “I'd always tell him, ‘You look so handsome in this.’ And I was just like, ‘I love being this close on your face. I'm so tight on your face right now and you look amazing.’ And he'd be like, ‘Dude, you could see my blackheads and my zits and stuff.’ I'd be like, ‘You look great. I actually think it makes you look better.’”

His quote alludes to the way Fielder carries himself in the movie: as something of an audience surrogate (at least during the first part of the film), and someone the audience can see themselves in. This marks a shift from Fielder’s previous on-screen personas, when he was a bit more of an on-screen joker or troll, egging his subjects a bit more into saying or doing something that will make them look silly. Here, he’s basically a blank slate for the audience to graft onto.

It’s interesting to read the quotes from Oppenheim, who last month also directed Primetime, a scripted A24 film starring Robert Pattinson. Pattinson, of course, is a traditionally handsome movie star, someone who’s played heartthrob roles like Edward Cullen in Twilight or literal superheroes like Bruce Wayne in The Batman. There’s, of course, a vision of masculinity in that. But when you look at someone like Fielder who is a normal guy—there’s even more of a mirror image being reflected to those of us watching in the theater or at home.

You Can See Everything understands the power in this unassuming parallel. Outside of Fielder, Evans is the only other male figure substantially present in the film, and he does partake in some more traditional expressions of masculine energy: He goes to the gym, he shoots guns in a range once he moves to Texas, and he even partakes in a shower scene of full-frontal male nudity as Fielder looks on. But it’s that parallel that Oppenheim implies is important—Fielder isn’t ever nervous or uncomfortable. “He's a very unconventionally hyper-masculine man,” Oppenheim said. “That's a compliment, I guess.”

The movie works because of the access we’re getting, and the way Fielder and Oppenheim set things up and draw us in and spit us out at the end. But it also works because Fielder so clearly knows who he is and understands who he is—blackheads be damned. And when the result is You Can See Everything, does anything else really matter?

#2760. Why Are Pacific Northwest Ski Resorts so Obsessed With Bigfoot?

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

(Photo: Courtesy of Sasquatch Mountain Resort )

Spend enough time skiing in the Pacific Northwest, and you’ll start seeing Bigfoot everywhere. The mythical creature appears in ski resort names, chairlifts, trail names, mountain restaurants, and even sculptures overlooking the slopes. In a region known for its towering forests and rugged mountains, Sasquatch has become part of ski country’s identity.

But why has Bigfoot become such a familiar presence in the Pacific Northwest, and how did the legendary creature find its way into the region’s ski culture?

At Mission Ridge in Washington, an 11-foot sculpture near the top of Chair 2 changes its appearance with the seasons. Built from recycled steel and chain, it looks shaggy and snow-covered during ski season. “In winter he’s coated in rime and snow and becomes a Yeti,” says Matt Kung, the resort’s marketing and data systems manager. “Come summer, the rusted chain shows through, and he’s a Bigfoot walking over the ridgeline.”

Bigfoot sightings and folklore are part of the culture in the Pacific Northwest’s mountain communities, too. On the way to Stevens Pass in Washington, skiers can stop at Espresso Chalet, a roadside coffee stand with a giant Sasquatch statue, Bigfoot-themed decor, and souvenirs. The café even ties back to the 1987 film Harry and the Hendersons, which featured the iconic creature. Farther north, at Big White Ski Resort in British Columbia, Sasquatch Sushi brings the theme to the mountain’s dining scene, with a name that nods to the region’s fascination with Bigfoot.

At Mt. Hood in Oregon, the same forests that draw skiers also fuel Bigfoot superstitions. The area reportedly has more Bigfoot sightings than anywhere else in the state, making a day on the slopes feel like an adventure into Sasquatch country. With some of the Pacific Northwest’s best skiing and snow, Mt. Hood offers plenty of opportunities to explore the terrain where the legend lives on.

At Sasquatch Mountain Resort in British Columbia, they take it a step further, weaving the myth into the mountain’s identity, from the resort name to the Sasquatch Triple Chair and Bigfoot run. The resort was previously known as Hemlock Valley Resort before new ownership rebranded it to reflect the area’s connection to Sasquatch folklore.

“Rather than adopting a generic mountain identity, we chose to celebrate something that is uniquely tied to the Harrison and Fraser Valley region,” says Nikita Romane, the resort’s inside operations manager.

The resort’s name reflects a broader connection between Sasquatch and the surrounding region. The nearby Harrison River Valley has embraced its reputation as Sasquatch country, with local folklore, reported sightings, and the area’s rugged forests helping keep the tale alive. Harrison Hot Springs even has a Sasquatch Museum dedicated to the creature.

Cliff Barackman, curator of the North American Bigfoot Center, sees a natural connection between Bigfoot and outdoor recreation.

He notes that reported encounters often occur in forests, mountains, river valleys, and other remote areas. Hikers, campers, hunters, anglers, and skiers spend time in these environments, making the outdoors a natural setting for Bigfoot stories.

The legend also has a cultural side. “Bigfoot has become such a recognizable symbol of the Pacific Northwest that it almost functions as a regional mascot,” Barackman says. He describes the creature as a symbol of wilderness, mystery, and the idea that some places still exist where people don’t know everything happening around them.

It’s easy to see how that idea translates to skiing. Skiers venture into the trees for fresh snow and to explore remote terrain, often in places where the forests stretch for miles. Bigfoot, a creature said to inhabit those same wild places, fits naturally into the experience.

Reported sightings aren’t scientific proof that Bigfoot exists, but the legend has become part of the Pacific Northwest’s identity. Ski resorts have found a way to bring that folklore onto the mountain, giving skiers a playful reminder of the region’s wild side.

Bigfoot’s connection to the Pacific Northwest goes beyond ski resort and restaurant branding. In British Columbia, the name Sasquatch comes from Sásq’ets, a word in the Halq’eméylem language associated with the Sts’ailes people that describes a “hairy man.” Sasquatch stories have long been part of Indigenous oral traditions in the area.

Nikita Romane at Sasquatch Mountain Resort recognizes that the mountain’s history extends beyond its playful branding. “While the resort embraces the Sasquatch legend as part of its identity, it is important to recognize that stories of Sasquatch-like beings have deep roots in the Indigenous cultures of the Pacific Northwest,” she says. She adds that the resort operates on the unceded territory of the Sts’ailes people and strives to respect those cultural origins while celebrating the legend.

For skiers, Bigfoot adds a little fun to a day on the slopes. A towering sculpture, a themed restaurant, or a beginner carpet named Littlefoot gives visitors something unexpected to discover along the way.

To Kung, Bigfoot embodies the freedom of the outdoors. “He’s never been pinned down; he goes wherever he wants, and he belongs completely to the wild places most people only visit,” he says.

In the Pacific Northwest, where forests and mountains have long fueled Bigfoot stories, the creature makes a fitting ski-country mascot. Skiers may come for the powder, but a little mystery along the way certainly doesn’t hurt.
