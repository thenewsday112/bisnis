“A Who Knew Carol” reworks the Muppet’s 1992 take on a Charles Dickens classic to spotlight product assortment, deals and express delivery offerings.

Walmart is reworking an iconic piece of pop culture history to bolster its holiday marketing and a “Who Knew?” brand platform starring Walton Goggins. The goal is to offer a unified marketing push with multigenerational appeal throughout the crucial gift-giving season.

The retailer in the past ran separate campaigns around different legs of the holiday season, such as the ramp-up to Black Friday, but has leveraged “Who Knew?” to streamline its storytelling. “Who Knew?,” which launched last year, was developed to dispel misperceptions about the brand and position it as more style- and trend-forward as Walmart wins over higher-income shoppers.

“The holidays are an incredibly cluttered period of time from an advertising standpoint, and so the ability to break through is really important,” Walmart CMO William White said in an interview. “Having one [theme], one campaign throughout the season allows a really quick read for customers to know it's Walmart.”

After transforming into classic Dr. Seuss character the Grinch in Walmart's holiday ads last year, Goggins has swapped out the furry green suit for the Victorian era digs of “The Muppet Christmas Carol.” He plays Ebenezer Scrooge, a character originally from Charles Dickens’ “A Christmas Carol,” alongside a cast of Muppets including Kermit the Frog and Miss Piggy. Also appearing is actor Stephanie Beatriz, another “Who Knew?” fixture who plays Scrooge’s niece and will lead Spanish-language spots.

The trio of specters haunting Scrooge have been themed around key seasonal beats: a ghost who showcases gift ideas, one who underscores savings available during Black Friday and Cyber Monday and another who promotes last-minute gifting, including Walmart Express Delivery in 30 minutes or less. Over the course of the campaign, Goggins’ Scrooge shifts from a humbug attitude to appreciation for Walmart, a journey that reflects the larger “Who Knew?” concept.

“We love ‘A Christmas Carol’ as a story because it's a reappraisal story, and the ‘Who Knew?’ campaign is a reappraisal story for us as well,” said White.

“A Who Knew Carol” was created with Publicis Groupe agencies Fallon, Leo New York, The Community, Digitas and Zenith. White views the Muppets as carrying broad consumer appeal and fresh relevance following a TV special featuring Sabrina Carpenter and their cameo at the World Cup final halftime show.

Walmart teased the collaboration in a social video last week in which Goggins meets with Kermit on a studio lot. The larger stage for a “A Who Knew Carol” is set in a long-form video debuting Monday that recreates “Scrooge,” the opening song from the original “The Muppet Christmas Carol.”

Physical sets echo the look of the 1992 film while the script winks to the retailer, such as when Rizzo the Rat complains that Walmart didn’t exist during the time period. Muppets not present in the original movie, notably Pepe the King Prawn, appear in the campaign to capitalize on their presence in meme culture, per Fallon CEO Nikki Baker. A series of additional videos and social content will be released throughout Q4.

The full media plan spans broadcast TV, streaming, online video, social, out-of-home, audio, podcasts, digital and creator networks. The concept will factor into Walmart’s NFL integrations, with on-air personalities referencing the campaign. Walmart is also leaning more into creators as part of a push to market itself as a fashion destination. On the style front, Walmart will deploy Miss Piggy in its holiday marketing, but kept mum on details.

“[Creators] are helping to drive reappraisal, they're helping to drive awareness and they're driving conversion all within one one moment, and so it's an important part of our media mix,” said White.

Retailers are entering the thick of a Q4 period where consumer sentiment around the economy remains sour, though the category could be insulated from some of those headwinds. Holiday spending on retail goods is expected to climb 10% year over year, according to Deloitte surveys. Walmart is coming off a robust fiscal Q2, when total revenue rose 5.9% to $187.9 billion. E-commerce grew 24% YoY and now represents nearly one-quarter of sales in the U.S., double its share from five years ago.

Target, Walmart’s chief brick-and-mortar rival, will begin ramping up its holiday marketing early in November. The brand is running a campaign centered on a fictional academy where an array of departments and faculty members specialize in different types of gift-giving.

“A Christmas Carol” is often revisited around the holidays, but it could receive additional attention this year. A new take on the tale titled “Ebenezer” stars Johnny Depp as Scrooge and is being marketed as a comeback moment for the actor following years of legal troubles. “Ebenezer” lands in theaters Nov. 13.

“I think it'll be a great momentum to the campaign and people will be engaged in the IP. That's a nice add-on” said White.

#22. Sudan paramilitary drone strikes cause mass power cuts in eastern states

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Rapid Support Forces strike five power stations in three states controlled by Sudanese army

Sudan’s paramilitary Rapid Support Forces have launched a coordinated drone attack against five power stations, causing mass power outages across the country’s east, a government official said.

The drones hit two power stations each in the states of Al-Jazira and Gedaref and one in White Nile state, “resulting in power outages across large parts of these three states”, the official said on condition of anonymity.

All three states are controlled by the Sudanese army, which has been at war with the RSF, its former ally, since April 2023.

The United Nations has repeatedly warned that regular attacks on power stations threaten health and services for millions of people who are already facing the world’s largest hunger crisis.

Babiker Youssef, a resident of Al-Fao, in Gedaref, said the power had been out there since early morning.

“My son is diabetic, his insulin has to be stored in the fridge,” he said over the phone, adding that he feared he would not be able to replace the medication if it spoiled. “We have no idea when the power will come back,” he said.

Electricity has been cut for days at a time after past paramilitary drone strikes on power stations and hydroelectric dams.

Sudan’s war is currently being fought primarily via drones, with both sides launching sophisticated attacks across the country, killing dozens of civilians at a time.

In the first three weeks of October, drone strikes killed more than 300 people and injured hundreds more, according to a UN tally.

Africa’s third largest country, home to an estimated 50 million people, has been ravaged by the war, which aid workers estimate has killed more than 200,000 people.

The majority of Sudan’s hospitals have been forced to close, at least 8 million children are out of school and swathes of the country cannot access basic services.

Abdelhafez Daafallah, a 54-year-old farmer, said by phone from Al-Junaid town in Al-Jazira: “The electricity had just started to improve three months ago, the power station the militia had destroyed was just repaired and we had hope our irrigation pumps would finally start running again.

“We hoped we would be able to finally get back to farming, but now the station has been hit,” he said, frustrated after nearly four years unable to work his land.

Farmers in Al-Jazira have struggled to rehabilitate and replant their land since the army recaptured the state from the RSF last year.

To the east, in Gedaref, Ibrahim Mohammed, 37, a grocer, said he was “already facing power cuts for hours before the strike”. “Now I have chicken in the fridge, we either lose our entire stock, or will be forced to sell it off at any price, which is still a loss,” he said.

Sudan is also grappling with unprecedented inflation and its currency has lost more than 25% of its value in recent weeks.

#612. Archaeologists may have just found the world's oldest shipwreck

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A shipwreck off the coast of Turkey is the oldest shipwreck in the world, our team of archaeologists believes.

There’s little left of the ship itself but based on clues from its cargo – including pottery vessels – a research team I was part of has tentatively dated the wreck to around 2700–2400 BCE.

That’s about 4,500 years ago. Prior to this discovery, the world’s oldest shipwreck was a wreck off the coast of Dokos, in Greece, thought to have been from about 4,200 years ago.

Much work remains to be done to finesse the conclusions, and a paper outlining the results is yet to be published in a peer-reviewed journal. Yet the discovery could transform our understanding of ancient Mediterranean connections during the Early Bronze Age.

Here, and perhaps even earlier, lie the foundations of extensive trade routes as we know them today.

Associate Professor Hakan Öniz of Akdeniz University, Antalya, has worked along this coastline for decades excavating ancient shipwrecks.

Acting on clues from local seafarers, Öniz found a shipwreck in shallow waters off Kekova, west of Antalya in Turkey, in 2024. Earlier this year, he contacted me and asked if I would help date some of the pottery vessels found on this wreck.

As an archaeologist specializing in desert sites, this was a singular experience. New technology, water-logged methods of excavation and laborious conservation techniques were the new normal. Yet there is always room for learning new skills in life.

Of the ship itself there is now nothing to see, owing to underwater exposure. Yet the research team has now estimated the ship and its cargo dates to around 2700–2400 BCE. However, the precise dating is still under consideration. Earlier or later dates on either side of this initial determination are also possible.

This discovery promises to re-write our understanding of early trade networks from around 4,500 years ago.

The ship is a “missing piece” in the story of networks, trade and commodity exchange in the wider eastern Mediterranean. This era includes the pyramid age of Giza and its mighty pharaohs.

What remains under the sea is a time capsule of ancient trade.

Although its direction was not known, the boat was evidently traveling along the coast of southern Turkey.

Now resting on the seabed between two islands, it is apparent the ship likely sought refuge in the lee waters during a storm. One could imagine the terror of the sailors – possibly coastal people from the Levant region – as their ship crashed against the rocks.

While timbers from the ship disappeared long ago, the remains of its precious cargo were embedded in the rocky slope along the Kekova coast.

Divers worked over the past four months to extract its pottery from barnacles clinging to the rocky underwater slope.

Pottery is like plastic: it never goes away and just breaks down into smaller and smaller pieces.

Yet humble pottery vessels hold many clues about the past.

In this case, the cargo included large ceramic storage vessels or amphorae which once contained products from regions of the eastern Mediterranean.

The pots were large ovoid, flat-based jars with two handles, many featuring a raised horizontal clay “belt” around the widest part of the jar.

Through these vessels was possible to ascertain many pieces of information. This includes the likely origin of the jars and the contents exchanged across the region.

Importantly, the shape and manufacturing technique of the pots themselves help us date the cargo. By extension, this includes also the date of other pottery, and the ship itself.

Compared to later periods, trade and exchange in the eastern Mediterranean during the third millennium (3000–2000 BCE) is poorly understood.

Maritime voyages during the that period are known from Egyptian texts, pottery vessels and raw materials from ancient tombs.

Around the same time as this ship struck the rocks, the ancient Egyptians were trading in the eastern Mediterranean through the port of Byblos in Lebanon.

Commodities such as cedar, tin/lead, oils, tree resins, lapis lazuli and silver, all materials that Egypt lacked, were imported by the Egyptian state.

Recent scientific research also reveals that silver was acquired by the Egyptian state around 2600 BCE. Analyses showed this silver was acquired from the Lavrion mines east of modern Athens.

While it is unlikely Egyptian ships made this journey, boats like the Kekova ship may have used the route as part of an extended international maritime network.

And what was in the jars? Remarkably there were still residues visible to the naked eye. Again, scientific analyses are pending, but could be some of the products mentioned in the Egyptian texts, possibly olive oil, wine or tree resin.

The ship may have acquired its pottery from several ports on their journey. Our best assessment at this stage of research is that some of the “belted” jars (or clays used to make them) may have originated in the Central Jordan Valley.

The coast of southern Turkey is littered with ancient shipwrecks. While maritime archaeology is a relatively recent phenomenon, some of the world’s most famous wrecks have been discovered there. Who knows what other secrets these waters hold.

The research on which this article is based was led by Associate Professor Hakan Öniz, head of the Division of Mediterranean Underwater Cultural Heritage Research, and the Department of Restoration and Conservation of Cultural Heritage in the Faculty of Art at the Akdeniz University, Antalya, Turkey.

#2826. How to Skin a Deer Like a Pro

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 26, 2026 9:38 AM EDT

Get everything from whitetail news and opinion pieces, to advanced hunting tactics and cutting-edge gear reviews.

If you ask a dozen hunters how to skin a deer, you’ll find that every one of them does something slightly different. While that may be frustrating for newer hunters, it should also be reassuring: There’s no one right way or right order to skin a deer. As long as you follow the general techniques and handy tips outlined below, you’ll be able to skin a deer in no time.

Once you do get the hang of it, skinning a deer is quick and easy. Butchering and packaging a deer can take all afternoon but “if it takes me 10 minutes to skin a deer,” says Cheyne Matzenbacher, a one-time deer processor and current deer outreach specialist for the National Deer Association, “that’s a long time.”

Read through the whole article or click on a topic below to jump ahead.

Although there are lots of tools and setups that can make skinning a deer much easier, the only thing you really need is a sharp knife. The best skinning knives are typically handy enough for maneuvering but durable enough for joint work. There’s nothing wrong with using a small folding skinning knife in the backcountry or a longer filet knife. Just don’t use a dull knife — it’s dangerous and takes longer to finish the job.

Before you can skin your deer, you need to gut or field dress your deer, which involves removing the internal organs from the chest and abdominal cavities. Once your deer is gutted, you’ll ideally want to hang it. It’s certainly possible to skin your deer on the ground or the bed of your pickup (I’ve done both many times), and if you plan to do this, you should study up on the gutless method of skinning deer and other big game. This process is preferred by backcountry hunters who quarter their deer in the field, slip the meat into game bags, and pack it back to camp or their trucks.

But for most whitetail hunters and Western hunters who hunt close to home, it’s easier and faster to skin a deer that’s hanging. The meat will also stay cleaner if it’s hanging instead of touching dirt, bacteria, and deer hair on, say, your tailgate. There are a dozen methods for hanging a deer, and they all depend on your location and the tools available to you.

Deer hunters are divided on this question. You can skin a deer that’s hanging head up or head down, but there are advantages to each method. When Matzenbacher worked as a deer processor for a season, he and his coworkers would hang deer by the head or neck to skin the animal, then flip it to hang head-down for butchering.

“Think about how a deer jumps over a fence: His legs are folded up against his body. Let gravity help you, and let the natural way a deer is going to fold up help you skin it,” says Matzenbacher. “Don’t fight it.”

I skin all my deer head-down, so that’s how I’ll walk you through this example. If you want to try the head-up method, the principles are all the same. Just start at the neck and work your way down. If you are planning to shoulder mount your deer, don’t follow all of these instructions; you’ll need to make very careful cuts near the forelegs and chest. These traditional instructions are for does or for bucks you plan to skull cap or Euro mount. Finally, redneck ingenuity abounds when it comes to skinning deer, and if you’re interested in working smarter, not harder, consider the golf-ball trick or skinning your deer with your truck.

Make identical circular cuts near the knee joints, circling the thin bone of the lower leg on the side closer to the hoof. (This is the only time you should be cutting down on deer hair. For all the rest of the cuts, insert your knife under the hide and cut parallel to the meat or away from the meat.)

Slip the tip of your knife under the hide inside one hind leg. Applying even pressure, cut up and out in a single smooth motion as you run your blade toward the pelvis until you reach the cut you made while field dressing. Do this on the other hind leg, then start to skin the hide away from the tendons where you’re going to put the gambrel and expose the knees. The idea is to remove just enough hide that you don’t have to skin around the metal of the gambrel once you hang the deer.

Thread the gambrel through the natural holes you just exposed in the hind legs (between the bone and thick tendon) and hoist your deer. Tie it off at a comfortable height to work on the hindquarters

Note: You can perform this at almost any point in the process. Some hunters prefer to do this before hanging their deer, others do it as needed throughout the process (as pictured). You can also use loppers or a reciprocating saw for this and may want a saw for the hind legs; just take care not to cut the big tendon on the hind legs.

Slightly bend the front legs and cut through the joint. Wiggle the leg to find the weakest point and use your knife blade to cut perpendicularly through the tendons and between the bones until the joint is free. This may seem tricky at first, but it’s simpler than it looks.

Run your knife between the lower leg cuts and the cut along the abdomen you made while field dressing. Do this on both sides of the inner hindquarters. Combined with the lower leg cuts (or removal) these are the major cuts that will allow you to skin most of your deer.

Using your off hand to lift the hide and your knife hand to make cuts parallel to the meat, begin to peel away the hide. Work from the inside of the hind leg around to the outside. Do this on both sides.

Separate the hide at the tail by either running your knife under the hide and pulling it off the tail like yanking off a sock, or cutting the tail off altogether. Use your knife to work between the joints or simply saw it off.

Now you can begin the easiest part of skinning a deer: letting gravity help you pull the hide off. You may need to raise the deer a little for this part. Once you’ve used your knife to separate the hide from the tail and the inside of the hind legs, set it aside and grab fist-fulls of hide. Using your body weight, pull the hide toward the ground. If the hide doesn’t pull free in places, you can use your knife to help release the hide by slicing the delicate white crackly tissue that connects the skin to the meat. You’ll likely need to do this around the belly area and occasionally along the back

The hide will start to form a tube as you peel it away and it gets closer to the chest and neck.

Before the hide gets in your way too much and starts to drape over the forelegs, make cuts inside the forelegs similar to the ones you made on the hindlegs, starting near the knee joint and running the blade along the backs of the legs until they reach the body and chest. (If you didn’t remove the lower forelegs before, be sure to do this before you try to pull the hide over it.) You can also just pull the skin over the forelegs by pushing down on the hide between the brisket and front of the cut front legs (see video above). It’s similar to pulling off a sock.

Continue to work the hide down over the shoulders and forelegs like you’re peeling off a sweatshirt. The cuts you made on the legs will appear and you can use your knife to free the hide.

Once the hide is over the shoulders, use your knife to separate it from the neck. The freed hide will be heavy now and starting to get in your way. Use its weight to your advantage so it pulls the at the hide still connected to the deer.

When you reach the base of the neck, use a saw or your knife to sever the head from the vertebrae. You’ll have to cut through meat and tendons on all sides until you reach the spine. If you’re using a knife, work through the vertebrae like you did with the knee joints, turning the deer’s head a little bit to help free space between the vertebrae. You want to make your cut at the atlas joint, the connection between the head and the first vertebra of the neck and spinal column.

Once you cut through the neckbone the hide should be completely removed from your deer, though still attached to the head. If you need to get your deer tested for CWD or plan to make a European mount, just slice away all that extra hide hanging off the head.

This step is only necessary if you plan to make your own skull mount. Start at the neck and cut away the skin as you did on the rest of the deer, making cuts parallel to the meat and skull until you’ve removed most of the skin. A flathead screwdriver can help free the hide at the base of a buck’s pedicles, which can be the trickiest part to remove. This is a more time consuming process and should be done carefully to avoid injury or scoring the skull with your knife, which can cause cosmetic damage.

No, you don’t have to hang a deer to skin it. I’ve skinned plenty of deer in the back of a pickup, and sometimes that’s your only option if you’re hunting in areas without many trees or access to a barn or meat pole. It’s definitely easier to skin a deer if it’s hanging since gravity helps remove the hide. It’s also easier to keep the meat clean if your deer is hanging when you skin it; skinning a deer on a level surface like a tailgate or outdoor table makes it more likely that you’ll get deer hair and possibly dirt or bacteria on the meat.

The only tool you must have to skin a deer is a reasonably sharp knife. There are plenty of additional tools that make the job easier, and those will depend on where you’re skinning your deer. If you’re working in the field, consider a hitch hoist for your vehicle and a pair of loppers to quickly remove legs. If you have the luxury of a garage, shed, or barn to hang your deer, along with electricity, you might invest in an electric winch and a Sawzall.

How fast you need to skin a deer depends on a few factors, including the temperature outside and your state’s carcass transportation laws. As a general rule, in the right conditions (cool and dry), a deer can hang hide-on for several weeks. (See below for more details.) If it’s hot and humid, skin the deer as fast as you can to help cool the meat and prevent spoilage. If temperatures are near or below freezing, you should skin a deer while the carcass is still warm and the hide is pliable; skinning a frozen deer is both difficult and time consuming. Meanwhile, CWD laws in your state may require you to debone all deer meat before crossing county or state lines. If such regulations apply where you hunt or live, you’re going to need to skin your deer so you can debone it before you hit the road.

A skinned deer can hang for up to a few weeks if it’s stored in the proper conditions. The deer needs to stay cool and dry, such as sheltered outside during the winter or (preferably) inside a walk-in cooler set to a consistent temperature. Experts recommend hanging deer for 7 to 10 days at a temperature between 35 and 45 degrees to allow enzymes to age, or tenderize, the meat. A deer will not age properly if it’s frozen.

Yes, you can age deer meat with the skin on. Opinions on this vary about which method (aging venison with or without the hide) is better. Any meat that’s exposed to air as it ages will develop a dried crust, and the hide can help protect that meat from drying. I once hung a doe, hide-on, in a walk in-cooler with the temperature set to 38 degrees for two weeks. It was surprisingly easy to skin after aging and I lost less meat than when I age completely skinned quarters. But if you let your deer hang for much longer than that (three weeks or more) the meat may over-tenderize and start to come away with the hide, forcing you to work slowly and possibly wasting meat. Still, letting deer meat hang for at least a day or more can help keep it dry, tenderize the venison, and concentrate flavor before butchering.

The best deer skinning knife is a sharp one. The type of knife—skinning, caping, boning, or filet—matters less than its sharpness and the cuts you make. Many hunters and wild game processors prefer different types of knives for different stages of the skinning process: There are skinning knives (usually slim, handy knives with a sturdy blade), flexible filet knives for working around awkward angles or different muscle groups, caping knives for skinning the shoulders and head of a trophy deer, and stout boning knives for separating knee joints. Try several types of knives to find the best deer skinning knife for you.

Remember, there is no single perfect way to skin a deer. Every deer hunter and processor I’ve met does something slightly different, and most swear their way is the best way. Find which tools and techniques work for how you hunt and what kind of facilities you have access to (if any). The only way to truly learn how to skin a deer is, well, to skin a deer. So sharpen your knife and get after it.

#630. AI detects “invisible” signs of pancreatic cancer years before diagnosis

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An artificial intelligence model called REDMOD can identify extremely subtle changes in pancreatic tissue that may signal cancer long before the disease becomes visible to doctors or conventional imaging technology. According to research published online in the journal Gut, the system can detect early signs of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, the most common type of pancreatic cancer.

Researchers believe this technology could help transform pancreatic cancer detection by identifying the disease at a much earlier stage (stage 0), when treatment is more likely to succeed. Instead of receiving a diagnosis after the cancer has progressed to an advanced, potentially terminal stage, patients might have an opportunity to begin treatment while the disease is still treatable.

Although REDMOD outperformed experienced radiologists in the study, more research is necessary before it can be introduced into routine medical care. In particular, the scientists emphasize the need to test the system in patients at high risk, including those experiencing unexplained weight loss or recently diagnosed diabetes.

Why Pancreatic Cancer Is So Difficult to Detect Early

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma has a low survival rate, largely because it is often discovered only after the disease has advanced. Early pancreatic cancer typically produces few noticeable symptoms, and the changes it causes in pancreatic tissue can be too subtle to detect through standard medical imaging. The cancer can also progress rapidly, making timely diagnosis especially important.

To address these challenges, researchers created an artificial intelligence framework known as Radiomics-based Early Detection MODel (REDMOD). The system analyzes extremely fine patterns in the texture of pancreatic tissue, known as radiomics (radiomics), that are not readily visible on conventional computed tomography (CT) scans.

REDMOD also automatically identifies and outlines the pancreas, separating it from nearby organs and tissues. This process eliminates the need for specialists to manually trace the organ's boundaries, reducing the inconsistencies that can arise when different people perform the task.

AI Finds Hidden Cancer Signals Years Before Diagnosis

To evaluate the system, the researchers examined abdominal CT scans from 219 patients treated at multiple hospitals. At the time of their scans, experienced radiologists had found no evidence of pancreatic cancer. However, every patient in this group was subsequently diagnosed with the disease.

The scans had been taken at different intervals before diagnosis. For 87 patients (40%), the images were obtained 3 to 12 months earlier. Another 76 patients (35%) had been scanned 12 to 24 months before diagnosis, while 56 (25%) had undergone imaging more than 24 months earlier (up to around 3 years). In nearly two thirds (64%) of the patients, the cancer developed in the head of the pancreas.

The researchers compared these images with CT scans from 1243 people who remained free of pancreatic cancer for up to three years afterward. The comparison group was matched according to age, sex, and the dates of their scans.

Patients who eventually developed pancreatic cancer had an average age of 69, with ages ranging from 34 to 88. Those in the comparison group were 64 years old on average, with the same age range of 34 to 88.

REDMOD successfully identified subtle imaging signatures associated with pancreatic cancer an average of 475 days before patients received their clinical diagnoses. These early warning signs had previously gone undetected during routine radiological assessments.

"This temporal window holds profound significance, as attaining such early detection would substantially augment the probability of cure and improved survival," highlight the researchers.

"In fact, modelling studies indicate that increasing the proportion of localized [pancreatic ductal carcinomas] from 10% to 50% would more than double survival rates, thereby underscoring that the timing of diagnosis is the single most critical determinant of survival outcomes," they add.

The results also showed that REDMOD was substantially better than experienced radiologists at recognizing early cancer signals.

The AI achieved a sensitivity of 73%, compared with 39% for radiologists. Sensitivity measures how effectively a diagnostic method identifies people who actually have the disease, rather than overlooking genuine cases.

The difference was even more pronounced when researchers examined scans taken more than two years before the patients were diagnosed. In these cases, REDMOD achieved a detection rate of 68%, nearly three times the 23% recorded by radiologists.

The researchers also assessed how well the system could recognize scans from people without pancreatic cancer. In an independent group of 539 patients from several hospitals, REDMOD correctly classified just over 81% of scans as cancer free. It performed even better on the publicly available US National Institutes of Health NIH-PCT dataset (80 patients), correctly identifying 87.5% as free of pancreatic cancer.

Additional testing suggested that the early warning signs detected by REDMOD were consistent over time. When the researchers analyzed scans taken from the same patients several months apart, the system produced matching results in 90 to 92% of cases. This consistency strengthened the evidence that the AI was identifying meaningful changes associated with the disease.

Could AI Transform Early Pancreatic Cancer Detection?

Despite the promising findings, the researchers caution that several questions remain unanswered. One limitation is that the study population did not include a sufficiently diverse range of ethnic backgrounds, which could affect how well the technology performs across different populations.

The system must also undergo prospective testing, particularly among people at increased risk of pancreatic cancer, before researchers can determine whether it will be effective in everyday clinical practice.

Even with these limitations, the findings suggest that AI could eventually help doctors identify pancreatic cancer while it is still at a much more treatable stage, potentially improving survival for patients who would otherwise receive a late diagnosis.

The researchers conclude: "This study validates REDMOD as a fully automated AI framework capable of identifying the imaging signatures of stage 0 [pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma] in normal pancreas, achieving this with substantial lead times and performance superior to expert radiologists."

They add: "While prospective validation is paramount to confirm clinical utility, the REDMOD framework represents a significant advance towards shifting the paradigm for sporadic [pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma] from a late-stage symptomatic diagnosis to proactive pre-clinical interception, offering tangible hope for improving outcomes in this challenging disease."

#1945. The Healthcare Marketing Tech Company OptimizeRx Is Going Programmatic

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Maria Cipicchio sits at the intersection of some of the world’s most dizzying three-letter acronyms.

She’s the SVP of strategic market intelligence at OptimizeRx, a software subscription service for healthcare providers, which plugs into their drug-prescribing and record-keeping systems. And OptimizeRx has taken its next big step into the world of programmatic through a new partnership this month with The Trade Desk.

OptimizeRx’s inventory is all within HCP electronic health records (EHRs). The company’s inventory is all strictly within the doctor’s log-in portal at the point of care, and its advertisers to date are all pharma brands.

“We knew that we needed to enter the [programmatic] space very carefully,” Cipicchio told AdExchanger.

OptimizeRx started working with DeepIntent earlier this year on a programmatic banner ad partnership. Doctors or healthcare providers (or HCPs) see these ads in their software UIs when they log in to set up a prescription or copay card for a patient.

Programmatic units, though, are loaded with more concerns, she said. For one thing, pharma marketers don’t want to have their ads placed alongside actual patient data. That’s not a government rule, but something that’s important to those marketers.

Then there are OptimizeRx’s concerns. The ad creatives must all be vetted, Cipicchio said. Aside from making sure there’s nothing inappropriate or off-base, it’s important not to serve an ad that feels errant or misleading to the doctor. The ad must always focus on relevant healthcare data regarding its product.

“But the buyer doesn’t ever see that aspect of it, which is one of the beauties of programmatic,” she said.

OptimizeRx is taking a conservative approach to programmatic. But it’s excited about the revenue prospects as well.

Which is why the company is making the leap from DeepIntent – which, as a healthcare-focused DSP, lives inside the same regulatory bubble as OptimizeRx – to working with The Trade Desk as well.

OptimizeRx’s inventory is all within HCP electronic health records (EHRs). The targeting is all based on a unique medical identifier assigned to every prescribing doctor, called a National Provider Identifier (or NPI – told you there’d be acronyms). Unlike web or CTV targeting, every advertiser on OptimizeRx knows exactly who’s seeing the ad, and the identifier is persistent for the doctor’s career. There are no bots; the ad is fully in view and is never “below the fold,” Cipicchio said, like other programmatic media.

Part of the promise of its Trade Desk partnership “is the OpenPath idea,” which would mean the DSP could analyze its ads served within the HCP EHR in comparison with campaigns on streaming TV or other channels.

A general programmatic partner like TTD also opens the door to new types of advertisers outside the prescription base, Cipicchio said.

But there are other types of marketers that would like the opportunity to serve an ad to certain doctors right before a patient is given their next steps.

“Every skincare product wants a doctor to recommend their product,” she said. It’s often the case that a doctor, even when they are going to issue a prescription or see a patient on an issue over the course of weeks or months, will recommend over-the-counter products, like NyQuil or Tylenol. An over-the-counter allergy brand might use TTD’s system to target geographical areas during allergy breakout seasons, like spring pollen.

At least, those are compelling sales pitches OptimizeRx might make now that it has the TTD integration.

To date, practically all of OptimizeRx’s ad business has been tied to prescription drug brands, Cipicchio said. Which makes sense.

“But there’s no restriction on that in the programmatic arena,” she said.

#2160. An Illustrated Guide to Famous (and Infamous) Figures from the History of Psychiatry

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

My writing career has been like a time-travel cruise studded with real-life celebrities. In my Sano Ichiro samurai detective series set in 17th and 18th century Japan, I got up close and personal with the shogun. In my two Charlotte Bronte novels, I hobnobbed with the famous author from Haworth, England and none other than Queen Victoria. I began my Sarah Bain series with a backstage pass to the Jack the Ripper investigation, starring some of the most famous cops in police history.

My new novel, Resurrection Kill, is set in a psychiatric hospital in upstate New York in 1953, a world just as dark as feudal Japan and Victorian England. The psychiatric profession had its own luminaries, who weren’t necessarily well known to the public but were revered—or reviled—among its own membership. Here I’ll introduce some major players. I’m a comics artist as well as a suspense novelist, so I’ve drawn these portraits of them.

Meet Dr. Frieda Fromm-Reichmann (1889 – 1957). A German psychiatrist and contemporary of Sigmund Freud, she immigrated to America during World War II. She invented the concept of the “schizophrenogenic mother,” theorizing that psychological disorders stem from mothers who neglected, overprotected, or otherwise mistreated their children. Her legacy is generations of moms getting blamed for all their kids’ problems. Dr. Emma Koffka is her fictional counterpart in Resurrection Kill.

And then there’s Dr. Walter Jackson Freeman (1895 – 1972), a neurologist who popularized the use of lobotomy for psychiatric patients. His form of lobotomy involved hammering an instrument that resembled an icepick through an eye socket into the brain.

Dr. Freeman traveled around the country, performing (sometimes fatal) lobotomies on thousands of patients. He even made house calls—drive-by lobotomies. What a sweetheart! In Resurrection Kill, he struts his stuff as Dr. Bradley Buscher (a.k.a. Dr. Brain-Butcher).

Last but not least is Dr. Ugo Cerletti (1877 – 1963), nominated for the Nobel Prize for his pioneering development of electroconvulsive therapy, a.k.a. shock treatment. While ECT nowadays can be an effective treatment for disorders that are resistant to other therapies, during the early years it was unregulated, primitive science that often caused brain damage. A major character in Resurrection Kill was the unfortunate recipient of recklessly deployed shock treatment. Here, I gave Dr. Cerletti a taste of his own medicine.

Laura Joh Rowland is the award-winning author of the samurai detective Sano Ichirō mystery series set in seventeenth-century Japan, as well as a historical suspense series starring Charlotte Brontë. Her work has been published in twenty-one countries; nominated for the Anthony Award, the Hammett Prize, and the Barnes & Noble Discover Great New Writers Award; won RT Magazine's Reader's Choice Award; and been included in the Wall Street Journal's list of the five best historical mystery novels. Laura holds a bachelor of science and a master of public health degree from the University of Michigan. She is a former aerospace scientist, a painter, and a cartoonist. She lives in New York City with her husband Marty.

#2175. Kevin Moore on Femme Fatales, Tropes, and Learning from the Women of Noir

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Barbara Stanwyck pauses at the top of the staircase in Double Indemnity. The camera lingers on the anklet around her ankle. In a single glance, both Fred MacMurray and the audience are lost. That may have been the moment I fell in love with noir.

Every generation seems convinced it has reinvented the genre or that it needs to. I don’t believe that’s true. I fell in love with women like Barbara Stanwyck’s Phyllis Dietrichson in Double Indemnity and Kathleen Turner’s Matty Walker in Body Heat. They were intelligent, seductive, dangerous, and unforgettable. They weren’t merely romantic interests waiting to be rescued. They drove the story, dictated the choices of every man who crossed their path, and often determined who survived.

Who could forget Miss Wonderly, the alias of Brigid O’Shaughnessy, an enigmatic, manipulative figure who hires Private Investigator Sam Spade? Spade moves through a world where everyone has an agenda, loyalty is negotiable, and morality lies somewhere between black and white.

It taught me something that has stayed with me as both a reader and a writer: the greatest noir stories are never really about solving the crime. They are about exposing human nature. Both Dashiell Hammett’s novel and John Huston’s film adaptation of The Maltese Falcon made such an impact on me that I used the names of Sydney Greenstreet and Peter Lorre, blending them with their characters’ names, in one of my novels (Kasper Greenstreet and Peter Cairo).

How could you not be fascinated by the femme fatale, one of noir’s greatest creations? Yet she’s often reduced to a stereotype, a beautiful woman who manipulates men and then vanishes into the shadows.

The truly memorable femmes fatales were always more complex. They embodied temptation, certainly, but also intelligence, ambition, survival, and power. They understood the rules of the world they inhabited and refused to apologize for playing the game better than anyone else. They were dangerous because no one fully understood them.

Barbara Stanwyck’s Phyllis Dietrichson wasn’t merely beautiful. She was fearless enough to gamble everything for the life she believed she deserved. Kathleen Turner’s Matty Walker carried that tradition into the modern era. Every smile concealed another layer of calculation. Long after Body Heat ends, she remains the character you remember, always one move ahead.

Then came Chinatown, where noir itself evolved. The greatest threat was no longer the gunman in the alley but the powerful man whose influence reached every corner of society. Corruption no longer hid in the shadows. It occupied the penthouse office or manipulated the City’s water supply.

It also reminded us that Los Angeles has always been noir’s perfect stage, a city where beautiful dreams coexist with darkness and where ambition and illusion walk the same streets. Hollywood sells fantasy to the world, yet just beyond the studio gates, noir quietly reminds us of the price some pay to chase those dreams.

Years later, Sharon Stone’s Catherine Tramell in Basic Instinct reminded audiences why the femme fatale remained one of fiction’s most compelling creations. Brilliant, fearless, and impossible to define, she became less interesting for whether she committed murder than for refusing to let anyone else define her.

I’ve always admired these women, not because they are role models but because they are unforgettable.

When I began writing Waking Sleeping Beauty, I wasn’t interested in replacing the femme fatale. I wanted to ask what she might look like today.

Mia More certainly carries the DNA of the women who inspired her. She is strikingly beautiful, mysterious, and comfortable among wealthy and powerful men. Like classic femme fatales, she understands that being underestimated can be a weapon. But I also wanted to give her something many of noir’s greatest women were rarely allowed: a fully realized emotional life.

Mia is capable of profound love…devastating grief…and may even be capable of murder. To me, those qualities don’t contradict each other. Both truths can coexist in the same person.

One of the relationships closest to my heart in the novel is Mia’s unexpected bond with Kiddy, a teenage hustler. Through him, she rediscovers what she believed she’d lost forever: the ability to love someone more than herself.

That awakening doesn’t erase the darkness within her. It complicates it. The question is no longer whether Mia is innocent or guilty. It’s whether someone capable of terrible things can also be capable of extraordinary love. That is the kind of moral ambiguity that first drew me to noir.

It also shaped the other women in Waking Sleeping Beauty. Detective Joanna Steele owes a debt to the hard-boiled investigators who defined the genre, yet she refuses to surrender her compassion in a city that constantly demands it. Lana DeMille lives on society’s margins, dismissed by almost everyone she meets, yet she sees truths others cannot.

In another era of noir, she might have existed only to be pitied. Instead, she becomes one of the story’s moral centers. Sometimes, those society overlooks see most clearly.

My admiration for noir runs so deep that I even created a fictional noir film for Mia’s Hollywood career, The Year of the Cat. It wasn’t just an Easter egg. It was my quiet nod to classics like Chinatown and L.A. Confidential, proving that Hollywood still has room for noir. I think that’s what every writer hopes to do. We don’t inherit great genres to dismantle them.

We inherit them to continue the conversation.

I still smile when Barbara Stanwyck descends that staircase, anklet in place. Kathleen Turner’s effortless command of every scene in Body Heat. Sharon Stone’s fearless performance in Basic Instinct. Those women remain among noir’s finest.

Mia More stands beside them not because she rejects what they represent, but because she carries their legacy forward. She reminds us that a woman, a femme fatale, can be dangerous without surrendering her humanity, wield power without losing her capacity for compassion, and awaken to love while still holding her own darkness.

Perhaps that’s why noir has endured for nearly a century. The shadows may have shifted. The cities may have changed. The crimes may have evolved. Yet the questions remain the same. Who are we when no one is watching? What happens when we lose our way?

After we’ve lost our way, is it still possible to awaken without losing the mystery, danger, and magic that made noir and the femme fatale unforgettable? I believe it is. Decades later, we still remember Barbara Stanwyck at the top of the staircase, and we’re still following her into the darkness.
