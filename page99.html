Plant a pantry of nutritious berries for winter birds and pretty-up your garden at the same time!

Feeding garden birds in winter is such an easy way to support wildlife, and growing berry-bearing shrubs is a great way to supplement birdseed and fatballs. So many avian species depend on berries to survive winter, so planting a berried shrub to feed birds is a great way to attract feathered visitors to your yard. Waxwings and American robins gather into berry-seeking flocks once temperatures drop and, once insects become scarce, many rare garden visitors such as bluebirds, warblers and woodpeckers can be tempted in with a feast of plump berries.

Not all shrubs with berries are created equal when it comes to winter food, however. Some berries are incredibly high in fat, carbohydrates or anti-oxidants, offering birds highly nutritious, energy-rich fuel to stay warm, and stay alive. Others are the equivalent of avian junk food. It’s good to offer a variety of berries, too: some super-tasty treats to be gobbled up in fall, and some that are too tart to eat before the freeze-thaw of deep winter sweetens them up to provide a late-season larder.

All these shrubs grow energy-dense berries for winter birds, so they’ll bring feathered garden friends flocking. All are well-behaved ornamental plants you’ll welcome in your garden, as they don’t take much looking after but easily earn their yard space with plenty of color. And all are best planted right now in fall so they have time to grow their roots deep into the soil before winter arrives. So which will you choose to feed birds in winter?

Ilex verticillata ‘FarrowBPop’ tops our list of berried shrubs for birds, producing a profusion of red berries rich in nutrients. It’s a dwarf cultivar of a native deciduous holly so it drops its leaves in fall to create a highly visible feast of berries that will act as a magnet for over 40 species of birds including waxwings, robins, bluebirds and northern cardinals. As it’s a compact and grows berries all along its stems, foraging birds like thrushes and sparrows will benefit, too.

Berry Poppins winterberry holly is a great asset to any garden as its berry-packed branches are an eyecatching sight for us humans, too. Because the berries start off firm and tart, it's an enduring display as the fruit only gets eaten up in January or February once it's sweetened up into a late-season lifeline for birds.

Growing to just 3-4 feet high and wide in zones 3-9, this is a neat, disease-resistant shrub that’s easy to grow. There’s one catch, however! Berry Poppins is a female plant, and needs a male plant nearby to produce berries. Mr Poppins winterberry holly, also available from Plant Addicts and a similar size, is the ideal choice of male plant to partner Berry Poppins. While Mr Poppins doesn’t produce berries, it’s a pleasant zero-maintenance foliage shrub and can be planted up to 50 feet away; one male plant will service up to five females, too.

Next on the list of shrubs for winter birds is Viburnum nudum ‘Bulk’, sold as Brandywine. There are so many berry-bearing viburnums to choose from but this cultivar wins hands-down for its abundant high-fat berries and incredible garden beauty.

Green berries start forming in late summer then, in fall, shift into stunning pink and blue shades, often with different-hued fruit in the same cluster. As the days grow darker, so do the berries, turning a glossy purple-black as the surrounding foliage takes on fiery red hues. Brandywine viburnum is another late-winter pantry for birds as the berries only sweeten once the deepest winter weather sets in.

Hardy in zones 5-9, this low-maintenance shrub reaches 5-6 feet high and wide in five or so years, and you will need to let it grow to its natural height as it flowers and fruits on 2-year-old wood. But then you can expect to see waxwings, robins, bluebirds, thrushes and even woodpeckers feasting on its berries.

If you don’t have the garden space for a Brandywine viburnum, then Berry Box Pyracomeles is a smart choice. Staying compact at just 2-3 feet high and wide, this evergreen shrub looks good year-round and while its berries are small, they start out a bright orange in late summer before transitioning to a vibrant red in winter, and the sheer number creates an impressive display. Because of its small size, Berry Box thrives in a container, making it a lovely choice for colorful fall planters that last into winter. Growing this shrub in a pot also means that if you’re a little outside its growing zones of 7-9, you can keep it in a sheltered spot.

Pyracomeles is a cross between Pyracantha and Osteomeles and has inherited the high-nutrient berry and heavy-fruiting genetics of the former and the compact nature of the latter. With fruit persisting on the branches well into winter, Berry Box berries are a valuable food source for robins, waxwings and thrushes, and small birds will find shelter in the shrub's dense evergreen foliage.

Aronia shrubs create a valuable winter resource for birds with dense growth offering shelter as well as long-lasting berries that offer a life-saving pantry when most other food has been gobbled up. Chokeberries have a high level of tannin which gives them a bitter taste but also acts as a preservative, so they last all through fall and early winter. Freezing weather breaks up those tannins, however, so these berries bring an emergency food supply high in antioxidants through the coldest days of late winter.

Chokeberry’s fire-engine-red fall foliage makes these low-maintenance shrubs fabulous plants for the garden, too, and this native shrub is a great substitute for invasive burning bush. And whatever the size of your yard, there’s an Aronia to suit.

If you have the room then Aronia ‘Viking’ should be your go-to as it was originally bred as an orchard crop so has big black berries and lots of them, that will attract American robins, eastern bluebirds, cedar waxwings and even blue jays; it grows to 4-6 feet high and wide in zones 3-8. Turn chokeberries into jam, jelly and syrup to enjoy the health benefits of these anti-oxidant-rich berries yourself, too. Berry Scape Chokeberry, available from Plant Addicts, is more ornamental with pretty bright red berries, growing to 4-5 feet high and wide in zones 4-9. If you’re short on space, then LowScape Mound Chokeberry, available from trees.com, only reaches 1-2 feet high and wide but still produces plenty of purple-black berries that ground-dwelling birds will appreciate.

Another useful emergency supply of late-winter berries is Proud Berry Pink coralberry. Symphoricarpos orbiculatus is a close native relative of the snowberry and, like chokeberry, has berries high in tannins that only become palatable after many freeze-thaw cycles. That’s a bonus for gardeners as well as birds because the large pink berries, that gradually become more vibrant as temperatures drop, are so decorative. Coralberry's arching branches and rounded blue-green leaves are pretty, too.

Ornamental coralberry, also known as Indian currants, grows to 3-4 feet high and wide but because it fruits on new wood, it’s easily kept smaller without sacrificing the berry supply. Hardy in zones 3-7, plant this shrub now for berries next year.

Of course, you need some berries for birds to scoff through fall and early winter, and Callicarpa is the garden-worthy beauty to pick. But don’t just plump for the native American beautyberry. Pearl Glam offers all the same nutritional, high-moisture berries that cardinals, robins and bluebirds love, but has a much tighter, upright habit, reaching 4-5 feet high and 3-4 feet wide in zones 5-8. As well as a profusion of violet-purple berries, Pearl Glam also boasts deep purple foliage (the native beautyberry has regular green leaves).

While Plant Addicts stocks Pearl Glam in a garden-ready #3 container, if you’re on a budget and have a little patience then Lowe’s offers the same Proven Winners shrub in a great-value gallon pot.

Any or all of these shrubs will give your feathered friends a feast and help them survive through winter. They'll attract birds into your yard, too, so be sure to plant them in view of a window. All well as adding winter color to your backyard, these beauties are easily smart and low-maintenance enough to work as front-yard shrubs that add curb appeal, too. They'd also make welcome Christmas gifts for bird-loving friends. And don't forget, it's also perfectly acceptable to gift your garden birds the best-ever present of a berry-bearing shrub!

#2510. I Was Treated for Depression for Years. Nobody Asked About ADHD.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Lifestyle  |  Read Source →

I wish I had been more curious to find deeper answers sooner.

I started taking antidepressants in my late teens. One didn’t seem to change much, so I tried another. I kept asking for help, taking the medications I was prescribed, and waiting for the point when everyday life would stop feeling like a group project I was somehow doing alone.

Some of those medications were incredibly difficult to come off of. And after all that effort, I was still exhausted. Still overwhelmed. Still wondering why things that looked ordinary from the outside took so much out of me.

I thought I was depressed. Anxiety seemed possible, too. Those explanations made sense with the information I had, so I kept trying to get better within them. When treatment didn’t change the parts of life I was struggling with, I didn’t think, Maybe there’s another question to ask. I thought, What the hell is wrong with me?

Then other people in my family were diagnosed with ADHD, and I began looking at my own history differently.

I want to be clear: A family diagnosis didn’t diagnose me. It made me curious. For the first time, I wondered whether what I’d understood as depression had been ADHD all along — or whether ADHD explained the difficulties that treatment had never touched.

The picture of ADHD I’d grown up with was a little boy who couldn’t sit still. I didn’t know to look for a woman who could appear to be holding it together while privately using every last bit of energy to do so.

A finished task looks like a finished task. You can’t see how long it took her to start it. An appointment she remembered looks like an appointment she remembered. You can’t see the reminders. A mom who gets everyone through the day looks like a mom who’s managing. You don’t necessarily see her sitting afterward, wondering why it took absolutely everything she had.

That invisible effort can become your normal. You assume everyone is working this hard behind the scenes. Then you blame yourself for needing so much effort in the first place.

I now host Chaos & Caffeine, an ADHD parenting podcast, and I’ve talked with ADHD specialist Dr. Jennifer Dall about ways ADHD can be missed in women. We discussed masking, burnout, emotional overwhelm, and the maddening experience of being unable to begin a task you genuinely want to do.

If you’ve never experienced that last one, I know how it sounds. Just do the thing. Thank you. Incredible idea. Why didn’t I think of that while spending half the day wanting to do the thing?

From the outside, difficulty getting started can look like laziness. Doing everything at the last minute can look like a bad habit. Being worn out after a day of keeping up can look like someone who needs to manage stress better. Over time, those judgments get inside your head. You stop describing what’s happening and start describing yourself as the problem.

Dr. Dall and I also talked about hormones. Puberty can make ADHD symptoms harder to manage or more noticeable. Later hormonal changes — during pregnancy, postpartum, perimenopause, and menopause — can affect how a woman experiences symptoms or how well her old coping strategies work. That conversation made me think about how young I was when I first sought help, and how much of my history I had never known to bring up.

I’m not saying every hard moment in my life was ADHD. Depression and anxiety are real, and they can exist alongside ADHD. Antidepressants help many people. I can’t go back and declare that everyone who treated me got it wrong.

But I can say this: I kept asking for help because something wasn’t getting better. I wish that had prompted more curiosity about what was still happening in my daily life.

If this sounds painfully familiar, please don’t use my story to diagnose yourself or stop taking medication. Some antidepressants need to be tapered with a prescriber’s help; I know firsthand that coming off certain ones can be hard. Instead, tell a qualified clinician what treatment hasn’t changed. Describe the tasks, the time management, the overwhelm, and the effort it takes to look fine. Talk about what you remember from childhood and whether ADHD runs in your family. Ask if a fuller evaluation makes sense.

You don’t have to walk into an appointment knowing the answer. You’re allowed to walk in saying, “Something still isn’t adding up.”

That’s what I wish someone had told me when I was younger. I wasn’t failing at getting better because I still needed help. I was trying to explain a struggle I didn’t yet have all the words for.

It took diagnoses in my family to make me ask a different question about myself. If another woman has spent years following a treatment plan and still quietly wondering why life feels so damn hard, I hope someone listens when she says it isn’t working. And then I hope they ask what else might be going on.

Danielle Kelly is the host of the ADHD parenting podcast Chaos & Caffeine, which reaches thousands of families each month and was recently named one of the Best Kids With ADHD Podcasts of 2026 by PodRanker.

Up top, the lightweight warp-knit upper provides breathability and comfort, with enough structure to help secure your foot when you pick up the pace. The eye-catching color options add a little more personality to your training miles.

Active recovery may not directly put the ball in the net, but the work you do between matches can help you feel ready for the next one. And the right shoe can make that work more comfortable.

#1232. A New Book Compiles the Collective History of The Studio Museum in Harlem

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Museum director Thelma Golden and editor Romi Crawford brought together oral histories, essays, and archival material to tell the story of the museum’s past six decades

In 1968, as the civil rights movement gripped the nation, The Studio Museum in Harlem began forging its own history, operating out of a small loft above a liquor store. The modest space would go on to become a sanctuary for Black artists, a space in New York City to correct the exclusion of artists of African descent across the city’s storied institutions. Over 58 years, the world changed drastically, and so did the museum, often adapting to find the best way to support Black artists, whether in local New York circles or on the global art stage.

“While the Museum has always been driven by its mission, it has maintained a willingness, an impulse even, to constantly transform and reimagine itself as its ambitions have grown, as a living institution that reflects the concerns of artists working today,” Thelma Golden, the museum’s director, tells Harper’s Bazaar.

The Studio Museum in Harlem now occupies seven stories on West 125th Street, a recent visionary expansion built to anchor its next generation of growth. The 82,000-square-foot building, designed by Adjaye Associates with Cooper Robertson as executive architect, opened in 2025 after seven years of construction, becoming the first space specifically tailored to carry out the museum’s mission after its humble loft and long-time residence in a historic bank. Its inaugural show, “From Now: A Collection in Context,” drew on the museum’s illustrious archives and featured about 200 works, offering a snapshot of the countless artistic voices the museum has championed for six decades. This same spirit is echoed in Studio Museum in Harlem: A History, a new book that traces the institution’s history through firsthand oral histories, archival photographs and documents, and critical essays, releasing on October 27.

“It only felt appropriate that we mark our momentous new building with the first-ever record of the people, ideas, events, dialogues, and, of course, artworks that have enabled and defined this Museum’s existence,” Golden says. “This chronicle tells a story of an institution that has navigated decades of complex social, political, and cultural paradigm shifts, and is a testament to the Museum’s predisposition to reenvision, reinvent, and transform itself alongside those in its orbit.”

Studio Museum in Harlem: A History is divided into five sections, meticulously edited by Chicago-based professor Romi Crawford. Section one compiles five essays that overview its influence and history, followed by firsthand accounts from long-time trustees and community members in section two. The third section comprises rare archival material, including letters and artwork. In the fourth section, Crawford curates essays from various art-world figures, including former Studio Museum curator Christine Y. Kim and photographer Deborah Willis. It is rounded out by a comprehensive list of exhibitions and prize winners. With this compilation, Golden and Crawford aim to capture the museum’s multi-vocal spirit.

The museum’s six-decade history is rooted in collective action. In 1968, a small group of activists and philanthropists, including politician Carter Burden, psychotherapist Frank Donnelly, the museum’s inaugural director Charles E. Inniss, and artist Mahler B. Ryder, came together to carve out space for Black artists. This founding cohort first envisioned the museum as a creative nexus, a place to champion Black artists and a space for these artists to create new work.

“It was a history that demanded multiple takes,” Crawford explains. “Thelma and I both didn’t think that it was a book that should have a single author. I definitely didn’t want to be an authoritative voice…. There are a lot of institutions that have a founder. This museum did not have that…It’s a history that really defies a single author because there was not a single founder of the museum, and its origins are complex and fascinating for that reason.”

One way the book frames this history is through Bridget R. Cook’s essay, titled “Black Galactic,” which traces the last six decades through the museum’s exhibitions. It begins with Tom Lloyd’s exhibition “Electronic Refractions II,” which featured light-based sculptures, then moves through shows by artists like Melvin Edwards (1978), Sam Gilliam (1982), Jack Whitten (1983–84), and Kara Walker (2003), among many others. This essay contextualizes the museum’s profound influence on Black art in the United States. It also details, perhaps above all, the breadth of the museum’s community.

Even as the museum evolved as an institution and built a permanent collection, it retained the word “Studio” in its name as a nod to its founding principle: nurturing the work of living artists. It directly alludes to the Artist-in-Residence program, which William T. Williams founded in 1969. In archival material featured in the book, Williams writes, “It is the aim of the Artists In Residence program to help crystallize this community as well as teach young prospective artists to recognize their talent and to use it in a constructive manner.” Since then, the program has supported artists from Willie Cole and Candace Hill-Montgomery to Kerry James Marshall and Mickalene Thomas.

A distinguished roster of curators stands behind these exhibitions. By prioritizing collective history, Crawford and Golden sought to champion not only Black artists and the museum’s support for them, but the wider network of Black creatives orbiting the museum. “What the Studio Museum has done is spawn this extraordinary opening for Black artists, and not only Black artists, but Black curators, Black writers, Black historians,” the late Gordon J. Davis, a Trustee, writes in his featured essay. This network is the ultimate result of the founder’s mission, whose aspirations, as Crawford says, meant to “embolden a community that did not yet exist in 1968 in America.” One can safely say that community exists now.

“It is a community of makers, curators, scholars, leaders, artists,” Crawford says. “That’s one of the other reasons the book really demanded a community or collective approach, a chorus.”

Studio Museum in Harlem: A History details the institution’s six decades as a cultural touchstone for Black art and an incubator for artists and the network that surrounds them. Crawford ensures its unique conception and trailblazing legacy are reflected in a mosaic-style compilation of letters and voices from those who have been touched by, or have touched, the museum.

“[The] collective spirit has served as a blueprint for the Museum’s fifty-eight years in operation and informs our approach to space-making today,” Golden says. “What readers will find is that our mission’s essence has remained the same: that the Studio Museum in Harlem is unequivocal in its necessity as a metaphorical home for artists of African descent and their practices.”

#2810. Does This Advanced Method of Scent Control Actually Work for Deer Hunting? Here’s What 5 Independent Testers Had to Say

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 26, 2026 1:06 PM EDT

Get the hottest outdoor news from Outdoor Life.

Many of the deer hunting strategies we rely on aren’t scientifically proven. For the most part, we go with the gear and tactics that have worked in the field, not necessarily in research studies. The most controversial example of this is probably scent control. Numerous products claim to minimize human scent, and some of them are gimmicks. In this world of scent-control, the most technologically-advanced strategy — as well as one of the most dubious — is to use ozone.

Ozonics is the company that first used ozone to minimize hunter’s scent in 2007. In the decades to follow, the company has sold hundreds of thousands of units, says Ozonics president Buddy Piland. The current topline Ozonics unit costs $456 (on sale), which is serious cash for most deer hunters. Piland admits that marketing Ozonics so it’s perceived as a legitimate product (and not a gimmick) has been a challenge over the years.

“I’m selling you an invisible molecule,” he says. “I’m saying ‘Hey, there’s something coming out of this box that will keep deer from smelling you.’”

So is the price of Ozonics worth it? Do these units actually work as advertised?

You can search around on the internet and find testimonials from a variety of deer hunting personalities promising that Ozonics is a “game changer.” But you can also find testimonials from hunting personalities and influencers promising that almost any gear item out there works, no matter how frivolous it might be.

Cynical deer hunters don’t believe the hype, and they might not even believe the anecdotes of outdoor writers and editors. As hard as we searched, we couldn’t find any scientific, peer-reviewed research on the effectiveness of using ozone to kill human scent in the field.

So we devised a totally anecdotal, but also totally unbiased, experiment of our own. We gave new Ozonics units to five diehard deer hunters who have no sponsorships, TV shows, or big social media followings, and had them test the units throughout their 2023 deer season.

They are all experienced and skilled hunters who have killed big bucks over the years, but they’re also regular dudes. Most of them work blue-collar jobs when they’re not hunting. They have no problem calling out bullshit. Most importantly, they spend a lot of time in the woods. Altogether, they had more than 400 deer encounters while running Ozonics units.

We sent each hunter a survey at the end of their season. We did not pay them for participating in the survey (and neither did Ozonics).

But before we can decide if Ozonics works, or doesn’t work, we need to understand ozone. As Piland puts it, ozone is a molecule made of three connected oxygen atoms (O3), which is unstable and wants to revert to its natural state. Ozone attaches to bacteria or scent molecules and oxidizes them. Ozone is used in a variety of industries (you can read all about it here) but the takeaway for deer hunters is that the ozone being created by an Ozonics unit is supposed to attach to our scent molecules and neutralize them, thus preventing deer from smelling us.

There are a variety of ozone-producing devices on the market that can be used to treat clothing (usually in a bag), but Ozonics is the only company that produces a device suitable for use in the field.

Explained very plainly, an Ozonics unit creates ozone by sending high-voltage electricity through a coil and then blowing air across that coil. Over the years, the company has experimented with different levels of ozone generation and modern units now have selectable levels of ozone to match a variety of field conditions.

Piland says the best way to visualize how ozone and scent work is to imagine a stream with rocks and eddies that disrupt and change the flow of water. That’s a lot like how wind works as it moves across the landscape. Now imagine pouring red dye (your scent) into the stream. It won’t flow consistently downstream. In some places it will move faster, in others it will stop and pool. Lastly, imagine dropping purple dye (the ozone) into the stream on top of the red dye. In some places the purple will completely blanket over the red (effectively destroying your scent). In others, the red and purple will blend to make a slightly different color (altering your scent).

“[With ozone] sometimes you’ll see deer and they’ll definitely smell something, but they don’t panic and run away,” Piland says. “It’s more like, ‘Oh, I’ve never smelled that before.’”

And finally, in some places the red dye and the purple dye will never overlap. The same thing can happen with your scent in the wind. The faster the flow of the stream and the more eddies it has, the more purple dye you’ll need in order to obscure or change the red. In the field, this means more wind and more unstable wind means you need more ozone to neutralize your scent.

“So what you need to do [while hunting in higher winds] is figure out how to best dump your ozone … by adjusting the angle of the unit and the distance it is from you,” Piland says.

All our hunters reported that Ozonics either had a “very noticeable positive impact” on their hunt or “some noticeable positive impact” on their hunting. All of them said that they would continue to use Ozonics in the future.

Combined, they had approximately 435 deer encounters and were clearly busted by deer a total of 24 times. That’s a rate of only 5.5 percent, with many of the encounters occurring downwind. Some hunters had zero deer bust them throughout the entire season. Their anecdotes from the field provide examples of how the units performed.

“A good example of the Ozonics helping was an evening hunt in late October. Four doe and fawns came in upwind. A mature buck followed them in about 15 minutes later and started pushing them a little. Three of the does eventually got downwind of me. They definitely noticed a smell they didn’t like, but never were able to get enough scent to spook. They spent 10 to 15 minutes walking around with their noses in the air trying to smell me. Meanwhile the buck spent close to 10 minutes within 20 yards of me making a scrape and just watching the does. I decided not to shoot the buck, because he had a smaller rack, but there is no doubt in my mind that I don’t get that opportunity without the Ozonics.”

“Yes, even when trying to play the wind, deer still often manage to get downwind at times.  I have noticed that with the unit on, their reaction is much less of an issue when they do get downwind. My experience has been that deer either walk right through my scent cone or if they do stop and try to wind me, they are unsure of what they are smelling and eventually continue past without snorting or blowing. The unit is not foolproof but it certainly helps, based on my experiences.”

“We had a questionable to flat-out-wrong wind a few times this season while sitting over food plots. Normally I would hunt something else or stay home in this situation, but I was curious about how well the system would work in this scenario. In one hunt, the deer entered the field, and they fed into my downwind side. The younger deer had zero idea that we were there. An older doe picked up enough to know that something was off, but she couldn’t seem to pinpoint our location or gather enough scent to sound the alarms. She moved about 15 yards, slightly alerted, but was calm and feeding again within a minute.

I was very skeptical before using the system. It’s impossible to completely fool a deer’s nose. However, hunting from the ground and having deer downwind of me on multiple occasions, and not having them blow out of the field in most cases was a bit mind boggling to me. The deer that would detect something seemed to be confused about what, or where, I was. And that definitely bought me more time to make a shot. I killed a nice buck in the bluffs of Minnesota where my wind was swirling fairly bad. He came to 20 yards and didn’t have a clue I was there. I’m fairly certain that scent was pushing up into the ravine at the time he came in, as I could feel the wind shift onto my neck blowing into his direction. Judging from my encounters with hundreds of deer and countless ‘scent destroying’ products, Ozonics was the only one that I believe to work as they advertise, in most cases.”

“I had many encounters with deer coming in downwind, with zero instances resulting in any deer blowing. I did, however, definitely have some encounters where I do believe the deer smelled something — whether it was the ozone or a hint of me.”

“I had deer walk to 10 yards downwind. They knew something was up but never busted.”

The hunters in our survey had a clear-eyed perspective on Ozonics. They all felt that it worked, but no one said that it was a silver bullet for fooling deer. They also agreed it was not a perfect unit — most agreed that it would be better if the unit was quieter. It didn’t spook deer, but it made it harder to hear deer approaching. It’s a bit of a hassle to set up. Plus, there are minor health concerns involved with using ozone. You should make sure to educate yourself on those issues if you’re considering springing for a unit.

But the numbers don’t lie. The hunters in our survey encountered a ton of deer and spooked very few. They all attributed at least some of that success to the Ozonics units working effectively. That might not count as scientific data, but in the mysterious world of scent control, it’s as good of a field test as you’re going to find.

#1287. The Portaluppi Bathroom is the New “It” Loo

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The dominance of the gray-and-white “spa bathroom” may be waning. You know the ones—spare, mostly white, flooded with light, and sheathed in acres of Calacatta Viola marble. The new look involves dramatically veined stone in dark green or dusky red, sculptural Art Deco–style fixtures, and rich woodwork. Think: the bathrooms of AD cover stars like interior designer Athena Calderone and supermodel Paloma Elsesser. The reference point is 1930s Milan, where bold material combinations and precise geometry turned these utilitarian spaces into showpieces.

Few designers are as identified with this look as Piero Portaluppi (1888–1967), the Rationalist architect behind the iconic Villa Necchi Campiglio and Casa Corbellini-Wassermann. While his dramatic entry halls and sweeping stairs have inspired designers for decades, it’s the rooms with plumbing that are currently having a moment.

The Inspiration: Piero Portaluppi's iconic bathroom at Villa Necchi Campiglio, built between 1932 and 1935

At Villa Necchi, mirrored walls multiply the swirling patterns of Arabescato Orobico marble, while at nearby Casa Corbellini-Wassermann, Portaluppi set vibrant green Verde Challant marble against graphic black stonework. “He frequently used contrasting materials to frame hallways, thresholds, and architectural elements, allowing these moments of juxtaposition to draw the eye and bring a sense of ceremony to the space,” explains Calderone, who visited Villa Necchi with architect Danielle Siggerud while reimagining her own Tribeca apartment.

Gregory Rockwell’s design for model Paloma Elsesser’s bathroom

AD100 designer Gregory Rockwell says he is drawn to Portaluppi's unconventional palette. “What I love about Portaluppi is how controlled everything is architecturally, but then he’ll do something extravagant with the marble or an unexpected color,” the designer says. For the primary bathroom of Elsesser’s Brooklyn town house, Rockwell and colleague Hester Hodde layered burgundy Rosso Francia marble, walnut-and-mirror paneling, and pistachio-green walls. “It’s a slightly strange combination when you think about it, but I love the colors together,” he says.

Sherle Wagner Pagoda Stone Lever Basin set

For Calderone, the magic is in the stonework. “There was such ingenuity in the way Portaluppi clad entire bathrooms in stone, often using smaller, individually composed pieces,” she says. For her primary bathroom, she edged sweeping expanses of pale stone with intricate inlaid tile borders and added Art Deco–inflected fixtures and geometric mirrors. Looking back at Portaluppi’s examples, she observes: “It feels almost at odds with how we approach stone today, where expansive, perfectly bookmatched slabs have become the prevailing ideal. He embraced the irregularity and variation in the veining.” That appreciation for the individuality of natural stone is also on display in her own powder room, which features a pedestal sink in deeply veined Verde Alpi marble.

Athena Calderone’s powder room includes a pedestal sink in deeply veined green marble designed by Calderone in collaboration with ABC Stone, sconces by Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann, and a 1940s Italian mirror.

This powder room designed by Giampiero Tagliaferri is enveloped in rosewood and features a sink of Verde Alpi marble, Pia Guidetti Crippa sconce and a Maurizio Donzelli painting.

Another sink in the same deep-green stone can be found in the powder room of a Milan pied-à-terre Giampiero Tagliaferri recently designed for a young art collector. “The custom pedestal sink takes its bold, geometric form from Rationalist architecture, but we kept the lines clean and the layout modern,” Tagliaferri says. As the designer explains, revisiting 1930s Milan isn’t about exactly replicating a Portaluppi bathroom. “Reinterpretation is about extracting a period’s core DNA, not literal replication.”

Another view of the malachite-wrapped San Francisco bathroom designed by Summer Thornton,

#548. We May Be Living Through a Sixth Mass Extinction—but Scientists Think Some of the First Five Never Happened

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

New AI-powered research questions whether a majority of Earth’s mass extinctions were as abrupt as previously believed.

If the story of life on Earth was divided into chapter titles, the planet’s five known “mass extinctions” would no doubt feature prominently. Stretching from the end-Ordovician (or Ordovician-Silurian) extinction 440 million years ago all the way to the end-Cretaceous extinction that forced all land-based dinosaurs to exit stage left, these dramatic, page-turning periods of biological upheaval each functionally wiped the slate clean for a new era of a life to take hold.

And while we usually think of these extinction events as abrupt changes—things certainly don’t get much more abrupt than an asteroid smacking the Yucatán at roughly 16 miles per second—new research presented at the Geological Society of America’s meeting in Denver suggests that a majority of these “mass extinctions” never happened, or at least not as we perceive them. To arrive at this bold conclusion, Shuzhong Shen at Nanjing University in China used an AI model called OneStrata (a reference to stratigraphy, or the study of rock layers) trained on fossil data to bring our fuzzy understanding of these past events into sharper focus.

“The canonical ‘Big Five’ mass extinctions have long been regarded as the principal drivers of Phanerozoic marine biodiversity history. This paradigm, however, was built upon taxonomic range compilations and stage-level time bins that cannot resolve ecological dynamics operating on timescales of thousands to tens of thousands of years,” the authors write. “Here we present OneStrata, a transformer-based AI foundation model that learns a shared probabilistic representation of geological time directly from fragmented stratigraphic observations.”

As Nature explains, OneStrata does this in much the same way that a large language model trains on texts to predict the next word in a sentence. But instead of texts, OneStrata relies on a huge dataset of fossil information called OneStratigraphy. Typically, stratigraphers group fossil specimens into time spans of millions of years. While this effectively blurs the overall timeline, especially at shorter intervals, it allows researchers to work with the large body of information more easily.

For years, Shen and his colleagues have leveraged groundbreaking technologies in an attempt to improve the resolution of these fossil timespans from millions of years to just tens of thousands of years. In 2020, for example, Shen and a team of Chinese scientists used a supercomputer to approach a resolution of roughly 26,000 years. Their dataset included 11,000 marine fossils from more than 3,000 stratigraphic sections, creating a new diversity curve that stretched from the Cambrian to the Triassic—roughly 300 million years.

The 2020 study showed that the Late Devonian mass extinction, around 360 million years ago, unfolded gradually, with biodiversity declining over roughly 50 million years. By comparison, most life vanished during the End-Permian mass extinction in just 63,000 years.

This new study uses OneStrata to predict where millions of fossils—from tens of thousands of different sites—will fall in the record, producing a resolution of merely 1,000 years. The analysis indicates that three of Earth’s five mass extinctions—the Late Devonian, end‑Triassic, and end‑Cretaceous extinctions—actually occurred over millions of years. However, Shen notes that this is a biodiversity curve, and it’s possible that species were actively going extinct while others were quickly taking their place.

Even the definition of a “mass” extinction is subject to debate. As New Scientist notes, estimates of the number of mass extinctions range from three to as many as 20. Many experts believe we’re currently living through a sixth mass extinction due to anthropogenic climate change.

Millions of years in the future, maybe even the devastation of today will be little more than an indecipherable, blurry chapter in Earth’s ongoing stratigraphic story.

#4266. Github competitor Entire is live now – how the agent-first platform works

Now that agents are writing so much code, Entire is trying to reinvent the coding platform itself.

AI has sped up the pace of coding, putting new demands on infrastructure and changing how developers work. Entire, a developer platform from former GitHub CEO Thomas Dohmke, aims to be the solution to those changes.

Also: ServiceNow and Nvidia launch Arc, a new AI agent for enterprise

The platform, available for use today, is built on Entire’s decentralized Git network, announced in July, which is designed to withstand the sharp increase in AI coding activity that has caused mounting GitHub outages.

Entire aims to be “a GitHub alternative for the speed that teams require to merge changes as fast as coding agents create them,” the company said in its announcement. The new git forge aims to make human-agent coding work more seamless by redesigning several features developers have become accustomed to. Here’s how it works.

The first change Entire makes to the standard coding experience is that it replaces pull requests with what it calls Trails.

“Unlike the classic Pull Request model of reviewing code changes with a diff, Trails offer developers a heads-up display similar to an airline pilot, with customized evals that are scored by agents on every code push,” the company said.

Also: Gartner’s 2027 tech trends just dropped – and they go beyond AI

“All data is available not only on Entire’s web interface, but also through the open-source Entire CLI. This allows coding agents to address findings in a loop, while the human developer validates the progress. When all evals are satisfied, they can merge the code without needing to look at the code itself.”

Entire said that the agents running this process are customizable, and each trail shows how agents are spending tokens.

“With Trails, Entire treats the idea, the agent trace, and the outcome of a developer’s sessions as first-class artifacts for review alongside the code,” Dohmke said. “It uses multiple agents to generate evals that verify the quality and success of AI-generated code to let developers confidently merge without reviewing every line in the diff.”

The company also claims it offers Git hosting “up to 89x faster than GitHub” and six times faster than Cursor origin, thanks to that aforementioned decentralized network. The platform lets developers create, migrate, and host private repositories in multiple locations across the world, currently starting with the US, India, Australia, and the EU.

Another differentiating factor is Continuous Memory, which keeps developer session logs next to code in the repo as a data source. Agents can use them to search for answers, which, Entire said, helps them use up to 71% fewer tokens. This is similar to other memory features in AI chatbots that reduce time spent gathering context by using past conversations or other agent interactions, where provided, to speed query response time. Entire said Continuous Memory is made up of a codebase graph and an “agentic brain” that uses previous sessions and prompts as lessons so agents don’t repeat mistakes.

Entire is available to try today for all developers.
