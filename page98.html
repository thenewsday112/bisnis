The James Webb Space Telescope captured a spectacular star-forming region glowing 161,000 light-years from Earth in a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way.

The NASA/ESA James Webb Space Telescope has captured a breathtaking view of a majestic star-forming region shining 161,000 light-years from Earth in a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way known as the Large Magellanic Cloud.

The deep-space image reveals a 230-light-year-wide swathe of the glowing emission nebula N159, threaded with denser filaments of reddish molecular hydrogen that hide countless energetic young stars.

Ghostly blue clouds of ionized hydrogen gas (H II) glow in the center of the image, having been energized by the ultraviolet radiation pouring out from nearby stars, whose winds helped create the chaotic structure of the vast stellar nursery.

The ethereal pockets are surrounded by roiling orange clouds of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons — a type of organic molecule that may account for 30% of all carbon in the universe, according to the U.S. Department of Energy.

N159 is estimated to be less than 3 million years old, making it one of the youngest star-forming regions in the Large Magellanic Cloud. It also has a markedly different composition than the stellar nurseries that spawn stars in our Milky Way, boasting a lower metal content that could mirror environments that existed in the early universe.

By pointing JWST at N159, astronomers open a window to the distant past. They can compare the characteristics of stars at varying stages of evolution, including pre-main-sequence stars that are still in the process of forming, or accreting from the vast interstellar cloud.

#4250. Opening the Box: Can Machine Learning Resolve Schrödinger’s Cat?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Publish AI, ML & data-science insights to a global community of data professionals.

Neural networks won’t settle the measurement problem — but they are quietly changing how we see, predict and protect quantum superpositions.

In 1935 Erwin Schrödinger came up with what he meant as a joke. Put a cat in a sealed box with a radioactive atom, a Geiger counter and a vial of poison. If the atom decays, the vial breaks and the cat dies. Quantum mechanics says that until someone looks, the atom is in a superposition of decayed and not decayed, so the cat is somehow alive and dead at once.

Ninety years later, physicists still argue about what “looking” actually does. Meanwhile machine learning has spread into nearly every corner of science. So the question for this article is a natural one: can ML solve Schrödinger’s cat?

The honest answer comes in two parts. ML can’t settle the interpretational puzzle, because that is a question about what reality is, and no curve-fitting can answer it. ML can, however, attack the operational version: working out the state inside the box, predicting when the superposition collapses, and stopping that collapse for longer. Those are the problems that decide whether quantum computers work.

The cat thought experiment really packs three separate problems together:

The measurement problem. Why does a smooth, deterministic wavefunction turn into one definite outcome?

The quantum-to-classical transition. Why do we never see cat-sized superpositions in everyday life?

The state-estimation problem. If you can only take a limited number of destructive measurements, how do you reconstruct what the state was?

Problem 1 belongs to philosophy, and it splits into Copenhagen, Many-Worlds, Bohmian mechanics, objective-collapse theories and others. Problems 2 and 3 are empirical, and that is where data comes in.

Key reframing: Decoherence theory says the cat is not “mysteriously both” for long. Its trillions of atoms become entangled with the environment within about 10−20 seconds. The superposition does not disappear, it leaks into the surroundings. That leakage can be modeled, and anything that can be modeled can be learned.

Describing a quantum state of n qubits takes 2n complex amplitudes. At 50 qubits that is about 1015 numbers, more than any laptop can hold. Standard tomography also needs exponentially many measurements. Without help, you can’t check what is inside a large box.

In 2018, Torlai, Carleo and colleagues showed in Nature Physics that a Restricted Boltzmann Machine can learn a compact representation of a many-body wavefunction from a manageable number of measurement snapshots. The network becomes a neural quantum state (NQS): you feed in a configuration and it returns the amplitude.

Seen as a data science problem, this is ordinary generative modeling. Each measurement is a sample from the distribution |ψ(s)|². We fit a model pθ(s) by maximum likelihood, the same way you would fit a VAE (Variational Autoencoder) to images. Measuring in several bases recovers the phases as well.

One detail is worth stating. The test system physicists use is literally called a GHZ “cat state”: (|000…0⟩ + |111…1⟩)/√2, where every qubit is “all alive” or “all dead” together. A well-trained model learns that its samples cluster at exactly those two extremes. It finds the cat’s two-faced nature in the data.

Real superpositions don’t last forever. They decay as the system interacts with stray photons, phonons and magnetic noise. The rate of that decay, the coherence time T₂, sets how long a qubit is useful, and it changes from moment to moment depending on conditions that are hard to model from first principles.

This is where ML does well. Groups at Google Quantum AI, IBM and several universities have trained models on calibration logs (temperatures, drive amplitudes, crosstalk, time since last recalibration) to:

Forecast T₁/T₂ drift with time-series models such as LSTMs (Long Short-Term Memory networks) and temporal convolutional networks, so a circuit can be scheduled before the qubit degrades.

Learn the noise spectrum directly from data with Gaussian processes, which gives the error bars physicists insist on.

Find non-Markovian memory effects that the standard Lindblad master equation misses.

In cat terms, we can’t yet say why the box picks “dead”, but we are getting very good at predicting when it will stop being undecided.

If decoherence is the enemy, the obvious next step is to fight it. In quantum feedback control, an agent continuously and weakly measures the system and applies corrective pulses to keep it in the target superposition.

Fösel, Tighineanu, Weiss and Marquardt (Physical Review X, 2018) showed that deep RL (Reinforcement Learning) agents can find quantum error-correction strategies on their own, without being told what a stabilizer code is. Later experiments on superconducting hardware used RL to stabilize bosonic “cat qubits”, which are superpositions of two coherent states of light inside a microwave cavity and are the most literal laboratory version of Schrödinger’s cat. Companies such as Alice & Bob and AWS are building fault-tolerant processors on these cat qubits.

We are training algorithms to be the most attentive cat-sitters in history: watching just enough not to wake the cat, and stepping in just in time to stop it from settling.

A few researchers have tried “interpretation-agnostic” learning: give a model raw experimental data and see whether the theory it learns looks more like collapse or more like branching. The 2020 SciNet work by Iten, Metger and colleagues showed that a neural network can rediscover physical concepts, such as the heliocentric model, from observations alone.

But every mainstream interpretation of quantum mechanics predicts the same measurement statistics. A model trained on outcomes has, by construction, nothing to separate them. The exceptions are objective-collapse models such as GRW and Diósi–Penrose, which do predict tiny deviations: spontaneous heating, or a loss of interference in massive objects.

Here ML has a real job. Anomaly detection on large interferometry and levitated-nanoparticle datasets can look for those faint signatures, the same way particle physicists use ML to pull new-physics signals out of LHC background. If collapse is a physical process, ML may be what finds it first.

Quantum state tomography is density estimation. If you have trained an autoregressive language model, you already know most of the method.

Decoherence forecasting is time-series regression with physics priors. Physics-informed neural networks and Gaussian processes work very well here.

Quantum control is a partially observable RL problem in which every observation disturbs the environment.

Be wary of the word “solve.” A model that fits the data perfectly can still say nothing about the question you actually care about.

Schrödinger meant his cat as a reductio ad absurdum. Today it is an engineering target: labs build cat states on purpose, and ML helps them see, predict and protect those states. Machine learning hasn’t told us whether the cat is alive or dead before we look. What it has done is make us much better at looking without killing the cat, and that is what quantum technology depends on.

The box is still closed, but we can now read a great deal through the cardboard.

#1973. The Supreme Court as agent of the people

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

In my first two entries for this column, I focused on the president’s removal power and Congress’ power to make law. This third entry focuses on our third branch of government and explains why the Supreme and inferior federal courts are properly conceptualized as faithful agents of We the People – not faithful agents of Congress. Recognizing the distinction helps explain why the Supreme Court can, and should, exercise the power of judicial review – something which, while an acknowledged part of the Supreme Court’s power for well over two centuries, has recently come under question.

Although many schools of interpretation can embrace the idea that federal courts are faithful agents of We the People, that conception of the judicial role has become a prominent feature of modern textualism. In 2017, then-Judge Amy Coney Barrett explained that “[t]he textualist commitment” to interpreting laws according to the perspective of an ordinary reader “might be explained by a … conception of faithful agency … that understands courts to be the faithful agents of the people rather than of Congress.” For support, she noted that Justice Antonin Scalia “did not think that a commitment to legislative supremacy casts courts in the role of Congress’s agents,” as he “characterized courts as agents of the people rather than agents of Congress.”

More recently, in an opinion joined by Justice Samuel Alito, Justice Neil Gorsuch defended the major questions doctrine – a canon of interpretation that requires administrative agencies to identify extra clear statutory authority before regulating in economically or politically “major” ways – by quoting then-Professor Barrett for the proposition that “clear-statement rules help courts ‘act as faithful agents of the Constitution.’”

As an initial terminological matter, there is not currently a meaningful difference between conceptualizing courts as faithful agents of the People and conceptualizing courts as faithful agents of the People’s Constitution. The Constitution’s Preamble describes the Constitution as being enacted on behalf of We the People. So under our present constitutional order, acting as a faithful agent of the People’s Constitution is a means of acting as a faithful agent of the People.

Although that conception of the judicial role has been widely held, it is nonetheless mistaken for at least three reasons.

First, the three federal branches are coequal; none is a principal or agent of the others. Second, and as I recently explained in this column, the Constitution does not empower Congress to ordinarily make law alone. Instead, Congress makes law through a multi-branch lawmaking process that includes the president. A federal jurist who claims to be a faithful agent of Congress therefore approaches statutory interpretation from a starting posture that mistakenly favors one of the two political branches that the People’s Constitution tasks with collectively making law.

The third reason why it is mistaken to conceptualize courts as faithful agents of Congress is that Congress can sometimes work with the president to make unconstitutional statutes. In those situations, federal judges who conceptualize themselves as faithful agents of Congress would seem to be required to dutifully give legal effect to Congress’ unconstitutional statutes. But that is not what courts should do if they wish to respect the Constitution’s limits. Instead, when courts are faced with constitutionally defective statutes, courts should exercise judicial review and demonstrate that the courts are themselves constitutionally prohibited from treating unconstitutional statutes as valid law. By doing so, courts demonstrate that they are faithful agents to the People’s Constitution, not Congress.

Federal courts’ power of judicial review is no doubt familiar to most lawyers, given Chief Justice Marshall’s 1803 decision in Marbury v. Madison. Less than 15 years after the ratification of the Constitution that vested “judicial Power” in the Supreme and inferior federal courts, Marshall explained that “[i]t is emphatically the province and duty of the Judicial Department to say what the law is,” and that if “the Constitution is superior to any ordinary act of the Legislature, the Constitution, and not such ordinary act, must govern the case to which they both apply.”

The familiar form of judicial review reflected in Marbury is consistent with the idea that federal courts are faithful agents of the People. Nevertheless, in an important new book, titled “Supremacy: How Rule by the Court Replaced Government by the People,” law professors Nikolas Bowie and Daphna Renan challenge the Supreme Court’s ability to review the constitutionality of federal legislation. The book offers an important argument against judicial review of federal statutes. And that argument would no doubt be of interest to constitutional framers working on a clean slate. But when it comes to working within the constitutional system already established on behalf of We the People, the book’s argument against judicial review is problematic.

The core of the problem with Bowie’s and Renan’s argument against judicial review of federal statutes is their suggestion that Congress has seemingly unlimited discretion to define its own constitutional limits. Bowie and Renan highlight, for example, the claim that “the only power the Constitution gives judges is ‘the judicial Power,’ which is a term that historically referred to British judges’ power to enforce, not second-guess, laws passed by Parliament,” and they contend that “[t]he most straightforward method of overcoming judicial supremacy would be for Congress to enact a statute that prohibits courts from enjoining or declining to enforce a federal law on constitutional grounds.” But as I have noted in earlier scholarship, such arguments risk conflating the “distinction between a supreme English Parliament and a legally constrained, coequal Congress.”

As the English constitutional theorist A.V. Dicey explained, “[t]he principle of Parliamentary sovereignty means” that “no person or body is recognised by the law of England as having a right to override or set aside the legislation of Parliament.” To recognize in Congress a power to statutorily prohibit the courts from declining to enforce a federal law on constitutional grounds would be to recognize in Congress a power that is not meaningfully distinguishable from the aspect of sovereignty that Dicey ascribes to Parliament. But as Justice Clarence Thomas has explained, “one of the fundamental differences between our Government and the British Government” is that although “Parliament was supreme[,] Congress is not.”

To be clear, conceptualizing federal courts as faithful agents of the People would not require accepting a form of judicial supremacy – or the idea that, even outside of the bounds of federal courts’ jurisdiction, every judicial opinion is binding law on the other two branches. To the contrary, the Constitution’s supremacy clause encodes a form of legislative supremacy into our constitutional order; so long as federal statutes are “made in Pursuance” of the Constitution, those statutes qualify as the “supreme Law of the Land” and thus can legitimately constrain executive and judicial decisionmaking.

But to conclude the Constitution establishes a form of legislative supremacy is distinct from concluding that the Constitution establishes a form of congressional supremacy. Unlike the sovereign Parliament, which Dicey described as having the “right to make or unmake any law whatever,” the non-sovereign Congress cannot enact whatever legislation it wants. Rather, Congress is constrained by the People’s Constitution. The supremacy clause illustrates this by, as mentioned, only treating some statutes – namely, those “made in Pursuance” to the Constitution – as the “supreme Law of the Land.” Courts thus act as proper agents of the People when they distinguish between statutes made in pursuance to the Constitution, which courts must treat as supreme law, and statutes that violate the Constitution, which the courts should set aside for purposes of resolving the case at hand.

One might nonetheless contend that judicial review of federal legislation is somehow inappropriate on democratic grounds. One version of that objection might contend that courts should act as agents of the democratically elected Congress – meaning that courts should enforce whatever statutes survive the democratic lawmaking process, even if a court concludes that the statutes violate the Constitution. Bowie and Renan, for example, argue for “democratic constitutionalism,” which they say means “a Constitution whose questions are decided not by the Court but by the people’s elected representatives through federal statutes.”

That type of argument has at least two key weaknesses.

First, federal judges are themselves selected and supervised through democratic means. To wit, federal judges are selected by a politically accountable president and confirmed by a politically accountable Senate. Federal judges may be impeached through the political impeachment process. Federal judges’ statutory rulings can largely be overruled through the democratic process with new legislation. And federal judges’ constitutional rulings can be overruled through the constitutional amendment process.

Second, and more fundamentally, the fact that federal judges do not stand for election is a constitutional feature, not a bug. In establishing the federal government, We the People tasked three coequal branches to act as the People’s agents; two branches (i.e., Congress and the president) stand for regular elections, while the third (i.e., the judiciary) does not. The fact that federal judges do not stand for election does not mean those judges are somehow not agents of the People. To the contrary, it simply means that the courts make up a different class of agents. And political minorities no doubt recognize the wisdom in empowering at least one class of agents to operate outside the daily whims of majoritarian politics.

Finally, it is of course the case that federal judges can get the law wrong – and as human beings, judges (like other humans) have the capacity to act immorally. (Bowie and Renan demonstrate as much by highlighting grave errors made by the Supreme Court throughout its history.) But Congress and the president can make legal and moral errors as well. So imposing a form of congressional supremacy would not fix the underlying problem that humans (even those that serve in Congress) are not angels. Part of the wisdom behind our Constitution’s structure is that it addressed human nature by limiting the consequences attributable to any one branch’s errors. And the courts play an important role within that constitutional design by exercising judicial review.

In sum, it is mistaken to conclude that accepting the validity of judicial review requires accepting rule by the courts rather than rule by the People. The courts, like Congress and the president, are agents of the People. And the courts rightfully act as agents of the People when they decline to give legal effect to statutes that violate the People’s Constitution.

Chad Squitieri is an Associate Professor of Law at The Catholic University of America, where he researches and teaches about federal administrative law and the Constitution's separation of powers.

#468. Volcanoes lurk behind the scenes of human history

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cold spells after eruptions may have spurred famine, disease, revolt and religious changes

German artist Caspar David Friedrich painted Two Men by the Sea in 1817, two years after Mount Tambora’s eruption in present-day Indonesia. Though far away, the blast spread volcanic aerosols around the world and may have inspired the image’s dark sky.

Caspar David Friedrich (Wikimedia Commons)

“The bright sun was extinguish’d … and the icy earth swung blind and blackening in the moonless air.” These words from Lord Byron’s 1816 poem Darkness evoke a hellish apocalypse. But Byron may have been inspired by the fallout of a real cataclysm — one that happened half a world away.

Mount Tambora, in present-day Indonesia, blew its top in 1815. More than 71,000 people in the vicinity died. And climate-cooling aerosols from the eruption ushered in extreme weather the following years in far-flung places. Cold weather in the summer of 1816, sometimes with frosts and snow, destroyed corn in New England, rice and buckwheat in China and wheat, oats and potatoes in Ireland. In some places, the rising cost of bread sparked protests and riots. The circumstances may have helped spread typhus. Some called 1816 “the year without a summer,” others “the year of the beggars.”

Like Tambora, volcanoes, and the cooling they cause, may be an underappreciated geologic force at times moving behind the scenes of human history.

“These climatic impacts of the Tambora eruption were recorded without any knowledge that this had anything to do with an eruption,” says Clive Oppenheimer, a volcanologist at the University of Cambridge.

While Tambora is the most well-studied eruption in history, others have also rattled the planet. Oppenheimer and his colleagues analyzed 38 large eruptions in the 2026 Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences. Their work pulled together studies linking the geologic events and their effects on climate with their potential societal fallout in food systems, the economy, unrest, culture and religion. The locations and exact timing of the eruptions that spurred some notable cold spurts aren’t known. But understanding how the events impacted the climate, environment and society can offer clues to the past, including how people coped with climate stress.

“Volcanic eruptions, they are kind of this like hammer blow to the climate system,” says Heli Huhtamaa, a climate and environmental historian at the University of Bern in Switzerland. Volcanoes may offer lessons about resilience for modern societies in a warming world, she says.

Volcanoes spew sulfur-containing gases. Over weeks, those gases react in the atmosphere to form particles that block sunlight. The particles can stay aloft for many months, riding jet streams to encircle the globe. Paintings from years after eruptions show bloodred sunsets or landscapes shrouded by hazy skies.

Because no two eruptions are exactly alike, it takes work to grasp each one’s impacts on weather and climate. “If we understand the climate response of one [eruption], that does not mean that we understand the climate response of another,” says Ulf Büntgen, a paleoclimatologist at the University of Cambridge.

A volcano’s explosivity impacts how high it shoots its gases and where they travel. Over time, those gases form particles that block sunlight. The particles can stay aloft for many months, riding jet streams to encircle the globe. In this computer model of a 1991 eruption, colors represent how high particles are in the atmosphere. Red is high and blue is closer to Earth’s surface.

Researchers find the traces of past eruptions in ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica. Scientists try to nail down the date on a sulfur-loaded ice core layer and compare it with tree ring data, a logbook of sorts for summer growing conditions since temperatures impact how trees lay down their wood each year. Scientists can pull together tree ring records from far-flung places and use statistics to work out annual temperatures in a hemisphere.

Cold spells sometimes jump out from tree ring records. For years when cool summers impeded the final stages of wood formation, a chemical method used to estimate temperatures turns wood layers blue. Büntgen and colleagues saw two blue rings around 1345. “And that is very rare. We never saw that two years in a row,” he says. The rings coincided with the timing of the Black Death’s spread in Europe, the team reported in 2025 in Communications Earth & Environment. The researchers traced the connections between chilly summers, poor grain harvests in the Mediterranean and shipping records showing how Italian city states imported flea-ridden grain from the Caucasus.

Oppenheimer, Büntgen and their colleagues broadened the detective work for their 2026 review. The team studied Northern Hemisphere temperature responses to the 38 largest eruptions between A.D. 1 and 1900. On average, each volcano caused about 0.5 degrees Celsius of cooling in the year after the eruption. Recovery to normal temperatures was slower for eruptions that occurred in times that were already colder on average, Büntgen says. In some periods, large or successive eruptions may lead to an elevated amounts of particles in the atmosphere for a long time. The team’s calculations show that high aerosol levels concur with some of the past two millennia’s coldest spells, such as the one preceding the Black Death.

Those cold snaps often had dire consequences. Out of 17 large-scale famines in Europe from 1300 to 1800, 11 followed major eruptions. In addition to faltering crops and famines, the most frequently societal responses described in studies collected by the review authors were disease outbreaks, political turmoil and migration.

The societal circumstances surrounding eruptions are as important as the amount of cooling, Huhtamaa says. She and her colleagues studied crop failures and farmstead desertion in three different volcanic eruptions that impacted Ostrobothnia, in what is now Finland. During the 17th century, Ostrobothnia experienced cooling from three sets of eruptions at different time periods.Although the latest eruption event, in 1695, was the coldest of all, peasants fared better than they had after eruptions earlier in the century, Huhtamaa and colleagues reported in 2022 in Climate of the Past. Though people still starved, only around 7 percent of peasants lost their farmland, compared with 20 percent in the other periods. This was because the crown provided tax relief, Huhtamaa says. This wasn’t humanitarian aid, as it was meant to preserve the tax base and prevent possibly decades of lost revenue.“When there is a climatic shock, it doesn’t matter if it’s 0.5 or 1.5 degrees cooler, because what really matters is the societal vulnerability,” Huhtamaa says.

Studies like Huhtamaa’s reveal how societies can tick on in spite of climate disasters. But historians haven’t always been keen to look to climate for clues to explain history. Human agency has often been considered the only reasonable explanation for human affairs, Huhtamaa says. “And climate explanations sounded lazy.”

Investigating societies’ preconditions and responses to the climate change caused by volcanoes could help people prepare for a now warming world. A recent report from the United Nations’ Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, noted that not enough research had been done on responses, Huhtamaa says. Researchers could examine one place’s response to different events, as in Huhtamaa’s work on Ostrobothnia, or parse different locales’ responses to the same event. Meanwhile, greater use of insights from ecology, agronomy and economics, for instance, can help with this detective work that reveals how humans respond to climatic events beyond their control.

Those responses appear in religion, too. For instance, lines in the famed 10th century poem Vǫluspá seem to reference the 939 eruption of Iceland’s Eldgjá volcano, with high-flying flames and blackened sun beams. “It’s like an eyewitness description of a phenomenal lava flood,” Oppenheimer says. The poem foretells a fiery end to pagan gods, such as Odin, who are replaced by one god. Vǫluspá, which explained the eruption in terms of divine displeasure,may have contributed to the island’s Christianization around 1000, Oppenheimer and colleagues suggest.

Famines, riots, disease, migration. The widespread climatic effects of volcanoes across the ages may be subtler than the hellscapes conjured by poets but more tangible in the marks they’ve left on history.

#3455. 6 Lactate Threshold Workouts to Build Speed Endurance and Race-Day Confidence

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Train your body to run faster for longer with these key runs.

Lactate threshold workouts are one of the most important parts of a distance-running training plan. Putting your body through this type of exercise will help you run faster and run farther.

“Lactate threshold training means building confidence,” says New York City-based USATF- and RRCA-certified run coach Kai Ng. “It means getting used to being comfortable with being uncomfortable.”

To get you familiar with these workouts and how to implement them into your training plan, Runner’s World connected with three certified run coaches and collected their top recommended threshold workouts that improve your speed endurance.

Working to improve your lactate threshold makes more difficult paces feel less exhausting, and therefore, makes you a more efficient and faster runner.

Alex Morrow, RRCA- and USATF-certified run coach and founder of Resolute Running in Birmingham, Alabama, likens the human body and its ability to flush out waste created while training at lactate threshold to a bucket with a hole in the bottom. “As you run, waste [the lactate you produce while running] is being thrown into that bucket,” Morrow explains. “As long as you’re running at the right pace, the waste going in the bucket drains right out the bottom, and you’re fine. When you pass that lactate threshold—you put too much waste in the bucket—it overflows and you slow down. Lactate threshold training creates a bigger bucket and a bigger hole at the bottom of that bucket.”

By building up your body’s speed endurance, you also boost your self-confidence as you maintain your pace during these tough workouts, and gradually go farther and farther while holding the pace. Ng says that experiencing this dose of self-confidence during a threshold workout will give you the reassurance that, come race day, you’ll be able to go out there and crush your goal speed.

Need more convincing to add these to your schedule? “Lactate threshold is the most important determinant of success in endurance-related activities and events, and the main goal of endurance training programs should be the improvement of this parameter,” according to an article published by the University of New Mexico by Len Kravitz, PhD, CSCS and Lance Dalleck, PhD.

Your lactate threshold is the exercise intensity level at which lactate—a byproduct of exercise that forms when creating energy from glucose, especially without sufficient oxygen—accumulates in the blood faster than you can remove it. At its core, it’s the line between low- and high-intensity exercise.

To find your lactate threshold pace, you could head to a lab. But for a more budget-friendly option, you can calculate this pace using a practical field test. Following a warmup, you’ll run at your best possible intensity for 30 minutes and use your average running speed during that effort to calculate your ideal threshold pace.

Generally speaking, runners find their lactate threshold pace to be about a 7 or 8 out of 10 effort on a rate of perceived exertion (RPE) scale, an effort broadly described as comfortably hard. The coaches we spoke with also describe this effort as one you can sustain for one hour, but not longer.

The key is going at an effort you can sustain—it’s not all out. “There’s something in us that we all want to go faster,” Tia Accetta, Run Tuscon co-founder and RRCA-certified run coach, tells Runner’s World. “But, with this lactate threshold training, don’t.”

First, it’s important to note the difference between tempo runs and lactate threshold workouts. Ng describes the term tempo run as an overarching umbrella when discussing workouts designed for specific paces. “Tempo runs don’t have to be at threshold [pace],” Ng explains. “But lactate threshold runs are one of the many types of tempo runs. Under the umbrella, you can also run [tempo runs] at marathon pace or maybe half marathon pace.”

To add threshold workouts to your training, we asked experts for their favorites. From short repetitions to 15-plus-mile days, these are the threshold runs that build speed endurance for every type of runner.

Why it works: Intervals work well for those new to threshold work, because the breaks between reps give runners time to reflect on how the previous rep went and also allow for an adjustment of pace in future reps.

Morrow also explains that many runners who are just starting to train at lactate threshold pace tend to struggle holding the pace for 20 to 30 minutes. So he eases them into it with intervals.

The versatility of these workouts means they benefit those training for 5Ks up to marathons.

Why it works: “Breaking down tempo runs into longer time-based intervals is a good way to practice,” says Accetta.

She adds that for 5K runners training for the shorter distance, just two of these eight-minute intervals could constitute a workout, whereas for marathon runners, four or even five eight-minute intervals at lactate threshold pace gets them a better payoff.

Why it works: Once you’re comfortable with lactate threshold pace and want to sprinkle longer, more sustained efforts into your training plan, all three coaches recommend this steady-state threshold run. “It’s simple, easy, and everybody loves doing it,” says Morrow. “It also teaches you how to handle that low-grade discomfort, which is what distance running is all about.”

Eliminating the breaks between intervals forces runners to battle through minor fatigue, as Morrow describes, and stick with their comfortably-hard pace.

Ng adds that all workouts are designed to simulate parts of a whole, and in this case, training your body to run at lactate threshold pace for 20 to 30 minutes teaches you how to push through those tough miles of any race.

Why it works: At a quick glance, progression workouts seem daunting, considering they cover the most mileage. However, changing paces throughout a long workout can be one of the best ways to train your body to maintain a pace during the later stages of a race like the marathon, says Accetta.

Keep in mind the first workout below requires a minimum of running about 15 miles, so you definitely want to build up to this progression. It’s especially effective for experienced marathoners, as it teaches you to run through fatigue and discomfort—both necessary for race day. “It’s not always fun, but it’s extremely effective,” says Morrow.

Why it works: “To me, this is just a really good representation of fatigued legs and getting tired, but still maintaining a threshold effort in the middle,” Accetta says. “I feel like everyone, after they do it, they have a good dose of confidence.”

This nine-mile workout, while employing a more gradual increase and decrease in pace than Morrow’s progression workout above, is considerably shorter in overall distance. You’ll still receive the benefits of simulating midrace fatigue, while sustaining your lactate threshold pace, prepping you for race day.

Why it works: Yes, you can incorporate hills into your lactate threshold workout. Morrow says he recommends hill repeats to runners who may want to take a step back from heavy mileage or don’t feel confident enough to tackle a full steady-state lactate threshold workout.

He reminds his runners doing this exercise that the uphill effort should amount to the ideal 20 minutes recommended for threshold workouts. The jog back downhill is simply a break, and should not be calculated into the amount of effort given.

Morrow’s recommended threshold hill repeat workout gives runners the endurance benefits of threshold training, while adding in positive aspects of hill workouts, including building leg and core strength.

Because of the hill, you don’t necessarily need to run this workout at threshold pace, but rather threshold effort. Basically, you’ll run slower because you’re going uphill, but you’ll still aim to give the same effort that you feel during your lactate threshold workouts on flat ground.

“We’re always looking for that sweet spot of 20 to 30 minutes of effort,” says Morrow. “Whether that’s short intervals, long intervals, it doesn’t really matter. Get that 20 to 30 minutes.”

As the weeks of training go by and time at threshold pace transitions from being comfortably hard to just comfortable, that’s when you know you’re achieving the desired result.

“The longer the break, the more likely you are to go out too fast,” says Accetta. “Technically, you shouldn’t need a break if you’re keeping your conservative pace. You want to go into the next [rep] still feeling a little bit fatigued.”

Lactate threshold training is all about maintaining your pace for a set amount of time, so trimming your break time down so you feel fresh—but not fully recovered—is essential to a quality threshold interval workout.

Lactate threshold workouts are hard. Not hard in the sense of high-intensity effort, but hard on your mind and its perception of your body’s ability to set and maintain your comfortably hard pace.

“Trust your legs more than your mind,” Ng says. “Your mind is going to be in your way the whole time. It’s going to tell you to stop, but your job is to fight it. When that thought comes to your head, I want you to hang in there for one more minute and I guarantee you’re going to be fine. The only time you should stop is when your legs stop.”

Make sure you’re putting these workouts into the correct phase of your training plan in order to maximize the benefits. Don’t start knocking out lactate threshold workouts two weeks from your race.

Improving your lactate threshold takes time, so in order to experience the most success from these workouts, Morrow says to incorporate them once a week into the strength and endurance-building phase of your plan, or about two to four weeks into a 16-week marathon training plan.

#3308. How to Diagnose and Fix These Common Bike Noises, Including Pedal Clicking

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A bike that squeaks, rattles, and clicks is annoying and frustrating. Use these tips to troubleshoot the most common and mysterious bike sounds.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

As cyclists, we hear a lot of sounds when riding. Usually, the noises come from chirping birds, the wind rustling leaves or your bike’s tires rolling over your favorite trail. But sometimes the noises sound bad and come from your bike, and those noises can range from annoying to anxiety-inducing as you ponder the cause.

Bicycle components are moving parts that wear out and sometimes need adjustment. These squeaks, creaks, clicks, and other strange sounds are often a sign that something on your bike needs repair or replacement. And if your bike seems extra noisy, it might be time for some routine maintenance.

Noises on bikes are sometimes frustrating and mysterious. It’s often a challenge to figure out exactly where the sound originates—especially if it’s hard to reproduce when not riding. So first, you’ll need to know how to diagnose it.

The most common noise makers are loose parts or bearings requiring adjustment, cleaning, or replacement. Performing regular preride bolt checks helps keep components tight and noises at bay. Several spots on your bike use bearings to rotate smoothly, including your headset, bottom bracket, hubs, and suspension linkages on mountain bikes. Keep these bearings properly adjusted to cut down on squeaks and knocking sounds.

Below are the most common culprits of pesky bike noises, including bike pedal clicking. Use these suggestions to help you narrow down and silence them. If you lack the proper tools to fix an issue (or fear a fix is above your skill level), seek assistance from your local bike shop or a trusted bicycle mechanic.

That creaky squeaking you hear as you pedal might mean that your bike’s chain needs lube. Cleaning and lubing your chain is usually a good place to start with any weird noise, but if it doesn’t solve the squeak, you may need to do other drivetrain maintenance or replace the chain.

Your chain should also be the first thing you check if you hear a rattling sound while riding. It could mean the loose, excess chain is contacting your frame (this is commonly called “chain slap”). It can also mean your derailleur is out of adjustment and not shifting properly.

A worn chain can also cause noise. Bicycle chains wear out with use as the bushings and rollers in the chain become worn down by grit and grime. This causes slop to develop and the distance between the pin centers to increase. (This is commonly called “chain stretch,” though the metal in the chain hasn’t actually stretched.)

Generally, when a chain “stretches” by 0.5 percent, it is time to change it. If you ride a lot, a chain checker is handy for knowing when it’s time to replace your chain.

Pro Tip: You can also use a ruler to check chain wear. A fresh chain measures 12 inches from pin center to pin center. If it measures more than 12-¹⁄₁₆ inches, change your chain.

If you notice a noise with every pedal stroke, it might be the pedals themselves. To remedy this, remove your pedals, grease the threads, and reinstall them on your bike (remember, both pedals tighten to the front of the bike). When installing, replace the washers (if any) between the pedal and crank arm.

Another source of squeaks might be your shoes’ cleats. Check that your cleats are tight—a loose cleat bolt can cause headaches. While checking the bolts, check for excess wear. Sometimes, the sounds are a sign that the cleats need replacement. An occasional drop or two of wax-based lubricant on two-bolt cleats can help keep the squeaks at bay.

Pro Tip: If your cleats and pedals get squeaky during a ride, a quick squirt of energy drink or something sugary like Gatorade can sometimes quiet them down until you get home and apply lube.

If your bike has internal cable routing, a rattling noise emanating from inside your frame when going over bumps (or knocking when turning) is commonly caused by derailleur housing or hydraulic brake lines. While the sound is rarely cause for concern, it drives riders crazy and can take you out of the zone when training.

Unfortunately, the fix for this issue isn’t usually quick or easy and requires some advanced mechanic skills (especially if dealing with hydraulic brakes). On some bikes—like Trek’s Boone, which has a port on the downtube for this purpose—it could be as simple as adding a zip tie to hold the cables. Other bikes might require the disassembly of the brake hoses and shifter housing (and even the stem and headset on some bikes) to apply sound-damping foam tubing to the lines.

Thankfully, the fix is much easier on bikes with externally routed cables and hoses that have rattling cables. This noise is usually caused when the housing slaps together over bumps on the trail or rough road surfaces. Some pro mechanics heat-shrink the housing together to prevent the sound, but electrical tape can also be used. Another solution is cable clips that keep the housing and hoses from contacting.

Before checking if the noise originates from your bottom bracket, check that the crank bolts (which affix the crank arms to the BB on many bikes) are sufficiently tight. You might hear a clicking when these bolts loosen. And if a crank bolt entirely backs out, it can cause a crankarm to fall off while riding or damage your crank or frame. Grab a torque wrench and tighten the bolts to the manufacturer’s recommended specification.

After you are sure the noise is not from a loose crankarm, stand next to your bike and grab both brakes. Then, put your foot on the pedal closest to you and press inward. If the bike still creaks or ticks when you put weight on the pedal, the problem likely comes from your bottom bracket.

If the clicking sound isn’t from your drivetrain or pedals (see below), your BB may be loose, or the bearings may be worn. Many bikes use a non-adjustable cartridge or press-fit BB that must be replaced when the bearings wear out. Some systems (such as SRAM Dub and RaceFace Cinch) use a preload ring to add preload to the bottom bracket bearings. These rings can loosen and cause a click while pedaling. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions to tighten it back up.

If you don’t hear anything, the problem is likely the bearings in your headset, hubs, or suspension parts. Bearings can wear out after a season, especially if you live somewhere wet or sandy. Your local bike shop can help you order and install new ones.

Not long ago, disc brakes were almost only found on high-end bikes and mountain bikes. However, these powerful stoppers are now common on almost all genres of bikes and at all price levels. And disc brakes make some unique sounds that can drive cyclists bonkers.

A chattering sound coming from your rotors might indicate a loose rotor. Check that the rotor bolts or lockring are sufficiently tight. Most disc brakes with 6-bolt rotors use a T25 Torx to tighten. For Centerlock rotors, a cassette lockring tool is used for internal-style lockrings. For external-type lockrings, a rotor/external BB wrench is used.

If your disc brakes are squealing and screeching when you apply the brakes, the brake pads or rotors may be contaminated. This commonly happens from oil residue thrown up from the road when you ride in the rain, contamination from aerosol lubricants, or any one of dozens of other causes.

If the contamination is not too bad, it can often be cleaned off. To do this, remove the wheel and thoroughly wipe down the disc rotor and pads with a clean shop rag or towel and isopropyl alcohol. If you notice significant discoloration of the rotor or grooves in the pads, either or both may need replacement. Your local bike shop can help get you the correct replacement pads and rotors for your brake system.

Pro Tip: If you swap wheelsets on your bike for different rides or races, recenter the disc brake caliper each time you change the wheel. It takes a few extra minutes but helps ensure brake pads wear evenly and reduces rotor rub.

The occasional zing and ting from disc rotors are normal, and these sounds often come and go during a ride as the rotors heat up under hard braking and cool down afterward. If you hear a continuous pinging noise from the rotors, your rotors might need straightening. Head to the below story for more detailed info on diagnosing and fixing common disc brake issues.

A loose headset usually makes a rattling or thunking sound. And a headset that’s too loose can make your bike handle poorly or even damage your frame or fork beyond repair. To tighten a threadless headset system, loosen the bolts on the side of your stem, then tighten the top cap bolt. Tight the bolt enough to pull the fork, headset, stem, and any headset spacers snugly together (but not so tight that the bars can’t turn freely).

After everything is snug and turns smoothly without play in the bearings, retighten the stem bolts. Use a torque wrench to bring the stem to the manufacturer’s recommended spec (this is sometimes noted on the stem in N/m). Because you have the torque wrench out, this is also a good time to check that your stem’s faceplate bolts are sufficiently tight.

If the rattling doesn’t go away after tightening the headset, take your bike to the shop to be safe. Sometimes, the headset might need a shim spacer—but it’s important to check that it’s not a problem with your fork or steerer tube.

Another common source of creaking is your seatpost—particularly if it occurs only while seated. To fix it, remove your seatpost by undoing the quick release (if there is one) or loosening the hex bolt at its base where it enters the frame.

Pro Tip: Before removing your post, measure the seat height (from the center of the crank bolt to the top of the saddle) with a tape measure. Or, mark the position with electrical tape on the seatpost where it meets the seat clamp or frame.

After removing the post, wipe off any grit on the seatpost and inside the seat tube with a rag, and apply a thin layer of grease or fiber grip compound (if you have a carbon frame or are using a carbon post) inside the seat tube. Finally, reinstall the post, tighten the binder bolt to the correct torque, and check if the post still creaks.

If the post still creaks after applying grease and retightening the binder, the noise might be coming from the head of the post or the saddle rails. To check this, undo the hardware that clamps the saddle and the seat from the post. Then, wipe down the hardware and saddle rails with a rag to remove any grit or grime that built up. Finally, reinstall the seat and hardware and tighten them to the manufacturer’s recommended torque.

Chainrings are noise makers when they loosen up and are often overlooked by riders. And if your bike has replaceable rings (with bolts versus riveted together), tightening chainring bolts is an easy fix.

Use the correct side hex or Torx wrench (often 5mm to T30) to the proper torque value. To help prevent the chainring bolts from coming loose again, apply some thread lock compound to the threads.

Pro Tip: Many chainring bolts use a slotted nut on the backside of the ring. If your chainring bolt spins and will not tighten, use a chainring nut tool to hold the nut while tightening the bolt.

If you hear a constant clicking (especially in certain gears) while pedaling, it might mean a shift cable is stretched. (This cable “stretch” is usually the cable housing and ferrules compressing). Cable stretch requires derailleur adjustment for the chain to sit correctly on the gears and not rub the derailleur cage.

First, ensure the derailleur is aligned and the hanger isn’t bent (see below). Then, adjust the cable tension using the barrel adjuster (usually on the rear derailleur on road or gravel bikes and the shifter for mountain bikes). If this doesn’t solve the clicking, you might need to replace your shifter cable and housing.

A bent or misaligned derailleur hanger can cause a constant clicking sound while pedaling. This part (which connects your rear derailleur to the frame) can bend in a crash or incident. They are often replaceable and cheaper to replace than an entire frame.

A bent derailleur hanger might be easy to spot. However, sometimes, a slight bend can cause clicking and poor shifting. (This is especially true for 12-speed drivetrains where even the slightest bend can cause the chain to skip.) If the derailleur hanger isn’t cracked or bent too far to be saved, a hanger alignment tool can be used to straighten it out.

Sometimes, creaking occurs from the derailleur hanger rubbing against the frame where it’s mounted. To remedy this, remove the derailleur hanger, thoroughly clean the frame and hanger, and apply some anti-seize paste to the surfaces that touch.

Pro Tip: Derailleur hangers always seem to break at inopportune moments (like the night before an important race or a holiday when shops are closed). Hangers are relatively low-cost ($25-$50) so, it’s good to have a spare or two in your toolbox. Derailleurhanger.com is a good source for many hangers, especially for older bikes and out-of-business brands.

If you’re purchasing a new bike soon, look for ones using the new Universal Derailleur Hanger standard. Since this hanger is used on many bikes, it’s commonly stocked by many shops. (It also ensures frame compatibility with SRAM’s latest-generation Transmission drivetrains.) Most bikes using UDH are mountain bikes, but it is now popping up on gravel bikes (like the Santa Cruz Stigmata) and road bikes (like the Ritte Esprit).

A noisy thru axle or quick release might need to be tightened. But if tightening it doesn’t quiet the sound, it’s likely one of two problems. A creak or squeak is often a sign of a dry or dirty thru axle or quick release. Remove the thru axle from the frame or fork, clean it, and re-grease it with a thin coat of bike-specific grease. A clicking sound could signify that a thru axle or QR is cracked or broken. This requires immediate replacement.

Pro Tip: If you remove your wheels to get your bike to and from rides, put the thru axles in the frame or fork during transport. This protects your bike from side-load damage and keeps your thru axle from being left behind on your workbench or the trailhead (or getting lost under a car seat).

Far more uncommon than the issues listed above, a cracked or defective frame can be the reason for a persistent, mysterious noise. Frame defects or cracks sometimes aren’t immediately visible and can be hard to diagnose.

Frame damage (from a crash, shipping damage, etc) can also be the culprit. Hairline cracks in carbon frames can be hard to find with the naked eye. When in doubt, bring the bike to an experienced shop for a full inspection. Some companies that repair carbon frames also offer frame inspections. Ruckus Composites in Portland, Oregon, offers ultrasonic inspection to check frames for cracks and damage.

Sometimes, simply thoroughly washing your bike and cleaning and lubing the chain is all it takes to set everything right again. But if you can’t figure out the issue or aren’t sure how to fix it when you do, it’s always a safe bet to take your bike to your local shop or mechanic for service.

Should you roll out a yoga mat or step onto a reformer? Three experts weigh in.

Yoga and Pilates have become fitness mainstays for good reason: Both are low-impact activities that work small muscles you didn’t even know you had. (Seriously, have the backs of your knees ever been sore?) They’re also effective forms of cross-training—especially for runners.

Incorporating practices like these into your routine can help you stay healthy, build endurance, and reduce your risk of injury. That’s because running is a one-directional movement, meaning you can develop imbalances, says Shannon Rowbury, a BowFlex fitness adviser and three-time U.S. Olympic middle-distance runner and medalist. Running is also repetitive, which means that any imbalances can amplify over time, she continues.

“Think about your car alignment—if it’s off, even just a little, over many miles a small wobble can lead to a major breakdown,” Rowbury says. “Cross-training is a fantastic resource for runners. Not only does it offload some pounding if your muscles and joints need some rest, but it also activates and strengthens the muscles that might tend to be overpowered by our prime movers.”

Both yoga and Pilates can achieve this, but in different ways. So, which activity deserves a dedicated spot in your training schedule? We break down how yoga and Pilates stack up for runners, their advantages and disadvantages, and how to choose the right fit for your goals.

Yoga can be a great complement to running, particularly for mobility, balance, body awareness, and recovery, according to Shavonne Wood, a certified yoga and Pilates instructor and marathon runner. When certain muscles feel tight, yoga can provide a structured way to move through different ranges of motion rather than just doing a few static stretches after a run, she explains.

In addition to physical benefits, there are mental benefits as well. Training for a race can become very performance-focused in terms of pace, distance, heart rate, workouts, and race goals, Wood says. Yoga can give runners an opportunity to slow down, focus on their breathing, and move without another performance metric attached to it.

Not to mention, yoga helps runners become more in tune with their bodies, allowing them to recognize when they might need a rest day from running or when they need to pull back to prevent burnout, overtraining, and injuries, according to Theresa DeLorenzo, yoga therapist, Pilates instructor, and owner of Nutrition for Optimal Performance.

Yoga varies enormously, according to Wood. A gentle mobility-focused class and a very intense power yoga class are completely different training stimuli. Runners need to consider where that session fits into their overall training load, she explains.

Wood also cautions against assuming that having more flexibility is always better. “Runners need adequate mobility, but they also need stability and strength,” she says. “Someone who is already very mobile may not benefit from continually pushing deeper into stretches, and they may be better served by focusing on controlled movement and strength through their available range.”

Rowbury agrees, saying that many of the more popular yoga movements are static stretches—held for 30 seconds or more—that increase range of motion. “If you’re a runner who tends to be stiff, you might need this. But if you’re seeking speed, you need some tension in the tendons to create that springy rubber-band effect. As a result, yoga might not be the best before a speed session,” she says.

Rowbury is a huge fan of Pilates, saying it had a positive impact on her running—especially postpartum. “I think of it as my core connector,” she says. “Before Pilates, my legs, arms, and core were each strong, but they weren’t well connected. Through Pilates, I was able to improve power transfer.”

Think about the running motion and how your arms swing in counterbalance to your legs—that power is transferred through the core, Rowbury says.

“I started doing Pilates postpartum in 2018,” Rowbury says. “After about a year of consistent work, I was running faster 200s as a 34-year-old mom than I ever ran in high school, college, or as a pro. Typically, you lose speed as you age, but Pilates has unlocked my speed by enabling me to activate my deeper core and connect all my parts.”

Research backs this up: A small 2018 study in PLOS One found that those who followed a 12-week Pilates program ran a faster 5K afterward than those who didn’t do Pilates workouts.

A limitation of Pilates is that it shouldn’t necessarily replace traditional strength training, according to Wood. “Runners still benefit from progressive resistance training and appropriately loading muscles and bones,” she says. “I see Pilates as an excellent complement to running and strength training rather than a complete substitute for strength work.”

Additionally, just like yoga, the quality and intensity of Pilates classes vary considerably. A class should be appropriate for the runner’s ability, injury history, and overall training load, Wood says.

Both yoga and Pilates are excellent additions to any runner’s cross-training routine, according to DeLorenzo, as long as you don’t have any preexisting back or neck injuries. Regardless, anyone looking to start a new workout routine should get the green light from their healthcare provider first.

With that being said, Pilates may have a slight edge over yoga, according to Wood. “Pilates provides more of the strength, stability, and movement-control components that complement the repetitive nature of running particularly well,” she says. “It can also incorporate a significant mobility and stretching component, so runners can still get some of the flexibility and range-of-motion benefits they may be looking for from yoga while also working on strength and stability.”

Rowbury suggests aiming for one or two Pilates sessions per week and two or three yoga sessions per week to keep your core engaged and maintain mobility and range of motion.

Ultimately, though, the best cross-training activity is the one you enjoy enough to do consistently, according to Wood. “If a runner loves yoga and hates Pilates, I would much rather see them consistently practice yoga than the theoretically ‘perfect’ activity they never actually do.”

#542. Archaeologists Just Found the Oldest Dwelling in the Western Hemisphere

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

This 13,800-year-old Alaskan base camp is rewriting how the Americas were settled.

Archaeologists uncovered something more than a hunting stopover just east of the Bering Land Bridge. Far more. An excavation team at Shég’ Xdaltth’í’—in what is now Alaska, roughly 34 miles southeast of Fairbanks—discovered the earliest known Nenana Complex occupation east of the Bering Land Bridge. It isn’t the oldest archaeological evidence of humans in the Western Hemisphere, but it may be the oldest spot where people actually settled in. It also delivered some intriguing news about domesticated dogs.

In a new study published in the journal Science Advances, the authors date the early base camp to 13,800 years ago, when it was inhabited by the Late Pleistocene people of Alaska.

While researchers believe human dispersal across the Bering Land Bridge helped populate the Americas, we still don’t fully understand the region’s archaeological record, in part because most known Beringian sites are scattered remains of small seasonal hunting camps. The Shég’ Xdaltth’í’ site in interior Alaska—a name that translates as “They Were There”—offers a rare focal point. The study’s authors wrote that the oldest array of cultural remains found there includes a “unique hafted-biface form, osseous tools, diverse fauna, and well-preserved activity areas, a record unparalleled for this age in Beringia.” The findings may all be a part of what is ultimately a historic discovery.

“The complex feature at Shég’ Xdaltth’í’ represents the earliest potential case of a habitation structure in North America,” the authors wrote. “Early Alaskans knew their surroundings well.”

Even though Shég’ Xdaltth’í’ has been studied for more than a decade, the experts weren’t expecting to unearth tens of thousands of artifacts. But that’s exactly what they found, highlighted by stone tools, bone needles, and an antler pendant—along with a hearth, a small pit, and a soil stain marking where a wooden post once stood, likely the remains of a lean-to or early tent. Nearby, the team also uncovered an earth oven filled with charcoal and animal bones, probably used to slow-cook meat for a large family group.

Hunting camps aren’t uncommon in the region, but this is something far beyond that, instead showing people “settling in” to a location they returned to intermittently over roughly 1,000 years. “I expected we’d find hunting tools and knapping [stone tool making] debris surrounding a hearth or two, but I had no idea that we’d stumble into a base camp with tens of thousands of artifacts,” Kelly Graf, the study’s first author and an archaeologist at the University of Kansas, told Live Science. “It was a total surprise.”

The latest archaeological and genetic research has the ancestors of today’s Indigenous Americans entering the continent around 21,000 years ago, so finding an occupied site in eastern Beringia—the Alaskan half of the landmass that joined northeast Asia and northwest North America during the last ice age—with cultural links running both west and south fits the narrative.

Those westward links show up in the stone and the stone sources. The team said the obsidian artifacts match a geological fingerprint hundreds of miles to the northwest, meaning people carried the volcanic rock a long way to the site. The hafted bifaces, meanwhile, were knapped from rhyolite and basalt into a distinctive diamond shape unique to central Alaska, yet they closely resemble tools from sites far to the west in Beringia, in present-day northern Siberia.

Then there are the finds that point south instead. Eight bone needle fragments tie the camp to sites farther south in North America, where the only other eyed needles had previously been recovered, making the Shég’ Xdaltth’í’ needles the oldest discovered in the Western Hemisphere. The antler pendant, with a hole drilled at one end and a point at the other, resembles personal adornments found elsewhere in North America as well.

“The co-occurrence of these cultural features at the single site of Shég’ Xdaltth’í’ is truly notable,” the team wrote, adding that the finds “highlight the strong cultural connection between early Beringians and Americans.”

Whether the residents themselves made those journeys remains unclear. “It would be hard to pin down whether or not they were moving back and forth during a lifetime,” Graf said, “but at least we can say there is a connection.”

The research uncovered one additional surprise. While bones from more than a dozen wolf-like animals create additional links to Asia, an analysis of one animal’s jaw showed it came from neither a dire wolf nor a Pleistocene gray wolf, but from a population of wolf-like canids unique to central Alaska. Because current genetic evidence suggests early domesticated dogs in North America descended from Siberian wolves, the site’s timing and location offer circumstantial evidence that these people may have been dog owners.

Still, further work is needed, both at Shég’ Xdaltth’í’ and elsewhere, to understand the dispersal and timing of population shifts east of the land bridge. “Alaska is a big place and so remote,” Graf said, “so there are really just a few small areas that have been intensively investigated.”

#1982. When the Supreme Court blessed zoning

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

James Metzenbaum was stuck on a train from Washington – slowed by a snowstorm – convincing himself that his opponent had successfully persuaded the Supreme Court against his position within the last few minutes of oral argument. He had just argued in Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty in defense of the city’s zoning plan. The poor weather meant he might not get back to his office in time to send a telegram asking Chief Justice William Howard Taft for more briefing before the weekend. So he improvised – opening the train car door, making eye contact with a man shoveling snow near the tracks, and tossing him a telegram wrapped in cash. The shoveler seems to have understood what was intended. He sent off the telegram, and a few days later the court granted Metzenbaum permission to file another brief.

Not long after that, the court granted a full rehearing, which was followed by a dramatic reversal in several justices’ positions. In a 6-3 decision, the court sided with Metzenbaum and in favor of Euclid’s power to pass comprehensive zoning, and against a local developer who had argued the town’s plan exceeded the city’s police power and destroyed the property’s value. Three justices dissented, though they never said why.

It was an odd start to the birth of modern zoning, and it happened almost exactly 100 years ago.

Many people think the term “Euclidean zoning” takes its name from the Greek “father of geometry.” Instead, it takes its name from Village of Euclid, which upheld the power of cities to zone.

But the city at the center of that lawsuit was, in fact, named after the ancient mathematician. In 1796, six surveyors working in the Western Reserve in Ohio demanded better terms for their punishing work. Together they secured an agreement to jointly own a township in return for settling a few dozen families there. They named it for the man regarded as their profession’s patron saint. And while they eventually abandoned the town’s frigid winters, the name endured.

By the early 20th century, nearby Cleveland had become an industrial metropolis and Euclid was positioned to develop alongside it. Ambler Realty owned 68 acres between Euclid Avenue, a major road for storefronts, and the Nickel Plate railroad, a hub of industrial freight – meaning the property had enormous industrial potential. But in 1922, Euclid adopted an ordinance dividing the village by use, height, and area. Near the avenue, Ambler’s land could accommodate single- and two-family dwellings, but not apartments, shops, or factories, which were relegated farther back toward the tracks. The company alleged that land worth $10,000 an acre for industry would fetch no more than $2,500 for residential use.

Ambler challenged the ordinance under the Ohio Constitution and the 14th Amendment’s due process and equal protection clauses. Government could protect health and safety, Ambler said, but it could not confiscate an owner’s freedom to use its property when the property itself posed no danger to the public.

Comprehensive zoning was still a relatively new experiment. New York City’s influential 1916 resolution, which (among other things) sought to regulate the size and height of the city’s buildings, was only a decade old when Euclid reached the Supreme Court. To supporters, zoning promised to bring scientific order to urban growth. To opponents, it entrusted the future of a city to officials who could mistake their preferences for the public welfare or succumb to lobbying.

Ambler retained Newton D. Baker, a former Cleveland mayor and Woodrow Wilson’s secretary of war during World War I. In overlapping litigation, he represented a Jewish orphanage seeking to build in University Heights, Ohio, where land-use restrictions became a tool of ethnic exclusion. Baker was thus acutely aware that a debate about the “residential character” of the city might actually be a debate about the people who wanted to reside there. Euclid’s attorney, meanwhile, was James Metzenbaum, who had helped draft the ordinance he was defending and lived on Euclid Avenue himself.

The first round went to Baker. Federal District Judge David Westenhaver, Baker’s former law partner, struck down the ordinance in 1924. According to Westenhaver, the ultimate effect of Euclid’s plan was “to classify the population and segregate them according to their income or situation in life.” But in defending the rights of both the poor and property owners, Westenhaver revealed his own prejudices: He argued that Euclid’s plan could not possibly be constitutional given that the Supreme Court had just struck down exclusionary racial zoning, which he believed was even more beneficial.

The village took advantage of a rule allowing it to appeal directly to the Supreme Court, which heard argument on Jan. 27, 1926, in the old Senate chamber (the court would move to its current location in 1935). Only eight justices were present, since George Sutherland was resting in Charleston, South Carolina, with high blood pressure.

Neither party requested a transcript, so no one knows exactly what was said at argument. But Metzenbaum later recalled a lighter moment. After Chief Justice William Howard Taft asked why Metzenbaum alternated between the term “realtors” and “real estate men,” Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes interjected that, judging from a case heard the previous week, one received more money than the other. Metzenbaum responded, “I presumed the distinction was about the same as that which exists between a statesman and a politician.” In Metzenbaum’s recollection, Taft shook with laughter, repeating the comparison.

By the time Baker finished his turn arguing, however, Metzenbaum’s good humor had evaporated. His anxiety followed him onto the train home, and, believing that Baker had misrepresented certain facts, he composed the telegram which would reach Taft by way of the snow shoveler. His worries were evidently justified. Justice Harlan Stone’s records show a 5–3 conference vote against the ordinance. But the court later agreed to hear the case again, perhaps so that Sutherland could sit for argument. The case was reargued on Oct. 12, almost exactly a century ago.

Additional argument admitted additional briefing. Cincinnati lawyer and planning advocate Alfred Bettman had missed the original amicus deadline. He sought permission to file late from Taft, whom he knew personally, and the court granted it, allowing his brief to be considered before the next argument date.

Bettman’s theory differed from Metzenbaum’s in important ways. The village’s lawyer argued for an expanding understanding of the state’s police power that would allow the government not just to regulate for public health and safety, but to promote the general welfare. Bettman, by contrast, argued that zoning was just like other nuisance laws, except it allowed localities to prevent nuisances from occurring in advance. Bettman’s theory threatened to say the quiet part out loud: certain defenders of zoning considered some people, rather than their property, a nuisance.

On Nov. 22, 1926, Sutherland announced the decision upholding Euclid’s ordinance. Taft joined him, along with Justices Holmes, Louis Brandeis, Edward Sanford, and Stone. Justices Willis Van Devanter, James McReynolds, and Pierce Butler dissented, but left no written opinion explaining why.

Sutherland’s most memorable illustration came from nuisance law: “A nuisance may be merely a right thing in the wrong place—like a pig in the parlor instead of the barnyard.” It was not exactly a flattering analogy for those who wanted to live in the type of higher-density housing banned by Euclid’s zoning ordinance. Later, Sutherland described an apartment “very often” as “a mere parasite,” benefiting from the surroundings its presence would undermine. Apartments could obstruct air and sunlight, generate traffic and noise, diminish the attractiveness of neighboring homes, and reduce the area’s residential character, he said. Though the plan would burden some businesses and owners who were not nuisances themselves, such overbreadth didn’t render the law unconstitutional on its face. (Interestingly, Sutherland mentioned how suppressing density was a means of suppressing fires, a rationale that San Francisco had used to systematically exclude primarily Chinese-owned laundries from the area and that was rejected by the Supreme Court.)

While Ambler had argued the plan was diverting industrial development away from Euclid, Sutherland concluded it had merely confined that development to certain areas. And while Euclid was engaging in a relatively new and more intrusive form of regulating property, Sutherland (quoting another opinion) responded that, “[w]ith the growth and development of the State, the police power necessarily develops … to meet the changing conditions.”

At the same time, the majority left room for future challenges. Sustaining the ordinance’s general framework did not validate every zoning plan or every application. Two years later, Sutherland himself wrote for a unanimous court in Nectow v. City of Cambridge, invalidating a residential restriction where the factual findings showed no substantial relationship to public health, safety, convenience, or welfare.

But since then, courts have overwhelmingly approved zoning and afforded localities considerable deference in crafting it.

Baker died on Christmas Day in 1937. The law firm that he helped found and that bears his name, Baker Hostetler, is one of the largest firms in the country, and buildings ranging from a public high school to a college dorm to a veteran’s hospital are named in his honor.

Metzenbaum turned his victory into a lasting professional identity, publishing “The Law of Zoning” in 1930. His wife, Bessie, had died in 1920, and he reportedly died on New Year’s Eve in 1960 trying to free his car from a snowbank while visiting her mausoleum.

Despite Euclid’s best laid plans, Ambler’s property eventually went to industrial use after all. During World War II, the federal government acquired the site to put up an aircraft engine and landing gear factory. The industrial complex now known as Nickel Plate Station sold for $18.5 million in June 2026. Apparently zoning could not predict what the community would need in the future.

A century after Euclid, the battle over zoning rages on. Proponents of zoning, as in Euclid, see it as a critical means of promoting public welfare and the surrounding environment. Opponents point to ample evidence that zoning has been used to exclude “undesirable” populations from certain parts of town, all while suppressing dynamism and inflating the cost of housing.

We have seen several Supreme Court cases establishing limits around zoning. In cases like Nollan v. California Coastal Commission and Dolan v. City of Tigard, the court limited the types of demands the government can make of property owners who apply for a development permit. And in Moore v. Cleveland, the court invalidated a zoning restriction that prevented a grandmother from living with her grandchildren. While the majority relied on the sanctity of the family, Justice John Paul Stevens wrote separately to emphasize that Euclid had “vastly diminished the rights of individual property owners,” but had not “totally extinguish[ed] them,” and thus zoning restrictions must have a meaningful relationship to the public welfare. But Euclid itself remains on firm ground, its holding having produced no written dissents but perhaps a century of consequences worth dissenting from.
