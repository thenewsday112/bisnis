A rare silver coin minted during the reign of a famous Viking king has been discovered in a massive hoard in Norway that now comprises 5,500 coins and counting.

The coin is inscribed with the name of the Norwegian king Olaf Haraldsson, who ruled from 1015 to 1028 and, as St. Olaf or "Olaf the Holy," is considered the national saint of Norway.

Fewer than 20 coins minted during Olaf's reign have ever been discovered, and the newfound coin is a "national treasure," according to a statement from the University of Oslo's Museum of Cultural History.

"Here we have a coin bearing the king's portrait and a cross, with another cross on the reverse," University of Oslo coin expert Svein Gullbekk said in the statement. "It is a Christianisation coin on a grand scale."

Olaf is credited with establishing Christianity in Norway — an early step toward its full conversion about a generation later.

"We can see how King Olaf used coinage to legitimise the new faith and to establish himself as a Christian king of European standing," Gullbekk said. "This is branding."

Haraldsson learned about Christianity when he joined Viking raids against England and France as a young man, and he was baptized in the French city of Rouen in about 1013, according to the statement.

He became Norway's king in 1015, and in about 1024 proclaimed that only Christianity would be legal, according to the University of Bergen. Much of Norway, however, remained faithful to the Norse gods during his rule, especially around the central Trøndelag region and in the eastern parts of the country.

The front of the silver coin portrays "Olaf the Holy," who is now Norway's national saint.

But Olaf was made a Christian saint shortly after his death in 1030. He is now recognized as one of the first Christian kings of Norway and the first Norwegian king to issue coins in his own name.

The newfound coin is part of the Mørstad Hoard, which was discovered near the village of Rena in April. At that time, the buried hoard was described as the largest ever found in Norway and was estimated to contain almost 3,000 silver coins. However, subsequent research has determined that it includes anywhere from 5,500 to more than 6,000 coins, the statement said.

Anne Skogsfjord, a museum researcher charged with cataloging the coins, thought the Olaf coin was an Anglo-Saxon penny at first. But then she read the inscription and realized it was minted in Norway under Olaf.

"I could see that it read ONLAF R+ NORMANORV. … Could this really be a coin of Olaf Haraldsson that I was holding?" Skogsfjord said in the statement. "And it was! It was a very special moment."

Olaf Haraldsson was one of Norway's first Christian kings; the other side of the coin features a cross.

The coin was struck between about 1024 and 1028, near the end of the Viking Age, and circulated for 20 to 25 years before someone deposited it in the hoard, the statement said.

Although the minting of the coin was a political and religious act, the coin also seems to have been used as currency; both sides bear numerous small cut marks, which was a common way of determining whether a coin was made completely of silver.

Gullbekk said Haraldsson himself had decided what the coins would look like and may have been present when they were minted.

"It is entirely possible that this very coin from Mørstad was once held in the king's own hand," he said in the statement.

What estranged parents and children need to know about memory, harm, and the limitations of our personal stories.

For many, this experience may sound achingly familiar:An adult child is naming a painful experience from their youth, often something a parent said or did that hurt them. They are typically hoping to share the truth of their experience and be met with compassion, accountability, and perhaps an apology or steps toward repair.

As family estrangement becomes more widely discussed, one critical question often goes unanswered: What do we do when two people’s memories of being in the same family are profoundly at odds?

As a hypnotherapist, I work with memory and long-held beliefs every day to support clients as they overcome fears and phobias, develop more confidence and trust in themselves, and navigate complicated family relationships.

Change is possible precisely because memory is malleable—while we may not be able to change our past, we can change the way we relate to it. This change ripples outward into our lives.

Over time and with the right support, the critical voice of a parent that we’ve carried for decades can begin to quiet, or a deeply held belief that we’re not good enough can give way to something new and more helpful. This flexibility is part of what makes healing possible, and also part of why two people can remember the same past very differently.

As memory researchers Kathleen McDermott and Henry Roediger explain, the process of remembering is reconstructive, rather than reproductive.

You might think of it like the difference between multiple iterations of an artist’s painting. Consider an artist like Monet, who painted his famous water lilies roughly 250 times over 31 years. As an artist continues to paint, their experiences, emotions, knowledge, and beliefs influence the look and feel of every painting—even those images of the same pond of water lilies. The same is true for memory. We selectively encode experiences into our memory, and then we reconstruct them every time we remember.

For one person, an important memory may grow stronger, more vivid, and more steeped in meaning over time. This can be especially true for a painful memory that gets revisited over and over, ultimately becoming part of the architecture of a person’s beliefs about themselves and the world around them.

This doesn’t mean the original memory never happened; it means that it has grown in importance and emotional charge over time. The opposite can be true for memories that aren’t deemed important or meaningful. Less significant experiences may never be strongly encoded in the first place, or may grow harder to recall over time.

This is especially important when we’re talking about differences in memory between parent and child. We remember events in part according to the meaning and emotional significance that they held for us at the time. The parent/child relationship is one that necessarily holds a significant power imbalance: a child depends on their parent for safety, emotional regulation, belonging, and survival, in a way that the parent simply does not. That can create an imbalance in meaning around even the smallest memories.

I’ve seen this with clients on both sides of estrangement. A parent shouting at a child for making a mess, being too sensitive, or doing poorly on a test may not register long-term for the parent while becoming a core part of the child’s narrative. Conversely, parents I have worked with may remember an entirely different version of reality—not the yelling or the critical remarks, but the stress they were under while working multiple jobs or going through a rough divorce.

Both memories reflect something real about each person’s experience, but because of the power imbalance, the impact may have been profoundly unequal. Any parent or adult child trying to repair their relationship needs to understand this. In fact, research also shows that memories of harm are often asymmetrical: people tend to remember being harmed more easily than harming others.

When an adult child shares a painful memory with a parent, they are rarely asking for a forensic accounting of the past. Sometimes, something as simple as “Wow, I don’t remember that clearly, but it sounds really painful. I’m sorry,” can be the springboard into a relationship with more room to grow.

On the flip side, can the grown child leave some room for the possibility that a parent may not remember the same event, not out of malice, but out of the fallibility of memory itself? When we understand this, we can explore with less reactivity.

Ultimately, repair doesn’t require that we come to an exact and agreed-upon version of the past. While we may never remember the same moment in the same way, we can become curious about why it mattered, about our impact on each other, and what we can do now in the present moment to repair.

Little did I know how frustrating that would be.

In its infinite wisdom, Google decided it wasn’t going to allow users to easily set a different app as the default mapping tool on the Pixel. It used to be very easy: install the new app, navigate to default apps, and set it as such. That’s no longer the case after the August Android update. All that said, I did finally figure out a way around this, and it’s actually kind of simple. Try it for yourself and enjoy a superior mapping app as the default on your Pixel phone. A note: I found I had to do this on other Android phones as well. The only phone type I wasn’t able to test it on was a Samsung.

The trick is easy. The first thing to do is install Waze via the Google Play Store. Once you’ve done that, if you open Waze, you won’t be prompted to set it as your default, and you won’t find a way to do so in the Waze settings.

Instead, you have to first disable Google Maps.

Open the Settings app and then tap the Apps entry.

You probably won’t see Google Maps listed at the top, unless you’ve recently opened it. If not, tap See all apps to expand the listing.

Scroll down until you find the Maps entry and tap it.

On the resulting page, you’ll see a button labeled Disable. Tap that entry so it then changes to Enable.

At this point, the only mapping app Android will be aware of is Waze. You can test that by opening an address from, say, a calendar appointment. Waze should automatically open. You might also be prompted to set Waze as the default; if so, do so.

To use Waze with Android Auto, you have to add its icon to the launcher. To do that, go to Settings > Connected Devices > Connection Preferences > Android Auto > “Customize launcher” and make sure that Waze is checked. Once added, you can then move it up in the list so it’s easier to access.

Bethany Joy Lenz didn’t just write The Betrayal of Cora Wexford. That art on the cover? She painted it, which feels especially fitting for a novel about a woman who is an artist in that very medium. Lenz is a longtime hobby painter (she does it for the joy of “slapping around on a canvas”), not a portraitist of the dead like her heroine, but she says the process gave her a window into Cora’s head: getting lost in meticulous work, where every stroke matters.

That attention to detail runs through the whole book, Lenz’s first novel after her bestselling memoir Dinner for Vampires. Set in 1888 London as Jack the Ripper terrorizes Whitechapel, it follows Cora, 38, single, childless, and running from her past, as she’s commissioned to paint the forgotten women whose deaths the city keeps shrugging off. All the while, her own relationship with a charming stranger named Miles starts to hint that darkness could be closer to her door than she even imagined. The Ripper may dominate the headlines, but Lenz is just as interested in the unspoken violence happening in living rooms everywhere, and in the people who look the other way.

When Scary Mommy caught up with the One Tree Hill alum, she opened up about everything from gaslighting and the psychology of looking away to why 45 feels like the most settled she’s ever been.

Scary Mommy: Which came first, Cora or the Ripper? How did you land on telling the stories of these "invisible" women against the backdrop of Ripper lore?

Bethany Joy Lenz: Cora was first, absolutely. The only reason I set it in Victorian times is because every time I thought about the story, that's the only way it would manifest itself in my brain. Probably because one of my favorite suspense films is Gaslight with Ingrid Bergman and Charles Boyer. So I wanted to set something in that world that maybe is like a companion to Gaslight. It's not the same story, it's not derivative, but it lives in that world and there are a lot of similar notes and themes. Once I realized that's where I wanted to set it, I actually picked 1888 because I wanted it to be around the World's Fair and the construction of the Eiffel Tower. I had to recalibrate and then realized, actually, I think this is perfect — I can parallel the heightened, feverish fear that everyone was experiencing around being a potential victim of physical violence while psychological violence was happening in living rooms everywhere, all over London. I mean all over the world, really, but for the purposes of this story, I just wanted to look at why is that so? Why is everyone wrapped up in the fear and fascination with just the physical violence when we're made up of so much more as human beings, and why doesn't the psychological violence and emotional violence get the attention that it deserves?

SM: Cora toes the line between authenticity and survival, which is what most women did then. How much of that tension felt distinctly Victorian to you, and how much kind of bleeds over into 2026?

BJL: You know what's funny is I thought that I was going to be discovering so many things that shocked me that were so different. They're not. I really thought, like, OK, so maybe we're not institutionalized now because we are experiencing fluctuations of menopause. Maybe we're not shut up behind closed doors because we might be eccentric or forced to be looked down on by society if we choose not to have children or be unmarried... I just don't think much has changed, unfortunately. So the more that I wrote, the more I researched, the more I was like, Oh, we're still here.

I think that's why I was also drawn to putting this in a time period that wasn't modern, so that it didn't feel threatening for readers to feel like it's too close to home. I think as they read, they're going to realize that it is close to home. I'm hoping that people will discover that as they're going — that things maybe have not changed as much as we would like to think that they have. But the thing is, it's timeless. These issues have existed for as long as we can imagine, and that's important to realize and remember.

BJL: It felt different writing fiction in the sense that context could be adjusted. I could take things and put them anywhere and any time and really craft the most succinct version of an amalgam of experiences that I've had, that women I know have had, and be able to tie it all together because the truth is these narcissistic personalities are so unoriginal ... It's funny because they believe that they're the most original, wonderful person that's ever existed. And they're so special that they're the most basic and beige and uninteresting people that have ever existed because they don't exist. They don't have a person within themselves to exist as.

SM: One thing that frustrated me most while reading, because I understood where Cora was coming from, was how much truth was sitting right in front of everyone and they just refused to see it. BJL: Thank you for mentioning that. I think you're right; it is very prevalent today. And what I've learned from being around this personality type for so many years of my life and seeing how the community tends to respond to the victim and the perpetrator, I think human nature just never changes, and we're always looking for the low-hanging fruit and the easy way out. The easiest thing to do is believe the person who is asking the least of you, which is always going to be the abuser or the narcissist in this scenario.

It's hard to ask people to consider that they were wrong and that they got something so wrong — that they could believe that there's this person in their community who is wearing a mask and presenting something completely false to everyone and legitimately fooling everyone, and to think that I got fooled.

It sends people into a whole well of introspection. That's the requirement in order to decide to believe a victim. You have to believe that you also have been hoodwinked, essentially, and understand that. That's really hard on the ego. And I don't mean to say it from a place of feeling judgmental because I've been in that situation too. I've been asked to believe something that I didn't see evidence for when I was much younger. #2275. How Old Is Too Old To Use a Chainsaw?

Understand the risks associated with operating a chainsaw as you age with expert-backed advice and safety tips.

While you may take great pride in your ability to take care of things around the house, be careful when it comes to using a chainsaw as you age. Unlike other powered lawn equipment, chainsaws carry a significant risk of personal injury— and can be deadly in the wrong hands.

Ahead, learn how to evaluate when you may be too old to operate a chainsaw and should call in a pro instead, with help from physical therapist Dr. Ron Miller and manual therapist Doug Bertram.

Chainsaw injuries can happen in a fraction of a second and are often due to losing control of the saw during operation. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reports roughly 36,000 chainsaw injuries each year, resulting in serious lacerations, traumatic brain injuries, limb loss or death.

While chainsaws are dangerous to operate at any age, the risks are even higher for older adults. “As we grow older, our ability to control our balance, grip strength, reflex times, eyesight and physical stamina dramatically decreases,” Miller says. “This makes it difficult to operate chainsaws.” It’s crucial that you have the strength and stamina to handle a chainsaw in all conditions to avoid injury.

If you’re not ready to put down the saw yet, consider these tips to do it safely.

Cut smaller logs and branches that are safely on the ground and avoid cutting while on a ladder or felling trees. Hire larger jobs out rather than tackling them yourself.

Consider using a lightweight, electric saw that is easier to control. Regardless of the saw, always make sure the chain is sharp and properly tightened before use.

Wear protective gear: Wearing eye and hearing protection, gloves and sturdy footwear is always sound advice, but don’t overlook chaps too, Miller says. Worn over pants and designed to stop a chainsaw chain, chaps can be the difference between a fatal injury and a superficial wound.

Never cut alone: Always have a cutting partner who remains nearby to help identify dangerous situations and call for help in the event of an accident.

Avoid working to fatigue: “Take breaks to stay awake and alert while operating a chainsaw,” Miller says. Stay well hydrated and fueled to keep your mind sharp during operation and stop when you start to feel tired.

“There’s no age that makes you too old to use a chainsaw,” Bertram says. “[But] you need adequate grip strength, adequate balance and good mental acuity.” It’s less about a specific number and more about your general health and ability. Conduct these physical fitness tests at home to gauge your grip strength, balance, leg and back strength, coordination and endurance. How you perform on these tests will give you a good idea of your functional fitness levels and whether handling a chainsaw is a good idea or not.  When in doubt, hire a pro.

For smaller limbs and logs up to 12-in. in diameter, a bow saw or reciprocating saw with a pruning blade are lower risk options.

Federal law prohibits anyone under the age of 18 from operating a chainsaw in a work setting, but there are no age limits for private chainsaw use on a residential property.

#3609. To Optimize Every Workout, Don’t Track the Same Stats. This Coach Explains the Pacing Metrics That Matter Most.

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Choose the data point that best matches the effort you’re training.

Imagine this scenario: You’re 20 seconds into a hard 400-meter lap on the track when your watch flashes a live pace 15 seconds per mile slower than your target. You surge to fix it. A few seconds later, the reading shows you’re now going 10 seconds faster than your goal, so you back off. By the end of the rep, you have spent more energy reacting to your screen than actually settling into the intended effort.

Sound familiar? Ultramarathon runner and USA Triathlon-certified coach Nick Farr, owner of P3 Fitness, calls this the “yo-yo runner” problem.

On most watches, current/live pace is based on recent GPS data, so it can lag behind rapid changes in speed. GPS interruptions from trees, buildings, tight turns, and other obstacles can make the number jump even more, and if you rely too much on it—especially during fast reps—your running starts to look similarly jumpy, which can result in a workout designed for one purpose taking on a completely different identity and failing to lead to the gains you’re looking to achieve.

“Live pace is a great reference point, but it should only be one of the data points that we use to make effective pacing decisions,” Farr says.

Instead of using live pace for every workout you do, prioritize the feedback that tells you whether the rep is unfolding as planned, which is not always simply how fast you’re running.

Depending on the session, you might use elapsed time, average pace across a full interval, or just the effort level you’re putting out. Farr’s approach matches those cues to the purpose of the workout, whether you’re building speed, stacking threshold work, or holding a steady aerobic effort over a long run.

Here’s how to choose the right pacing cue for each workout in your arsenal so they can deliver the precise dose of stress they’re designed to provide while preserving the recovery you need to keep training strong.

Before we dive any deeper, it’s important to differentiate between the pace fields that appear on your watch. They may all read minutes per mile, but each one measures a different slice of the run. Mistakenly using the wrong one can leave you chasing a number that isn’t completely accurate, takes too long to calibrate to be useful for a quicker interval, or plainly not the best way to measure your output.

Current pace is your watch’s estimate of your speed right now. It can prove helpful during a sustained effort, Farr says, but GPS delay and signal interruptions can make it slow to respond or erratic during quick changes of pace.

Lap pace averages your speed over the interval or lap you’re currently running. It’s similar to current pace, and takes a little time to settle in, but gives you a steadier read on whether longer reps are tracking toward your goal.

Overall average pace reflects the entire run (or race). It is less useful for managing a short interval, but it becomes valuable when the goal is to assess the cumulative effort of a steady run, long run, or race.

Now that you know what each of these fields actually measures, you can select the one that gives you the clearest feedback for the work in front of you, not just the default one that appeared on the first screen of your watch the day you powered it on. “The best metric is really dependent on what we’re trying to accomplish in that run,” Farr says.

For 30- to 90-second reps, especially on a track, Farr wants runners to treat the watch primarily as a stopwatch.

“Time and distance never lie,” Farr says. And in this case, the track provides the distance and the watch provides the time.

For example, during a 400-meter repeat, Farr suggests you check your elapsed time every 100 or 200 meters, and compare it to your target pace. These fixed checkpoints offer a more trustworthy pacing reference than a live GPS pace reading on a tight oval, he says. For example, if your 400-meter rep target is two minutes, you should pass the 100-meter mark (the straightaway) at 30 seconds, the 200-meter mark (½ lap) at 60 seconds, and the 300-meter mark at 90 seconds, before crossing the line right on time.

The same principle works on roads and trails, too. Use elapsed time, distance, and landmarks to see whether you are on track. On a two-minute quarter-mile rep, reaching roughly 0.12 to 0.13 miles around the one-minute mark tells you that you’re pacing correctly.

Farr also encourages runners to estimate how hard they are working on a 1 to 10 scale for rate of perceived exertion (RPE), with 10 equaling all-out effort. “If pace is the outcome and effort’s the input,” Farr says, “it’s important to pay attention to that effort piece.”

Once an interval lasts long enough for lap pace to stabilize—think 800-meter reps, mile repeats, cruise intervals, and longer tempo segments—it becomes a more useful pacing guide, Farr explains.

Lap pace shows how each interval is progressing as a whole, rather than reacting to every short-term fluctuation. You can use it to make a measured adjustment while keeping the effort controlled. If you are slightly off your target pace midway through a rep, a modest pickup may help bring your average pace closer to your goal, Farr says.

RPE still matters, though. As mentioned above, external factors can change the effort required to run a particular pace, making your perception of your own effort as important as your lap pace.

For a steady long run, overall average pace becomes more useful because the goal is managing the cumulative load of the run, not so much individual peaks and valleys.

In addition to average pace, Farr suggests using lap time in your long runs, but not the mile-by-mile auto-laps that come standard on most watch setups. Manually creating a few half-mile or mile splits in the late stages of your long run can reveal a late fade that the overall average might hide, while still allowing average pace to preserve a broader view of the run as a whole.

This is also when to let terrain shape your pace. “Maintain the same effort, not the same pace,” Kai Ng, a New York City-based RRCA- and USATF-certified run coach, previously told Runner’s World. In other words, let your pace slow on climbs and quicken on descents when the goal is a consistent aerobic effort.

Farr’s best piece of advice to runners: A new watch does not arrive with a screen that automatically fits your training. Take a few minutes to build one that makes the useful information easy to find when you need it.

Farr’s basic setup includes current pace, lap time, lap distance, and lap pace on his main screen, with heart rate on a second page. That gives him a simple foundation for most workouts. If he is running an interval session, he can hit the lap button at the start of each rep—or have his laps programmed into his watch beforehand—and immediately get a fresh set of data. On a regular easy run, leaving the lap alone preserves the total time, distance, and average pace for the full effort.

Create your own screens around the workouts you do most often. For track intervals, put lap time and lap distance where you can see them immediately. For longer intervals and tempo work, add lap pace. For long runs, surface overall average pace and a recent lap pace. On easy days, consider a stripped-down screen with time, distance, and heart rate—no pace data at all. Then practice switching screens and using the lap button on easy runs, when there is little pressure to nail a perfect split.

As you move through workouts over the next few weeks, use the above metrics to check your effort, then notice what that effort feels like. This combination can sharpen your sense of pace one rep, interval, and long run at a time. Eventually, your watch becomes a tool for confirming what you already feel, rather than a screen that dictates how you run.

Easy runs offer a case where most watch metrics should take a back seat to how you’re feeling. Farr encourages his athletes to get away from the screen as much as possible on easy runs, even covering the watch face when they need help breaking the habit of glancing down every few steps.

Use breathing rate, heart rate (yes, it’s okay to use your watch for this one—as long as you’re not locked in on your wrist to see every single fluctuation in real time), and subjective effort to keep these runs easy. Most coaches recommend keeping these runs at a 3 or 4 out of 10 RPE.

External factors like heat, humidity, and elevation can all make a familiar pace more demanding, so chasing a pace number can inadvertently turn a recovery day into a harder workout if you’re not carefully paying attention to your effort level.

Strides are short accelerations, often lasting just 15 to 30 seconds. They usually end before live pace can catch up or lap pace can become meaningful.

Farr recommends using RPE as the primary guide. Each stride rep should feel fast and powerful—aiming for a 7 to 9 RPE—while remaining controlled. It’s not an all-out sprint. Focus on relaxed shoulders, quick turnover, and smooth mechanics.

#682. The Eggs Scene in &#8216;You Can See Everything&#8217; Scrambles Reality

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

One of the most revealing scenes in the Nathan Fielder doc shows Elizabeth Holmes beating eggs — and what happens next

This review contains spoilers for the film You Can See Everything.

I saw You Can See Everything — the new documentary about disgraced Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes, co-directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim — on Thursday, and I still feel like my brain is trying to punch its way out of a bag, or like I’ve just awakened from an Inception-esque dream within a dream. It’s difficult to describe the winding, twisting, nearly three-hour film to someone unfamiliar with its many layers of context, from Fielder’s Dadaist, boundaryless approach to his subjects to Holmes’s hypnotic affect, which is either highly effective gaslighting or blinding, self-deluded earnestness. (Which is it? I still don’t know.)

What happens when you take two unreliable narrators — make that three, if you include Holmes’s partner, Billy Evans, whose on-camera persona morphs from protective, naive tech bro to unscrupulous grifter — put them in a jar, and shake them? The result is that the more questions are asked, the further the truth drifts out of reach. It’s like staring at a Magic Eye image, slightly crossing your eyes, desperate to make out the hidden 3D subject but never quite managing to see it. Eventually, you start worrying your eyes might get stuck that way.

The discourse surrounding the film is already abundant, but we’re here to talk about the eggs.

If you’re familiar with the hallmarks of Fielderian programming, you know that Fielder is prone to hyperfixating on seemingly mundane details that later return to completely upend viewers’ expectations. In You Can See Everything, one such detail is observed in an exchange between Holmes and Evans as they make eggs. How much can be gleaned from watching someone make breakfast? A lot, apparently.

In the first half of the film, Fielder is fully embedded in the rental home of Holmes and Evans, who have invited the filmmakers to document the final weeks before Holmes reports to federal prison. (How and why they agreed to this arrangement is incomprehensible if they knew anything about Fielder’s work.) During this time, the narrative takes a surprisingly sympathetic turn for Holmes, who seems intent on convincing the filmmakers — and Fielder specifically — that she is actually a sweet, misunderstood victim of circumstance, not a Machiavellian con woman who defrauded investors. Frankly, aspects of this claim feel convincing at times. Viewers are invited to witness her ostensibly less guarded moments, including a scene in which she makes breakfast with Evans. As Fielder interviews her, Holmes somewhat unenthusiastically and limply beats a bowl of eggs with a fork. The camera lingers on her feeble whisking technique. Then, after a few seconds, another set of hands enters the frame: those of Evans. Without a word, he takes the bowl and fork from Holmes and begins whipping the eggs with considerably greater fervor.

Now, backseat driving in the kitchen is hardly uncommon among couples, though it feels like a fast track to bickering. (Evans tells Fielder that he and Holmes “never fight”; Holmes is also emphatic that she’s never lied in her life. Again, unreliable narrators.) What’s less common — or at least generally considered pretty rude and aggressive — is grabbing something out of your partner’s hands mid-task to take over. Fielder points this out, asking Evans why he felt the need to grab the eggs; Holmes explains that Evans doesn’t like the way she mixes them and thinks he can do it better.

It’s a tiny interaction, but a remarkably revealing one. Evans feels comfortable taking control of something Holmes is doing, without so much as asking, and seems largely unaware of how his behavior might come across on camera. Holmes, meanwhile, appears accustomed to the intrusion, offering an explanation rather than an objection. (Granted, unlike sustained and unblinking eye contact, beating eggs does not appear to be one of Ms. Holmes’s strong suits. Typically, one does so vigorously to aerate them and make them fluffier for an omelet or scramble.)

But with this being Elizabeth Holmes, we must also ask: Could it be a case of weaponized incompetence? Is it possible that, not wanting the task of beating the eggs, she intentionally does so insufficiently to make Evans take over? Is she being controlled, or is she getting someone else to do her work for her? Or is this evidence of her “different” way of thinking, that she truly doesn’t know the “right” way to beat them?

In interviews in the film, Holmes portrays the Theranos years as a period during which she was manipulated by her former boyfriend and business partner, Sunny Balwani, and maintains that her intentions were ultimately good for humanity, regardless of the consequences for investors and patients who received unreliable test results. Whether she actually believes this account of events, or simply knows how to sell it, remains one of the film’s central mysteries — as does the extent to which her romantic relationships lead her to take the fall for the actions of bad worse men.

These questions reverberate in the second half of the film, when — with Holmes now in prison — we learn some unsettling things about Evans. He has power of attorney over Holmes, and has been using her X account to post odd, profanity-laced remarks that seem entirely incongruent with her own manner of speaking. Suddenly, the eggs scene looks like an early indication that Evans is far more domineering than the protective-partner persona he’s been presenting. His ostensible vigilance toward Holmes may be genuine on some level, but it’s difficult to reconcile with his willingness to license her image for billboards promoting another (bizarre-seeming) entrepreneur’s Theranos obsession, or his apparent acceptance of the possibility that her AI-generated likeness could be used to create pornography or other content antithetical to her interests.

And yet, even as Evans emerges as an increasingly unsavory character, Holmes remains inscrutable. Does she know what he’s doing? Does she approve? Is he exploiting her circumstances, or are they working toward a shared objective — reputational, monetary, or otherwise? The film offers kaleidoscopic evidence for competing interpretations, including that even if Holmes is being dominated, she may be content with that dynamic.

What’s extraordinary about Fielder and Oppenheim’s documentary is its optical-illusion-like layers of “truth.” Despite the film’s many harrowing close-ups of microexpressions, there is what Holmes and Evans say, then there is what they do, or have done, or plan to do (launch a Theranos 2.0 called Haemanthus, apparently). There is also Fielder’s own history as a provocateur, a faux-earnest magician who will go to virtually any lengths to actualize a concept, and who seems remarkably capable of keeping a stone-cold straight face through any scenario.

We know enough about Fielder to question his sincerity, too. There are several moments in the film where I — and the several friends I went to see the film with — wondered if there was palpable sexual chemistry between him and Holmes, and whether Fielder would act on it. If he did, we couldn’t agree on whether it would be genuine or an act of performance art, or whether he has any barriers between those things.

The film’s most revealing moments are the ones that feel off-script for everyone involved: a fleeting facial expression, an odd word choice in an otherwise emphatic statement, a series of gestures exchanged over a bowl of scrambled eggs. The subjects, who have invited themselves to be watched, have few moments when they seem to forget that; those instances are when we ostensibly have the clearest insight into their reality. Frankly, it’s still murky, and that seems to be both the point and the irony in the film’s title.

You Can See Everything elevates Nathan Fielder’s reputation far beyond simply a master of cringe humor; there is, here, a glimmer of genius. This story feels almost fateful in its ability to showcase his gifts, illuminating the bottomless strangeness, the infinite complexities, of how humans interact with one another. Even when an idea is big, it’s the little things that matter the most. And in a documentary that promises you can see everything, we learn that it’s up to us to look closely — and even then, what we see might look different in another light.

Natasha Carrington, you’ll remember, is the shady Russian go-between who set up the dodgy media meeting between Damian Cantor and some Russian string-pullers. It’s the meeting that would, in her words to Cantor, “land the biggest media deal in Russian history.” It turns out she’s an oligarch’s billionaire daughter with Kremlin ties.

Taverner finally admits she’s compromised and that her only option is to come clean and confess to treason. But Lamb, as usual, has a plan. “There’s a way out of this… you just have to fucking listen to me.”

Meanwhile, in Suffolk, Louisa, Standish, Moira and Shirley have joined up with Roddy, Emma Flyte (an ex-Dog) and Reese Nesmith (the son of the corrupt ex-Dog whom Harkness killed in episode 1). They’re holed up in Taverner’s holiday home, quaffing plonk and bickering like a family on day three of a caravan holiday. Then River arrives to tell them that Coe is dead and Sid is missing again.

And finally, bang! Harkness—River’s father—turns up with his two new henchmen to shoot the living daylights out of them. Smoke grenades shatter the windows, bullets fly, guns are grabbed and cover is dived for. It’s the Alamo in cable-knit jumpers, with less ammunition.

But back to that in a minute, because Lamb is enacting his plan… at a Russian child’s birthday party. He finds Natasha’s mansion and walks in brazenly for a chat: “I think the GRU has had you running an off-books assassination squad on British soil because the man at the top wanted to settle scores. And now all that’s gone tits up, who do you think is going to take the rap?”

She’s interested. He continues: “What if you could give Moscow the double-agent mastermind behind Kazan?”

“There is no double-agent mastermind,” she says, all skeptical.

“I’m not asking if you can buy it,” he says cryptically. “I’m asking if you can sell it.”

Lamb’s plan is simple: get Natasha to tell Moscow that it was Cantor, not MI5, who set up the Kazan hit. It was his money, after all, that inadvertently paid for it, so the paper trail does most of the talking. This also gets Taverner off the hook. She’ll pin the dirty money on Judd, the corrupt ex-Home Secretary who was holding Cantor’s cash when she confiscated it in season 4, and save her job in the process.

“Lamb hasn’t told the Russians what you used my money for yet, has he?” an increasingly panicked Cantor asks Taverner at a secret meeting in one of his hotels.

“Oh yes, he has,” she says, as icily as a Siberian winter.

And there, for the first time, Cantor loses his cool completely and tries to flee in his chauffeur-driven Bentley. Only Lamb’s in the driving seat. Cantor tries to bargain, to quibble, to prevaricate. No dice. Lamb wants one thing: for Cantor to ring Harkness and revoke Harkness’s license to kill. Cantor does it, believing it’ll save his skin. It won’t. Lamb drops him at a secret military airbase, where a plane is waiting—from Russia with love. He’s flown far, far away to face the “Kalinka,” where a grim fate awaits.

Back at Taverner’s holiday pile, all hell is breaking loose. The gang has fled the house and holed up on the beach, but things are getting desperate. Louisa has been shot in the stomach (though she will survive), and Emma Flyte has been killed by Harkness after heroically taking one of the henchmen with her (a sad end to a wonderfully hammer-and-tongs performance by Ruth Bradley).

It’s all happening at pace now. Harkness’s surviving henchman finds Roddy, Reese and Shirley hiding behind a dead tree. Just as the henchman is about to shoot Roddy in the head, Reese fires a harpoon (salvaged earlier) into the henchman’s back, and he crumples to the sand with a gurgle.

All that’s left is Harkness, the final boss. But just as he arrives on the beach, with a clear shot, he gets the call from Cantor to stand down. The bloodshed is over—except for River, who is back at the house, where Jane has finally arrived. She’s angrier than a Russian doll who’s just found out she’s the smallest one. She’s come to finish the job.

She ambushes River. There’s a fight: she has a gun, he has a table lamp, and they roll around, gouging, scratching, and biting. She flings him down the stairs. But Harkness is in the kitchen, fixing himself a drink. With a single motion, he shoots Jane in the head as she comes down to finish River off. Whatever else he is, he has no intention of killing his own son. (Another kitchen-sink performance from MyAnna Buring, by the way, sadly cut short.)

What follows is a reunion between father and son about how all parents fuck up their kids, as Larkin said. At one point, Frank actually quotes the Librarian from Hull. Then, as the choppers arrive to rescue the team, Harkness slips out the back door and escapes. Crisis over . . . But that’s not quite the end.

Finally, we return to where this piece began: River’s apparent death at his grandfather’s house, where Sid is waiting for him with a bath running. He knows she’s waiting because she left a note with Standish, worded as if it were from a neighbour, saying that a light had been left on at the house. It’s a cute reference to a story River told her in an earlier episode about how his grandfather always left a light on to let River know that he was home.

She doesn’t know River’s crept in, bottle of red in hand, ready to surprise her and finally pour his heart out. But then, before he can say a word, his literal heart stops. He appears to have a heart attack on the kitchen floor. The last thing we see is his twitching foot through a doorway. Then nothing. Man, this is no time to die—just as he was about to get a bit of how’s-your-spy-who-loved-me…

And so, folks, we reach the end of another season of Slow Horses. Taverner is back on the MI5 throne; Lamb returns to his rightful place among the detritus of Slough House with what’s left of his team.

And River? Well, if he really is dead, there is, I suppose, a quantum of solace to be found in Jack Lowden’s future. Will we see him again on Her Majesty’s Secret Service? His diary appears to have opened up, and I think there’s a lot more to Jack Lowden than meets the GoldenEye.

#3374. Stop Mashing the Pedals: 6 Lower-Body Moves That Help Cyclists Ride Smoother and Stronger

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Build balanced leg strength and a smoother pedal stroke with six cyclist-friendly moves.

Talented riders make cycling look easy. It’s true that we are all gifted with the natural ability of riders like Tadej Pogacar or Mathieu van der Poel. However, those riders didn’t get to where they are without dialing in the fundamentals.

The concept of mastering the fundamentals is a thread that runs through conversations with anyone associated with a team in the professional peloton. It can be easy to dismiss high-level riders as in a different league—in many ways they are. However, we can all improve on the basics, the fundamentals, that give a proper foundation to get faster from.

Improving consistency in your pedal stroke is a great place to start with these fundamentals. As a reformed pedal masher, I know this process all too well. I once received advice from a mentor in a bike shop who told me to think of my pedal stroke as a clock—try to target even pressure on each number around the circle. Admittedly I don’t take that approach every time, but the concept remains planted in the back of my mind as I start to mash up a hill.

Part of improving your pedal stroke comes down to improving any muscle imbalances you have in your legs, so it starts with training off the bike. Start to develop this strength as we roll into the winter. Many riders have their sights set on goals for 2027 while they bring their bikes into the local shop for the final tune-up of the year. Make this your first workout to get the season started on the right foot.

“Single-leg exercises can reduce muscle imbalances and strengthen weaker muscles,” says Nicole Simonin, a certified personal trainer. Incorporate these lower-body exercises to increase and balance out your cycling-specific strength.

How to use this list: Each of the exercises below is demonstrated by a certified personal trainer so you can master the proper form. Perform each move for the set number of reps with 30 seconds of rest between each exercise. Repeat the entire circuit 1 to 2 times for a complete lower-body workout, then add it to your routine 1 to 2 times per week.

Start standing in front of a low box or stair. Place left foot on the edge and let right foot hang free. Press through left forefoot to lift heel up, pause, then slowly lower. Perform 15 reps, then repeat on other leg.

Besides building strength and being in the right position on the bike, proper pedaling form also involves focused technique. “Incorporating the entire circle requires you to pull your foot back and up at the bottom of the stroke,” explains Simonin. “In the final up-stroke phase is a feeling of lifting your knee so you can incorporate your hip flexors. Using the circle technique allows you to not just rely on the quadriceps, but to also incorporate the hamstrings and hip flexors.” Basically, there should not be any room for wasted movement in your pedaling. Think of moving the pedals forward, down, back, and up. And keep these valuable tips in mind.

Among the best ways to achieve more fluidity in your pedal stroke are one-legged drills. One-legged pedaling forces you to pedal all the way through the stroke and develop your muscles accordingly. Whether you do this outside or inside on a trainer, try to make it a regular component of your off-season training. Start with 20 revolutions per side and increase to several minutes at a stretch. Repeat two to five times per ride for each leg.

Opt for a gearing that will help slow down the motion and really allow you to focus on your form. The idea is to eliminate the dead spot in your pedal stroke. Remember that you want to keep your toes relaxed at all times. Resist curling them up or clenching the sole of your shoe.

When on your bike, think about pulling your foot back and up as if you were scraping something off the bottom of your shoe. You should feel tension in your hamstrings, and when the pedal is lifting up, the feeling should be one of lifting your knee with an assist from your hip flexors.

Before you even start to practice pedaling circles, make sure your bike is set up correctly. “Your position on the bike is key,” says Jill Gass, a USAC-Level 1 Coach and certified skills instructor. “It is important to start with a proper professional bike fit that also looks at shoe and cleat setup. Proper bike position gives you the biomechanical foundation to improve your pedaling style and efficiency.” Specifically, proper cleat and seat height position mean the knee will be lined up correctly, and getting this trio dialed is how you can max out your power, says Gass.

If your saddle is too high, you’ll rock back and forth. If it’s too low, you’ll put undue stress on your knees. To determine proper saddle height, sit on your bike while leaning against a wall. Now drape both feet straight down. If your saddle height is set correctly, your heel should just graze the pedal when the crank arm is in the 6 o’clock position.
