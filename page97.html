#1734. Why Clorox shifted from creating brand campaigns to systems of relevance

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Let Marketing Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

CMO Eric Schwartz dug into efforts by Pine-Sol, Brita and Hidden Valley at ANA Masters of Marketing.

ORLANDO — The age of marketers being able to predictably plan, launch, measure and repeat campaigns is over. Most consumers expect brands to keep up with quick-moving online culture, and marketers have rapidly scaled up their content production to meet those needs. Artificial intelligence — both its adoption by consumers and integration into marketing organizations — is accelerating everything.

“The approaches and intuitions that came along with growing up in a world that was about big-bang campaigns actually can work against us all now,” said Eric Schwartz, CMO of The Clorox Company, during a presentation at the Association of National Advertisers Masters of Marketing conference on Oct. 22. “If the world is going mad and traditional campaigns aren't enough anymore, what replaces them?”

For Clorox, the marketer of cleaning supplies and other household brands since 1913, campaigns have been replaced with systems of continuous relevance that identify opportunities for brands to learn, adapt and improve. The marketer has deployed such systems for brands including Pine-Sol, Brita and Hidden Valley.

“It's not about showing up once with a big idea. It's about showing up consistently in the moments that matter for choice,” Schwartz said. “Instead of taking months to frame a brief and get a dozen decision-makers aligned to queue up and launch a yearlong campaign, how do we create something different?”

Clorox is building these systems with an engine that is powered by people, enabled by AI automation and amplified by partners. The people, “brand lions” in Clorox’s parlance, are bold, curious marketers who quickly spot signals and turn them into action. The power of AI is giving brand lions that ability to move faster, make small bets and enjoy freedom within a framework. Finally, agency partners across creative, media, technology and measurement help Clorox identify, execute and scale opportunities.

“When our marketers, technology and partners work together, something powerful happens. Less time talking, less time deciding, more time doing and learning. Ideas get into market faster. Learning happens faster, and improvement happens while those opportunities are still alive out there in that fast-moving world. All of this drives better consumer experiences, stronger business results. It catalyzes growth,” Schwartz said.

The executive, appointed CMO in March 2022, dug into several examples of a new model for Clorox brand marketing that prioritizes “intentional but flexible” and “insightful but bite-sized” efforts.

In Clorox’s new marketing system, Pine-Sol, the nearly 100-year-old household cleaning brand known for its signature scent and cleaning power, needed to rethink social media as an always-on system for learning from and acting on consumer signals.

The result is “OMG Da Pine,” a social-first brand world on platforms including Instagram and TikTok where Pine-Sol can connect with consumers through original audio and meme graphics. After a year, Pine-Sol took its alter ego from content to commerce, launching four limited-edition products on TikTok Shop that sold out in hours. The brand has also brought learnings around scent to traditional retail environments with a seasonal apple cinnamon-scented product that launched for fall.

With the OMG Da Pine world, the brand — which was previously nearly dark on social — averages more than 150 organic posts a month, creates and tests content with more than 80 cohorts and averages a 13% engagement rate.

“Listen, adapt, expand and follow where consumers take the brand, and the results speak for themselves,” Schwartz said. “The most important one for CMOs is the marketing mix analytics that showed that we were increasing dramatically our effectiveness and our efficiency in the world of social as a system.”

Clorox was able to put a similar system in place for Brita, the water filtration brand, as a way to reach Gen Z consumers who are on their own for the first time. Social listening uncovered an insight around how Gen Zers see staying hydrated as an achievable act of self-care during complicated, overwhelming moments. The insight led Brita to launch a social-first, high-volume content model that organically produces more than 100 assets per month.

“At this volume, our algorithmic reach provided signal, as did engagement and dialogue, continually shaping the interaction and the content we created,” Schwartz said. “It was a whole different pace, a whole different metabolism for us inside the organization.”

Brita found success with a lo-fi 3D shark animation and an original pop song that reached 10 million views before the brand spent anything on paid media. That effort was the spark for the “At Least I’m Hydrated” creative platform that pairs hydration with daily struggles using awkward animations, stock backdrops and deadpan humor.

The efforts yielded 22 million engagements with a 8.17% engagement rate and a 1,742% year-over-year increase in organic impressions, per data Schwartz shared on stage. According to media mix analysis, the brand’s effectiveness and efficiency was “off the charts,” the executive said.

“Brita’s approach worked because the team turned a real Gen Z truth into platform-native content, and then used continuous listening and learning to scale what resonated with that audience,” Schwartz said.

Clorox in January launched Clorox Pure Allergen Neutralizer, a new line of air and fabric sprays, with a campaign created with agency BBDO Chicago, but even traditional ads are evolving within Clorox’s new systemic approach.

By using AI, Clorox was able to use in-market signals to optimize ad creative to better reflect which occasions and products were driving consideration and trial of the new brand. The new model allowed Clorox to rework its creative asset, without the cost of a reshoot or the time needed for a traditional campaign post-mortem. But the speed and savings were not the point.

“Even on big, well-supported launches [and] campaigns, the Clorox Pure team didn't launch and walk away. They launched, they learned, they optimized and they kept improving while the content was still live,” Schwartz explained. “The content is no longer finished at launch. It's built to keep getting better.”

This summer around the World Cup, Clorox’s ranch dressing brand Hidden Valley had an unexpected moment in the spotlight as TSA weighed in on international travelers packing the condiment in their carry-ons. Within hours of TSA posting about its policies, Hidden Valley replied with its own content and quickly developed a plan.

Clorox tapped social, PR and even a team of interns to build an organic content machine that generated more than 600 pieces of social content. The brand’s broader effort notched more than 6 billion impressions, 2,500 media placements and helped turn around market share that had been dragged by inflation.

“That's what happens when the relevance engine really starts to pur. Years of brand building gave us the foundation, and our people made it relevant in real time, with partners helping amplify it,” Schwartz said.

For marketers of all sizes and budgets, Schwartz provided three ways to bring Clorox’s lessons to life: move away from campaign calendars to continuous learning loops; remove friction between signal and action; and act before certainty exists. A new world requires a new way of marketing.

“Our old instincts won't keep up. Sometimes they won't even be right,” Schwartz said. “The only solution to match the pace is learning to build systems of continuous relevance.”

#679. A Solar Storm Heading Toward Earth Suddenly Changed in Ways Scientists Didn’t Predict

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Scientists have uncovered surprising behavior inside a powerful solar eruption that challenges existing models and could change how researchers predict space weather.

A massive eruption on the Sun sent a cloud of magnetized plasma racing toward Earth, where it underwent an extraordinary transformation. Over a distance of just 13 million miles, the cloud expanded by 21% as the plasma inside became three times hotter, defying expectations about how these solar structures evolve.

Researchers led by the University of Iowa documented the rapid growth using observations from two spacecraft positioned along the cloud’s path. Their findings challenge existing models of solar storms and could improve predictions of space weather events capable of disrupting satellites, navigation systems, and electrical grids.

The cloud originated from a coronal mass ejection on November 4 and 5, 2021. These powerful solar eruptions release enormous quantities of hot, electrically charged gas threaded with magnetic fields. Some form magnetic clouds containing twisted bundles of magnetic field lines that travel through interplanetary space.

As the crescent-shaped cloud approached Earth, the Solar Orbiter spacecraft recorded its structure approximately 78 million miles from the Sun. The Wind spacecraft later measured the same cloud about 91 million miles from the Sun. Both spacecraft happened to lie along nearly the same line, allowing scientists to compare the cloud’s properties at two relatively close points in its journey. The eruption also produced a major geomagnetic storm on Earth.

“First, it’s rare these two spacecraft would be aligned on a Sun-Earth axis,” says Shirsh Soni, a postdoctoral research fellow in physics and astronomy at the University of Iowa. “Then, second, for them to be aligned like that during an interplanetary coronal mass ejection is so much rarer.”

The measurements showed that the magnetic cloud expanded at approximately 192 kilometers per second (119 miles per second), substantially faster than the typical range of 50 to 100 kilometers per second (31 to 62 miles per second). The researchers called this unusually rapid growth a “super expansion.”

The process began when the cloud encountered the surrounding solar wind, a continuous stream of charged particles flowing outward from the Sun at speeds that can reach approximately 1 million mph. The interaction initially compressed the cloud, but its internal plasma subsequently heated to three times its earlier temperature, driving the dramatic expansion.

“In a typical interplanetary coronal mass ejection, when it encounters the solar wind, it could decelerate or accelerate,” Soni explains. “In this case, it expanded a lot.”

The team also discovered that the cloud’s magnetic field pressure remained unchanged despite its substantial growth. This behavior differed from predictions produced by existing models, suggesting that magnetic clouds may undergo more complex changes during their journeys through the solar system than previously recognized.

Because the two spacecraft observed the same cloud along its path, the researchers could directly track changes that would have been difficult to distinguish using a single observation point. Such measurements are particularly valuable for understanding how coronal mass ejections develop before reaching Earth, where their magnetic fields can interact with Earth’s magnetosphere and trigger geomagnetic disturbances.

“We have assumed that magnetic clouds from interplanetary coronal mass ejections will evolve in the same way,” says Soni, the study’s corresponding author. “In this study, we show the magnetic cloud can expand dramatically in a short period of time and space, which could have impacts to Earth that we wouldn’t have known about.”

Reference: “Superexpansion of interplanetary coronal mass ejection observed by Solar Orbiter and Wind spacecraft within 0.14 au radial separation” by Shirsh Lata Soni, Ankush Bhaskar, R Selva Kumaran and David Miles, 24 April 2026, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society. DOI: 10.1093/mnras/stag350

#3447. “My Runs Are Soft, Smooth, and, Once Again, Satisfying”

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

After three runs in the On Cloudsurfer 3, this writer finds her groove again.

I’ll let you in on a little secret: Until recently, I’d been dreading my runs. All the joy in them was gone. They felt more like a chore than an escape. They were just…hard. Every time out, my body felt heavy, my mind wandered, and staying in bed seemed so much more appealing. I needed to find my spark again: the glide, the levity, the freedom.

That’s why I decided to try On’s Cloudsurfer 3 for a few days. I was ready for a major change, something that would help me enjoy my runs again, and maybe this was it. After decades of logging miles, I wasn’t ready to accept that running wasn’t working for me anymore, mentally or physically. Luckily, after three of the softest running experiences I’ve ever had, I know I have still have many miles left in me.

Here’s my day-by-day account of testing the Cloudsurfer 3 across New York City parks, stairs, and hills:

I’ve been resting for a couple of days and my plan is to do a couple of easy miles. Unlike my other shoes, the Cloudsurfer 3 is cushioned—hyper-cushioned compared to anything that I have run in before. After lacing them up, I like how the upper mesh forms around my feet and makes them feel tucked in.

Then, I stand. Instantly I feel taller, which throws me off. I figure the extra height will translate into a soft ride, since that’s the whole point of the Surreal™ superfoam in the midsole and the geometry of the outsole. I know I’ll get used to it.

Once I’m outside, the moment of truth comes with my first propulsion. Immediately, everything seems lighter, smoother, softer. The extra height seems to be worth it. The levity and the spring from when I first started running are back; my gait is smoother than it’s been in a while, and I keep going. My plan to keep this run short gets smoked. The final tally is 5.25 practically effortless miles. When I return home, my legs don’t feel heavy and my post-workout yoga isn’t met with the normal “oohs” and “eeks.”

Last night, I dreamt about running. I can’t remember the last time that happened. My body feels good. No aches and pains like I usually have after my first run of the week. There might be something to a cushioned shoe after all. I decide to tackle some stairs and hills with a short out-and-back route to Central Park’s Bethesda Fountain stairs.

Running the steps, my toe strikes feel bouncy on the down phases and I’m able to get my knees up higher on the up. Both my heart rate and RPE rating—an 8 or 9 out of 10—tell me this is a tough workout. I climb the hill back home in a run-walk to recover, and this is when the rolling motion of the shoe feels more pronounced to me. The hill seems easier than in the past, and my heart rate recovers sooner. So I decide while exiting the park that I’m going to do streetlight intervals, alternating running and sprinting from one light to the next.

I dreamt about running again. Specifically, running Harlem Hill in the park. For those who aren’t familiar, this hill is a bear. Even though it’s only 0.3 miles, the incline can reach close to 8 percent at the top and averages around 4.5 percent. It’s brutal, and I haven’t run this hill since a half-marathon I did over a year ago, but I’m game to tackle it when I wake up. Yesterday’s runs piqued my curiosity about the Cloudsurfer 3’s performance on a more substantial incline.

Navigating the downhill switchbacks to get to the hill’s base is controlled and fast. I don’t feel like I’m pounding or lumbering down. Each step rolls from heel to toe, supported at every touchpoint. It’s a soft, smooth ride. But the real test of the day is still to come.

As I head up the hill, the roll continues to help propel me forward. The effort is greater than the day before (but so is the incline). There’s less pain than in previous passes of this run. When I get to the top I’m still winded, but my legs and glutes aren’t nearly as tired as I remember. I finish the second half of my run easily. When I get home, my overall pace says it all: 45 seconds faster per mile compared to day 1.

Thanks to Cloudsurfer 3, I found my running groove again.

#1326. I’m a Chef on a GLP-1. I Believe the Future of Food Is About to Change Dramatically.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

”I don’t want less pleasure. I want less eating that isn’t actually giving me pleasure.”

I HAVE SPENT much of my adult life traveling the world in pursuit of food. As a chef, obsessive eater, and amateur food anthropologist, I have planned trips around eating: multi-day street-food crawls, three or four restaurants in a day, 10-, 20-, even 40-course tasting menus. At the time, it didn’t feel excessive. It felt like research. It felt like joy. It felt like my job.

But my relationship with food started somewhere very different.

I grew up modestly and was taught early that you cleaned your plate. Food was something you respected because you didn’t always have an abundance of it. Wasting food was unacceptable.

That lesson followed me into my career. As a chef, I’m acutely conscious of food waste—not only because I know how much labor and resources go into getting food onto a plate, but because I think constantly about the environmental cost of what we eat.

So for much of my life, I had two ideas operating simultaneously—food was precious and shouldn’t be wasted, but food was also something to pursue, experience, and celebrate as fully as possible.

I entered my late 40s mid-pandemic, facing a dramatically slower metabolism, COVID weight gain, and a torn meniscus. I knew how to lose 40 pounds. I had done it a few times before, but the usual cardio, weightlifting, intermittent fasting, eating keto, and weekly 24-hour fasts weren’t delivering the results they once had. Losing the weight took twice as long. Despite the initial public backlash around GLP-1 medications, I saw a potential lifeline from the weight-loss yo-yo I’d experienced so many times before.

I’ve been on tirzepatide for two years and it’s worked. I’m surprised by how the medication has changed my life in many ways—but especially when it comes to my relationship with food.

I still love bold flavors, food with depth and intensity, and dishes that make you pause after a bite because there is so much happening. But what has changed is my decreased appetite for everything around that experience—the extra bites, the sweets I ate because they were there, the late-night food I reached for because I was tired rather than hungry.

Before taking a GLP-1, dinner was often a bowl of meat scraps over rice at 10:00 pm after dinner service. Dessert was sometimes an emotional event—something sweet because I was tired or stressed. That behavior has largely disappeared. The meds have quieted my noise and laziness around eating.

And guess what? I actually enjoy food more now.

When I’m actually hungry, I want food that delivers. I want something nutrient-dense and deeply flavorful—protein, fruit, vegetables, grains, spice, acid, texture—food that satisfies me physically and gastronomically. A perfectly seasoned and grilled piece of charred-skin salmon with citrus and spices and some sautéed greens can be electric and gives me everything I want from a meal: richness, umami, freshness, depth.

And my new mindset has made me consider something I have examined throughout my career: the American restaurant culture of abundance. The 24-ounce ribeye, the double-stack burger, the loaded fries, outrageous dessert mash-ups.

Pre-GLP-1s, the rise of places like Sweetgreen and the broader fast-casual wellness movement showed that people were hungry for food that was fresh, nutrient-dense, customizable, and satisfying without necessarily being enormous. High-protein, plant-forward, whole-food, and “clean eating” language had become part of mainstream food culture. GLP-1s have only accelerated this trend.

Yet we're still surrounded by the message that more is better. We're encouraged to maximize protein, add supplements to our smoothies, track macros, and optimize every meal. The “healthy” bowl has to look packed with ingredients. But now, on a GLP-1, I wonder whether satisfaction has ever really been about volume.

I have always been drawn to foods that are satiating not because they are nutrient-dense but because they are gastronomically complete—a dish with acidity, heat, texture, richness, and enough flavor that your brain registers an experience. Give me a bite of juicy, skin-on chicken thigh over an oversized skinless breast any day. The thigh brings richness and flavor; the breast is always one overcooked minute away from becoming a punishment.

That feels particularly relevant now, as nutrient density has become central to how we think about a meal. The conversation is increasingly about getting more value from what we eat rather than simply eating less.

And that is how I think about it now. I don’t want less flavor. I want more flavor per bite. I don’t want less pleasure. I want less eating that isn’t actually giving me pleasure. I didn’t lose my appetite. I lost the noise around it.

And I still absolutely love indulgence. Recently, I was at a friend’s restaurant and the food was so good that I simply could not stop eating 15 dishes in, I was over-stuffed but also wholistically full. I don’t regret a single bite.

That matters to me because I don’t want the lesson of the GLP-1 revolution to be that eating less is always better. It isn’t. Food is pleasure. Food is culture. Food is celebration. There are meals worth surrendering to if we have the privilege to have them.

I still want the extraordinary meal. I still want the dish that I remember forever. I still want to travel across the world to eat something I’ve never experienced before. What has changed is that I no longer feel compelled to eat everything simply because it is there.

As a chef, I don’t think the goal is necessarily to make people eat more. It’s to make them care more about what they eat. Food is meant to feed our bodies and souls. Let’s keep it that way.

Gregory Gourdet is the author of the cookbook Everyone’s Table, and the chef and owner of Kann, a wood-fired Haitian restaurant in Portland, Oregon.

#2913. Climber Falls to Death on Ama Dablam

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Search teams have found the body of Wolfgang Waschiczek, 57, of Germany, who went missing after he reached Camp 3 on his way down from the summit. Evidence shows he fell 1,700m to his death.

Waschiczek was one among over 200 climbers who lined up toward the summit of Ama Dablam this past weekend. He reached the top on October 25 with guide Sonam Sherpa, but for some reason the two of them stopped descending at a rather high point on the mountain.

“The pair decided to stay at Camp 3 overnight after borrowing a sleeping bag and other gear from other climbers,” his expedition outfitter, Real Himalaya Nepal, told The Tourism Times.

Climbers above Camp 3 on Ama Dablam. Photo: Dorjee Gyalzen Sherpa

According to the outfitter, the climbers prepared to continue down this morning. Lama asked his client to stay in place while he returned the sleeping bag to the tent where he had borrowed it. When the Sherpa returned, Waschiczek was nowhere to be found, but his crampons were still there, which suggested a freak fall. A search helicopter was called, and the remains of the climber were found this afternoon at around 4,500m.

Waschiczek is the first fatality on Ama Dablam this season.

Together with Manaslu, Ama Dablam is the most popular fall mountain in Nepal. The latest update from Nepal’s Department of Tourism on October 17 showed that over 300 foreign climbers had applied for a climbing permit. That day, the rope-fixing team opened the route to the top, and a massive summit wave started a couple of days later.

Virtually all foreign climbers are guided by one or two Sherpas, so the summit rates and numbers are remarkable. Local media estimate that over 200 climbers, among locals and foreigners, have topped out so far this season. Further teams should join them over the rest of October and November. However, no matter how tamed with ropes and anchors Ama Dablam gets, a fall from its steep slopes is usually lethal.

ReadyOn has detailed a four-layer tenant isolation architecture for its Harmony workforce intelligence platform running on Amazon Elastic Kubernetes Service (EKS). The design combines Kubernetes namespaces, dedicated Karpenter node pools, Amazon VPC security groups, and per-tenant Amazon Aurora databases to limit cross-tenant access across the Kubernetes, compute, network, and data layers.

Harmony processes payroll data, organizational hierarchies, and operational analytics for enterprise customers. ReadyOn maps its controls to a 10-stage multi-tenant threat model, with particular attention to lateral movement from a compromised tenant workload. The company says adversarial exercises have not produced a successful cross-tenant path to date.

Mohammad Akbarpour, professor at Stanford Graduate School of Business, discussing ReadyOn’s security architecture in a LinkedIn post, described the broader challenge as:

pushing AI capabilities forward while making the boundaries around inaccessible data and workflows difficult to cross.

William Antonio G, principal AWS engineer at Sparq, similarly emphasized the change in the security model when multiple tenants share infrastructure. In a LinkedIn post discussing the architecture, he wrote,

For single-tenant clusters, hardening is a well-documented challenge. But when you add multi-tenancy, the threat model fundamentally changes.

The first layer uses a dedicated Kubernetes namespace for each tenant, with RBAC, resource quotas, admission controls, and default deny network policies. ReadyOn uses Argo CD ApplicationSets to generate these resources from Git based configuration, providing the same baseline controls across tenants and automatically reconciling unauthorized changes.

The second layer isolates compute. Karpenter provisions dedicated node pools for each tenant using a dual taint strategy. Pods must tolerate both a tenant-identifying taint and a workload-type taint before they can be scheduled. An admission controller also verifies that requested tolerations correspond to the tenant associated with the namespace.

ReadyOn’s Four Walls: namespace, compute, network, and data isolation and the cross-tenant paths blocked by each layer (Source: AWS Blog Post)

The third layer moves enforcement below Kubernetes. Per-tenant VPC security groups restrict database connections to the corresponding tenant node pool, while Kubernetes network policies enforce default deny behavior between namespaces. The EKS API endpoint is private and accessed through VPN, OpenID Connect, and multi-factor authentication.

The fourth layer separates tenant data through dedicated Aurora clusters rather than relying on row-level filtering in a shared database. ReadyOn also uses tenant-scoped AWS Secrets Manager paths, short-lived credentials through IAM Roles for Service Accounts, and dedicated observability backends.

AWS documentation notes that Kubernetes namespaces, RBAC, quotas, and network policies provide logical isolation but do not prevent workloads from sharing underlying nodes. It recommends node selectors, affinity rules, or taints and tolerations when stronger workload separation is required.

The layered model has also prompted discussion about whether isolation controls can be verified effectively without corresponding observability.

Juan Pablo Gaviria, an engineer who commented on the architecture on LinkedIn, argued that traces need to connect workload identity with database and network enforcement to expose gaps between controls.

Isolation depth without observability depth is indistinguishable from false confidence.

ReadyOn therefore retains a shared EKS environment while extending tenant boundaries beyond Kubernetes into scheduling, AWS networking, and dedicated data infrastructure. The architecture treats the controls as overlapping defenses rather than assuming any individual layer provides complete isolation.

A buyer can arrive through Google after making the important part of the decision somewhere else.

Imagine a buyer asks an AI assistant which supplier fits a particular problem. Your business makes the shortlist. The buyer discusses the alternatives, checks a concern, then opens Google and searches for your company.

They visit and buy. Depending on the attribution setup, Google may receive the credit. The assistant need never have sent a visit at all.

What happened before the visit we managed to record?

In GEO is a distraction, Marie Haynes reports that ChatGPT sessions amounted to 0.62% of Google organic sessions across her sample of 100 sites. Her conversion analysis also favours Google on total volume. Those are useful observations about the properties she examined. I haven’t inspected the underlying GA4 data.

Marie also recognizes the prospect of transactions happening without a site visit and argues for preparing for agentic commerce. I share her concern about chasing mentions or treating weak evidence as the basis for a new discipline. Where I disagree is how far referral figures can take us in judging the broader effort to influence how AI represents a business.

A report about arrivals cannot, by itself, settle what influenced the people arriving.

Around me, I see people increasingly asking an assistant first and using Search afterwards. That’s an observation about the people I encounter, rather than evidence that everyone behaves this way.

It does make me question what a Google visit means. Someone searching for a company could be starting their investigation. They could also be following a recommendation or checking a shortlist made during an earlier conversation.

A buyer might use an assistant to understand a problem before they even know which category of product they need. The assistant could introduce a company, or describe an alternative so poorly that the buyer rules it out. In either case, the answer could influence the decision without sending the buyer to a website.

That gives us a reason to investigate. It doesn’t tell us how often this happens or how much business it creates.

I’m also wary of treating difficult attribution as evidence that a large amount of invisible value must exist. The gap leaves us with a question about what happened before the visit. It doesn’t give us a revenue estimate, however plausible the journey sounds. I’d want to see that evidence before putting a revenue number in the proposal.

I’ve been skeptical of using new initialisms for work SEO already covered. When someone asks what GEO does that SEO doesn’t, I think that’s a fair question. If we’re going to use a separate name, I want to know what work I couldn’t already describe as SEO.

The distinction that makes GEO useful to me is parametric memory: the knowledge and associations a model has learned through training, represented in its numerical weights. Influencing what it learns about a brand, and how it can use that knowledge in relevant searches and answers, is the separate job I mean by GEO.

Retrieval supplies external information while the system is answering. In retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, the model uses retrieved material alongside what it has already learned. The original RAG paper explicitly combines the two.

Imagine asking a specialist colleague for advice. They bring what they’ve already learned, and they can open a reference folder to check something they need for your question. Changing the folder changes the information available for that consultation. Changing what the colleague has learned involves a different process.

A model’s weights don’t work like a person’s memory, and its answer doesn’t neatly identify which contribution came from which source.

SEO addresses the web discovery and retrieval work: making relevant sources discoverable, accessible and suitable for the question. A page being available, being retrieved and contributing to an answer are separate outcomes. SEO doesn’t own the entire answering system.

For Google’s AI search features, Google itself says established SEO practices remain relevant and no special optimization is required. Calling familiar SEO work GEO gives it a new label. I’d still like to know what changed in the work.

My working shorthand is AI visibility = SEO + GEO: information available through retrieval, alongside associations already learned. Instructions and user context also matter, so the shorthand doesn’t describe everything that shapes an assistant’s response.

A page could be retrieved today and become candidate training material later. Separating the two routes helps explain how the information reaches the answer, even when the same page might eventually contribute through both.

When a language model generates search queries, its learned associations may influence where the search starts. Google describes query fan-out as issuing related searches across subtopics and data sources.

There is relevant evidence in What Drives AI Citations?. Researchers compared brands recommended without search with brands included in the system’s generated queries. In the OpenAI API configuration they tested, brands recommended without search appeared in queries in 67% of cases, against 11% otherwise.

Asking without search tells us what the model recommends under those conditions, rather than revealing everything it knows. The comparison is useful, though it leaves open whether changing the training would change the searches. That would need a different experiment.

For me, this adds another reason to care about parametric memory. An association between a brand and a buyer’s problem could give that brand a head start in the searches used to investigate the problem. Memory could therefore affect which evidence gets a chance to reach the answer.

The same study observed the system picking up niche-vendor names from an initial round of search results. A category search can introduce an unfamiliar brand, and a user can supply a name the model didn’t previously know. Instructions and retrieved information can also shape subsequent searches.

My interest is in testing whether learned associations change the searches a system makes. What a model recalls may affect the question it sends to Search, before any resulting visit appears in analytics.

The Outlandish paper shows one way training can change associations.

Researchers inserted texts into controlled training runs. Learning an association could raise a keyword’s probability in other contexts, especially within the same theme. They call that spillover priming. Putting the text in a prompt produced substantially less of this effect than training on it.

A keyword appearing in an irrelevant answer could make the answer worse. I’d want to read the answer before celebrating the extra mention.

Repetition raises a different question. It can improve a model’s ability to reproduce a passage from its training data. That is a form of learning, but successful recitation doesn’t establish that the model can use the facts when asked a different question. In controlled biography experiments, researchers trained smaller models from scratch and found that changing how the same facts were presented during training improved factual answering.

That gives us something we can study. With open-weight models, whose learned parameters are publicly available, you can download a model and run controlled experiments. Where training code and data are available too, you can change the material, compare versions, and test what the model does differently. Research resources such as Pythia provide models saved at different stages of training, alongside code and data.

A well-controlled comparison can establish an effect within the experiment’s conditions without revealing its entire mechanism. What we test depends on what we want to establish: improved factual answers and different generated searches are distinct outcomes. When testing learned associations, we also need to control the information supplied through retrieval or the conversation.

In those experiments, the researchers control training. A business publishing pages doesn’t control whether a provider includes those pages in training or what the model learns from them. Seeing a page published, crawled, or cited therefore doesn’t establish that the model has learned something useful from it. An uncited reply alone wouldn’t establish a change in weights either.

My interest is commercial too. I work at geoSurge, which has been doing this kind of research for a couple of years now. That experience is part of why I take the possibility of influencing learned associations seriously.

We can’t assume that findings from open-weight models carry over to the closed models that OpenAI, Anthropic or Google use in production. The experiments give us evidence about the models and conditions tested. Whether those findings hold in a particular production system needs separate evidence.

The outcomes are statistical. I want to know how often a result holds across the questions and conditions tested, and how large the change is. A useful improvement needn’t mean every future answer will go our way. I’d want evidence across those conditions before drawing a conclusion, rather than relying on a few responses that happened to look encouraging.

I’ve made this distinction before: counting an appearance and explaining it require different evidence. These studies give me reasons to investigate how facts are taught, beyond counting copies. They don’t establish a publishing recipe for brand recommendations; whether an approach produces a useful result for a business still needs commercial validation. Putting the paper on a sales slide doesn’t perform that study for you.

Nati Elimelech recently posted publicly on LinkedIn about the economics of running a recipe site. Their Search traffic was rising after a successful refactor. They were questioning the business model even while the SEO was working.

For a service business, an assistant’s recommendation can still lead to someone being hired. With a recipe, the ingredients and instructions may be the whole thing the person wanted. Once those have been supplied elsewhere, the publisher can lose both the visit and the chance to turn that person into a regular reader. The recipe has already done its job for the reader. The publisher still has to pay for producing it.

In my reply, I focused on the production pipeline. I think the harder question is what gives someone a continuing reason to depend on the original source after consuming one of its answers.

Training, assistant fetches, and AI search each need their own case for what the publisher gives up and receives. I wouldn’t dismiss blocking as pointless. My doubt is that blocking alone can solve the longer-term business problem.

The direction I want to investigate is how producing and maintaining information could make the source worth consulting again. That might involve information whose value depends on continued testing or verification. I mean giving the source continuing value through that work, rather than simply increasing the volume of pages published.

I expect many websites to evolve towards fewer pages, with more of the business accessible through API endpoints and MCP connections that let assistants use its data and services. For some businesses, those interfaces could become the main way customers interact with them, leaving a smaller site for people to browse. An assistant could request current information or use a service on someone’s behalf without sending them to a page. If visits remain our default KPI, we’d be judging those interactions by a visit that never needed to happen.

A recipe doesn’t become obsolete simply because an assistant has read it. Some publishers may find no viable return from allowing a particular kind of access. Even a system that keeps consulting the source needn’t cite it, send a visitor, or pay for it.

A publisher could remain useful as a source while losing the visit that funded the work. Keeping a system dependent on your information would still leave the question of how you get paid. Before choosing a visibility metric, the publisher needs a business case for continuing to supply that information.

For a supplier seeking qualified demand, I want to know whether relevant buyers encounter the business, understand what it offers, and become more likely to choose it. Observations about answers can help investigate that. They don’t automatically measure the resulting demand.

Asking an assistant about a named company supplies the name. Asking it to help solve a buyer’s problem leaves it to identify possible suppliers. A mention could then be a recommendation, a warning or a correction. A dashboard could color all three green because the name appeared. A warning about your company is a strange thing to put in the wins column.

Buyer research and self-reported discovery can help investigate journeys that referral reports miss. Where feasible, controlled comparisons can test whether an intervention improves an outcome. Neither a rise in branded searches nor a nicer answer, taken alone, proves incremental revenue.

I want to understand what happened in that earlier conversation. The weak pitches around AI visibility don’t make the question go away. An agent capable of completing a transaction still has to arrive at a choice.

In the journey I opened with, the supplier has to earn the sale, and Google helps the buyer reach it. The assistant also has a part in that journey: it helped decide whose name went into the search box.

Google gets the thank-you. I’d like to know who made the introduction.

#874. Nerds Is Selling A New Frozen Dessert Inspired By Gummy Clusters: ‘O-M-G!’

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Nerds Gummy Clusters have officially taken over the candy world, and now they’re coming for the freezer aisle.

The brand announced the new Nerds Crunch Pop, a frozen dairy dessert with a gummy candy center and a crunchy Nerds coating. It's not available yet, but it’s expected to reach stores soon.

There’s currently one flavor announced, featuring strawberry-flavored frozen dessert and Very Berry Nerds. But once the treat rolls out, we can only hope the lineup eventually gets even bigger.

This also isn’t the first time we’ve seen a candy company make the jump to frozen desserts. Chocolate brands like Snickers, Butterfinger, and Reese’s have long had a presence in the freezer aisle. And last year, Trolli debuted Gummi Pops inspired by its Sour Brite Crawlers. Now Nerds is putting its own spin on the candy-meets-frozen-dessert idea.

Candy lovers immediately took to social media to share their thoughts on the new product.

“O-M-G! This sounds yum. I hope ice cream trucks and/or Dollar Tree will carry these,” one Instagram user wrote. “My 10-year-old self would love to have had these,” another commented.

But there are already some complaints, specifically about the term “frozen dairy dessert.”

“Why always dairy dessert how hard is it to give us real milk?” one Instagram user asked. Another added, “I’ll be excited when you start making actual ice cream again…”

For the record, Nerds Crunch Pops do contain real dairy: The ingredients list includes milk, cream, and buttermilk. “Ice cream,” however, has its own FDA standard of identity with specific composition requirements, so a product containing dairy doesn’t automatically qualify for the name. Nerds Crunch Pops are labeled frozen dairy dessert instead.

Whatever you call it, between its gummy center, Nerds shell, and frozen strawberry filling, we expect Nerds Crunch Pop to become our new favorite frozen treat.

#3845. Joyce Wang Studio Returns to Mandarin Oriental The Landmark a Decade Later

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

More than a decade after first reimagining Mandarin Oriental The Landmark in Hong Kong, Joyce Wang Studio has renovated the hotel a second time. The new work covers its 109 rooms and suites, the mansion foyer, the spa, and the former MO Bar.

Joyce Wang, the studio’s principal, says the latest brief placed greater emphasis on the hotel’s connection to Hong Kong. “We took inspiration from the colonial country manors frequented by the Tai Kwuns of a bygone era to celebrate the beauty of these historic homes that still fascinate today,” she says. “Our vision this time has a greater focus on local relevance and the heritage of the building.”

The redesign is organized around the idea of a lived-in mansion, a type with many historical examples in Hong Kong. Green and orange anchor the palette, drawing on the city’s lush gardens and the terracotta tones of its colonial architecture. In the lobby and arrival sequence, classic architectural gestures meet contemporary detailing in stone, timber, textured wallcoverings, and custom eglomisé glass. An undulating staircase, artwork bays, and sculptural plinths guide guests through an arrival sequence modeled on that of a grand residence.

The city’s skyline runs through the building as a motif. The studio created custom artworks of the Hong Kong silhouette and produced them as carpets, tapestries, and eglomisé glass for the lobby, the elevators, and the guestroom corridors. In the rooms, custom rugs take their pattern from Hong Kong’s historic brickwork. They sit on lightened timber floors alongside cracked gesso wall finishes and a Fromental wallcovering incorporating hand-painted and silk elements.

Art was integrated into the design program from the start. Wang says the artwork program “is rigorous in that there is a story behind each piece.” The collection brings together work by emerging and established artists, both local and international. Wang describes the hotel as a pied-à-terre for guests to experience the city up close. Sculptural vases, artisanal vessels, and other objects were selected to look like things gathered over time in a private home.

Together, these details give the hotel the layered character of a private residence, while the references to Hong Kong’s architecture, landscape, and artistic community keep the design firmly rooted in its setting. Rather than simply revisiting the studio’s earlier work, the renovation offers a more distinctly local interpretation of the luxury hotel.

#1937. Mobile Ad Tech Holding Company Affle Wants To Sell CTV Ads Like They’re App Installs

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

After two decades of getting people to tap “install” on a six-inch screen, mobile ad tech has its eye on the 65-inch one in the living room.

“Every dollar an advertiser spends should drive some kind of outcome, including on connected TV,” said Sameer Sondhi, North American CEO and chief strategic investments officer at Affle, a holding company of mostly mobile ad tech providers that’s based in Singapore and publicly listed in India with designs on the US market.

Affle has built its business largely through acquisitions, not unlike Zeta Global, Infillion and also Verve Group, where Sondhi previously served as CRO and CEO of ad tech before joining Affle earlier this year.

On Monday, Affle launched MediasmartTV, a self-serve CTV platform housed within Mediasmart, the omnichannel DSP it bought in 2020.

Buyers can use the platform to plan, run and manage their CTV campaigns, and supply comes from SSP partners, including Magnite, PubMatic, Index Exchange and FreeWheel, as well as TV manufacturers like LG and Samsung.

All together, Affle says that adds up to more than 1 trillion monthly ad opportunities in the US alone.

Big numbers, but none of that is unique, and Sondhi doesn’t pretend otherwise. Plenty of CTV players pitch performance, and getting advertisers to integrate yet another measurement pixel is a pretty hard sell.

The difference, Sondhi said, is Affle’s cost-per-converted-user (CPCU) model.

“CPCU” is a whole lot of acronym for a simple idea.

Rather than buying impressions and hoping for the best, advertisers are only charged when someone who was exposed to an ad on their TV goes on to take an action, like installing an app or making a purchase.

If someone buys something or installs an app on another device within an agreed window, the advertiser’s ecommerce or measurement platform sends Mediasmart a postback. That postback is what Affle bills on. Purchases are tracked through ecommerce integrations with the likes of WooCommerce and Adobe Commerce.

Affle built its targeting and optimization models on mobile, where device IDs are (relatively) plentiful. The “TV-first” version of its algorithm, as Sondhi describes it, has been retooled to work without them. It learns from Mediasmart’s mobile campaigns, advertiser first-party data and whatever signals it can pull from the TVs themselves.

What Mediasmart doesn’t have yet is direct publisher integrations, which means that contextual signals are limited, but Affle is evaluating ACR and ID graph partners to fill in the gap.

Affle has a pithy little slogan for all this – “TV to cart” – and it’s hoping the phrase catches on. Sondhi said Affle is even trying to trademark it, along with “TV to travel,” “TV to education” and “TV to finance.”

“We want to keep it simple,” he said. “No more acronyms, no technical jargon.”

Paying only for results may sound logical, but it requires a mindset shift. Most CTV is still bought on impressions, Sondhi said, and judged afterward by cost per install and/or return on ad spend.

“There needs to be a lot more education around this,” he said.

So Affle is starting off with the advertisers who need the least convincing, as in the app marketers, mainly gaming-focused, it already works with on user acquisition and reengagement campaigns. “You’d be surprised how much gaming user acquisition we’re already doing through CTV,” Sondhi said.

But there’s also interest beyond gaming, including from rideshare, finance and reward apps, some of which are spending millions on TV, he said.

Next up, Affle wants to court independent agencies using joint “lunch and learn” sessions together with its mobile measurement partners to explain the model, followed by outreach to the large holding companies – and that one will be a challenge.

The big holdcos tend to have a “very upper funnel” mentality, of which Sondhi is not a fan.

“I really do hate the upper-funnel, mid-funnel, lower-funnel pyramid,” he said. “Let’s be honest, it’s not like advertisers spend money without expecting to get something back.”

Winning over new buyers is also part of a bigger push into the US.

North America accounts for roughly a third of Affle’s business, but the company has been underrepresented stateside, Sondhi said, and “that’s where the big dollars are.”

Affle was founded in 2005 when “Nokia was king and the BlackBerry was considered very cool,” he joked. Today, it trades on India’s stock exchange with a market cap above $2 billion.

Mediasmart is one of eight specialized point solutions Affle has assembled since 2012, primarily through acquisitions. The others include omnichannel marketing platform Vizury, app discovery platform Appnext, user acquisition specialist Jampp and gaming-focused programmatic app marketing platform YouAppi.

In June, Affle bought AdColony’s brand and SDKs from DT, formerly Digital Turbine; just the tech, though, not the team.

Sondhi said he spends more than half his time on M&A and to expect more deals soon. He declined to name targets, but did say that anything Affle buys will tie back to CPCU, with CTV as an obvious area of interest.

Further out, Affle is hunting for its next screen.

With LLMs upending the web and mobile, Sondhi said, the company is eyeing “autonomous integrated connected devices,” or “AICDs,” everything from the watch on your wrist to screens in a retail store, because “these industries are evolving so fast, we don’t want to just bet on one thing.”
