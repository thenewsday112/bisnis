#2045. The Mahmoud Khalil and Badar Khan Suri cases: an explainer

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

During the early months of President Donald Trump’s second term, the federal government detained and sought to deport several students and academics because of their support for Palestine. The Supreme Court could soon take up the cases of two of those detainees, Mahmoud Khalil and Badar Khan Suri, both of whom have since been released. The issue at the center of these cases is whether federal trial courts have the power to consider challenges to the two men’s detentions, or whether (as the Trump administration argues) those challenges are instead barred by federal immigration law.

The Trump administration has urged the Supreme Court to grant both petitions, telling the justices that the court’s “review is warranted to resolve” a conflict among the federal courts of appeals “over a question of immigration jurisdiction that affects tens of thousands of cases.” “Left unchecked,” U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer wrote, “such suits risk producing an overwhelming litigation burden on both the government and the courts.”

Sauer made that claim in the case of Suri, an Indian national with a Ph.D. in Peace and Conflict Studies who came to the United States in 2022 for a postdoctoral fellowship at Georgetown University. Suri’s wife is a U.S. citizen of Palestinian descent; her father once served as an adviser to a leader in the Palestinian militant group Hamas.

In March 2025, Suri – who contended that he had “never even been to a protest” – was approached outside his suburban Virginia apartment by masked officers who told them that they were from DHS, that he was being arrested because of his “social media,” and that the government planned to deport him that day. (An analysis by The Hoya, the Georgetown University student newspaper, found that Suri’s social media posts “frequently express support for Palestine, especially since the Oct. 7, 2023, attack on Israel by the Palestinian militant group Hamas and Israel’s subsequent invasion of Gaza,” but that Suri “has never openly advocated breaking U.S. law.”) Secretary of State Marco Rubio had determined that Suri’s “Hamas associations and advocacy compromised the United States’ foreign-policy interests, thereby rendering him deportable.”

Suri’s attorney went to federal court in Virginia to seek his release. He argued in a habeas petition, which is a brief asking a federal district court to review the legality of his detention, that (among other things) Suri had been detained in retaliation for his speech. The district court issued an order on March 20 that barred the government from deporting Suri, and in May it instructed the government to release him on bail.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the 4th Circuit turned down the government’s request to pause the district court’s ruling while it appealed, and in an opinion on July 23, 2026, that court upheld the district court’s decision.

The Trump administration came to the Supreme Court on Sept. 21, asking the justices to decide whether a noncitizen like Suri who is placed in immigration detention while the government seeks to deport him can challenge his detention in a habeas petition when his claims are also related to whether he can be removed. Sauer wrote that federal immigration law generally requires all challenges “arising from” deportation proceedings or the decision to begin such proceedings to go first to a federal appeals court, rather than a district court. “That naturally includes claims raised by an alien that reduce to the assertion it is illegal to detain him for all of the same reasons that it is ultimately illegal to remove him.”

Sauer told the justices that, in addition to granting the Trump administration’s petition for review in Suri’s case, they might also want to take up the case of Mahmoud Khalil, an Algerian citizen who was born in a Palestinian refugee camp in Syria and became a lawful permanent resident of the United States in 2024. Doing so, Sauer said, “would guard against the possibility that either case might become moot” – that is, no longer a live controversy – if, for example, either man leaves the United States.

Khalil was arrested on March 8 at his apartment building in New York City, where he had recently received a master’s degree from Columbia University. Khalil, who is married to a U.S. citizen, describes himself as a “lifelong advocate for Palestinian human rights” who has also “condemned anti-Semitism.” In the wake of the Oct. 7 attacks in Israel, Khalil participated in the pro-Palestinian protests at Columbia.

The government sought to deport Khalil based on Rubio’s determination that Khalil’s “participation and role[] … in antisemitic protests and disruptive activities” would “compromise a compelling U.S. foreign policy interest.” On March 19, a federal district court in New Jersey barred the government from deporting Khalil to give that court time to consider his habeas petition. Khalil alleged, among other things, that his detention violated both the First Amendment, because it was intended to retaliate against him for his support of Palestine and to deter others from expressing that same support, and the Fifth Amendment, because it was intended to punish him. Approximately three months later, the New Jersey district court ordered Khalil’s release on bail.

In January of this year, a divided three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 3rd Circuit ruled that the district court did not have the authority to consider Khalil’s challenges. “The scheme Congress enacted governing immigration proceedings provides Khalil a meaningful forum in which to raise his claims later on—in a petition for review of a final order of removal,” the majority wrote.

After the full 3rd Circuit declined to weigh in, Khalil came to the Supreme Court on Monday, asking the justices to take up his case. He stated that federal law “channels most challenges to the validity of a final order of removal through the administrative process and” petitions for review “of a final removal order to the court of appeals. But it does not, and could not, consistent with the U.S. Constitution, strip habeas courts of” the power to hear “constitutional challenges to executive detention that is retaliatory in violation of the First Amendment and punitive in violation of the Fifth Amendment.”

Suri’s response to the government’s petition for review is due on Nov. 20; the government’s response to Khalil’s petition will also be due in late November. The justices will likely consider both petitions at a private conference sometime early next year.

#2636. Karla Souza Talks Narrating The New 'Thomas & Friends' Special, Mom Guilt, & More

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The HTGAWM star and mom of three talks narrating the new Spanish-language Thomas & Friends special, and how she’s leaning into her latest parenting era: minivan life.

Karla Souza has accomplished a lot in her career. She famously played a law student covering up multiple murders in How To Get Away With Murder. She won two International Emmys for producing and starring in Dive (La Caída). She’s built a filmography that fluently bounces between Hollywood and Mexico. But it took a little blue engine to impress maybe the most fickle audience of all: her three kids, ages 8, 6, and 2.

Souza is the Spanish-language narrator of Thomas & Friends Railway Stories: Really Helpful Engines, a 30-minute special that kicks off a new era for the beloved franchise through refreshed character designs, three original songs, and a story about friendship, teamwork, and helping others. She’s also, as she learned after signing on, the first woman — and first Mexican woman — to narrate Thomas & Friends.

Scary Mommy caught up with the charming Souza to talk about the special, why “balance” is a myth, officially surrendering to minivan life, and more.

Scary Mommy: Your kids are 8, 6, and 2, which is prime Thomas territory. Were they Thomas fans before? Do they have a sense of what you’re doing?

Karla Souza: This is actually the first project that I'm able to watch with them, so it was very special. I was definitely chomping at the bit, wanting to be able to share something for the first time with them. They were very confused as to why I wasn't making the train sounds and why that was somebody else. There was a lot of confusion with that part of it, but I love this new chapter of being able to share these kinds of things. And it was the first thing that I did for that kind of age range.

I just find it so special to think that maybe, when they have kids, they'll be able to show old Grandma… there’s something to be said about doing projects that can live on for generations.

SM: What does it mean to you that kids can hear Thomas in Spanish, told by a Mexican narrator?

KS: I wasn't aware that this is the first time that a woman and that a Mexican woman is the narrator of Thomas and Friends. I am so honored, and I'm very excited that female voices get to guide these new generations in seeing women in these kinds of positions.

I love how Thomas and Friends has content that isn't in overdrive, as a lot of the kids' content nowadays is extremely saturated with, I don't know, stimulation is just being thrown at you. I find that this type of content is not only going to provoke imagination, but it's also going to turn on their hearts how to be a better person, how to work as a team, how to learn from mistakes in a calmer way. So it's been a great thing to watch with my 2-year-old as well as with my 6- and 8-year-old. That doesn't really get to happen as much, so I'm really grateful for that.

SM: Three kids, a career as an actor and producer, you split your time a lot between two countries. What does balance actually look like for you, or is that nonexistent?

KS: Yeah, it's not possible. I think that word is thrown around as if it were possible to have a balanced life. I mean, anyone who's near my life is like, "How do you do this?" I think the trains have systems; they're on the tracks. There are times when the train leaves. If there's a delay, there's an announcement to all the people that there's a delay. It's the same. If I didn't have all these systems and a community of people around me to support me, my husband, my family, our nanny, the school, everything, we would not be able to do this. My mom didn't work, per se, so I never saw what that would look like. So it's been a learning curve for me to understand how to juggle all of it.

There's a lot of mom guilt still there that, every therapy session, I'm trying to get to why I still feel that way. It's real, it's hard, but I will say that also the ability to see my kids watching me do something that I love, watching me impact society in a way, watching me live out my purpose, is as important as being with them 24-7.

SM: Such a good reminder that we’re not alone; we all feel like this in a way. Your youngest is 2. What difference strikes you the most about parenting your third compared to your first?

KS: My 2-year-old started saying words that were like, "Where did she hear that?" I'm like, "Obviously from us; there's no other place. This word isn't going to magically appear in her mouth. That is why she is saying that word." It's funny how you're in denial of, like, "I don't do that,” but surely we do.

I think having children is a great mirror for who you are and the work you have to do on yourself, because they call BS so quickly when we try to teach them about, like, breathwork. And yet when I get frustrated, I'm not practicing what I am trying to teach. So as much as I try to think that they aren't as perceptive, they are. They just absorb so much of how we live our lives, of what we do.

Whenever I've noticed that working on myself is actually the best way to show up for them and to model it, that's how I see it with my 2-year-old and my 8-year-old — my 2-year-old is an imitator of everything she sees from my 8-year-old. Whatever my 8-year-old wants to do, whether it's gymnastics or staying up late or reading a book, my 2-year-old goes off and does it. It just shows you how interaction and who you surround yourself with really is very important at this stage in their lives.

It’s an interesting sort of case study on life and how we are as humans and what makes us feel like we belong.

SM: Yeah, it is fascinating how it shows up like that.

But yeah, the 2-year-old obviously is the queen of the household, and my 8-year-old is as well, but the 2-year-old is coming in like a storm. She's a spitfire. So we do what she says. My 8-year-old is a great sort of... I try to not put the caretaking role on her as much because a lot of people try to have the older kids kind of become the caretakers, and I don't want to do that. But I will say she has a lovely pull towards taking care of her baby sister.

I love having that separation of years, but yeah, the 2-year-old's my last one. We just bought ourselves our first minivan, so I've given up. That's it; I'm a mom of three. I've never had a minivan in my life, and we just got one. I'm like, "All right, that's who I am now."

SM: We rented a minivan for a girls’ trip a few years ago. Five moms driving up the coast in a rented minivan; it was hilarious. But it was the first one I drove as well, and I was like, “You know what? There’s something to this. It’s super comfortable.”

KS: I know. The kids just get in and they get out, and it's just so comfortable. I feel more powerful driving it than I thought I would be. I'm like, "This is actually cooler than I thought." But yes, I've given up on the cool, sexy cars now and I'm like, "All right, I'm going to embrace it."

SM: As you should. Let's make minivans cool and sexy.

KS: Yes. Hey, you did it on a girls' trip! That's hilarious.

SM: Pivoting a bit, I saw you post that How To Get Away With Murder just marked 12 years since its premiere. Does the ending hit differently now that you’re a mom of three?

KS: Man, I am in awe of Laurel being able to be a mom at that period of her life. I'm still shocked. And I'm shocked that I, as a human being, was able to... I think I was pregnant when I did that scene on the elevator where she's having these contractions and there's a potential stillbirth. Nowadays, knowing so much more, I'm like, "I don't know how I could do that today." But I will say that at least playing to be a mom definitely prepared me in the smallest of ways, but it did prepare me to be a mom. It kind of gave me that vote of confidence because I never thought I could be a mom.

I didn't grow up with that faith of I can do it. I was like, "I still feel like I'm a child and I'm trying to parent my own child, my inner child every day." But that's why I thought I wasn't sort of made for motherhood, and look at me three kids later: here I am.

So for all the moms out there, all the women out there, who feel like they wouldn't be able to do it, I was that person. I was definitely thinking, "How can I still have a career and do what I love and travel with my friends in a minivan? How can I do all these things and still have kids?" It's possible. Does it all look the same as when I was single? No, it doesn't, but it is possible. It's tough, but that's the caveat. It's the silver lining, but yes.

The latest for gun hunters and competitive shooters.

The good news first: If you want to hunt waterfowl with premium shotshells this season, you should still be able to get your hands on some. The bad news: those shells are going to cost much more than you’re (already) used to.

As we reported ahead of turkey season, tungsten prices skyrocketed in 2026. Bismuth, copper, and steel have also gone up, and those prices are translating to limited availability of raw materials, reduced supply from ammo makers, and higher prices for consumers.

That means many duck and goose hunters who have been shooting premium non-tox in recent years will be returning to steel shot.

“It’s definitely going to be the season of steel, for sure,” says Jimmy Wilson, Winchester Ammunition’s director of product strategy and a mechanical engineer by training. “It’s frustrating because people want the higher performance of alternative metals. But they’re still going to get great performance from steel and they’re not going to be spending as much money.”

In response to bismuth scarcity and high prices, ammo makers across the industry have been scaling back their bismuth shotshells for 2026. If you do manage to find bismuth on the shelves, it’s likely leftover from last season’s manufacturing run and will not be restocked.

“Bismuth is still not available [to manufacturers] for any reasonable price,” says Wilson. “There’s still a Browning waterfowl load that has bismuth shot in it. That’s Wicked Blend, and it’s still out on shelves and reasonably priced, at around $40 a box. So if someone is looking for bismuth, they’re likely to still find that in most of the offerings we originally had.”

A quick look at major waterfowl brands online reflects this reality. Many pure bismuth loads from Remington and Hevi have been discontinued. While Federal is still making its Premium Hevi Bismuth, a single box of 2 ¾-inch 12-gauge No. 4s will run you $60, or $2.40 per round at big-box stores.

Like Winchester, Hevi manufactures a blended bismuth and steel load, called Hevi Hammer, that’s widely available and relatively affordable since it’s mostly steel. Each Hevi Hammer round contains about 15 percent bismuth, compared to the Wicked Blend’s 30 percent.

This summer the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service officially approved the new copper-pellet loads from Boss Shotshells for waterfowl hunting. And in early waterfowl seasons in the North, duck and goose hunters are having good success with them.

While the Boss copper CuProX loads are by no means cheap, they are substantially more affordable than the tungsten or bismuth loads currently on the market. A box of 20 3-inch 12-gauge No. 3/5s CuProX will run you $40, or about $2.15 per shell.

When it comes to pellet density, non-toxic copper has a density of about 8.9 g/cc. That means copper ranks below bismuth (about 9.6 g/cc) and above steel (about 7.8 g/cc). Based on the density numbers alone, these copper pellets should not perform better than bismuth at the same velocities, but our testing of the new loads revealed a slight edge over bismuth in patterning and penetration — with a better price tag.

You can find the full Boss Copper test results here.

Boss says it sources its copper from Michigan, where the company is based. Although powder availability was previously the main bottleneck in the ammunition supply chain, copper has taken that crown due to its expense. Copper is the main component — about 70 percent — of brass.

“The price of copper continues to go up. It’s over $6.50 per pound for commodity prices right now, which is 50 percent higher than two years ago,” says Wilson. “It’s quite unprecedented, and you see that affecting ammunition prices in general … When you have a huge increase like that in copper, it’s going to affect the brass price, and it causes ammo makers to have to increase our price to cover that.”

The good news — for waterfowlers, at least — is this mostly impacts rifle and pistol ammo prices.

Like bismuth, many familiar TSS and tungsten loads have been discontinued, such as 20-gauge Black Cloud TSS. Some loads are still available online and in some stores until they sell out. Others aren’t available at all. Case in point: our favorite duck load we’ve ever tested, Hevi XII, named for its 12 g/cc tungsten pellets (no steel), is currently unavailable from the manufacturer and online retailers.

As we saw with turkey loads for the spring 2026 season, many manufacturers tried to combat rising tungsten prices by lowering the density of the tungsten pellets themselves. Expect varied availability, particularly from larger manufacturers. You’re most likely to find steel loads blended with varying densities of tungsten and at decreasing proportions. For instance, Hevi Hitter is still available from some retailers, which contains 80 percent steel and 20 percent tungsten pellets (12 g/cc).

“TSS is probably dead [for Winchester] just from a cost standpoint this [bird] season,” says Wilson. “The punitive tariffs make it cost-prohibitive to import. There’s a chance we’ll have some sort of waterfowl load that has some tungsten shot in it, but the quantities produced won’t be very big because it’s going to be expensive.”

Meanwhile Apex Ammunition, the Mississippi shotshell company that built its business around tungsten waterfowl loads, is still offering full TSS loads this fall. Apex says it hasn’t struggled with tungsten availability for its shotshells — just the cost of the material, which customers will see reflected in higher shotshell price tags.

“Our prices for waterfowl [loads] are slightly elevated from last year,” says Apex co-founder and CEO Jason Lonsberry, who noted the company was actively shipping steel and tungsten blends when we spoke in early October. “We’ve got tungsten in-house. We are actively producing blends and full TSS waterfowl loads right now.”

Lonsberry estimates Apex’s waterfowl loads cost between 15 and 27 percent more than 2025. The increase depends on gauge and payload, not shot size, he says.

Note that although Apex is churning out tungsten loads, supply is still lower across the country than in past years because bigger ammo makers aren’t supplying the premium shotshells to market this year. Blended and pure TSS loads are sold out on Apex’s site, though you can find those blends for about $2.28 per shell at retailers like Rogers Sporting Goods and Mack’s Prairie Wings.

Lonsberry says Apex has been trying to provide customers with different purchasing options given higher prices.

“We used to have some 10-shell box options, now they’re five [for turkey hunting]. The per shell cost shot up, but we were trying to make the impact to the register drop a little bit. Like, ‘Hey man, you only need five shells versus ten, just buy what you need.’”

Tungsten prices spiked at historic highs earlier this year, and have since fallen from astronomical levels. Still, ammonium paratungstate — the raw ingredient in TSS — remains roughly six times as expensive as it was in June 2025, depending on the market. Chinese tungsten prices have declined by about 70 percent from peak while the European market has only fallen about 10 percent.

Prices remain high for a few reasons: China still dominates tungsten manufacturing, tariffs have passed along the cost of import to American consumers, the U.S. hasn’t gotten domestic tungsten production back online, and tungsten is a key component of modern defense and warfare applications.

Lonsberry says new tungsten suppliers in other countries have started operating in the past six months, so tungsten is easier to source than it was in the spring — but it’s still expensive.

“There’re plenty of sources to get tungsten from,” says Lonsberry. “It’s just really trying to mitigate the cost.”

Despite that price, Apex’s own forecasts for tungsten demand are actually elevated from the past.

“A lot of that is probably because we’re accepting [tungsten ammo] orders because no one else is,” says Lonsberry. “I think a lot of other companies have decided to just bow out of it for this next year until prices stabilize some … They’ll probably wait for tungsten [prices] to correct and get back in the game because there’s plenty of demand there. But we just knew that if we didn’t [make TSS loads this fall], no one else is going to do it.”

Prices for the low-carbon steel used in shotshell pellets have also gone up steadily (roughly 68 percent over last year), but steel is also more stable and affordable than denser pellet materials.

Steel is, of course, the least dense pellet material available to waterfowlers. But manufacturers have been tinkering with shot sizes, wads, and other technological improvements in recent years.

“The hot ticket item for steel is definitely a stacked steel load with different shot sizes,” says Lonsberry. “That’s become a number one thing.”

Apex, Winchester, Federal, and Migra all offer stacked steel loads. Although mixing steel shot sizes might be trendy, it doesn’t offer practical lethality advantages at farther distances. Smaller steel pellets will not penetrate sufficiently at long range and there are fewer large steel pellets in the pattern. In other words, these stacked steel loads will work fine for close and mid-range shooting — just like all quality steel loads will.

Waterfowl hunters shooting steel this season should stick to the basics: pattern your gun, choke, and load together, keep shots inside 40 yards, and carefully match your pellet size selection to the size of the birds you’re hunting. After all, the fun part of duck hunting is getting birds in close.

While we may actually be Millennials and Gen X, it’s become clear that the kids think we are … old.

As I lay on my bed with one of my teens next to me, laughing over a Reel before we forward it to my mom, I find myself suddenly the butt of a joke. “Why do you poke your phone like that, mom? You’re such a Boomer. That’s so unc.”

I paused, hovered over the sharing airplane, and looked at the seemingly normal (to me) way that I am tapping my phone. Was I acting like an old lady? Was I “unc,” the new gender-neutral uncle-derived slang term the teens currently use to describe anyone annoyingly older than them?

After a moment of denial, I settled into the belief that my kid was actually right: I am old. No, not as old as my parents or as old as most U.S. politicians, but when it comes to the benchmarks that define age, I am decidedly more crotchety than I was five years ago.

Today, as I sit in my dining room typing this missive on the morning of my 43rd birthday (Yes, I am an geriatric millennial), I am faced with two important thoughts: Why is my neighbor’s car engine so loud, and what kind of bird is chirping in the tree outside of my window?

I pause to send my friend a recording of the bird. She replies that it is a mockingbird (likely mocking me, like my children) and I get back to work. She’s a Boomer now too, I assume. She recently invited me over to learn about plants — and take a few clippings. Wild times for the Real Housewives of Pittsburgh as we all work remotely from our messy homes.

I begin to mentally list the things that now piss me off: Overhead lighting, too many people talking at once, small talk at professional events (or anywhere), pants with a waistband and shoes without good arch support. I am furious that every movement in life requires a new app, deeply concerned about the safety of e-bikes and gas station drugs and the state of our local roads, and trialing my fourth set of cooling bed sheets. It’s a weird juxtaposition when you’re parenting in middle age, raising kids that are on the cusp of adulthood. In many ways I feel like I’m in the prime of my life. I’ve whittled down my friend group to the besties who can stand me, my career is satisfying and moderately successful, and I’ve finally found a hairdresser who gets me. Some days I walk down the street feeling like a million bucks — only to be cut off at the knees by a side-eye at my shoe choice from my teen daughter.

I ask my friends, my comrades in aging semi-gracefully, if they feel the Boomerness creeping up on them, too.

“For sure,” one friend tells me, “Or like how my boyfriend always talks about how we used to be able to get a hamburger for $5 or what the cost of gas used to be.” I remember… it was under a buck the day I got my license.

Another friend says her kid dissects the “unc-ness” of her text messages. Emoji choice is a dead (☠️) giveaway, apparently. So are the television shows and movies we reference as “recent.” Hold hands while I tell you this: The final episode of Parks and Recreation aired well over a decade ago; ten whole human years ago. Don’t even get me started on the OG version of SATC.

So I’ve chosen to accept my fate. In many ways, we are becoming the Boomers now. Not literally — generations are fairly well-defined, and Boomers were born between about 1946 and 1964. But in the funny, small ways that matter, many of us Elder Millennial and Gen X parents are showing our age.

After some soul-searching in my backyard inflatable hot tub, which helps with the aches and pains of old age we’ve barely touched on, I’ve decided this isn’t such a bad thing.

So I am worked up enough about e-bikes to briefly consider a run for town council? That’s because I care about the safety of kids in our community; call me Karen. I’m frustrated with the condition of roads or schools or public infrastructure? I won’t apologize for being old (or mature) enough to realize civic engagement matters. If we want to live in a thriving and bustling community, we are the adults that must create that now. We can’t decry the loss of a village if we aren’t willing to get out there and make one.

We’ve also, as a group, taken healing quite seriously. I am proud of us for this, more than anything else about our generation. While we might bitch about the cost of everything or the state of the world in those Boomerish ways, we are also a generation that is serious about breaking cycles, building new patterns, and parenting conscientiously.

We talk about our therapists in ways that normalize taking care of our mental health. We find mental health help for our kids when they need it, too. We mess up with our kids—and we apologize. We try, we screw up, we try again. Our hips and back and knees may be less flexible than they once were, but I am proud that we are trying to keep our minds flexible. Our Gen Z and Alpha kids poke fun at us and insist the way we dress is awful, but I choose to believe that they see we are working so hard.

So, to my fellow Millennial-and-Gen-X-Boomer-but-not-really-moms, pull up a supportive chair and join me. Bring your latest craft project, your current book club read, or your stack of political postcards that you need to get out before the midterms. Bring your comfy soft pants, your persistent chin hair, and your achy lower back. We will turn off the Big Lights, skip the small talk, and bitch about hot flashes while our kids make fun of us in the next room. Honestly, I think we’ve earned this.

#3938. Millions take fish oil for brain health. A two-year study found no benefit

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Millions of Americans take fish oil supplements in hopes of protecting their memory and keeping their brains healthy as they age. These products have become a major business, with Americans spending more than $1 billion each year on supplements containing omega-3 fatty acids. These essential nutrients help build and maintain the connections between brain cells that support memory, learning, and other cognitive functions.

But a new study suggests that getting more omega-3s into the brain through supplements may not provide the protection many people expect.

Researchers at Keck Medicine of USC found that even when high doses of omega-3s successfully reached the brain, they did not produce measurable improvements in memory, thinking abilities, or protection against brain shrinkage.

The findings, published in eBioMedicine, come from a two-year clinical trial involving older adults considered to be at increased risk of developing Alzheimer's disease. Despite taking omega-3 supplements daily, participants showed no significant cognitive advantage over those who received a placebo.

"We all wish there was a silver bullet for preventing Alzheimer's, but our findings showed that fish oil supplements do not appear to protect brain health," said Hussein Naji Yassine, MD, director of the USC Center for Personalized Brain Health and lead investigator of the study. "While omega-3s play an important role in forming brain cell connections needed for cognition, our results do not support fish oil supplements as a preventive measure against Alzheimer's."

A Two-Year Study Tests Fish Oil's Brain Benefits

To investigate whether omega-3 supplements could protect the aging brain, researchers enrolled 365 adults between the ages of 55 and 80. All participants rarely ate fish, a major dietary source of omega-3 fatty acids, and were considered at elevated risk for Alzheimer's disease.

Nearly half (47%) carried APOE4, a gene variant associated with a substantially higher risk of developing Alzheimer's later in life. Although having this variant does not mean someone will necessarily develop the disease, it is the strongest known genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer's.

Participants were randomly assigned to receive either a daily fish oil supplement or a placebo, an inactive treatment used for comparison. The supplements provided 2,000 mg of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), an omega-3 fatty acid that is especially important for the brain.

DHA is a major component of brain cell membranes, helping maintain the structure and function of neurons. It also supports communication between nerve cells, making it an important nutrient for normal brain activity.

The study was double-blind, meaning neither participants nor researchers knew who was receiving the active supplement during the trial. This approach helps reduce bias and makes it easier to determine whether any observed benefits are actually caused by the treatment.

Omega-3 Levels Rose, but Memory Did Not Improve

Before evaluating whether the supplements improved brain health, researchers needed to answer a fundamental question: Were the omega-3 fatty acids actually reaching the brain?

To find out, they measured DHA concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid, the clear liquid that surrounds and cushions the brain and spinal cord. Examining this fluid can provide information about substances entering and circulating within the central nervous system.

After six months, DHA levels in the cerebrospinal fluid had increased by an average of 17% among participants taking the supplements. This provided evidence that the omega-3 fatty acids were reaching their intended destination.

However, getting DHA into the central nervous system was only part of the goal. The more important question was whether those higher levels would translate into better brain health.

Researchers assessed participants' memory and other cognitive abilities at the beginning of the trial and again after two years. Despite the increase in DHA, those taking fish oil performed no better on the cognitive tests than participants receiving the placebo.

Brain imaging revealed similarly disappointing results.

The supplements did not prevent shrinkage of the hippocampus, a region deep within the brain that plays a central role in forming and organizing memories. The hippocampus is particularly vulnerable to changes associated with aging and Alzheimer's disease, making its size an important indicator researchers use when studying brain health.

Taken together, the results suggest that simply increasing DHA availability in the brain is not enough to produce measurable cognitive benefits in this group of older adults.

Why Fish Oil Supplements May Work Differently Than Food

The unexpected findings have raised a new question for researchers: If omega-3s can reach the brain, why don't higher levels necessarily improve its function?

Yassine and his colleagues are now investigating how the brain absorbs, processes, and uses these nutrients. Their earlier research suggests that omega-3s may be more beneficial when consumed as part of a broader dietary pattern rather than taken in isolation as a supplement.

One possibility involves the Mediterranean-style diet, which emphasizes foods such as fish, vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and olive oil. This eating pattern naturally supplies omega-3 fatty acids alongside other nutrients and has been associated with a lower risk of Alzheimer's disease.

The researchers suspect that the combination of nutrients in a balanced diet, along with an individual's overall health, may influence how effectively omega-3s support the brain.

"We're focused on better understanding how the brain processes omega-3s and whether factors, such as poor health, dietary pattern, genetic risk and age, may change the brain's ability to effectively absorb and use omega-3s," said Yassine. "We are working to develop medications that may help the brain better utilize these nutrients to preserve cognitive function."

Understanding these differences could eventually help scientists identify why certain people might respond better to omega-3s than others and whether new treatments could improve the brain's ability to benefit from these essential fats.

Importantly, the findings do not mean omega-3 fatty acids are unnecessary or that eating fish offers no health benefits. Instead, they indicate that taking high-dose DHA supplements alone did not provide measurable protection against cognitive decline or hippocampal shrinkage in the population studied.

Healthy Habits Remain Key to Alzheimer's Prevention

Although the trial focused specifically on fish oil supplements, the researchers emphasize that protecting brain health requires a much broader approach.

Rather than relying on a single supplement, they recommend maintaining healthy habits throughout life, including regular physical activity, sufficient high-quality sleep, and a nutritious, balanced diet.

These behaviors support overall health and may help reduce factors associated with cognitive decline. Physical exercise, for example, promotes cardiovascular health and blood flow, while sleep plays an important role in memory formation and normal brain maintenance.

"Staying healthy throughout life remains the most powerful tool we have for reducing Alzheimer's risk, including regular exercise, quality sleep and a balanced diet," said Yassine. "Living a healthy lifestyle is the brain's equivalent of getting regular car maintenance and high-quality oil changes. The brain is more likely to lose greater function if health issues in other parts of the body go unaddressed, in the same way that car engines stop working if regular maintenance is skipped."

While these lifestyle factors were not directly tested in the trial, the researchers believe they offer a more promising foundation for long-term brain health than depending on fish oil supplements alone.

The results also highlight an important distinction: A nutrient can be essential for healthy brain function without providing additional protection when consumed in large doses as a supplement. Understanding why that happens may open new avenues for research into Alzheimer's prevention and the nutritional needs of the aging brain.

#2482. Switchblade Ban Violates Second Amendment, Fifth Circuit Holds

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From Knife Rights, Inc. v. Blanche, decided yesterday by Fifth Circuit Judge Jennifer Walker Elrod, joined by Judges Don Willett and Cory Wilson:

This case presents a Second Amendment challenge to the Federal Switchblade Act (FSA)'s restrictions on switchblade knives. {Section 2 of the FSA, codified as 15 U.S.C. § 1242, … place[s] a fine of up to "$2,000 or imprison[ment] not more than five years, or both" for anyone who "knowingly introduces, or manufactures for introduction, into interstate commerce, or transports or distributes in interstate commerce, any switchblade knife."}

Applying the framework articulated in New York State Rifle & Pistol Association, Inc. v. Bruen, we hold that the FSA is unconstitutional.  We determine that switchblade knives qualify as "Arms" and are covered by the "plain text" of the Second Amendment. Because the regulation implicates the amendment, the government bears the burden of establishing that the FSA comports with well-established principles of the Second Amendment and its regulatory tradition. The government fails to meet its burden. Specifically, the FSA's restrictions on the introduction, manufacture, transportation, and distribution of switchblades fail step two's "how" analysis….

Amongst other definitions, Heller highlighted that the founding era understood the term to mean "any thing that a man wears for his defence, or takes into his hands, or useth in wrath to cast at or strike another." Switchblade knives have surely been used for "defence" and to "strike another."

Knives have been used for defense throughout human history, including the American founding. During the colonial period, "[e]veryone had a knife," and "as long as a man was required to defend his life," a "knife was a constant necessity." In the 1700s and during the American Revolution, knives were used for scavenging, hunting, and defense, and "[b]elt and pocket knives" accompanied frontiersmen "in the developing eastern settlements and on the western frontiers." Well into the 1800s, "men of all walks of life" were "accustomed to wear a knife as a necessary part of their garb."

Switchblade knives are no exception. "Springblade knives" (precursors to the modern switchblade) "have been made for hundreds of years because there has always been a need for a pocketknife that can be opened easily when the hands are wet, cold, or covered with gloves." In the 1700s, such knives were a part of certain types of flint-lock pistols: "[i]f the pistol mis-fired (which frequently happened) the person would touch the release button and the blade would open up, and be available for use." After the Civil War, patents for switchblade knives proliferated, and the large-scale production of "switchblades in the United States began in the 1880s, shortly" thereafter. Then, "[o]ver a 50 year period from the mid 1890s to the mid 1940s there had been approximately 20 different companies who had manufactured switchblade knives in this country." In short, some forms of switchblades have been commonly used for defense for hundreds of years. They are "arms."

Accordingly, the government's not-in-common-use argument fails. In addition to the wealth of history indicating the founding-era uses of switchblades for defense, the record indicates that there are "millions" of automatically opening knives in the United States now. The record further reflects that switchblade knives are lawfully used for "every day utility use, hunting, fishing, recreation, and camping and hiking." Despite the FSA, there are still lawful sales of switchblade knives in the United States. Consequently, switchblade knives are "arms" "in common use" both then and now….

We proceed to step two [of the Bruen analysis]. Here, we assess whether § 1242 is consistent with the principles that flow from the Second Amendment's history and tradition….

The government makes its stand primarily by citing a host of nineteenth-century cases in support of its argument that there existed a well-established tradition of regulating "inherently concealable weapons." The concealable nature of these weapons, the argument goes, makes them "particularly dangerous." The government connects this with the clear congressional history that suggests that Congress enacted the FSA because it believed that switchblades posed a unique danger and therefore needed to be restricted. This line of argument is focused on answering the "why" portion of the step two inquiry.

However, we do not reach the question of whether the government meets its burden as to the "why" analysis because it fails the "how" analysis. As mentioned, the Supreme Court has informed us that "[e]ven when a law regulates arms-bearing for a permissible reason," it may not be "compatible with the right if it does so to an extent beyond what was done at the founding." So, even if we grant the government's argument that there is a well-established tradition of regulating "inherently concealable weapons," this only addresses the "why" inquiry.

The government cites no historical analogue that regulates the manufacture, transportation, or distribution of "inherently concealable weapons," much less switchblade knives. Rather, its analogues suggest that there is a tradition of regulating the manner of carrying concealable weapons….

The government's best attempt at meeting its burden is through a single citation to a treatise-length law review article. See Gov't Br. at 16–17 (citing David B. Kopel & Joseph G.S. Greenlee, The History of Bans on Types of Arms Before 1900, 50 J. Legis. 223 (2024)). It cites the article to show how "statutes regulated concealable weapons in a variety of different ways, including banning carry or concealed carry, prohibiting their possession in specific locations, subjecting their sale or possession to taxes, or restricting the sale to minors." The referenced section of the article catalogs nearly two hundred pre-1900 statutes regulating daggers, dirks, and sword canes.

Of the nearly two-hundred statutes, only two regulate bladed weapons in an analogous manner as § 1242. See 1881 Ark. Acts 191, 192 (proscribing the "sell[ing], barter[ing] or exchang[ing]" of "any dirk or bowie knife, or sword or spear in a cane"); 1837 Ga. Laws 90, 90–91 (making unlawful "for any merchant, or vend[o]r of wares or merchandize in this State, or any other person or persons whatsoever, to sell, or offer to sell … Bowie, or any other kinds of knives, manufactured and sold for the purpose of wearing, or carrying the same as arms of offence or defense, pistols, dirks, sword canes, spears, &c"). Two laws out of nearly two hundred bring us into outlier territory….
