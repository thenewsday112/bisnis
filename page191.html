#946. Spiced Beef Baked Potatoes

📅 Oct 08, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Baked potatoes may not seem like a dish that requires much technique, but there’s a difference between a good baked potato and a great one. Wrapping them in foil can lead to a tender interior but render the skin too soft. For the most crisp skin, bake them right on the oven grate at quite a high temperature. (To make it taste almost like a potato chip, rub those scrubbed russets with vegetable oil and season with plenty of salt.) Shockingly, this doesn’t prevent a fluffy interior—that comes down to the internal temperature reaching at least 212°. Sure, it may seem strange to take your potato’s temperature, but it’s a fail-safe route to the most enjoyable potato.

Some, including the Idaho Potato Commission, swear that the way a baked potato is opened affects the texture, eschewing a knife for a fork to gently poke a plus sign into the surface. Others, like beloved food writer Nigel Slater, prefer to strike the potato as though it were part of a martial arts competition. “Bring your hand down fast and sharp as if you were doing a karate chop but only hard enough to crack open the potato skin,” he writes in a piece for The Guardian. (Whether or not to attempt this feat is literally in the hands of the potato-baker.) We found a single fork-pricked line down the length of the spud is the most effective.

Once opened, a baked potato can be filled with anything from tuna salad to cheesy baked beans. Here, it’s a picadillo-inspired mixture with ground beef (packed with protein, iron, and creatine—though ground turkey or chicken work nicely too) adorned with golden raisins and stuffed olives. A spicy tomato sauce will slowly soak into the potato, further seasoning it with every passing minute. Make sure to use a chili powder that’s a mix of spices like chiles, cumin, oregano, garlic (not just dried ground chiles).

Healthy cooking doesn’t have to be complicated. In fact, often the simpler it is, the better it tastes. In Good for You, Test Kitchen editor Rebecca Firkser shares feel-good recipes for any day of the week. Read more: White Potatoes Are Not Empty Calories. They’re Actually Great for You.

Place a rack in middle of oven; preheat to 425°. Prick 4 large russet potatoes (about 10 oz. each), scrubbed, patted dry, all over with a fork, then rub all over with 1 Tbsp. vegetable oil and sprinkle with 1¼ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¾ tsp. Morton kosher salt. Bake directly on oven rack until skins are crisp and flesh is very soft (an instant-read thermometer inserted into the center should register 212°–220°), 55–75 minutes. (If thermometer registers 210° before beef mixture is finished, turn off oven and leave potatoes inside until ready to use.)

Meanwhile, pick enough leaves from ½ bunch cilantro (about 2 oz.) to measure ½ cup; set aside for serving. Finely chop remaining leaves and all of stems (you should have about ¾ cup); set aside separately.

Heat remaining 2 Tbsp. vegetable oil in a large skillet over medium. Add 1 medium red or yellow onion, finely chopped, 5 garlic cloves, finely chopped, and ½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¼ tsp. Morton kosher salt and cook, stirring occasionally, until onion is softened, 8–10 minutes. Add ¼ cup double-concentrated tomato paste and cook, stirring constantly, until deepened in color and slightly tacky, about 2 minutes.

Increase heat to medium-high. Add 1 lb. ground beef, 2 Tbsp. chili powder, and 1 tsp. Diamond Crystal or ½ tsp. Morton kosher salt. Cook, stirring and smashing often with a heatproof rubber spatula to break up meat, until cooked through, about 4 minutes.

Reduce heat to low. Add ½ cup golden raisins, ¼ cup sliced pimiento-stuffed green olives, reserved chopped cilantro, and 1 cup water. Cook, stirring occasionally, until sauce thickens slightly and flavors come together, 8–12 minutes. Taste beef mixture and season with more salt if needed. Remove from heat and cover. Let sit until ready to use.

Transfer potatoes to a platter or plates and let cool 5 minutes. Using a fork, prick a 3"–4"-long line in the center down length of each potato, leaving 1" unpricked on each end. Using your hands, firmly press ends down toward platter to split potatoes open. Smash flesh as desired, then season inside of potatoes with salt. Divide beef mixture among potatoes and top with reserved cilantro leaves.

#1893. YouTube Retention Strategy: How to Hook, Hold, and Convert Viewers

📅 Oct 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Want to stop losing YouTube viewers in the first few seconds of every video? Wondering how to structure long-form content that keeps people watching, builds trust, and drives real business results?

In this article, you’ll discover the BINGE method, a five-part YouTube scripting framework that boosts retention, generates engagement, and turns viewers into customers.

Retention, or average view duration, measures how much of a video’s total length a viewer actually watches. The higher that number, the more YouTube’s algorithm distributes the video through browse, suggested, and search. That distribution compounds over time.

Adam retired his main channel two years ago, and in the 24 months since his last upload, the channel has continued to work for him. In the last year alone, it generated more than 260,000 views, earned roughly $1,500 in AdSense revenue, and produced consistent monthly inquiries for high-ticket programs he no longer even offers.

The distinction between YouTube and short-form social media comes down to focused attention versus distracted attention. Focused attention means a viewer has chosen to sit down and watch, fully engaged with the content. Distracted attention is the passive scrolling that dominates short-form platforms.

Short-form platforms deliver what he compares to eating every meal from a vending machine: quick dopamine, instantly forgotten. After an hour on TikTok, most people can’t name a single creator they watched. YouTube, by contrast, is a four-course meal. TV viewership on YouTube has jumped by more than 60% in the past year alone, a signal that audiences are increasingly treating YouTube like Netflix or HBO, sitting down and giving creators their full, focused attention.

That focused attention translates directly into business outcomes. Adam maintained an 83% close rate on sales calls for nearly three years in his previous business because prospects already knew his delivery style, his personality, and his expertise from watching his videos. One client, Dave, watched just eight minutes of a single video before wiring tens of thousands of dollars. The sales conversation wasn’t about convincing him; it was about logistics.

Google’s own 7-11-4 research supports this dynamic: consumers typically need 7 hours of content, 11 touch points, and interactions across 4 platforms before they trust a brand enough to buy. A few long-form YouTube videos can cover a significant portion of those hours, making the path to purchase dramatically shorter than dozens of short-form clips.

Adam developed a scripting framework called the BINGE method, built from 16 years on YouTube and refined by studying which of his own videos retained viewers and which lost them. Each letter represents a structural element: Banger Hook, Build Intrigue, Nudge Forward, Generate Engagement, and End in Momentum.

Adam frames the BINGE method within a larger model he calls ARC: Attract, Retain, Convert. The ARC model positions YouTube retention at the center because attracting a viewer who leaves after three seconds produces no business value, and converting a viewer who never stayed long enough to build trust is nearly impossible.

The framework isn’t limited to fully scripted videos. It works with outlines and improvisational styles as long as you understand the underlying YouTube script structure. The overall flow mirrors movie and screenplay structure: one storyline gets resolved while another opens up, alternating between tension and resolution to keep the audience locked in.

The B in BINGE stands for banger hook, sometimes called a bombshell hook. This is the first thing the viewer hears after pressing play, and its job is to produce a visceral emotional reaction. That reaction can be any emotion on the spectrum: happiness, grief, anger, fear, or FOMO. The point is to stop the viewer from scanning the suggested videos sidebar and pull them fully into the content.

The hook and the intrigue that follows it need to land in roughly eight seconds. The common advice that creators have 30 seconds to hook a viewer is what Adam calls “2016 math.” Attention spans have compressed further.

The hook works by putting the creator in the viewer’s shoes and speaking directly to what they’re feeling, not just what they know. Adam draws a sharp line between the two: knowing a problem and understanding the feeling behind it are two very different things.

Rather than opening with “in this video, I’m going to teach you five ways to get unstuck on YouTube,” a more effective hook might say: “So you maxed out a credit card on that fancy camera. You just spent six hours this week that you didn’t have doing a video that nobody’s watching. You did that last week and three weeks before that. And unfortunately, it’s your fault. You just don’t know it yet.” That language mirrors the viewer’s emotional state, creating an immediate connection.

The I in BINGE, build intrigue, follows immediately. Intrigue functions as hook part two: it explains why the viewer should keep watching without teaching anything or giving away spoilers. The shift is from emotional trigger to teasing the transformation. Adam describes the flow as: feeling leads into teased solution, which leads directly into the first talking point, with no seams visible to the viewer.

Adam teaches a pronoun-shifting pattern he calls the Yo-Yo Method (originally described as “you, we, I, we, you”) to structure the combined hook and intrigue section. The pattern moves through five beats:

For example, “You’ve been told you can’t eat at McDonald’s, you can’t eat bread, you can’t eat ice cream. We’ve been told we have to eat dry chicken, broccoli, and rice six times a day. I got sick of it and started looking at what actually made a difference: macronutrients, micronutrients, hormonal levels. I think if we all do that individually, we can break that cycle. You have an option.”

The N in BINGE, nudge forward, is the element that prevents viewers from finding a natural exit point in the video. A nudge closes one loop while simultaneously opening another, and it happens in the gap between talking points where distraction would otherwise set in. Adam compares the underlying rhythm to a video sales letter: open loop, close loop, alternating between dopamine, serotonin, and cortisol. The viewer feels good, then gets a little uncomfortable; feels good, then gets a little uncomfortable. That cycle keeps attention locked in.

The biggest retention killer Adam sees is the numbered listicle format. In an era of large language models (LLMs) where people skim for answers and move on, giving away the structure makes viewers feel they’ve already gotten what they came for.

When creators say “step one, step two, step three,” they remind viewers exactly how long they’ve been watching and how much remains. Worse, when the introduction lays out all the steps upfront (“in this video, I’m going to show you how to increase your email delivery rates by doing this, this, this, and this”), viewers assume they can connect the dots themselves and leave.

The alternative is what Adam calls the “useful, but incomplete” approach: each talking point delivers real, actionable value, but the transition into the next section signals that something essential is still missing.

For example: “Optimizing your bio is definitely going to make a huge change in how much momentum you could pick up quickly. But if you’re not fully understanding the next part, nobody’s going to stick around.”

The viewer has learned something actionable but recognizes there’s a critical gap they haven’t filled yet.

The nudge forward appears not just between the intrigue and the first talking point, but at the end of every subsequent talking point throughout the entire video. The structure within each section follows a repeating pattern: micro hook, talking point content, nudge into the next section. Adam notes that when this is done intentionally, each talking point can also be extracted as a stand-alone short-form video with no additional editing.

One technique is asking viewers to take a specific action and report back. Rather than “leave a comment below,” Adam might say: “Go to your analytics, look at this specific metric, and tell me what it says on yours. I’d love to hear, and let me know what your channel is about.” That request gives the viewer a reason to pause, investigate, and return with something personal to share.

A second technique, Adam calls the public proclamation, takes engagement further. The public proclamation is a scripted moment where the creator asks viewers to make a commitment in the comments.

The script might say: “Most people are going to watch this video and not do anything with it. So I want you to proclaim in the comments, if you’re brave enough, that today is the day my channel finally breaks free.” Adam puts that exact phrase on screen in quotes so viewers can see others making the same declaration.

The psychology behind this is twofold. People want accountability and to be seen. When a viewer sees other commenters making the same proclamation, social proof kicks in. As Adam puts it, “Nothing attracts a crowd like a crowd.”

Engagement also sends direct signals to YouTube’s algorithm. When viewers comment, YouTube knows they’re actively watching, not folding laundry across the room with the video playing in the background. An active audience is more valuable to YouTube because active viewers are more likely to see and engage with ads. Channels with higher engagement rates tend to get more distribution as a result.

Adam also recommends responding to every comment until the channel grows to a point where that becomes logistically impossible. That interaction, even brief, transforms the relationship from passive consumption to something closer to a face-to-face conversation. The effect on conversion is significant: viewers who have interacted with a creator, even once, move toward a buying decision faster than those who watch passively for years.

When it’s time to pitch a lead magnet or direct viewers to a resource, Adam uses what he calls the “by the way, anyway” technique. The pitch is woven into the script at a point where it feels like a casual thought, not a sales segment.

For example: “Hey, speaking of which, I put everything you need into my Ultimate YouTube Growth Guide. It’s a perfect companion for all these free videos here on YouTube. I’ll have a link in the description box below. But if you take time to download it, do something with it, please. Otherwise, don’t waste your time. Anyway, back to the video.”

The goal is never to pull viewers off the platform during the video. The resource is something they should explore after they finish watching. The key is that the pitch never shifts the creator’s tone. Adam compares it to a barbecue conversation: if a friend mentions they need a specific car part and you happen to have one in your garage, you’d casually offer it and go right back to talking about football. The moment a creator switches into a polished sales voice with urgent language and countdown timers, viewers feel the bait-and-switch and disengage.

The E in BINGE, end in momentum, addresses a mistake Adam sees constantly: creators signaling the video is ending long before it actually does. Telling viewers “we’re about to wrap up” with six minutes remaining in a seven-minute video is an invitation to leave.

The goal of ending with momentum is to move the viewer from one video to the next, increasing session time and improving overall YouTube retention. The more videos a viewer watches in a single session, the stronger the trust signal sent to the algorithm, and the deeper the relationship between the creator and the viewer.

Adam had a client recently tell him on a group call that she had watched every single video on his channel. That level of consumption doesn’t happen accidentally. It’s engineered through momentum at the end of each video, pushing the viewer into the next one.

Adam also recommends using unlisted YouTube videos as thank-you page content.

When someone downloads a lead magnet or completes a purchase, the confirmation page displays an unlisted YouTube video rather than one hosted on Vimeo or another platform. Even if the viewer watches only a few seconds, YouTube registers a connection between that viewer and the creator’s channel.

From that point forward, the creator’s public videos begin appearing on the viewer’s YouTube homepage through browse and suggested feeds. Adam describes this as “organic retargeting without it being an ad.”

#1679. Pushing for equal pay: Everything you need to know

Glassdoor Team | Author & Career Expert at Glassdoor | Oct 8, 2026

Glassdoor research found the gender pay gap doubles over a career, from 12% for workers with no experience to 25% after 30 years.¹ Equal pay for equal work has been federal law since 1963, but the gap persists.

This year's Equal Pay Day fell on March 26, 2026, marking how far into the year women work to match men's prior-year earnings.

Women earned about 85 cents per dollar men earned in 2024, up from 81 cents in 2003, per Pew Research Center's median hourly earnings data.

Pew finds that factors such as occupational segregation and work experience explain much of the gap. Chris Martin, a senior economist at Glassdoor, adds that "bias can drive pay and promotion gaps" and "women still shoulder more caregiving work."

"I definitely make less than my male coworkers and rarely get promoted as much as they do," said one senior analyst on Glassdoor Community when speaking about the pay gap.

Pay transparency gives workers the data to spot gaps, starting with knowing coworkers can talk about their salaries.

Glassdoor has reported that pay transparency can help women negotiate fair pay. Ask your human resources (HR) department to list salary ranges and audit for gender pay gaps.

"From what I've seen, companies are skirting around this by providing ranges that are so wide they're essentially useless," one human resources manager wrote on Glassdoor Community.

A growing number of states and cities require pay ranges through pay transparency laws.

The wage gap is more than a feeling. Many women hesitate to negotiate, fearing penalties.

Without salary data, it's hard to know where you stand. The right tools help you ensure you're paid fairly.

Here are four ways to help you do just that.

Check a company's diversity and inclusion rating on Glassdoor, plus its demographic ratings and salaries.

Nudge your employer to commit to equitable pay by conducting a self-audit, and lead it if you can. Check the benefits tab on any Glassdoor employer profile and compare the company's values, benefits, and perks to your own.

Check Glassdoor Salaries for what your role pays locally. As of October 2026, Delta flight attendants report about $67,100 per year and Southwest flight attendants about $60,400.

Ask Glassdoor Community's anonymous Salary Negotiations bowl how companies handle interviews and negotiation.

Is a $600 cycling shoe nearly 70 percent better than a $350 cycling shoe?

That’s the question I kept asking myself this summer as I rode in Sidi’s $600 Shot 3 ($550 at some retailers) and $350 Genius X Wyve.

And to be clear, I don’t consider a $350 cycling shoe inexpensive. These are both high-end shoes from one of the most storied brands in cycling. This isn’t a comparison between a bargain shoe and a race-day dream shoe. It’s about what spending more gets you, and whether you’ll notice the difference once you’re clipped in and riding.

I started with one of the clearest differences between the shoes: their soles. Sidi rates the Shot 3’s “R1FC carbon sole with Nexus Bridge” at 12 for stiffness. The Genius X Wyve’s “R3CC carbon-composite” sole gets a 6.

That’s a big difference on paper. Stiff carbon soles are a calling card of expensive cycling shoes, with an implied promise: Stiffer is better, and better is worth more money.

Research co-authored by Tripp Hurt, a biomechanics researcher at the University of Colorado Boulder, suggests the answer is more complicated than shoe marketing makes it sound. In one study, trained cyclists sprinted in shoes with soles of different stiffness. The researchers found that performance began to fall below a certain level of stiffness, but the differences between the stiffest and more flexible soles were small. Hurt has argued that, for most riders, chasing stiffness may matter less than finding a comfortable shoe that fits well.

Could I feel a difference on the bike? Yes. The Shot felt stiffer, but it didn’t feel twice as stiff as the Genius. Maybe, maybe, 10 percent stiffer at most. More importantly, I noticed no difference in performance or perceived effort that I could attribute to sole stiffness.

A bigger difference was weight. On my scale, the size 41.5 pair of Shots weighed 516 grams; the Genius pair weighed 627 grams. That’s a 111-gram difference, or 55.5 grams per shoe. To me, the lighter shoes feel easier to kick around. And when each foot is going around 80 to 100 times a minute, carrying less weight on each foot seems like it should add up.

But is that enough to spend about 70 percent more on the Shot? I’ve always preferred a light shoe, but even I struggle to articulate a compelling benefit beyond “It feels better.” For some riders, that may be reason enough.

If you’re obsessive about climbing and watts per kilogram, the Shot’s 111-gram weight advantage does reduce your system weight. All else equal, that could make you marginally faster on a climb where gravity is the main resistance. For an 80-kilogram rider-and-bike system, 111 grams is about a 0.14 percent weight reduction.

Both shoes use Sidi’s Millennium fit, which the company describes as having a “slightly wider forefoot” to accommodate a broader range of foot shapes. I used to love Sidi’s classic slipper fit, but my feet have changed as I’ve gotten older, and now I need more room. On my feet, the Millennium fit is notably roomier than Sidi’s classic shape and closer to how Specialized’s and Shimano’s shoes fit. But the Millennium fit is not a “wide fit” cycling shoe in the traditional sense.

I find it excellent: My toes can spread without hot spots, while the shoes remain secure and supportive.

But on Sidi’s recreational-to-performance fit scale, the Shot scores a 10 while the Genius scores a 6. I found that confounding. If both shoes use the Millennium fit, what explains the different ratings? I reached out to Sidi to help decode, but the company didn’t respond by publication.

On my feet, the Shot’s upper felt firmer and more supportive. The Genius was plusher, with a little more give. The shoes had about the same room, and both held my heel equally well. I’d describe the difference I felt like this: The Shot felt like a bear hug; the Genius, a swaddle.

I initially preferred how locked in the Shot felt. But as I did more and longer rides, the Genius proved more comfortable.

Both shoes use Sidi’s “NUUN 001C” aluminum dials, but arrange them differently. The Shot has both dials on the tongue, where they tighten laces that pull the shoe together. The Genius puts its dials on the side: The upper dial tightens an adjustable-length forefoot strap, while the lower uses the lacing to pull the closure together.

The Shot’s cleaner arrangement should be more aerodynamic and may help explain why it’s lighter. But I preferred the Genius’s closure. I like my upper closures as tight as possible, just a little bit shy of painful, and its adjustable forefoot strap gave me more ability to fine-tune the fit; and, at a similar tightness, the Genius felt more comfortable than the Shot. That let me cinch it tighter and get a more secure fit without sacrificing comfort.

The Genius is physically bulkier, so it was more likely to brush the crankarm as I pedaled. That never happened with the Shot. To prevent it, I needed to set the Genius cleats for a slightly wider stance; with the Shot, I could use a narrower Q-factor. Crankarm rub is a sensation I find very annoying. For me, the wider stance wasn’t a problem. I’m not particularly sensitive to Q-factor, but I know some riders are, so I wanted to share the difference.

One thing about both shoes I want to highlight: Sidi offers replacement parts for many current and older models, including dials, buckles, straps, and heel pads. Sidi shoes have a reputation for lasting, and access to replacement parts can help extend their life. When you’re spending $350 or $600 on a pair, that matters.

Frankly, both the Shot and the Genius are excellent shoes. But I ultimately preferred the less expensive Genius. It was simply more comfortable, and that comfort paid dividends in my riding. The Shot’s stiffness and weight advantages were noticeable, but they didn’t pay off in a way I valued.

Is the Shot a nearly 70 percent better shoe than the Genius X Wyve? For me, no. If you want the lightest, stiffest shoe and value those differences, the Shot may be worth the extra $250. But if comfort matters more, the Genius is the better shoe—and it costs less.

Moving heavy furniture becomes more hazardous as you age, posing risks like back injuries, joint sprains, pulled muscles, fractures and heart attacks.

According to Armstrong, moving furniture poses the greatest risk of injury to the lower back and shoulders. More layout diversity, new typefaces, and restructured archives.

You may have noticed that things look a little different around here. We’ve just launched the third major redesign in Nieman Lab history. Hope you like it!

My No. 1 goal for this design (internal codename: Roscoe) was increasing design diversity on article pages — to enable a greater degree of art direction. In our previous design, we’ve had three basic layout choices for an article — which basically came down to how large we wanted to play its art. Now we have vastly more room to tie the design to the article — multiple layouts, multiple arrangements of headlines/decks/art, multiple background colors, multiple optical sizes, and more. For about a decade, we’ve divided our stories into “hubs”: Business Models, Mobile & Apps, Audience & Social, Aggregation & Discovery, and Reporting & Production. Those had started to feel a little outmoded; it’s been a while since “mobile” was anything other than the middle of the mainstream, for instance. So we’ve replaced them with six “beats” more attuned to the times: AI & Journalism, Search & Platforms, Audience & Creators, Local News, Reporting & Production, and Business & Revenue. With a little AI assistance, we’ve populated those collections with all 12,000-plus articles we’ve published since 2008.

Those 12,000-plus articles were one of the biggest challenges — making sure they all made the transition cleanly. More than 100 of those articles used custom CSS; each one had to be checked (and most of them updated) to match new layout structures and typefaces. In all, there are more than 100,000 unique URLs on niemanlab.org that now have a new look. And we now have enough history to finish off the homepage with stories we’ve published on this day over the decades.

Our new primary typefaces are TypeTogether’s Literata as the serif and Bakken & Bæck’s Schibsted Grotesk as the sans. (Schibsted Grotesk is, of course, the custom typeface designed for the European media house.) My thanks to the Freight collection (Text, Display, and Sans) for its decade-plus of typographic service.

I’ve designed and coded every iteration of niemanlab.org since founding the Lab in 2008, and it’s been a point of perverse pride for me to be a journalist with some technical ability. But it also means that, over the past 18 years, our backend has accumulated the handcoded cruft of a zillion PHP bugfixes and CSS hacks. So this time I was happy to do the vast majority of the work in Claude Code, which made some of the more ambitious ideas I had far easier to implement. The code is clean and organized in a way that should make it easier to build further.
