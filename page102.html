#1313. Why Full-Length Mirrors Are Disappearing From Hotels

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

But not from this NYC lobby loved by Law Roach and Alix Earle.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

I was recently at a hotel in the U.K.—I won’t say which—and despite all of its critical acclaim and hefty price tag, my partner pointed out a critical flaw in our room.

“Oh my god,” he whispered. “There’s no full-length mirror in here.”

We were shocked. We were running late for a very posh dinner and, understandably, were hoping to give ourselves the old up-and-down at least once before walking out the door. We checked both sides of the closet doors, looked in the bathroom once again and ultimately stared at each other in disbelief. Five-star rooms, it turns out, now come with every conceivable luxury except the ability to see what you actually look like.

The presence or absence of a full-length mirror in your guest room is, in fact, a hotly contested aspect of hotel design these days.

Just ask Lyon Porter and Jersey Banks, the husband-and-wife co-founders of hip hotel group Urban Cowboy—they’re divided down the middle on the issue. “We put so much thought into the character of each room, and a standard full-length mirror can feel like a purely functional addition that doesn’t connect with anything around it,” said Porter. His workaround is to hunt down antique full-length mirrors or large framed ones that look like part of the décor. “I understand the need for one,” he added. “I just want it to feel as considered as everything else.”

Banks, however, feels differently. “This is a long-standing debate at Urban Cowboy between Lyon and me, and I’m firmly pro full-length mirror,” said the co-owner. The argument is one that anyone who has ever put a scintilla of effort into getting dressed will recognize: you should be able to see how your shoes work with your outfit “while doing normal human things like standing and strutting, not counter yoga, with one foot propped up on the bathroom vanity trying to catch a glimpse.”

So why is the full-length mirror disappearing from hotel rooms? A number of factors both actual and proximate are driving the change—the sky-high price of real estate, the growth of profit-churning private equity funds getting into the hotel biz, an ever-increasing cost of doing or buying anything. But what it boils down to is that hotels are prioritizing per-square-foot revenue optimization, which means that the average hotel room size is shrinking, and, therefore, sacrifices have to be made. Historically, standard American hotel rooms were about 350 square feet; the average new build project these days is closer to 180 to 250 square feet. And when your hotel’s rooms start getting smaller, sacrifices have to be made.

Not everyone has caved. Jacob Dondzila, general manager of the Shinola Hotel in Detroit, has watched the trend with some dismay. “Many hotels have traded the full-length mirror for a cleaner wall or a bigger TV, and guests feel that the moment they’re getting dressed,” he said. Shinola, which blends historic and new structures, went the other way with custom-made furnishings, including a full-length mirror in every room.

Moxy Hotels is leading the way with pint-sized hotel rooms—across their locations, almost all room categories have done away with full-sized closets in favor of wall hooks, and bathrooms have been rejiggered with sliding barn doors and by placing the sink outside the shower and toilet spaces. One hotel staple they haven’t skimped on as part of their big shrink? Full-length mirrors. Moxy Hotels uses its to write personalized, cheeky messages to guests, which, naturally, end up in plenty of mirror selfies. While snapping yours, you’d hardly notice that you’re simultaneously standing in your entry, next to your bed area, and in front of your bathroom sink all at once.

“Being intentional with space is a big part of our design philosophy,” said Mitchell Hochberg, president of Lightstone, which has developed seven Moxy hotels. “The full-length mirror is one element we never considered compromising on.”

The Peninsula New York takes a more white-glove approach. While every room contains a full-length mirror, managing director Samir Ibrahim said the hotel can happily add extra mirrors and enhanced lighting to a guest’s setup “for glam nights and getting ready.”

Two themalian hotels have found a more discreet solution. At Portrait Milano, near-floor-to-ceiling mirrors framed in dark wood hide behind the curtains, appearing only when guests need them, with additional mirrors fitted to the inside of the sliding bathroom doors. And at Portrait Roma, they’re tucked inside the wardrobes—there when you need it, invisible when you don’t.

And then there’s The Dominick in SoHo, which has full-length mirrors in some, though not all, of its rooms, but makes up for it with a showstopper in the lobby. The dramatic wall-size mirror has become a favorite of stylist Law Roach and has turned up on Alix Earle’s Instagram. The mirror’s jumbo size practically begs passersby to capture, tag, and hope for a repost.

So the next time you’re booking a hotel, by all means study the thread count and the bath amenities. But before you hand over your credit card, make sure the room comes with one unassuming amenity: a mirror that lets you see your entire outfit. Because nothing kills the fantasy of a five-star stay quite like having to do counter yoga to see if your shoes go with your pants.

#686. Why Do Some People Get Fevers After Vaccines? Scientists May Have Found the Answer

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The microbes in your gut may help explain why you develop a fever after a vaccine while someone else feels fine.

Two people can receive the same vaccine and have very different experiences. One develops a fever and feels miserable for a day, while the other barely notices anything. Scientists have long sought to understand why these reactions vary so widely, and new research suggests that part of the answer may lie in the trillions of microbes living in our intestines.

An international research team found that people who developed stronger fevers after vaccination already showed signs of heightened inflammation and distinctive gut microbial activity before receiving their shots. When researchers transferred their gut microbes into mice, the animals also developed stronger inflammatory responses to vaccination. The findings point to a bacterial protein called flagellin as a possible contributor and raise intriguing questions about whether diet could influence how the body responds to vaccines.

Vaccination prevents infectious diseases and protects public health, but concerns about fever and flu-like symptoms can discourage people from getting vaccinated. Understanding why these reactions vary could help researchers find ways to reduce them and address a source of vaccine hesitancy.

The gut microbiome is a plausible place to look because its trillions of microorganisms continually communicate with the immune system, helping shape its baseline activity. Because microbial communities differ between people, the same vaccine may encounter very different immune starting points.

To investigate, researchers led by Ruth Ley at the Max Planck Institute for Biology Tübingen established the µHEAT (Microbial-Human Ecology and Temperature) study. With support from Meral Esen at the Institute of Tropical Medicine in Tübingen, they recruited healthy young adults receiving a SARS-CoV-2 vaccine in 2021 and 2022.

Participants provided fecal samples for microbial analysis and blood samples for immune measurements. They also regularly recorded their temperature during the week before and the week after vaccination.

Organizing the study during the pandemic required the team to recruit participants before they had already received their vaccines, while keeping visits and measurements manageable.

“It was a challenging time to set up a new human study, with the pandemic ongoing,” says lead author Kelsey Huus, who led µHEAT during her postdoctoral research and is now an assistant professor at the University of Ottawa in Canada. “We didn’t want to burden participants with too many measurements or visits, and it was a bit of a race against time to get the study organized before everyone was already vaccinated. But it was absolutely worth it, because the project yielded some very exciting results.”

Some participants developed substantial fevers. Before vaccination, their gut microbes showed greater activity in genes involved in bacterial movement, particularly those used to make whip-like swimming structures called flagella.

Flagella are built from proteins called flagellins. Because pathogens such as Salmonella also produce flagellins, the immune system recognizes these proteins as potential danger signals and responds with inflammation. Blood and fecal measurements suggested that participants who developed stronger fevers already had higher levels of inflammation before vaccination.

The human observations revealed an association, but the researchers needed an experiment to test whether the microbes themselves could influence vaccine responses. They transferred participants’ fecal microbiomes into mice that lacked intestinal microbes of their own, then vaccinated the animals.

Mice receiving microbes from participants with stronger fever responses developed more severe inflammation than those receiving microbes from people with milder responses. The effect depended partly on the animals’ ability to sense flagellin, providing evidence that the bacterial protein contributed to the response.

“Vaccination provides a controlled way to understand why people can respond so differently to the same immune challenge,” says Ley, director of the Department of Microbiome Science. “Our findings show that microbial flagellin is one contributor to this baseline immune state, providing a potential link between differences in the gut microbiome and differences in immune responses.”

The team next explored what might encourage gut microbes to produce more flagellin. Participants eating plant-based diets tended to have weaker fever responses, making diet a candidate for further investigation.

“We could see that certain food additives increased flagellin production by gut microbes in vitro,” explains Benoit Chassaing, Inserm research director at Institut Pasteur. “Interestingly, these findings show that components of an industrialized diet may promote the growth of flagellated gut bacteria and increase overall flagellin production.”

Animal experiments offered further evidence. “We found that a high-fat Western-style diet enhanced inflammatory vaccine responses in mice,” says co-author Hirohito Abo of Georgia State University in Atlanta.

“While we could not show that diet determines vaccine responses in humans, our study findings suggest that diet is one factor capable of modifying a pro-inflammatory microbiome,” Huus says. “Interventional studies in people will be needed to determine whether dietary changes can alter microbial flagellin production in humans and, in turn, vaccine responses like fever.”

Future trials could investigate whether interventions targeting the microbiome can reduce strong fevers after vaccination. More broadly, the study suggests that gut microbes help establish an immune state that may influence responses to challenges beyond vaccines.

“Ultimately, this work adds to growing evidence that the gut microbiome plays a central role in human immune health,” Huus adds. “Understanding these interactions could now open new opportunities to improve how we predict, prevent, and manage individual differences in immune responses.”

Apple's folding iPhone has, by all accounts, got off to a cracking start. Pre-orders quickly sold out, with delivery dates now extending well into 2027. It seems that after a string of somewhat disappointing launches such as the iPhone Air and Vision Pro, Apple has a bonafide hit on its hands. But as always happens in the first few days of a brand new product launch, a few snags are coming into focus.

One of the more curious issues facing some iPhone Duo units is the screen cracking in one very specific location. This involves a thin crack down the centre hinge, and a line of stuck bright white pixels across the right side of the display. The result has aready led to some amusing wallpaper designs making light of the issue.

But why are multiple Duo models breaking in the same way? Well, now that teardowns of the device have begun, we might have an answer. iFixit has revealed an area of concentrated components around the hinge, with a different shape to the rest of the hinge, which could be causing excess stress on the screen.

"To see where the stresses build up from folding, we built a 3D hinge model from teardown footage, patents, and CT scans, then compared it with the reported damage locations," reports iFixit. "The breaks cluster near the transition from the gap for the crossing flex cable to the central hinge support. The damage we’re seeing in photos isn’t right where the display cable passes through the hinge, but slightly lower, at a point where several edges meet and change how the thin display is supported as it bends... The screen’s curved fold comes down onto it, creating a small stress hotspot near the reported break location."

#500. Exercise doesn't just strengthen your heart. It rewires it

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Exercise does more than make the heart stronger. New research suggests it also physically reshapes the nerves that control the heart, revealing an unexpected difference between the left and right sides of the body.

Scientists at the University of Bristol (UK) discovered that moderate aerobic exercise changes the structure of nerve cells involved in regulating heart activity. Even more surprisingly, the changes were dramatically different on the two sides of the body. The findings, published in Autonomic Neuroscience, could eventually help doctors develop more precise treatments for irregular heartbeats, chest pain, angina, and stress-induced 'broken-heart' syndrome.

The discovery was made in rats, so further research is needed to determine whether the same changes occur in humans. Nevertheless, it offers a new perspective on how physical activity influences the nervous system that keeps the heart functioning.

Exercise Changes the Heart's Hidden Control System

The heart is regulated partly by the autonomic nervous system, a network of nerves that automatically manages essential functions such as heartbeat, breathing, and digestion. One branch of this system helps the heart beat faster when the body needs more oxygen, such as during exercise or stressful situations.

Small clusters of nerve cells called stellate ganglia play an important role in this process. Located on both sides of the lower neck and upper chest, these nerve hubs send signals that influence heart rate and other aspects of heart function.

The new research suggests that regular exercise changes these nerve clusters in ways scientists had not previously recognized.

Study lead author Dr. Augusto Coppi, Senior Lecturer in Veterinary Anatomy at the University of Bristol, said: "The discovery points to a previously hidden left-right pattern in the body's 'autopilot' system that helps run the heart.

"These nerve clusters act like the heart's dimmer switch and we've shown that regular, moderate exercise remodels that switch in a side-specific way. This could help explain why some treatments work better on one side than the other and, in future, help doctors target therapies more precisely and effectively."

Just 10 Weeks of Exercise Produced Striking Nerve Changes

To investigate how exercise affects these nerve clusters, the researchers examined rats that underwent 10 weeks of moderate aerobic training. Aerobic exercise is physical activity that increases heart rate and breathing, such as brisk walking, swimming, or cycling.

The study involved researchers from University College London (UCL) in the UK, the University of São Paulo (USP), and the Federal University of São Paulo (UNIFESP) in Brazil.

The team used advanced three-dimensional analysis techniques known as stereology to examine the nerve cells. This approach allows scientists to estimate the number, size, and distribution of cells within tissues.

The results revealed an unusual imbalance between the two sides of the body. In the trained rats, the cardiovascular nerve cluster on the right contained roughly four times as many neurons as the cluster on the left, a striking difference from the pattern observed in untrained rats.

The nerve cells also changed in size, but in opposite directions. Neurons on the left nearly doubled in size, while those on the right became slightly smaller.

These findings suggest that exercise does not affect all the nerves controlling the heart equally. Instead, it produces distinct structural changes on each side, potentially providing clues about how the nervous system adapts to regular physical activity.

Could the Discovery Lead to Better Heart Treatments?

The findings may have important implications for treating conditions involving abnormal nerve activity around the heart.

Arrhythmias occur when the heart beats too quickly, too slowly, or irregularly. Angina is chest pain or discomfort typically caused by insufficient blood flow to the heart muscle. Another condition, commonly called 'broken-heart' syndrome, involves a sudden weakening of the heart muscle that is often triggered by intense emotional or physical stress.

In some patients, doctors treat these problems by reducing excessive nerve signals reaching the heart. Procedures such as stellate ganglion nerve blocks temporarily interrupt nerve activity, while denervation involves disrupting certain nerve pathways.

Understanding how the left and right nerve clusters differ could eventually help physicians determine which side should be targeted for the greatest benefit.

Dr. Coppi explained: "Irregular heart rhythms, known as arrhythmias, stress-induced 'broken-heart' syndrome, and certain types of chest pain are often treated by dialing down overactive stellate ganglia -- the paired small nerve hubs in the lower neck/upper chest area that send 'go faster' signals to the heart.

"By mapping how exercise changes these ganglia on each side, the study offers clues that could one day fine-tune procedures like nerve blocks or denervation to the side most likely to help. The findings are early-stage and in rats, so clinical studies would need to follow."

What's Next for Exercise and Heart Research?

Although the structural changes were substantial, scientists still need to establish exactly how they affect heart function.

The research team plans to investigate whether the differences between the left and right nerve clusters influence how the heart behaves during rest and physical activity. They also intend to look for similar patterns in other animal models and eventually in humans.

For human studies, the researchers hope to use noninvasive markers, which could provide information about nerve activity without requiring surgery or tissue removal.

If the same left-right differences are found in people, the discovery could help guide more personalized treatments for arrhythmias, difficult-to-treat angina, and stress-induced 'broken-heart' syndrome.

Dr. Coppi added: "Understanding these left-right differences could help us personalize treatments for heart rhythm disorders and angina. Our next step is to test how these structural changes map onto function and whether similar patterns appear in larger animals and humans."

#695. Scientists Discover a Protective Gene That Could Help Keep the Brain Young

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Boosting a protective gene helped aging mice preserve memory, suggesting that strengthening the brain’s own defenses could offer a way to delay cognitive decline in people.

Some older adults remain cognitively resilient even when their brains show signs of Alzheimer’s disease. Researchers investigating the biological mechanisms behind this resilience have identified a gene called Pla2g2f that may help protect the aging brain from memory loss.

In a study published in Nature Neuroscience, researchers found that increasing Pla2g2f activity in aging mice protected against synapse loss, inflammation, and disruptions in fat metabolism. Boosting the gene in middle-aged mice before substantial cognitive decline developed also changed the trajectory of age-related memory loss.

The study’s lead and senior authors are Cinzia Vicidomini, PhD, and Amar Sahay, PhD, respectively, of the Mass General Brigham Department of Psychiatry, the Harvard Stem Cell Institute, and the Broad Institute of Harvard and MIT.

As the brain ages, protective and harmful biological processes compete to influence its health. When harmful changes become dominant, synapses deteriorate, inflammation increases, and problems with lipid metabolism can interfere with normal brain function.

“As humans age, both protective and harmful biological processes shape brain health. When the balance shifts in favor of harmful processes, age-related changes in the brain can accelerate, including synapse loss, disruptions in lipid metabolism and chronic inflammation,” said Vicidomini.

To identify factors that might counteract these changes, the researchers searched for genes activated in response to synapse loss in the hippocampus, a brain region essential for memory. They focused on dentate granule cells, neurons involved in forming and processing memories.

Their investigation identified Pla2g2f, a gene that produces a secreted enzyme called a phospholipase. Its role in the brain had previously been unknown.

The researchers discovered that Pla2g2f levels increased in the hippocampus during aging and under conditions of brain inflammation. The gene was also expressed at higher levels in older adults whose brains showed signs of Alzheimer’s disease pathology but who remained cognitively resilient.

These findings suggested that Pla2g2f could be part of a natural protective response that helps the brain withstand age-related damage.

To investigate the gene’s function, the team used genetic and viral tools to decrease or increase Pla2g2f activity in the brains of aging mice.

Reducing the gene’s activity worsened several features of brain aging, including synapse loss, inflammatory changes in microglia, increased reactivity in astrocytes, and impaired formation and maturation of new neurons. The mice also experienced disruptions in lipid metabolism, accumulation of fat droplets in brain cells, and greater memory impairment.

Increasing Pla2g2f activity had the opposite effect. It protected against many of these changes, helped maintain healthy communication between neurons, and preserved memory.

“Remarkably, boosting Pla2g2f in middle-aged mice before they developed substantial cognitive decline altered the trajectory of age-related memory loss,” said Sahay.

The results suggest that strengthening this protective mechanism before significant damage occurs may be especially valuable.

The researchers also uncovered a connection between Pla2g2f and the regulation of fats in the brain.

Microglia, the brain’s immune cells, help clear waste, maintain synapses, and support neurons. During aging and Alzheimer’s disease, however, these cells can accumulate lipid droplets, making them less effective and contributing to chronic inflammation.

Experiments using human microglia derived from induced pluripotent stem cells showed that neuronal PLA2G2F could influence these immune cells. Increasing its activity reduced the accumulation of lipid droplets and cholesterol esters, a form of stored cholesterol. Both are commonly found at elevated levels in microglia associated with Alzheimer’s disease.

Further experiments showed that disrupting the enzyme’s catalytic site prevented its ability to mobilize lipid droplets and reduce inflammatory changes in brain cells. This suggests that the enzyme’s biochemical activity is important for its protective effects.

Together, the findings point to PLA2G2F as a protective signal released by neurons that helps maintain healthy lipid metabolism and limit inflammation across brain cells.

Aging is the greatest risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease, making the discovery of natural mechanisms that preserve memory particularly important.

The researchers believe that increasing Pla2g2f activity could eventually offer a way to strengthen cognitive resilience and potentially delay the onset of mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer’s disease.

“Our findings suggest that increasing Pla2g2f activity during aging could potentially help maintain cognitive resilience and delay or prevent the onset of mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease,” said Sahay.

However, the evidence for memory protection currently comes from animal experiments. Although the researchers also demonstrated beneficial effects in human microglia, it remains unknown whether increasing Pla2g2f activity would safely protect memory in people.

“Additional studies will be needed to determine whether this approach can be safely translated into therapies for patients,” Sahay said. “We are actively exploring potential ways to safely increase Pla2g2f levels in the aging brain and in Alzheimer’s disease as a possible therapeutic strategy.”

#528. A 2,700-year-old solar eclipse just revealed something surprising about Earth

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

An international research team has revisited one of the oldest precisely dated records of a total solar eclipse, uncovering new details about how Earth's rotation has changed over thousands of years. By combining historical geography with modern astronomical calculations, the scientists reconstructed an eclipse that occurred in 709 BCE and determined how it would have appeared from Qufu, the ancient capital of China's Lu Duchy.

The researchers also examined an early account that may describe the solar corona, the Sun's faint outer atmosphere that becomes visible to the naked eye during a total solar eclipse. Their analysis of its possible shape suggests that the Sun was returning to a more active phase following an extended period of unusually low activity.

Published in Astrophysical Journal Letters, the findings provide more precise evidence of Earth's rotation speed in ancient times. They also offer potential independent support for previous reconstructions of solar activity based on radiocarbon measurements.

An Ancient Chinese Eclipse Record Holds a Crucial Clue

On July 17, 709 BCE, observers at the court of the Lu Duchy witnessed a total solar eclipse. The event was preserved in the "Spring and Autumn Annals," a historical chronicle compiled approximately 200 to 300 years later. The original account stated that "the Sun was totally eclipsed."

An additional description appears in the "Hanshu" (Book of Han), which preserves a passage written about seven centuries after the eclipse. This later account contains an unusual observation that may refer to the Sun's corona.

"What makes this record special isn't just its age, but also a later addendum in the 'Hanshu' (Book of Han) based on a quote written seven centuries after the eclipse. It describes the eclipsed Sun as 'completely yellow above and below.' This addendum has been traditionally associated with a record of a solar corona. If this is truly the case, it represents one of the earliest surviving written descriptions of the solar corona," explained lead author Hisashi Hayakawa, an assistant professor at Nagoya University's Institute for Space-Earth Environmental Research and Institute for Advanced Research.

Initially, however, the historical account appeared to conflict with modern astronomy. When the team calculated the eclipse's path using existing estimates of Earth's ancient rotation, their results indicated that a total eclipse should not have been visible from the previously assumed location of the Lu court in Qufu.

The researchers traced the discrepancy to a mistake in the city's geographic coordinates. By examining archaeological excavation reports and historical geographic evidence, they determined that earlier calculations had placed ancient Qufu approximately eight kilometers from its actual location.

Correcting this relatively small error made it possible to reconstruct the eclipse more accurately and extract valuable information about Earth's rotation and the Sun's appearance.

"This correction allowed us to accurately measure the Earth's rotation during the total eclipse, calculate the orientation of the Sun's rotation axis, and simulate the corona's appearance," explained Hayakawa, who holds doctorates in solar physics and oriental history.

Why Ancient China Preserved Such Detailed Astronomical Records

The survival of this remarkable observation reflects the importance of astronomy in ancient Chinese society. Successive dynasties employed specialists to monitor celestial events because unusual phenomena in the sky were often interpreted as omens.

This practice, known as omenology, was closely connected to political beliefs. Eclipses, auroras, and other extraordinary astronomical events were sometimes considered warnings that an emperor had acted improperly.

As a result, court astronomers carefully documented changes in the sky. The records maintained across successive dynasties eventually became some of the world's most valuable historical sources for studying ancient eclipses.

Even so, the researchers emphasize an important distinction between the original eclipse observation and the later description of the corona.

The 709 BCE event is regarded as the earliest explicit written record of a total solar eclipse that can be securely dated. It may also be associated with one of the oldest surviving descriptions of the solar corona, but that interpretation remains uncertain because the relevant passage appears in a source written centuries after the event.

Although the corona account requires caution, the established timing of the eclipse provides a reliable basis for investigating Earth's historical rotation. The possible corona description also offers a valuable comparison with other studies of ancient solar activity.

How a 2,700-Year-Old Eclipse Reveals Earth's Faster Rotation

Earth does not rotate at a perfectly constant rate. Over long periods, its spin has gradually slowed, partly because of friction associated with ocean tides generated by the Moon's gravitational pull.

Consequently, Earth rotated slightly faster 2,700 years ago than it does today.

Using the revised position of ancient Qufu, the researchers obtained improved constraints on Earth's rotation during the period spanning the eighth through sixth centuries BCE.

Their calculations placed delta T (ΔT), a measurement used to describe variations in Earth's rotation relative to a uniform time scale, between 20,264 and 21,204 seconds for the eclipse of 709 BCE.

"This new dataset fixes coordinate errors in previous Earth rotation studies. Additionally, it improves the accuracy of dating and reconstructing historical astronomical events," said coauthor Mitsuru Sôma of the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan.

These measurements help scientists better understand changes in the length of Earth's day over thousands of years. They can also improve calculations of when and where other ancient astronomical events would have been visible.

Evidence That the Sun Was Awakening From a Long Quiet Period

Beyond revealing Earth's rotational history, the eclipse may provide evidence of an important transition in the Sun's activity.

Scientists have previously reconstructed ancient solar cycles by studying radiocarbon preserved in tree rings. The possible corona description from 709 BCE offers another way to examine those findings.

"This unique historical addendum for the possible solar coronal structure is critical for providing a spot reference on solar activity reconstructions from tree rings and ice cores, as well as providing independent validation of solar activity models," explained coauthor Mathew Owens, a professor of space physics at the University of Reading.

Trees absorb carbon from the atmosphere during photosynthesis, including small amounts of radioactive carbon known as radiocarbon. Some of this material becomes locked into annual growth rings, creating a natural record that researchers can examine thousands of years later.

Radiocarbon levels are influenced by cosmic rays reaching Earth's atmosphere. When the Sun becomes more active, its magnetic influence generally reduces the number of cosmic rays that reach Earth, affecting radiocarbon production.

By measuring changes in radiocarbon concentrations across successive tree rings, scientists can reconstruct how solar activity varied long before modern instruments existed.

The Sun normally follows an activity cycle lasting approximately 11 years, during which the number of sunspots and the strength of solar magnetic activity rise and fall.

Occasionally, however, the Sun enters much longer periods of reduced activity called "grand minima." During these intervals, sunspots become exceptionally rare, and the usual cycle is much less pronounced.

One such interval, known as the "Neo Assyrian Grand Minimum" or "Homer Grand Minimum," lasted from approximately 808 to 717 BCE.

The eclipse of 709 BCE took place shortly after this prolonged quiet period ended.

According to the researchers, the possible description of the solar corona is consistent with a Sun that had resumed its regular activity cycles. Its apparent structure suggests substantial magnetic activity and indicates that the Sun may have been near the peak of its approximately 11-year cycle at the time.

This interpretation agrees with earlier solar activity reconstructions derived from radiocarbon measurements in tree rings, although the uncertainty surrounding the historical corona description means it cannot be considered definitive proof.

Ancient Astronomers Are Still Helping Scientists Understand the Universe

The findings demonstrate how observations preserved for thousands of years can continue to contribute to modern scientific discoveries.

By bringing together historical documents, archaeological evidence, geographic research, and sophisticated astronomical models, scientists can recover information that would otherwise be impossible to measure directly.

In this case, a correction of just eight kilometers helped transform an ancient eclipse record into a more precise source of information about Earth's rotation and a potential window into the Sun's distant past.

"Some of our ancestors were very skilled observers," noted coauthor Dr. Meng Jin of the Lockheed Martin Solar and Astrophysics Laboratory. "When we combine their careful records with modern computational methods and historical evidence, we can potentially find new information about our planet and our star from thousands of years ago."
