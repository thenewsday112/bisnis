#1521. Benjamin Moore Just Revealed Its 2027 Color of the Year and It Nods to Tradition

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Webster Green joins a growing roster of green paint predictions.

Benjamin Moore is going green for 2027—and its choice is already in the archives. The paint company has named Webster Green (HC-130) its Color of the Year, selecting an established shade from its Historical Color Collection. The green has a clear presence without the jewel-toned intensity of emerald or the softness of sage. And, the Benjamin Moore team wanted their selection to feel timeless. “We intentionally chose a green that doesn’t reminisce,” Hannah Yeo, Benjamin Moore’s senior manager of color marketing, tells ELLE Decor.

The choice is also the latest indication that green will feature prominently in the year’s paint forecasts. Sherwin-Williams already selected Celery, a light yellow-green, and Behr chose Grounded, an olive with brown undertones.

Webster Green follows Silhouette (AF-655), Benjamin Moore’s 2026 Color of the Year, a deep brown with charcoal notes. Where Silhouette offered a dark neutral, Webster Green puts a more distinct color at the center of the forecast. Both selections, however, reflect the company’s interest in shades that balance a substantial presence with everyday versatility.

According to Yeo, the 2027 discussions began last winter and repeatedly returned to the speed of trends. The team was drawn to new ideas but found their turnover difficult to keep up with. That led to a consideration of what people might want to keep in their homes, rather than what would simply feel current. The goal, she says, was to balance “something that feels new and exciting” with “something that reassures and endures.”

To explore that idea, the team looked through Benjamin Moore’s archives, including hand-drawn room illustrations, and examined green’s recurring role in interiors. Yeo cited the earthy shades of Arts and Crafts design, Art Deco’s emerald and teals, and the moss, avocado, and olive greens of later decades. Research through trade shows, industry presentations, and travel helped reinforce the selection.

“When we looked across the past, present and future of color trends, variations on green consistently demonstrated remarkable staying power,” Andrea Magno, Benjamin Moore’s vice president of brand, color, and integrated marketing, says in a statement. “Webster Green HC-130 reflects this timeless appeal by pairing reassuring stability with optimistic energy.”

Webster Green anchors an eight-color palette that includes creamy white, pale blue, gray-inflected pink, and deeper earth tones. The collection extends the emphasis on warmth and historically informed color, but the central selection is less about recreating an old interior than reconsidering a familiar shade. As Yeo put it, “this is not a new shade, but it just feels particularly relevant in the way that people want to live today.”

#1513. Benjamin Moore Just Revealed Its 2027 Color Trends Palette

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The brand’s annual selection brings together eight shades chosen for warmth, character, and lasting appeal.

Green may get top billing, but Benjamin Moore’s 2027 color forecast doesn’t stop at one shade. Released alongside Color of the Year Webster Green HC-130, the company’s annual Color Trends palette brings together eight shades, with an emphasis on warmth, nuanced undertones, and colors that can accommodate differing decorating styles.

Selected from Benjamin Moore’s library of more than 3,500 hues, each shade is intended to work independently or in combination. White Down, Nelson Blue, and Wild Aster are the lighter options, while Shakespeare Tan, Antique Copper, Durango, and Townsend Harbor Brown bring golden, olive, and burgundy tones into the mix.

This broader collection is an established part of Benjamin Moore’s annual announcement. Last year’s eight-color palette accompanied the espresso-brown Silhouette and emphasized detail, craftsmanship, and refinement. For 2027, the brand focused on the balance between fresh inspiration and lasting appeal.

The team began with a familiar problem: keeping up with trends without constantly rethinking. Hannah Yeo, Benjamin Moore’s senior manager of color marketing, tells ELLE Decor that the goal was to find a balance between “something that feels new and exciting” and “something that reassures and endures.”

For Benjamin Moore, that means a more restrained approach to color, not less of it. Yeo sees the 2027 palette as a continuation of the company’s shift from the brighter shades of its 2023 forecast toward softer, more adaptable hues. Even the neutrals have distinct undertones: Shakespeare Tan can read as amber or olive depending on the light, while Durango brings a deeper olive note to the collection. “It feels very lived in, but also has a lot of color and personality,” Yeo says.

The deeper shades also invite some less expected applications. Yeo references the use of Townsend Harbor Brown in a sunroom, where its burgundy tones create a more enveloping atmosphere than the pale colors usually associated with the space. “It is not your typical selection for a sunroom,” she says, noting how comfortable the darker treatment feels. The willingness to reconsider a familiar room runs through the collection: softer colors needn’t be reserved for restful spaces, and deeper ones can work beyond the study or dining room.

The full Color Trends 2027 palette is available to explore on Benjamin Moore’s website.

#3509. The New Colnago G5-X Isn’t a Bargain Gravel Bike. It’s a Fast, Refined Race Bike With Serious Italian Mystique

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Riding it in Tuscany, we found a faster, more capable gravel bike that still feels like a Colnago.

The artistry of Italian masters surrounds you as you walk through the Uffizi Gallery. Botticelli, Caravaggio, Da Vinci, Michelangelo, Raphael. Hundreds of priceless paintings and sculptures fill the world-renowned museum’s halls and passageways. Artwork you have undoubtedly seen in books and magazines, on television, and in films lies around every corner.

But I wasn’t in Florence to study Renaissance and Baroque paintings; I visited the Uffizi only to burn off some jet lag before four days of riding in Tuscany. The next day I headed south to the region’s legendary strade bianche gravel roads to sample a new bike from one of cycling’s great Italian masters.

Many words have been written about Colnago and the brand’s history over the last few decades. For those new to bikes, Colnago is among cycling’s most storied marques and produces some of the world’s most sought-after bicycles.

The 72-year-old Italian company built much of its prestige and aura on its dedication to racing; numerous road cycling icons have raced on the brand’s steel, titanium, and carbon bikes. Colnago-made frames have won seemingly every Grand Tour, Monument, and spring classic event over the last six decades. And Colnago’s palmarès has only grown in the last decade.

The brand’s recent success and visibility have been inextricably tied to Tadej Pogačar’s dominant race-day performances. The Slovenian star has won two World Championships, five Tour de France titles, one Giro d’Italia victory, and several classics on Colnago bikes. Simply put, he is the most dominant road racer of his generation.

Yet, while Colnago is known to virtually every road racing fan and road bike aficionado, the brand isn’t usually the first to spring to mind when riders think of gravel bikes. Some of this can be chalked up to the discipline’s American roots; big U.S. brands like Specialized and Trek often dominate the bicycles, athletes, and events on the west side of the Atlantic. Even smaller American brands, such as Salsa and Enve, often come up in discussion before legacy road brands like Colnago.

A big part of this perception is Colnago’s relatively late entry into the gravel world. Like many European peers, Colnago was fashionably late to the gravel party, launching the G3-X in November 2019. Some American brands had a five-plus-year head start: Specialized’s Diverge and the GT Grade in 2014 and Salsa’s Warbird in 2012 were among the first purpose-built gravel models to hit shop floors.

That first Colnago gravel bike was based on the brand’s successful Prestige cyclocross platform (Wout van Aert won a CX World Championship on a Colnago in 2016). Compared with contemporary gravel bikes—and even some of the era—the G3-X handled quicker, with a taller bottom bracket and a shorter wheelbase. It also only featured 42mm tire clearance; while that sounds narrow in 2026, it was in the ballpark for a gravel bike seven years ago. Lauren De Crescenzo won the 2021 Unbound 200 on the G3-X.

In early 2024, Colnago followed up its initial gravel bike with two new gravel models. First up was the premium, Italian-made C68 Gravel; the G4-X followed a few months later. While both were excellent bikes, they fell more toward the all-road spectrum than gravel—as our review described: “the G4-X felt more like an aggressive all-road bike than a traditional gravel bike.” The G4-X and C68 Gravel geometry still closely matched the G3-X and had a stiff, responsive ride feel similar to Colnago’s road platforms. The bikes also lacked the tire clearance, frame mounts, and internal storage found on many top-end gravel models.

After launching the G4-X, Colnago listened to rider and racer feedback and quickly began work on a new gravel platform. The result, the all-new G5-X, is a bike designed from the ground up for modern gravel racing that distinctly rides like a Colnago. The brand’s R&D team—led by Davide Fumagalli, who podiumed in his age group at the 2022 UCI Gravel World Championships—focused on four key improvements for the brand’s third-generation gravel bike: Tire clearance, geometry, storage, and ride comfort.

I spent two of my four days in Tuscany riding the G5-X with Colnago’s product team on a blend of classic strade bianche white gravel roads, doubletrack, and technical singletrack (where a full suspension mountain bike wouldn’t be out of place). While not a full, long-term evaluation of the G5-X, it was a sampler plate of the bike’s potential.

Gravel tire widths seem to grow wider every season to improve traction, comfort, and speed in demanding terrain. Colnago increased the G5-X’s official clearance to 53mm nominal width, 8mm more than its predecessor. It now sits—along with bikes like Specialized’s Crux 5 (55mm clearance)—at a midpoint between wide and narrow tire clearance.

While certainly an improvement over earlier Colnago gravel bikes, the G5-X’s increased clearance doesn’t quite put it in the category of 57mm-clearance bikes like Canyon’s new Grail, Factor Elana, Argon 18 Dark Matter, 3T Extrema Italia, Allied Able, or Ari Shafer. On the flip side, the G5-X offers slightly more room for rubber than the Enve Mog and Trek Checkpoint (both at 50mm), and a bit more space for the rear tire than the Cervélo’s Aspero 5 (45mm rear, 51mm front) or the Cannondale Super-X (48mm rear, 51mm front).

The G5-X’s I sampled were fitted with 50mm-wide, Goodyear XPLR Inter tubeless tires. Optimized for the Zipp 303 XPLR SW wheels (also on the bikes), the Inters worked well and rolled fast on the smoother gravel roads. Their compound feels a little firm, but they have sufficient tread for braking and knobs for cornering.

However, I found the Inters lacked grip when the going got chunky, and I tore a sidewall on one while descending. These are the conditions where a wider, mountain bike-style tire would have been more appropriate. A 29”x2.1” tire (depending on the rim) would press Colnago’s 53mm max width. Perhaps a 29”x2.0” Continental Dubnital would have fit the conditions?

Colnago notes that its 53mm clearance is with the ISO-mandated 6mm clearance. So, depending on course conditions and rim width, you might be able to run slightly wider rubber on the G5-X on some days. But that also highly depends on your comfort with potentially sawing through the stays of a pricey Colnago frame.

Colnago also updated its gravel geometry to improve control over rough terrain and at higher speeds. The brand targeted three main points: A slacker head angle, a lower bottom bracket, and a longer wheelbase.

The G5-X’s slacker head angle (0.5 to 1.0 degrees on most sizes) gives riders more confidence on steeper terrain and at higher speeds. To balance the slacker HT, Colnago’s engineers also increased fork rake (by about 5mm). This maintains quick steering and avoids the vague feel of some slack bikes.

Along with the slacker head angle, Colnago increased the frame’s bottom bracket drop by 10mm, and that’s a big deal. A common trait of mountain bike geometry, it provides stability over rough terrain and a planted feeling in turns by lowering the rider’s center of mass relative to the axles. The increased drop also helps offset the taller height from wider tires and keeps the BB height from rising.

Colnago also lengthened the platform’s wheelbase. As with the head angle and bottom bracket height, this improves stability and control. The bike’s chainstays grew by 5mm across all sizes (to 435mm) while the front-center and reach measurements also increased.

On paper, this all sounds good; in the real world—especially on singletrack—the geometry proved to be brilliant. The G5-X is super composed and planted when descending and over choppy gravel road sections. The bike feels precise and refined—pick your line and take it.

Integrated storage for tools and flat-repair necessities is a must-have for many gravel riders. Sure, you can carry your pump, tube, and multitool in your jersey pocket or a seat bag, but then they rattle around or get dropped on rough roads. In-frame storage keeps those items tucked safely inside your downtube, and the weight is located low on the frame instead of swaying around on your back.

In my experience, the storage access doors on gravel and mountain bikes can be hit or miss. They all generally work, but some are overly complicated (like the scissor-design on the Cervélo Caledonia) or need to be positioned exactly right to stay in place (like the Fidlock one on the State Carbon All-Road). Finicky designs only cause frustration when trying to fix a mid-ride flat—or worse, when losing time in a race.

Colnago’s system is well thought out, feels robust, and works with a simple lever turn. The brand includes a neoprene baggy big enough to include a tube, two CO2 canisters, and a multitool.

In addition to the integrated downtube storage, the G5-X has mounts on the top tube for a bento box/feedbag and on the fork blades. However, unlike some adventure-minded gravel bikes, it has no attachment points underneath the top tube for bags, nor mounts on the rear triangle.

This seems like a missed opportunity by Colnago. While pure-racers may not need them, many riders use their gravel race bikes for overnight and light-duty bikepacking trips. Adding hidden mounts under the TT barely adds weight and looks stealthy. Plus, who wants to wrap Velcro straps around their Colnago and ruin the gorgeous aesthetic?

In our reviews of G4-X and C68 Gravel, our editors criticized the bikes’ firm ride. They rode like road race bikes, not plush, forgiving gravel grinders. The G5-X’s carbon layup and frame shaping prioritize comfort and compliance ahead of all-out sprinting stiffness.

Additionally, instead of the D-shaped post on previous gravel models, Colnago’s product team opted for a standard 27.2mm diameter round seatpost. This gives riders tons of options to tune seatpost offset, use a dropper, or choose a post with more inherent flex than Colnago’s stock carbon pillar. Like the post, other frame details make the G5-X easy to service and find replacement parts. It uses a 68mm BSA bottom bracket, a 1-⅛-inch stem, and standard-size headset bearings.

While I didn’t test any of Colnago’s previous gravel models, I do not doubt my colleagues’ impressions that those platforms were overly stiff. And while I typically like spry, racey bikes, my 50-year-old body doesn’t always agree after several hours in the saddle.

In my limited time on the G5-X, I never felt it was too stiff or overly harsh. Conversely, it did not feel muted or dull. Colnago is regarded as a master of refined ride quality, and the G5-X feels like the brand’s product team put a lot of time and effort into achieving the right balance for this new model. However, I need more ride time on the G5-X on home roads and trails to confirm if Colnago indeed ticked this box.

Colnago’s G5-X complete bike offerings are refreshingly straightforward. Two colors, three groupsets, and five frame sizes. The G5-X is (currently) not on Colnago’s customizer program or available as a frameset option.

The G5-X comes in two colors, green and blue. Both are beautiful, though perhaps not as wild as some of Colnago’s road offerings. But the bike is brand new, so we can hope to see more color variants in the future.

Colnago isn’t exactly known for producing low-cost bikes, and the G5-X certainly isn’t a budget gravel bike. However, it’s also not as eye-wateringly pricey as some of the brand’s road offerings. Colnago offers three SRAM component builds—Red XPLR AXS, Force XPLR AXS, and Rival XPLR AXS—priced at $10,500, $7,500, and $5,500, respectively.

All three builds use the same frame; Colnago doesn’t substitute a lower-grade frame on any build kits. The frame is 2x-compatible, but Colnago ships all builds with 1x13 groupsets.

The Red and Force models come with a wider-flare version of the brand’s in-house one-piece carbon cockpit, while the Rival model uses a traditional bar and stem. The Force and Rival bikes feature Vision TC45 carbon wheels, and the Red model ships with Zipp 303 XPLR SWs.

The only notable component “miss” on the G5-X bikes is the lack of power meters. All three tiers use SRAM cranksets with a 40T ring and 10-46T cassette, but oddly, none are power-equipped. In an age when riders of all disciplines and ability levels are data-hungry, a power meter is practically required equipment—especially at the Red AXS level.

Colnago’s G5-X sizing is numbered (45, 48, 51, 54, and 57), not t-shirt size-style (XS, S, M, L, and XL). But those numbers don’t exactly correspond to traditional metric frame sizing, so triple-check your measurements and Colnago’s geometry charts. Colnago dealers are often very knowledgeable about the brand and its nuances. But when in doubt, get a proper bike fit to be sure you are on the correct size.

Overall, the G5-X is an excellent bicycle. But this isn’t the bike for you if you want the gravel bike with the most tire clearance, every gear-carrying option, or the lowest price. Plenty of other bicycles hit any or all of those points, and they will ride great.

The G5-X rides beautifully and does the things a good gravel bike should do in 2026. It has a few deficiencies, but they probably aren’t deal-breakers for most riders who will want this bike. Riders will seek out the G5-X because the name on the downtube practically guarantees it goes very fast and handles beautifully—price will most likely be a distant consideration. After all, no one buys a Ferrari because it’s a “good deal”; they buy it for the brand’s heritage, prestige, and what it signifies.

The same goes for Colnago. You can buy a gravel bike with more tire clearance than the G5-X. You can also buy a lower-priced gravel bike, or one with a power meter, hidden bag mounts, and the exact tires you want. But none of those other bikes are a Colnago, and that’s what actually matters.

Although Ernesto Colnago sold a majority share of his fabled company to an Abu Dhabi-based investment fund in 2020, Colnago bikes are still special. In an era of look-alike frames, most bikes still don’t match Colnago’s ride quality and refinement. And sure, maybe some of that is mystique or hype. Many artists can paint beautiful pictures you can hang in your home; only a select group of masters’ artwork hangs in the Uffizi Gallery.

#3517. New Pedals, No Problem: How to Install Pedals Without Damaging Your Bike

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Avoid stripped crank threads and costly repairs with this step-by-step pedal swap guide.

There are few part swaps I have seen cause more damage on a bike than an incorrectly installed set of pedals. I’ve seen experienced and beginner cyclists make a pedal installation mistake causing damage to both the pedal and the crank—resulting in anywhere from a little under $100 of damage to over $500. In some cases threads can be cleaned up and parts can be saved, but too many times this small part install can lead to hundreds of dollars in repairs.

Two of the most common ways I have seen this mistake happen are from a pedal going in at an angle into the threads of the crank or the pedals being installed on the wrong side of the cranks. If the system is damaged beyond repair, then parts need to be replaced.

Another way this can go wrong is if the pedals are not fully torqued during the installation to the cranks. When I ran a shop I’ve had customers come in that tried to install pedals at home, but the pedals backed out mid ride. When this happens it can be both a safety issue for the rider and cause damage to the bike. The good news is when done right this is a straightforward install process that can be done at home. The right tools, an easy-to-follow guide, and some attention are all you need to avoid some of the most common pedal installation issues. Below we have your guide on everything you need to know about installing your new pedals.

One thing to note on this method—I always begin the removal process and the final torquing of the pedals with the bike on the ground. This is because the torque that goes into the removal of the pedals and to meet the final torque specifications can cause stress on the frame and seatpost if the bike is clamped in a stand—especially for carbon posts and frames. When removing the pedals, put the bike in the stand after they don’t require force to loosen. Conversely, take the bike out of the stand during install after the pedals are properly screwed into the cranks, but before they get their final needed torque.

With that in mind, then, here are a few jobs worth tackling now to help your plants make it through the colder months – and to make the most of any vegetables still growing in your beds.

One of the first things that caught my eye in Pamela Anderson's video was her tending to her roses, pruners in hand. Still, fall generally isn't the time for a dramatic cutback, as hard pruning can encourage vulnerable new growth or leave plants more exposed to winter weather.

Instead, focus on what your roses really need in October and stick to removing dead, damaged, or diseased stems, along with fallen leaves and other debris around the base of your plants. And, while you may also be tempted to deadhead every spent bloom, remember it's worth exercising a little restraint. Some roses develop attractive rose hips that provide winter interest and a food source for wildlife, so you don't necessarily want to remove every fading flower.

If you're unsure whether your roses need attention, a little tidying is often better than reaching for the pruners with too much enthusiasm. Save any substantial pruning for the appropriate time for your rose variety and local climate.

Pamela Anderson's garden isn't just about beautiful flowers. In the video, she also gathers carrots, tomatoes, and peppers, which is a timely reminder to check what's ready to come inside before colder weather sets in.

If you've been growing vegetables this year, fall is a good opportunity to make the most of your hard work. Carrots can often remain in the ground until you're ready to harvest them, provided the soil conditions are suitable and it isn't at risk of freezing. Tomatoes and peppers, meanwhile, should be picked as they ripen, with any remaining fruit monitored as temperatures fall.

Regular harvesting also gives you a chance to assess which plants have finished producing and which might continue for a little longer. Once your vegetable plants are spent, you can remove them and clear away any diseased material, helping prepare your beds for the next growing season.

Just remember that the best timing depends on your local climate and USDA zone, the varieties you're growing, and the forecast. If a frost is on the way, you may need to harvest tender crops sooner rather than later.

Still, there's something especially satisfying about bringing a basket of homegrown vegetables into the kitchen on a crisp fall morning. It's a lovely reward for all those hours spent watering, weeding, and tending your plants through the summer.

Between the soft light, the whimsical music, and her gentle reminders to pay attention to the world around her, it's a welcome change of pace from the usual rush to get everything ready for winter.

Of course, not every garden needs the same jobs at the same time. Your climate, the plants you're growing, and the weather ahead will all influence what needs doing. #4267. 7 types of anxiety disorders (and how to deal with each one)

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

Your heart starts racing the moment you walk into a crowded room. Even when nothing’s wrong, worry looms in the back of your mind. The thought of a specific fear, like snakes or needles, makes you feel sick. If any of that sounds familiar, anxiety might be part of the picture.

Anxiety doesn't look the same for everyone, which is sometimes why it can be so hard to name. The term "anxiety" covers several disorders, each with its own signs and triggers — though they often overlap, both in how they show up and in what helps.

Anxiety disorders affect roughly 1 in 5 U.S. adults every year. The good news is, they’re typically quite treatable. Let’s break down the seven main types of anxiety listed in the DSM-5 (the manual used for clinical diagnosis), and how to find some relief.

The seven anxiety disorders below share a general thread: they’re rooted in fear or worry that sticks around and gets in the way of daily life. They’re also the most common types, though a few related conditions are worth knowing too (covered further down).

GAD is a persistent, hard-to-control worry that spans multiple areas of your life and shows up more days than not for at least six months. Everyday stress usually has a clear cause and fades once the situation resolves. By contrast, generalized anxiety moves from topic to topic and rarely lets up, because your mind treats any uncertainty as a threat.

This disorder often comes with physical and mental symptoms such as restlessness, muscle tension, fatigue, trouble concentrating, irritability, and disrupted sleep.

What helps: Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is widely used for GAD. It helps you spot and shift the thought patterns that fuel your worries. A regular mindfulness practice can also ease tension by training your attention to stay in the present instead of running through what-ifs.

A panic attack is a sudden rush of intense fear that brings on physical symptoms—racing heart, tight chest, dizziness—and usually peaks within minutes. A single panic attack doesn't mean you have panic disorder — plenty of people experience one and never have another.

Panic disorder is defined by what comes after the attack, such as ongoing dread about when the next one might hit and routine changes made to avoid triggering one.

This fear response tends to feed itself in a cycle. A normal body signal, like a fast heartbeat, gets misread as danger. That triggers more fear, which leads to more physical symptoms.

What helps: CBT can help you learn to read these body signals as harmless rather than dangerous. It’s often paired with interoceptive exposure, where you deliberately bring on mild versions of those same sensations until they stop feeling threatening. During this process, slow breathing can signal safety to your nervous system.

Social anxiety disorder is a strong fear that you’ll be negatively perceived in social or performance situations, often severe enough that dread sets in days before the event itself.

It's much more than being shy. At its core is a belief that you'll do something embarrassing and be judged for it. That belief may lead you to avoid those situations altogether, which can bring short-term relief but reinforces the fear over time.

What helps: Exposure-based CBT has the strongest evidence. It gradually builds your comfort in the situations you've been avoiding by facing them in small, manageable steps. Self-compassion can also make a real difference. Since social anxiety tends to run on harsh self-judgment, practicing kindness toward yourself can help quiet that inner critic.

Not sure if you have social anxiety? Here are signs to look out for.

A specific phobia is a fear of one particular thing—like heights, flying, needles, dogs, or tight spaces—out of proportion to the actual danger. A fear becomes a phobia when it impacts your behavior, whether that’s rerouting a drive to avoid a bridge or skipping a checkup because of needles. Even thinking about the trigger can set off anxiety, so avoiding it becomes your default.

Phobias often start with a scary experience or a fear picked up early in life. Avoidance keeps the fear alive, since your brain never learns that the trigger is safe.

What helps: Exposure therapy is the leading treatment, shown to outperform placebo and other therapies. Pair each step with grounding and breathing techniques to make the process feel more manageable.

Agoraphobia is a fear of being somewhere you couldn't easily escape or get help if panic or embarrassing symptoms hit. This can include public transit, open spaces, crowds, lines, or long stretches away from home alone. Many people with agoraphobia avoid those places altogether.

The DSM-5 classifies it as its own diagnosis. Left untreated, this avoidance can shrink your world, sometimes making it hard to leave the house at all.

What helps: CBT with step-by-step exposure is the first-line treatment, and it helps you return to avoided places gradually. Since agoraphobia and panic often overlap, treatment usually addresses both. To build confidence, try calming techniques at home first, then in triggering places.

Separation anxiety disorder is an excessive fear about being away from people you're close to. Once seen as a childhood condition, it’s now known to affect adults too. It can show up as real distress when a partner travels or constant worry that something bad will happen to a loved one.

Underneath it is a fear of loss: of the person or of your own ability to cope without them. You may find yourself seeking reassurance or checking in repeatedly, but these responses tend to feed the anxiety rather than calm it.

What helps: CBT can help you reduce your need for reassurance and build comfort with time apart. Mindfulness encourages you to pause when an anxious thought arises and let the worry pass. For severe cases, a doctor may suggest therapy alongside medication.

Selective mutism is an inability to speak in certain social settings where talking is expected, like school or work. It's rare, affecting under 2% of people, and it usually shows up in childhood, though it can last into later years.

This isn't stubbornness or a speech problem — it's fear. You may want to speak, but hold back because of it in those environments.

What helps: Behavioral therapy that slowly builds up speaking in those settings, often involving family or school staff, is the main approach. Early intervention tends to lead to better outcomes, so it’s worth seeking professional guidance as soon as symptoms appear.

While the disorders above are what most people mean by "anxiety," a few related conditions are helpful to understand for the full picture.

Two more fall under the DSM-5's anxiety chapter:

Substance or medication-induced anxiety disorder: This anxiety is caused directly by a substance, whether that’s too much caffeine, a prescription drug, or withdrawal from alcohol. It can be overlooked, which is why a doctor will often ask what you're taking before making a diagnosis.

Anxiety disorder due to another medical condition: A physical illness—such as an overactive thyroid or a heart or lung problem—can cause anxiety. A checkup can rule out this possibility early on, and treating the underlying illness often eases the anxiety too.

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) get grouped with anxiety disorders a lot, as they also involve overwhelming fear and distress. But the DSM-5 classifies them separately: OCD centers on unwanted thoughts and behaviors, while PTSD comes after a traumatic event.

Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) and panic disorder differ mainly in how the worry builds and shows up. GAD involves a slow-burning worry about many areas of life that rarely fades. Panic disorder centers on sudden, intense attacks and the fear of having another one. It’s possible to have both, and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) works well for either.

The difference between having social anxiety and being introverted comes down to preference versus fear. Introversion is a preference about how you recharge — social time drains your energy, but it doesn't fill you with dread. Social anxiety, meanwhile, brings a fear of judgment or embarrassment, often strong enough to make you skip things you'd enjoy.

It’s not unusual to have more than one anxiety disorder at the same time, and they can show up alongside other conditions including depression. A doctor can help you sort out which ones you’re dealing with, and treatments like CBT can target several disorders at once.

A specific phobia and agoraphobia differ in what the fear is tied to. A specific phobia is about one clear trigger, such as spiders or flying. Agoraphobia is broader: it's a fear of being somewhere you couldn't easily escape or get help, like a crowd or public transit.

The most common types of anxiety in adults are specific phobias, social anxiety disorder, and generalized anxiety disorder. Altogether, anxiety disorders affect close to 1 in 5 U.S. adults each year and are the most prevalent type of mental health condition.

Anxiety disorders can ease on their own for some people, but many stick around or return without treatment. Fortunately, treatment can meaningfully improve your quality of life. Most people find that therapy, medication, or a combination makes a noticeable difference.

The most well-supported therapy for anxiety disorders is cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), often paired with gradual exposure work. Medication is another option with strong evidence, either used alone or alongside therapy. The right fit depends on your specific disorder and its severity, so it's important to consult a professional.

There are several natural ways to manage anxiety, though they aren’t a replacement for clinical treatment. Regular exercise, quality sleep, and cutting back on caffeine and alcohol can all help alleviate how anxious you feel day to day. Mindfulness practices (like breathing exercises or grounding techniques) are especially well-supported, since they teach you to notice anxious thoughts or sensations without getting swept up in them.

Soggy, matted leaves sitting on a crown can smother it.

Once the last tomatoes and squash come out, an empty vegetable bed has room for a deep layer of leaves. Spread 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) of shredded leaves over the soil and dig or till them in. They’ll keep breaking down through winter, and by spring the bed has more organic matter and microbes, better tilth, and better drainage to show for it.

It can take a lot of trips to haul that many leaves, and Home Depot's True Temper poly wheelbarrow has a roomy 6-cubic-foot (170 L) tub. On smaller beds, you can turn them in by hand with a fork – I like the Truper spading fork, available on Amazon. And don’t worry about oak leaves souring the soil. They’re acidic when fresh, but they break down to roughly neutral.

Want more fireflies and butterflies next summer? Then leave some leaves. Not every corner of the yard has to look raked and finished. Pile some leaves under a shrub or along a back fence and leave them there through winter, and you’ve made shelter for beneficial insects.

Mated bumble bee queens hibernate an inch or two (2.5–5 cm) down in the soil, and a layer of leaves on top gives them extra protection. Firefly larvae use leaf litter for cover as well. So do woolly bears, the fuzzy black-and-rust caterpillars that become Isabella tiger moths. Look closely at an old leaf pile in early spring, and you might spot a swallowtail chrysalis or luna moth cocoon that’s hard to tell from the leaves around it.

Lawns are the exception to all this leaf-saving. Any thick layer needs to come off before winter, and if you miss a spot and find a flattened, soggy mat in early spring, rake it up right away so it doesn’t hold back green-up. The True Temper 24-inch leaf rake from Home Depot has wide, clog-free tines that suit the job.

Keep an eye on the gutter out front as well. Leaves washed into a storm drain usually wind up in the nearest creek or lake, and rotting leaves there release phosphorus that feeds algae blooms.

In the beds, though, you can relax. Whatever falls under shrubs, around trees, and between perennials will slowly rot down and give nutrients back to the soil, just like leaves do in the woods. It’s kinder to the insects tucked in there, too, if you put off cleaning out beds until spring has properly warmed up.

I always remember what my grandpa always said. “That rake ain’t going nowhere, and neither are the leaves – there’ll be another round of them next October, and the October after that.” Each of those piles is free mulch and compost for the taking, so it seems a shame to send them to the curb.
