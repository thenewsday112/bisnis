#972. Are Men Ready for the Return of the Shiny Dress Shirt?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The designer Julian Louie remembers the first shiny dress shirt he ever saw: an iridescent purple number worn by director Todd Haynes during the press tour for his 1998 film Velvet Goldmine (fittingly, a movie about 1970s glam rock). Louie came across that image of Haynes in the 2000s, and something about the shirt stuck with him. That rakish, slightly decadent menswear became central to Aubero, the label he launched in 2022. Earlier this year, Pedro Pascal showed up on the red carpet in a shiny Aubero shirt that rivaled the juicy, luminously green interior of a freshly cut lime.

Pascal isn’t the only movie star embracing a little sheen these days. Riz Ahmed delivered a one-two punch on the recent Digger press tour, during which he sported both a black silk habotai shirt by Lemaire and a silk chartreuse tee under a gray Saint Laurent suit. Last month, the actor-skateboarder Nico Hiraga showed up at the Toronto International Film Festival in a supremely shiny mauve shirt, worn unbuttoned to his sternum. Heated Rivalry’s Connor Storrie, who is a Saint Laurent brand ambassador, has made a habit of wearing the label’s silky shirts. Meanwhile, Resident Evil star Austin Abrams took it a step further and opted for a shiny two-piece suit from Maison Valentino. Hollywood’s leading men, it seems, are looking a whole lot glossier.

Anyone old enough to remember the early 2000s knows this isn’t the first time shiny shirts have had a moment in Hollywood. Think of Brad Pitt’s wardrobe as Rusty Ryan in the Ocean’s franchise, which basically mirrored the actor’s own red-carpet fashion at the time (and sometimes even in the present day). Or the bros on Entourage, whose fondness of shiny “going-out shirts” sometimes seemed eerily interchangeable with the actors who played them. The glossy dress shirt has a documented history of appearing both impossibly cool and slightly ridiculous, so even as other Y2K styles surge through menswear, it’s little surprising that the shiny button-up has come around again. (Proceed with caution, though: A few wrong turns and guys will be back in bedazzled jeans and gelled faux hawks.)

Sophie Jordan, the menswear buying director at luxury retailer Mytheresa, points to the spring 2026 collections as a turning point. “Gucci’s first collection under Demna showcased this shiny evening shirt in a sexy way but still a strong edge of masculinity,” she says, noting that Haider Ackermann’s Tom Ford—always the proprietor of sexy, nightlife-ready clothes—also offered printed silk shirts that same season, which were a hit with Mytheresa’s shoppers. Since then, designers (including Demna) have just kept ’em coming.

Jordan places the shiny shirt within a broader category she calls the “evening shirt,” which encompasses everything from silk and sequins to velvet and sheer fabrics. The appeal isn’t necessarily to dress like, say, the Roxbury Guys of Saturday Night Live fame, but rather to tap into some of menswear’s more sensual currents. “This style of shirt plays into that without being too over the top. Fundamentally, the shape and silhouette is very classic,” she says. “When styled with a pair of tailored pants, it’s very easy to wear and adds an evening feel to the styling without being overdone.” In other words, a little shine can is a relatively low-lift way to make an impression.

“I like the pairing of a very expressive material used on a super straightforward silhouette,” says Louie, the Aubero designer. For some of his label’s earliest designs, Louie deconstructed mid-century silk taffeta cocktail dresses and turned them into board shorts. “It was about taking a material that has always been associated with a more feminine couture sensibility and applying it to a men’s wardrobe in an unexpected way,” he says.

Todd Haynes in 1998, wearing the shirt that inspired Aubero designer Julian Louie.

A look from Aubero's fall 2026 collection.

But, as with most things, the shiny dress shirt never truly went away. It’s a perennial fixture on the runway of labels like Tom Ford and Dolce & Gabbana. Dries Van Noten elevated the garment with prints and patterns into something approaching high art, while the cult Swedish brand Our Legacy continues to turn out the wonderfully weird shirting that has continually wooed menswear’s more adventurous dressers. But what’s changed is how many guys seem willing to give the look a shot. It certainly helps that shiny dress shirts slide into today’s slouchy tailoring as well as the sexier, more retro fare à la Anthony Vaccarello’s final work at Saint Laurent (as seen on Storrie) or the French breakout label Husbands.

For those curious but are perhaps afraid of veering too close to “lounge lizard” territory, there are plenty of halfway steps. Many labels, including Auralee and Tom Ford, offer dress shirts in cotton-silk blends that feature more dull cotton than shiny silk, for a finish that catches the light without going full disco ball. For newbies, “it’s a smart idea to build up to it,” says Jordan. A little texture and a little sheen will go a long way for guys who don’t typically spend their evenings walking red carpets.

Brad Pitt as Rusty Ryan in Ocean's Eleven (2004).

In recent years, the stronghold of “quiet luxury” and tasteful neutrals has been so enduring that it’s easy to forget that clothes are allowed to be a little ridiculous, a bit sexy, and hell, even fun. Perhaps that’s what all these newfangled glossy dress shirts are getting at. Or as Louie put it: “Maybe this means we’ve moved on from a moment of banal clothing.”

#2742. One Wrong Move Could Be Fatal. So Why Are These Climbers Competing at Free Soloing?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Michael Reese at the top of the First Flatiron in Boulder, Colorado (Photo: Courtesy of Michael Reese)

About our reporting: Climbing reporter Holly Chen interviewed three free soloists and one additional speed climber in Boulder, researched fatalities in Accidents in North American Climbing, and pulled from her own experience free soloing the Second Flatiron to answer questions about the world of competitive free soloing.

I stood waiting in front of a health food restaurant in south Boulder, Colorado. The Flatirons rose high above the building, their slabs of red sandstone jutting out of the mountainside like the spines of a prehistoric monster.

On September 3, 32-year-old climber Michael Reese broke the First Flatiron’s fastest known time (FKT) from the dirt at its base to the summit anchor, with no rope. He climbed the Direct Route (5.6,R; 1,000ft) of the East Face in 7 minutes 51 seconds, breaking what he believed would be the last minute barrier of this generation. The previous record was 8 minutes 33 seconds, set by Joe Kennedy in 2022.

Which one is Michael Reese? Everywhere I looked at the restaurant, less than a 10 minute drive from the Chautauqua trailhead, I saw La Sportiva and Scarpa running shoes, Salomon running vests, and folded ultralight running poles sticking out of ultralight backpacks. Any of these people could have just come off the climb.

The ease was the first thing I noticed about Reese. For someone who holds multiple FKTs in the Flatirons and one on Yellow Spur (5.8; 550ft) in Eldorado Canyon, I expected him to be cocky, confident, maybe a little elitist; he was anything but. Reese smiled easily, laughed easily, and spoke candidly, unperturbed by the recorder sitting between us. He was leaving for Yosemite the following day, and we exchanged stories of our experiences climbing in the Valley. When I joked about my personal best up the First Flatiron–somewhere shy of an hour, although I did not officially keep time–he congratulated me genuinely.

“You can do it,” Reese urged. “Get some racing shoes. Do some warmups … You’ll be going faster.”

As climbing has grown, it has increasingly legendized free soloing and going fast—but we’ve rarely seen these two disciplines combined and going without a rope. The Oscar-winning film Free Solo (2017) made the term so mainstream that it recently appeared in a Taylor Swift song, and speed climbing records have made headlines again and again. But these fantastical feats of risk and speed are usually in the hands of highly experienced climbers, such as Alex Honnold, the spectacular pendulum of Dean Potter, or Yosemite Search and Rescue members. Additionally, most of these races happen on cliffs that the average person can’t easily ascend, such as El Capitan.

Reese is a different kind of figure. A Boulder local, he is not sponsored, and not a professional. Reese started climbing at the University of Colorado (CU) Boulder in 2014. The first time he went up the First Flatiron, he said to himself, “If I get off this [alive], I’m never climbing again.” And the First Flatiron is far from inaccessible; with the base a 1.3-mile hike from the trailhead, for many people, the barrier to entry is only a pair of shoes.

The new FKT leaves me with an uneasy mix of admiration and doubt. A person’s risk is not entirely his own when he takes it in a busy place like Chautauqua Park, where hikers, runners, picnickers, and climbers share the same space. So is competitive free soloing on low fifth-class terrain something to celebrate or a toxic addiction to discourage? A fatal fall is always possible, and the barrier to entry is so low. In fact, anyone writing about it, including myself, is working on blurry ground where reporting and the glorification of risk are hard to tell apart. Does this recognition create a feedback loop that incentivizes more to partake in the risky sport?

To understand how competitive free soloing actually operates, I had to go back to the people who have been going fast on this rock for decades before Reese.

The history of speed records in the Flatirons runs through a small, unusual club called The Satan’s Minions Scrambling Club, which Bill Wright started in 2001. Three of its members told me they don’t “race” each other. Instead, they have “personal time trials,” to avoid the ire of the Parks & Open Space regulations, which prohibit races.

The club keeps books. Stefan Griebel, a longtime member, whom a mutual friend described to me as someone who holds “every record under the sun,” has a meticulously kept log book detailing every FKT on a Flatiron dating back to the 1980s. Today, members’ times are verified by Strava entries.

Griebel met Wright in 2003 and joined the club’s annual Flatiron scrambles, which have now run for about 22 years. Each one starts at a trailhead, free solos a Flatiron, and ends back in the parking lot. Buzz Burrell, a trail-running legend then 52, reset the trailhead-to-trailhead mark at 35 minutes and change. Griebel, who was half Burrell’s age at the time, couldn’t keep pace.

“[Buzz] just dropped every single person that day,” Griebel reminisced.

A year later, trail runner Dave Mackey took Burell’s trailhead-to-trailhead FKT down to 33:19. But being a trail runner, Griebel knew he would have the upper hand on rock. In 2010, Griebel closed the gap, and took the record to 33:05. The record changed hands to Mathias Messner in 2015 and Kyle Richardson in 2019.

In the spring of 2020, Reese returned to Boulder from a biotech master’s program at California Polytechnic State University. The COVID pandemic had canceled the triathlon nationals he’d spent months training for, and he had a season of fitness and nothing to spend it on. He decided to see how fast he could go on the Flatirons. It began as a text-message rivalry with Joe Mitchem, a friend from CU’s cross-country team, over Freeway (5.0; 600ft) on the Second Flatiron. Reese didn’t have Strava and didn’t know the records existed until the two of them realized they’d gone under the standing Freeway mark of about 6:20. That fall, he entered the club’s time trials and finished second.

Griebel remembers meeting Reese on the First Flatiron. The two soloed to the summit and trotted down together. “He can run uphill like nobody I’ve ever seen,” Griebel said.

The base-to-summit FKT, the one Reese prefers, was still Griebel’s in 2020, at 9:25. Reese told me the time stunned him. That summer, Reese broke it by seconds. Then Joe Kennedy, a California athlete, broke that in 2022 to 8:33. That sent Reese into harder training than he’d done in 2020 or 2021, and this September brought the record down to 7:51.

The club looks different today. It is still heavily male dominated; Griebel estimates eight men for every one woman participant. And membership is less casual. “We have some entrance requirements now,” Griebel said. “We don’t want to invite people into the club that aren’t okay with the risk and that are also very solid, obviously, on the rock.”

What the club shows is a culture more wary than toxic. It vets its members, keeps careful books, and wants to stay on good terms with the land managers. It also shows a feedback loop; each record pulled in the next challenger. Reese told me every broken record “ingrains it more as something to go for.” He felt inspired when he learned of Griebel’s record. Camaraderie and friendly competition keep people incentivized and returning. But this wariness only goes so far; while the Satan’s Minions can vet their members, they can’t actually stop others from trying competitive free soloing, too.

Reese said he has now climbed the First Flatiron 800 times. It taught him that the risk “is never zero,” but he believes it to be smaller than most people imagine.

Even inside the scene, no one agrees on what speed does to the odds of surviving a free solo. Griebel says that when he’s pushing hard, heart pounding and moving upward, he’s “less likely to make a mistake.” Kate Kelleghan, who holds the women’s Naked Edge (5.11b; 460ft) record with Laura Pineau, says free soloing only makes up about one percent of her climbing. “This is super high risk and low reward,” she told me. “I’ve always felt way safer and stronger on a rope.” John Alcorn, who holds the Diamond speed record, free soloing Casual Route (5.10a; 900ft), says going fast piles more risk onto something already dangerous. “You get one shot,” he told me, “and if you blow it, you’re dead.”

In his trip report, Alcorn wrote, “I accept the position that climbing without a rope is a pretty dumb thing to do, and I believe that it shouldn’t be glorified, normalized, or encouraged in any way.” When a friend’s wife called it “incredibly stupid” over dinner, he agreed.

Alcorn said the FKTs are “extremely competitive. We’re essentially all competing with each other. But all these guys are my friends.” He admitted that if someone took his Diamond time by a minute or two, “Oh, I’d be pissed.” Then he caught himself: “That’s just pure ego … one of the worst reasons to climb without a rope.”

According to Accidents in North American Climbing, there have been six fatalities in the Flatirons since 2016. All of them involved free soloists, although none of them involved speed climbers. That number puts the danger in soloing, not the speed.

Low fifth-class terrain pulls in runners, a demographic far larger than climbing. “The accessible, low-angle slabs of the Flatirons frequently lure inexperienced or ill-prepared scramblers,” wrote the ANAC editors in 2017.

Reese has noticed more crossover of hybrid athletes; people coming from a very high level running background. Alcorn saw the same shift from the trail running side: As running records multiply, people are “diversifying what counts as a goal,” and he finds it hard to say where running and scrambling stop and climbing begins. Griebel says the overlap between strong runners and people comfortable soloing is thin, and that overlap is the only reason he has ever held a record.

This summer Reese found a 20-year-old about 100 feet up at dusk, shaking, with no headlamp, his feet slipping. Reese guided him down a gully. By the time they reached the ground, it was too dark to see his own outstretched hand.

I have felt that same dread myself. In the summer of 2025, I took out-of-town friends up on a free solo of the second Flatiron. Near the top, three college-aged men were climbing without a rope, one in climbing shoes and two in running shoes. The leader, about fifteen feet above me, was shaking badly. I waved my friends to the left, afraid that if he fell, we would be in his path.

But inexperienced climbers aren’t always the ones to watch. In 2020, a man fell to his death on Freeway, even after he reportedly had climbed it “hundreds of times.”

The scrambling club’s precautions don’t reach the people who get in trouble because those people aren’t in the club. And just because one is solid on rock doesn’t mean they’ll never fall. Its members can be practiced and clear-eyed about the risk, but the rock belongs to everyone. “They don’t do it because they see somebody going fast,” said Griebel, referring to inexperienced scramblers. “They just see somebody soloing.”

The stereotype is that this is a content game. Climbers like Lincoln Knowles, who posts his free solos to a large and young following, make the impression that free soloing is about social media fame and monetization.

The record-holders I talked to disagree. Reese began timing himself in 2020 without Strava, unaware the records existed. “If there was no social media, no recognition of any kind, would you still do it?” I asked Reese. The answer was an unequivocal yes. “I’m more interested in doing the actual thing than the broadcasting of what I’ve done,” Reese said.

Alcorn never posted his Diamond record, a choice he called intentional. “I just don’t even want ego in the loop,” he said. Griebel’s Instagram account is private except to close friends. Reese’s posts reads more like a logbook than a boast.

Still, the pull of recognition isn’t zero. Reese allows that recognition probably motivates him now that it exists, but he still climbs for himself and not a like button. Alcorn admits that without a culture of records he probably wouldn’t have tried the Diamond record. Kelleghan sits in the middle. She has a following and has been paid, including for a watch ad, but says she has never climbed something just to post it.

Whatever these climbers are after, audiences both on and offline take their own lessons from the best in the sport.

On the Flatirons, free soloing and speed have become synonymous; on the Naked Edge, a short drive south, they still sit apart, for now. Some Boulder climbers are free soloing the Naked Edge already, Kelleghan said, and she knows at least one who has soloed it quickly, though not for a record. This climber, who Kelleghan refuses to name, believes the speed record will eventually belong to a free soloist. Kelleghan disagrees. A free soloist can’t push as hard, she argued, because “if you’re simul climbing, if you fall, it would be unpleasant, but you wouldn’t die.”

The people I spoke with showed that records have a way of recruiting. If someone does set a free solo speed record on the Naked Edge, will it stay an outlier, or become the standard, the way that free solo ascents became the standard on the Flatirons? Kelleghan is betting against it.

So for most people who hold these records, it isn’t a content game. But a motive doesn’t have to be public or monetized to be contagious. Each time a record falls, it tells the next climber what is possible. The risk the last person accepted becomes the new baseline.

For all the attention these records draw, free soloing mostly regulates itself. Most people look at the First Flatiron and never think of climbing it, let alone without a rope. Alcorn points to an innate fear of falling “baked into us” and doesn’t expect the number of people doing this to explode just because a few records have been broken.

Some people chase records, but does that make this a toxic culture? From what I saw, it doesn’t. The record-holders I met were generous rivals, wary of their own egos, more interested in doing the thing than in being seen doing it. But “not toxic” isn’t the same as “not influential.” We are not solitary creatures, we have family and friends who love us, so a risk taken is never entirely one’s own.

Reese was as easy at the end of our conversation as at the start. By the end of our meal, I had decided we were friends, so as we said goodbye, an uneasy feeling settled in my stomach.

Walking to my car, I looked up at the Flatirons. A friend of mine, Bailee Mulholland, still holds the female trailhead-to-trailhead speed record on the First. She belonged to the same scrambling club Reese does now. In 2023, she died free soloing a 5.4 route on Ypsilon Mountain (13,520ft) a few hours north. I remembered Mulholland’s unwavering bubbly personality; she smiled easily and laughed easily, too.

I don’t think the scene she came from is toxic. But I know what losing her did to the people who loved her. The risk may belong to the one who takes it. The grief never does.

Discover the best looks worth saying “I do” to.

New York Bridal Fashion Week was a whirlwind of lace, tulle, and breathtaking craftsmanship, with established names unveiling their latest creations alongside emerging designers making their debuts. To bring you the inside scoop, we spent the week crisscrossing Manhattan, attending intimate previews and presentations to get a closer look at the collections and uncover the biggest bridal trends worth having on your radar.

For some expert insight into what’s new and noteworthy in bridal fashion, we also turned to Kyha Scott, founder and creative director of bridal label Kyha, beloved for its elegant, minimalist designs, and Jaclyn Whyte, the Toronto-based designer behind her namesake bridal label. When asked which silhouettes are gaining momentum among designers, Scott pointed to a resurgence of bold, maximalist styles. “Quiet luxury has become less quiet as brides look toward opulent silhouettes for statement-making looks. We are seeing ball gowns and A-line styles capture the imagination of both brands and customers,” she shares. Whyte, meanwhile, has noticed a growing desire for individuality and exclusivity among brides. “We have always offered bespoke services at our Toronto atelier, but lately we’ve noticed a spike in brides wanting exclusive, one-of-a-kind gowns,” she shares. “Real exclusivity matters so much more to today’s bride than any passing trend.”

Whether you’re a bride-to-be, a bridal stylist, or simply someone who appreciates a spectacular wedding dress, there’s no shortage of inspiration to swoon over. Certain design details recurred time and again, from the return of dramatic Basque waistlines to intricate, all-over lace that covered gowns from neck to hem. Accessories were just as worthy of attention, with glamorous, flapper-inspired headpieces offering a chic alternative to traditional veils, while unique bridal bags and shoes made for captivating finishing touches.

Ahead, we’re breaking down the standout trends from the latest collections, complete with dreamy imagery and expert insight into the textures and materials designers are embracing, plus a closer look at what brides are actually choosing for their big day.

Designers are all about grandeur this season, with Bridal Fashion Week showcasing voluminous silhouettes, from understated ball gowns with clean-lined bodices and sweeping skirts to extravagant, Rococo-inspired creations featuring lavish layers of silk and lace.

“Brides are becoming more willing to take risks,” says designer Kyha Scott, pointing to a resurgence of maximalism in bridal fashion. “There’s a definitive trend toward our customers looking for something unique and pieces that feel like a true reflection of themselves.” Her latest collection, Josephine, reflects this shift with statement-making gowns ranging from understated elegance to unapologetic extravagance.

The hem is an unexpected place for brides to show personality, and designers are meeting them with some exciting ideas. Early-aughts trends like the bubble skirt and high-low hem have come back to life in the bridal world too. Leg slits got a flashy upgrade with can’t-look-away flourishes at Mia Atelier; meanwhile, the tiers got texture at House of Gilles and Marmar Halim. Such details are fit for brides who want to look stylish from head to toe—literally.

Who said bridal dresses have to be white? As more brides (especially Gen Z brides) choose to break from tradition on their big day, that sometimes means embracing more color. Blush pink is a welcome entry point—it’s still soft and romantic—as seen at brands like Marrow and Monique Lhuillier. Vintage lovers can even find archival options at shops like Atlanta’s Vionnette and Brooklyn-based Cha Cha Linda. Other rising shades include butter yellow (see: Vivienne Westwood), light blue, and soft mint green (like Nadia Manjarrez). But if your style is more maximalist, you could choose metallic bespoke styles from Kyha, or even a polka-dotted gown from Galia Lahav.

Lace has always held a special place in bridal fashion, but this season, the romantic fabric was everywhere, with intricate designs extending from neckline to hemline. Among the standouts was Rita Vinieris’s latest collection, The Feeling, which featured a breathtaking gown with visible corsetry, delicate lace overlay, and coordinating long sleeves.

Bridal designer Jaclyn Whyte has noticed a similar fascination with lace among her clients, and for her, the material is often where the creative process begins. “Many times we design from the fabric first. For us, it's not just a material. It’s the starting point for how a dress is structured,” she shares. Her latest collection, The Exhibition, reflects this approach, with intricate detailing and an emphasis on texture. “Lace is definitely a favorite for us. We try to reinvent our lace gowns each year by layering all types of lace to create something totally original. I've noticed other designers embracing and experimenting more with lace these past few seasons.”

In contrast to the maximalism seen throughout Bridal Fashion Week, designers also embraced the understated elegance of ’90s minimalism. For brides drawn to a more effortless aesthetic, sleek column gowns and slinky slip dresses à la Carolyn Bessette Kennedy brought clean lines and fluid silhouettes, with all the more room to experiment with bold accessories. Australian bridal label Jane Hill revisited its own archives for its latest collection, Heirloom, drawing inspiration from the classic silhouettes and luxe fabrics of its founder’s ’90s designs. The 14-gown collection reintroduces mikado and Royale satin, offering a contemporary interpretation of the styles that first defined the house.

A chic handbag is an effortless way to elevate a bridal look, and lately, we’ve noticed a shift toward daintier silhouettes, from delicate drawstring pouches to petite bucket bags adorned with intricate beading, silky fringe, tassels, and other romantic embellishments. For brides looking to keep their phone and lip gloss close at hand for quick touch-ups, there are several charming options on the market that bring a little personality to their wedding-day ensemble. We’re especially fond of Anya Hindmarch’s clutch, featuring an intricately embroidered horseshoe as a token of good luck, and Anna Sui’s floral-adorned bag, complete with a delicate, net-like fringe design.

Brides can opt for more than just the traditional veil for their wedding. Bows, headbands, pearls, flowers, and feathers—lots of feathers—appeared in presentations this season. At Jennifer Behr, there were ostrich plumes and floral hairpins, while at Honor, unexpected headbands were paired with gothic gowns. Hats ranged from 1920s-esque beaded caps—a dressed-up version of this summer’s crochet cap—to structured pillboxes. As for veils, fitted, scarf-like styles are having a moment.

The engagement ring isn’t the only thing worth noticing on the wedding day. Bridal accessories are taking a more extravagant turn, with an abundance of jewels and a move away from strictly minimalist styling. Picture diamond tennis necklaces layered with dramatic drop earrings, or richly colored gemstones that bring an unexpected dimension to an otherwise traditional bridal look. “A lot of our family hangouts center around work hikes on the AT,” says Peterson. Homer and Therese have logged around 7,000 and 11,000 work-hours, respectively. Their children and grandchildren routinely pitch in.

For her and her brother, Bennett, the bond between trail and family was cemented early on. It stems, in large part, from a 2002 thru-hike they took with their parents. “A lot of people say thru-hiking the AT is transformative, and that’s exactly what it was for us,” says Bennett, who was 8 when the Witchers hit the trail. There was a distinct before and after that left a deep impression on their mutual and individual lives.

Homer and Therese discovered the Appalachian Trail early in their courtship. They shared a love of hiking and nature, but were oblivious to the iconic route. Then they encountered a sign on a walk in Shenandoah National Park in the early 80s.

“We were awestruck,” says Therese. The couple had walked countless miles on the AT without knowing it existed. The idea of a trail that was so long and had so much history seized their imaginations.

The two returned to their Roanoke-area home and dove into research. Books like Ronald M. Fisher’s The Appalachian Trail fanned their interest to an obsession. The reality of a 2,200-mile path dotted with hundreds of backcountry shelters and maintained by groups of regional volunteers felt radical. The Witchers wanted to experience that camaraderie firsthand. The desire led them to the RATC, where they started attending regular work hikes around 1983.

“The volunteer structure that supported the AT seemed so different from what you’d expect for a recreational asset of that size,” says Homer, who then worked as an administrator for a regional hospital group. He loved the way hikers, naturalists, and outdoorsmen banded together to set the stage for trekkers to have the best trail experiences possible. Pitching in to clear fallen trees, build stairs, and paint shelters helped Homer and Therese forge friendships. It also helped Homer balance the demands of an extremely stressful job. Two of his children from a prior marriage often tagged along, which created a family bond around the AT.

“We went from zero to all in pretty much immediately,” says Therese. “It felt like we’d found both a calling and a home away from home.”

When the Witchers weren’t working on the AT, they walked it. Section hikes became a staple of their weekends and vacation time. The couple also started driving hiker shuttles. By the time Taylor and Bennett were born, Homer and Therese had been working on and hiking the AT for a decade.

“We camped with them as babies and would often bring them on work hikes in carrier packs,” says Therese. By the close of 1999, both kids had tagged along for a few hundred work-hours and participated in a sizable chunk of the Witcher’s ongoing 1,400-mile AT section hike. Therese floated the notion of a family thru-hike after Taylor’s 8th birthday.

“We’d completed some pretty long and tough sections at that point, and both kids were extremely at home in the woods,” says Therese. Taylor could carry a 10-pound pack on her own and tackle double-digit days with relative ease. That made her feel confident the family could spend the next few years preparing and that Bennett, then 5, could do the same by the time he turned 8. More importantly, the children seemed to love the family adventures—even when the weather turned nasty.

“We always had so much fun, so we thought it would be a dream come true to hike the full AT together,” says Homer. Spending extended periods of family time on the trail felt so natural, it was easy to forget that most moms and dads considered the activity mildly insane.

The siblings responded with glee when their parents proposed a 2002 thru-hike. Homer and Therese went to lengths to explain what the trip would entail and ensure the children were fully onboard. They wanted the kids to understand they’d miss six months of school and be away from their friends. While starting was a commitment, they said, if anyone stopped having fun, they could quit and go home at any time.

“It was very much a conversation,” says Taylor. “Mom and Dad were adamant about making sure we understood we had a sense of agency and that our opinions mattered.”

For her and Bennett, the pros outweighed the cons. And the countdown began.

Carving out time for the trip demanded considerable maneuvers. Therese convinced her husband, then 60, to take early retirement. She, meanwhile, took a leave of absence from her job as a medical technologist.

“It required some convincing, but my wife insisted that the stress was going to kill me sooner than later and that we could get along just fine without me working,” says Homer. Therese framed the trek as the opening chapter to a second act centered around family and the AT. Homer imagined a life sans office drama, budget gripes, and equipment shortages and said, “Count me in.”

Then there were the children. Therese talked to principals and arranged for standardized tests to be taken in advance. She also filed a notice of intent to homeschool with the school system and submitted curriculum plans so Bennett and Taylor—then 8 and 11—could learn on the trail. Last came strategies for what would happen if one or both of the children decided to call it quits.

“By then, Homer and I were set on walking the whole thing,” says Therese. Her AT-loving step-kids were both in their twenties and offered to serve as temporary guardians if one or both of the children chose to quit. “We felt pretty confident that wouldn’t be necessary,” Therese says. “But we wanted to have a definite plan, just in case.”

Bennet has zero recollection of the drive to Springer Mountain that March. But the rocky, 729-foot plunge of Georgia’s Amicalola Falls stands out in his memory like a Fourth of July grand finale.

“To my 8-year-old self, that waterfall was monumental,” says Bennett. “I was so excited and in such a state of awe. I remember being flooded with this overwhelming sense that we were on a grand adventure.”

Still, it took the kids a week or two to adjust to what six months on-trail really meant. “There was this moment where it was like, ‘Oh, this is just what my life is now,’” says Taylor.

The family rose early and hiked a slow and steady 12 to 13 miles each day. The line between them and nature thinned to a sensory wash of birdsong, windblown leaves, rushing water, and silence. Cottonball clouds drifted through clear skies. Thunderstorms raged. Groves of fairytale rhododendron and dwarf oaks gave way to views that hilled into unfathomable distances. They set up camp, cooked dinner, pondered dancing flames or bright stars. Bedtime stories felt alive.

“One of the things I remember most was how Taylor and Therese developed a habit of singing together as we walked,” says Homer. “It was soft and magical, and, after a while, seemed almost like part of the forest. It filled my heart with so much joy—and that joy is what I feel when I think back on our hike.”

Another prominent feature of their walk was what came to be known as the “Witcher Wagon.” A full-family thru-hike was virtually unheard of at that time. Curious hikers would spot the quartet and fall in to ask questions. They’d often stick around.

“We shared miles with lots of different hikers along the way,” says Therese. When they stopped at their home in Daleville, Virginia, for instance, the troop was 16 strong. Part of that was Homer’s easy and avuncular sociability. Younger hikers—many of them lonely and missing home—seemed drawn to the sense of familial safety. But the kids were also a factor.

“They made so many friends it was wild,” says Homer. One trekker with painful shin splints jumped on the slow-moving wagon around Erwin, Tennessee, and continued for more than 500 miles. He’d babysit and parent the children’s imaginary twins, or play trail-inspired games of “I Spy.” Marriage was impending, however, and he left the trail at Shenandoah National Park. Tearful goodbyes ensued. “By then he was like a part of our family,” says Homer. They traded addresses and went on to exchange letters and Christmas cards for years.

“I look back on those friendships as an adult and it seems so incredible,” says Taylor. Nowadays, news and social media feeds can inspire distrust of everyone and everything. “But those experiences helped me feel safe and excited about meeting new people and getting to know who they are,” she says.

The parade of fellowship, adventure, and sights helped the miles go down easy. Ditto for a parental strategy of including the kids in decisions. They’d study maps, consider premade plans, and discuss potential tweaks as a family. Taylor and Bennett began to look ahead for trail towns like Damascus and advocate for extra miles here or there to create a near-o or zero day.

“We made a rule that we had to earn our days off in advance, not after the fact,” says Therese. The kids respected the imperative and sometimes traded big 18 and 20-mile days for opportunities to kick back in a nifty spot like Nantahala Outdoor Center.

“It was wonderful to see the kids take ownership of their experience like that,” says Therese. “We’d told them time and again to ‘hike your own hike,’ and they took that concept and ran with it.”

While good times dominated, there were some crucible moments. Therese’s came in Tennessee’s Bald Mountains. The family was making the long climb from Flint Gap to Lick Rock. It was cold and rainy. Her legs ached. She missed home and job worries stampeded through her brain like a crazed rhinoceros.

“Then I hear myself panicking, ‘What on earth was I thinking that we could do this?’” says Therese. The kids intervened immediately. “They said, ‘Mom, we’ve got this, we’ll do it together, one step at a time,’” Therese continues. A calm came over her; the bleak day looked suddenly beautiful. Her doubts vanished and did not come back.

Bennett’s moment came after a break near the end of Shenandoah National Park. The family took a zero to check in with friends and roadtrip to an amusement park. When it was time to get back on trail, he refused to leave the van.

“I realized how badly I missed my friends and that they were going on with their lives without me,” Bennett says. A Witcher Wagon member, Big Red, intervened. He palmed Bennett’s shoulder, smiled and said, “‘Come on, Rockslide, I’ll carry your pack.’ And just like that it was over. I heard my trail name and knew I wasn’t ready to quit.”

Both Bennett and Taylor say they had trouble readjusting to daily life after the thru-hike. Taylor entered a new middle school that was five or six times bigger than her elementary school. Classes had started and cliques formed. She felt distant from the other kids, uninterested in the TV shows, music, and he-said-she-said gossip that engrossed them. Even longtime friendships seemed off.

“I’d spent so much time around adults and experienced so much responsibility that I’d forgotten how to be a normal kid,” she says. “I didn’t know what American Idol was or the songs that were trending, so I felt really out of the loop and out of place.”

Bennett experienced similar difficulties. “I was fine with my old friends, because we shared interests and loved the outdoors,” he says. But making new pals was tough.

Luckily, the issues didn’t last long. Both children adjusted and came to see their amplified maturity as a boon. “I realized that being popular wasn’t something I wanted to spend my time and energy chasing,” says Taylor. She learned to follow her own inner voice, invest in personal interests, and seek out like-minded peers. Deeper relationships with her parents and brother, meanwhile, helped smooth transitions into teen years and early adulthood.

“We’d become extremely close and, as time passed, I realized how valuable and rare those relationships were among family members,” Taylor says. Spending so much time together on the trail taught the Witchers to share space and feel comfortable in silence. Helping one another persevere through an onslaught of mutual hardships opened the door to deeper honesty, trust, and communication.

“I can tell my brother and parents anything and know that I’ll have their support no matter what,” says Taylor. And Bennett feels the same. The family, they say, won that respect on the AT.

The positive impacts were not lost on Homer and Therese. They delight in anecdotes like a two-entry Christmas list filed by Bennett around age 11. “I asked him, ‘Isn’t there anything that you want?’” says Homer. “And he looks up at me and says, very matter of fact, ‘There are plenty of things that I want, but not much that I truly need. The trail taught me that.’”

Therese recalls Homer consoling Taylor after a high school breakup. He assured her this too would pass, that she’d make it to the other side and feel better. Taylor snapped out of the funk and replied, “I thru-hiked the AT, so I know I can handle this. But I’m going to pity myself for a few minutes before I do.”

The trek also reframed perceptions around trail work.

“I felt so much gratitude for the people and organizations that made it possible for us to have our once-in-a-lifetime experience,” says Homer. “I take deep pride that, in my own small way, I have and continue to play a role in creating that opportunity for others.”

But perhaps the biggest takeaway for Taylor and Bennett was a lifelong craving for and openness to adventure. Bennett has spent weeks at a time hiking on long trails in the Western U.S. and Swiss Alps. He’s logged countless miles on the AT and goes on frequent overnight paddling trips with Taylor. She walked a 1,000-mile stint after graduating college and had each of her two kids on-trail within months of birth. Plans for a summer 2027 hike of South Carolina’s Foothills Trail with her 3- and 6-year-olds are currently in development.

“For me, the AT has been such an incredible and generous teacher, and I want my children to have access to those lessons,” says Taylor. The trail tested her, as it tests all thru-hikers. But it helped her appreciate the rejuvenative beauty of nature, discover the value of family, and find out who she really was.

LOS ANGELES — In the early 1990s, Paul Pfeiffer began producing a series of striking interventions in found video footage, often featuring celebrated sports figures, to highlight the effects of media and fame on not just individuals but also the larger body politic. Using innovative post-production techniques, he removed key elements from scenes of professional boxers and basketball players, isolating them in various arenas, teasing out the uncanny undercurrents in such late modern spectacles.

Pfeiffer’s work further heightened the stakes of these scenes of pop culture by invoking art historical figures from classical sources, including caryatids from his eponymous 2003–4 series, and modern painting, as seen in his 1999 digital video installation “Fragment of a Crucifixion (after Francis Bacon).” He has also developed a wide-ranging practice of drawing and sculpture, as seen in his series of sculptural maquettes and imagined plans for a million-seat stadium, all named some version of Vitruvian Figure, and woodcarvings of pop singer Justin Bieber in the style of Jesus on the Cross — “Justin Bieber Legs Crucified (Seville)” from 2026 — which feature in his current exhibition at Galerie Perrotin in Los Angeles.

#3395. Dave Obelkevich, 83, Will Run His 50th NYC Marathon on Sunday. He Shares the Tips You Need for Your Best Race.

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The NYC Marathon’s longest-streak runner shares hard-earned course tips for all five boroughs.

Dave Obelkevich’s first experience with the New York City Marathon was in 1973, when it had fewer than 400 entrants and took place entirely in Central Park. He was on the west side of Manhattan near 100th Street when a pack of runners in bibs flew by. He asked a few onlookers what was going on. “It’s the marathon,” they said.

A recreational runner himself, he was both impressed and intimidated by the lead runners’ speed. He knew he couldn’t keep up with them for 26.2 miles, but he figured he could probably finish one of the four loops of the park at a respectable pace. So, on their final lap, he jumped in and finished in 43 minutes. After that, he immediately signed up for the following year.

Obelkevich admits that it took a couple of years for his training to gel. In the months leading up to the 1974 race, he did just one six-mile training run a week. “I took six days off,” he says, laughing. He still managed to finish in 4:20:27, but the experience itself was “tough,” he says.

The now 83-year-old registered again in 1975, but didn’t finish the race. He’d started strong but suffered a series of dizzy spells. With five miles to go, he’d stopped to take a break when the late Fred Lebow, race director and cofounder of the New York City Marathon, drove by. Discouraged by the time he’d lost, Obelkevich asked Lebow for a ride to the finish line. “He should have said, ‘Hell no, you finish by walking!’ But instead, he said, ‘Yeah, get in the back.’ So it’s his fault I didn’t finish,” Obelkevich says, jokingly.

In 1976, the marathon course changed to include all five boroughs of New York City. “It ran through much of the city I’d never seen,” Obelkevich says. He grew up in a suburb of Binghamton, New York, and moved to Manhattan in 1961 to attend Columbia University, but he’d always lived on the Upper West Side. “I don’t think I’d ever been to Staten Island. It was an eye-opener,” he says.

By the time he reached the start line of that first five-borough marathon, Obelkevich was, thankfully, running more than six miles a week. He’d joined the Millrose Athletic Association, where he’d picked up some solid training habits and gained a whole new social circle.

He’d discovered that, in a group of runners who shared similar goals and challenges, he was more extroverted than usual. “I opened my mouth and spoke to a complete stranger. It’s not that hard to do. It’s just a matter of getting started. So that changed my whole personality,” he says. That year, he finished the marathon in 3:22:44.

From that point on, Obelkevich has never missed a New York City Marathon, making him the runner with the longest streak on record: 49 races. This November, he’ll share a 50th anniversary with the course as it’s known today.

Over the past five decades, both Obelkevich and the marathon have evolved. The race, which included just over 2,000 participants in 1976, now hosts nearly 60,000 runners from all over the world. “When I was running the first few years, you look around, and everyone is from the Northeast. Now a quarter of the people are from another country,” he says. The whole experience feels more inclusive and interesting, he adds.

Obelkevich’s approach to the marathon has changed, too. Several years ago, he dealt with a life-threatening aneurysm in his left leg. He has since recovered, but he’s never felt quite the same; he gets winded after a couple of minutes of running. So now he walks the course with Athletes with Disabilities (AWD), and his daughter, whom he’s raced with before, will be his guide.

He trains by doing physical therapy exercises and walking almost every day, with his longest walk this training cycle totaling 15 miles. “People say you should do several 20s, but I didn’t have time for them. If you’re going three miles an hour, it takes a long time,” he says.

He also gives himself permission to skip a day when the weather is bad or something hurts. “One of my techniques of avoiding injury is, if I have a pain somewhere, I’ll try for five minutes. If it hurts, I walk home. If it hurts the next day, I’ll stay home,” he says.

In the late 1970s, Obelkevich’s goal was to break three hours, which he did in 1979 with a time of 2:50:18. This year, his goal is twofold: to finish the race—he estimates it will take him a little over eight hours—and have fun. “That’s more important than anything else for me,” he says.

If you’re running the marathon this year, look for Obelkevich in Staten Island. He’ll be repping New York Road Runners as part of the 2026 Team Inspire, starting at 8:22 a.m. Until the gun goes off, he makes a point to chat with other runners. In fact, it’s one of his tips for passing the time and growing your network.

“Don’t be shy about introducing yourself. Everyone has different stories,” he says. “It’s a great way to meet people and pass the time. Have some business cards with you. So, if you want to keep in touch with someone, you can say, ‘Here’s my card.’”

Here are a few more of Obelkevich’s tips, insights, and observations, based on 50 years of running the New York City Marathon.

There’s no shortage of organized prerace shakeout runs in the city, and you can always loosen up with a couple of easy miles on your own. But be careful running on the city’s streets and sidewalks, which can be uneven and full of unexpected obstacles, Obelkevich says.

He recounts one year when, the day before the race, he tripped over a misplaced barricade near the finish line and fell. Luckily, he was fine, but it could have been catastrophic.

The early morning ferry ride to Staten Island is one of those experiences that’s unique to the New York City Marathon. (How many other races start with a boat ride?) So, if that’s your assigned mode of transportation, take it all in, from the views of the New York City skyline and the Statue of Liberty to the energy of your fellow passengers.

Before Dave began running with AWD, which provides a bus to the start, he’d typically take the ferry and use the opportunity to chat up runners from all over the world. “That was always a lot of fun,” he says.

Rather than draining your phone’s battery with nervous scrolling, take along some reading material you can toss once it’s time to line up. “It helps to bring, say, The New York Times. It’s something you can read, and then you can leave it there,” Obelkevich says. (One time he had the idea to write a letter while he waited, but he couldn’t find a mailbox. So, maybe skip the postcards.)

Lots of runners stay warm with a “throwaway layer,” sweatpants, sweatshirts, ponchos, hats, and gloves they plan to discard right before their wave. (Volunteers then collect the pieces to donate or recycle.) But be careful about tossing all your extra clothes, Obelkevich says.

“I suggest that people, if they’re going to have a jacket or something, once they warm up, don’t throw it away because it’s going to take a while after you finish to get to your clothes. You have to walk a distance,” he says. There’s about a mile-long walk from the finish line to the street, and it’s even longer if you need to walk to the subway or your hotel. If you’re sweaty, depleted, and cold, that walk can be especially miserable.

If you tend to get chilly after a race, consider keeping something lightweight that you can ball up and shove in a fanny pack or tie around your waist or shoulders.

On a difficulty scale of one to 10, Obelkevich, who has run marathons and ultramarathons all over the world, rates the New York City Marathon at six, but with a caveat.

“The crowd is so supportive in New York. Often you can hear two bands playing at once—one ahead and one behind you. So, all that makes the pain go away faster,” he says. If it weren’t for the two million or so cheering spectators, he’d rank the difficulty level a lot higher.

The only downside to all that positive energy is that it’s easy to get carried away and start out too fast. Obelkevich has always used the “talk test” to keep his effort in check. “If you can’t talk, you’re going too fast,” he says.

Out of the five bridges you’ll cross, the Queensboro Bridge is the most grueling, according to Obelkevich. It begins around mile 15, when some runners may start feeling fatigued. The bridge itself is 3,725 feet long, but because it’s eerily quiet (there are no spectators on the bridge) it can feel like it lasts for miles. If you get energy from the crowd, he says, this part of the course can be really tough.

But rest assured: On the Manhattan side of the bridge, there are thousands of cheering spectators lining First Avenue. That section of the race is often referred to as “the wall of sound,” and once you come off the bridge, you’ll know why.

Obelkevich has noticed that a lot of runners tend to struggle around mile 20 in the Bronx. That said, he’s never had an experience with hitting the wall.

“That’s partly because I’m planning to finish with some gas in the tank,” he says. He recently read an article about a celebrity runner who was planning to finish the marathon with “no gas in the tank,” a goal he finds questionable.

“Once you finish, you still have to walk a bit. You can’t be exhausted at the finish line and fall down,” he says. Knowing that he has to preserve a little energy to get himself out of the park and home keeps him from pushing too hard during the race.

No matter how much pain you’re in, turning into Central Park at 90th Street, at mile 24, feels like a homecoming. The park is a recognizable landmark, the crowds are electric, and you know you’re going to finish.

“Central Park is something that I’ve been in almost every day, and people know it backward and forward and upside down, so it’s very reassuring to come into Central Park,” Obelkevich says. “I would say that, except for the finish line, which is most impressive, that’s what boosts my spirits the most—coming into Central Park.”

Once you’ve crossed the finish line, collected your medal, and found your way out of the park, your journey home or back to your hotel begins. Even a technically walkable distance may feel insurmountable, so plan to take public transportation.

“Don’t expect to find a cab,” Obelkevich warns. Even if you can get an Uber, road closures make traffic impossible to navigate. “One nice thing about mass transit is that you have to either walk up or down stairs, and that’s great for recovery,” he says. “And trust me on this: If you’re going downstairs, turn around, hold on to the railing, and go down backward. It’s 50 percent less painful.”

Sure, it feels a little awkward, but New York City straphangers have seen far weirder things.

After the race, “I would suggest people forget about running for maybe a week,” Obelkevich says. Instead, he recommends going for a walk, which can help with circulation and recovery, without adding more stress.

But don’t forget to celebrate your accomplishment. After a hot shower, Obelkevich plans to have dinner with his wife, Lyn, and their family at home. There’s no particular ceremony or menu, but there is one non-negotiable: “Beer is very important,” he says, noting that his go-to brew is Miller Lite. “Just one. But one beer is very important.”

Enrollment increased at U-M, the state's flagship, by 693 students, or 1.3%. Enrollment at UM-Flint, rose by 139 students, or about 2%.

At Wayne State, one of the largest institutions, enrollment functionally held steady, with a small increase of 14 students.

Michigan's enrollment challenges could grow in the coming years. The state's population of high school graduates is expected to drop by a fifth from 2023 to 2041, according to a forecast from Western Interstate Commission for Higher Education.
