Some of the components unlawfully purchased and exported to Russia by Suvorov and his procurement network can be used in military communication and radar systems, as well as precision location systems.

Starting October 30, business accounts will consent to Meta scraping their websites if they continue using Facebook, Instagram, or WhatsApp.

The update, set to go into effect on October 30, will include news publishers with business accounts. These accounts allow companies or public figures to manage Facebook pages, Messenger accounts, Instagram profiles, or WhatsApp channels from one place. While journalism isn’t the only industry that will be affected by this update, virtually every news organization has at least one Meta business account, including Nieman Lab.

The new terms broadly cover business accounts that manage pages and groups, run advertisements, push marketing messages, and use the platform’s business account dashboard. As written, the new terms could be used to argue that Meta can scrape content from news sites and use that content to train the company’s AI products, according to three legal experts who reviewed the terms at Nieman Lab’s request.

In early October, Meta alerted users to the update in a blog post and on the Meta Business Suite. The updated terms explicitly state that Meta can use the content it collects from associated websites to “train” Meta products. Elsewhere in its terms of service, the company defines “AI at Meta” as one of those products.

Here’s the new language about scraping in full:

You authorize us (or any entity acting on our behalf) to use automated software (such as web scrapers, bots, robots, spiders, crawlers, and user agents) and create test credentials to access, index, cache, analyze, collect, retrieve, or crawl the domains, web pages, or URLs (and any content available through them) that are associated with your products or services, or that are made available through your or users’ use of Meta Company Products and services, and to periodically pull updates from such domains, web pages, or URLs. Meta may use any content from such domains, web pages, or URLs to provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain the integrity of, the Meta Company Products, services, and features provided under these Commercial Terms and any other applicable supplemental terms.

“For news publishers, this clearly represents permission to Meta to train AI on any content from their sites and possibly to deploy some of that content, even in competition with the publishers themselves,” said James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell Law School. “On the broadest possible interpretation, this would be used to develop a comprehensive archive of all content that Meta would then serve as it wanted to directly or through AI.”

Currently, there is no option for business accounts to opt out of these new terms ahead of October 30. The update reads, “If you do not agree to the updated terms, please stop all access or use of our Products for business or commercial purposes.”

In short, “publishers either have to trust that Meta doesn’t mean the full extent of what these terms say, or they have to stop [using business accounts],” said Grimmelmann.

On its developer page, Meta currently has instructions for how to block its crawlers using robots.txt, a file that tells bots which parts of a website they can and cannot access.

However, legal experts said the new language creates an opening for Meta to argue its commercial terms supersede other blocking instructions. The update also states that Meta can create “test credentials” in order to scrape websites associated with business accounts. Grimmelmann said this would technically allow Meta to “sign up as a fake user” to bypass registration walls on a publisher’s site to access content. Meta declined to answer Nieman Lab’s questions about whether the “test credentials” would bypass paywalls or other paid content.

Sobel compared the move to Google’s recent policies around its AI search products. In order to appear in Google’s traditional search results, publishers previously had to consent to their content appearing in AI Overviews and AI Mode. As of August 2026, all websites now have the option to opt out.

Mississippi Today had a question. Did the stories around its Pulitzer Prize-winning investigative reporting affect the way those investigations were received?

It’s the kind of question local newsrooms can’t easily test on their own. But researchers from Local NExT Lab at Syracuse University could.

In a national survey, 1,768 adults were shown the same investigation surrounded by a different mix of stories. In one, the investigation was shown alongside positive community stories. (Think: “How a local fossil park changed science history.”) In another, the investigation was featured alongside other investigative stories.

The investigation was rated more accurate, important, and relevant when it was surrounded by community-focused stories, not other investigative stories. (Audience reaction to a third story mix, where the investigation was surrounded by stories about legislation, was not consistent.)

The result was described in the first report from the Local NExT Lab. Joshua Darr, director of the lab and associate professor at Syracuse University, believes that although dashboards and engagement analytics are “very important tools” for newsrooms, they aren’t enough.

“Engagement data can’t tell you what caused audiences to engage, settle a hunch, or isolate your contribution,” the report reads. “Randomization can.”

“The key word there is ’caused,'” Darr added in an email. “Without randomization, you can tell when engagement is up or down or if a particular story is doing well, but it’s hard to know exactly why that is happening.”

#2552. 6 ways to make mornings smoother when your child has ADHD

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Home / Health & Wellness / Children's Health

A child psychiatrist on the small systems that take the pressure off the hardest 20 minutes of the day.

By Sara Goldstein Updated Oct. 15, 2026

It’s 7:40, the bus comes at 7:52, and one shoe is missing. You’ve asked about the backpack four times. Your kid is half-dressed on the floor, watching dust move through a sunbeam.

If that’s your morning, you have company. About 1 in 9 US children, roughly 7.1 million kids, has been diagnosed with ADHD, according to CDC data. For a lot of those families, the hardest stretch of the day happens before anyone heads out the door.

There’s a reason mornings hit this hard, and it isn’t that your kid doesn’t care. Michael Morse, MD, a board-certified child and adolescent psychiatrist who directs the ADHD & Anxiety Center at Alvord Baker and is an associate professor of psychiatry at Georgetown University, says the morning routine requires a child “to do the most with the least help.” Many kids with ADHD run on a later internal clock, he says, so at 7 a.m. the brain regions that handle planning and time aren’t fully up and running, and yet, that’s when we ask kids to move through a string of boring steps against a deadline.

Executive function, the mental system for getting started, tracking time and shifting gears, tends to develop more slowly in kids with ADHD, so what looks like refusal is often a brain that can’t get itself going yet without help. “Kids do well when they can,” Morse says. For parents, that means making a mental shift from asking why your kid won’t do something to examining what’s getting in the way. That reframe can often help you resist a lecture and lend a hand instead. “Punishment can’t teach a skill a child doesn’t have yet,” he says, “but support can.”

“The best mornings usually start the evening before, when there’s time to think and nobody is racing the bus,” Morse says. Sign the permission slips, settle on what’s for breakfast, and, if your kid is old enough, have them pack their backpack and lay out clothes for the next day. (For younger kids, you’ll want to supervise this process.)

He also suggests a five-minute planning talk earlier in the day to look at what’s ahead for the day together, whether that’s gym, a test or a game after school, which helps a kid build their own sense of what’s coming.

Sleep is the biggest lever of all. “A tired brain makes every ADHD symptom worse,” Morse says, and it gets less benefit from medication. Sleep trouble is common here, affecting as many as half of kids with ADHD by some estimates. A steady bedtime, screens off about an hour before bed, and a warm bath or shower all make it easier for your child to nod off at night, which sets the stage for smoother mornings.

Kids with ADHD often can’t hold a string of instructions in mind, so “‘go get ready’ is really six instructions in three words,” Morse says. A visual sequence takes that load off their memory. In practice it’s a simple chart on the bathroom mirror listing each step in order: get dressed, eat breakfast, brush teeth, shoes on, backpack. Use pictures for younger kids and let them check off each step as they go, and build the chart together, since kids are more likely to follow a plan they helped make.

When your kid stalls, skip the fresh reminder and point back to the list with a quick “What’s next on your chart?” Do it long enough and “the chart becomes the reminder instead of the parent,” Morse says.

Many kids with ADHD experience time differently, something “called ‘time blindness,’ though some people prefer not to use that term,” Morse says. Either way, 10 minutes from now and an hour from now can feel about the same, so the tools that help are the ones that make time visible. A visual timer with a colored disk that shrinks as the minutes run out lets a kid see at a glance how much is left, and timers that announce the minutes work well for a kid dressing in another room.

Morse says “a simple timer that does one thing beats a phone,” since phones are designed to pull attention toward games and messages, the opposite of what a kid needs while getting ready.

Every choice in the morning is another chance to stall, and each stall may need an adult to jump-start things again. So take the decisions out ahead of time. Have your child select their outfit the night before or keep a few go-to options on rotation. “Some kids wear something close to a uniform and are relieved not to have to think about it,” Morse says. For breakfast, offer two pre-approved options, ideally with some protein like eggs or yogurt to keep energy steady throughout the day.

Give the backpack, shoes and jacket a single launch spot, one basket or hook where they always live, so nobody’s hunting a missing shoe at 7:40. The thread running through all of it, in Morse’s words, is “moving the thinking out of the morning and into the calm of the evening. Fewer decisions also mean fewer arguments.”

If your kid takes medication, when they take it can matter as much as whether they take it. “No ADHD medicine works the moment it’s swallowed,” Morse says, so a long-acting stimulant taken at breakfast may not kick in until a child is already at school, leaving the hardest part of the morning uncovered. Some families, with their doctor’s guidance, give the dose closer to wake-up so it has more time to start working, and some formulations are made to be taken the night before.

This one is a conversation for your prescriber rather than a change to make on your own. Timing is one factor that can impact a medication’s efficacy, so it’s worth raising at the next appointment.

“A few fixes come from love and still backfire,” Morse says. Dressing a dawdling second grader or racing a forgotten worksheet to school smooths the morning but teaches a kid they can’t manage on their own. Repeatedly raising your voice tends to pull everyone into a louder and louder standoff. And consequences rarely do the teaching, because “the problem was memory, not caring,” Morse says.

When a morning does come apart, the goal is to keep it from becoming a bad day between you and your kid. Once everyone’s out the door, the moment for teaching has passed, and a lecture in the car just sends a child into school ashamed and anxious. Try something simple instead, like “That was a rough morning. I love you, and we’ll figure it out.”

Later, when everyone’s calm, look at what broke together, since it’s usually one step you can fix without overhauling the whole routine. Go easy on yourself too. (And if you need a bit of support, Morse points parents to the work of psychologist Kristin Neff on self-compassion.)

None of this makes mornings frictionless. It hands the remembering to a system instead of to you, so you’re not the last thing standing between your kid and the 7:52 bus.

#2562. To make mom friends, I rediscovered my religion

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Having a hard time connecting with other moms? My advice: Head to your favorite house of worship.

By Hannah Pasternak Updated Oct. 15, 2026

A woman, in her life, will make several types of friends. Childhood friends, camp friends, college friends, work friends, and, for those who opt in during the reproductive years, mom friends. The lattermost of these always seemed like an endpoint to me—the hardest level in a game I’ve spent three decades playing. A rite of passage that marks the final frontier of girlhood.

Finding your people in motherhood is difficult. Until your child is in school, you are left floating through fix-ups with friends of friends and families you meet at the playground. Daycare can feel transient, and making solid friends at the small in-home program my daughter attends took more than a year.

You may, like me, have pre-existing close friends who have kids around the same time as you, but they may not live in your neighborhood, let alone your state, and therefore are categorized in their primary, or original, friendship bucket.

Mom friends are unique, made and dependent upon another relationship—that of your child and their child, and what they have in common—and time-dependent, too. Can you meet up on a whim? Would they like to grab that post-drop-off coffee? (This, I’m convinced, is not really something that exists in real life, only in the 90s and on television.) Is it ever really that easy?

Perhaps you have had better luck than me, but the only way I’ve managed to make mom friends is by relying on the oldest trick from, well, the oldest book: religion.

Nearly every Saturday morning, my husband and I  take our daughter to our synagogue at 10 a.m for “Tot Shabbat,” as it’s commonly called. It is just two blocks from our apartment, in a century-old building with magnificent stained glass windows and a ceiling that may be caving in. I take my daughter to a multipurpose room with basketball court floors while the main Sabbath prayer service goes on in the sanctuary. The kids, ages five and below, play together on a blue carpet, with blocks and Magnatiles and cars.

The moms and dads drink our coffees and schmooze. After a while, we do Circle Time, where a wonderful twentysomething teacher leads the kids in songs about gratitude and spiritual tradition. Pom-poms are involved. We are usually there until noon. Oh, and none of us have our phones. And that is how I finally made mom friends.

I realize this makes me an outlier. In a 2023 column for the New York Times, Jessica Grose described the decrease in church attendance as perhaps the “largest and fastest religious shift in America.” The following year, Grose wrote again about young women in particular fleeing organized religion in droves, largely in response to traditional ideologies around bodily autonomy and gender roles. A 2025 study showed that more than one in four Americans consider themselves unaffiliated with organized religion. I can tell you that many people bristle when I share that we observe the weekly Jewish Sabbath, assuming it’s restrictive and overly conservative or anachronistic. Culturally, amongst moms my age who live in urban centers, it just doesn’t seem to be en vogue.

When organized religion is embraced, it’s often by Gen Z, who are curious about its benefits—community, ritual, connection, belief—in an otherwise isolating time. The Wall Street Journal recently claimed that New York City’s newest hotspot is Sunday Mass. Last year, the New York Times wrote about the resurgence of the cross necklace amongst White House staffers and pop stars alike. Elle covered the cross-as-jewelry-trend even earlier, in 2024. As far as modern takes on traditional Judaism go, a hospitality concept called Shtick, founded by producer Jacqueline Lobel, creates editorial Friday night dinners and other parties that celebrate “the quintessential Jewish foods and customs found here in New York City.” In 2025, one such event included a Shabbat dinner for the Council of Fashion Designers of America.

So perhaps there is something to be said about what turning inside yourself, or to your family and its denominational history, can offer. Perhaps there is something to be said about the foundation that a shared belief or tradition can provide. A place of worship, if you find the right one, is also a place that naturally builds local community. (A mommy and me class there can, for what it’s worth, also cost less than a trip to the children’s museum.)

When I started bringing my daughter to our synagogue, it was more of a survival strategy than anything else. She was just six months old, a poor napper and always happier when we were out of the house. She could barely sit up, let alone play with other kids. The other mothers seemed seasoned and poised like veterans, worlds ahead of who I was and what I knew about raising a child. I mostly kept to myself and tried to listen in to everyone else’s conversations. Some moms were nice, asking me my name and gawking at my baby. I kept on showing up.

After my daughter turned one, there was a great shift. A new wave of babies joined the group, all learning to walk and eat chicken and extend their attention spans. This would be her “class,” a group of girls all born within the same six-month period who had finally dropped their morning nap (“What morning nap?” my daughter, if she could talk then, would have said) and were ready to build towers and clap during Circle Time.

Together, my daughter and I hit our groove. Soon we started to get invited for Shabbat lunch at her new friends’ homes, and we invited them to ours. As the mothers stuff the children into their coats and wipe kugel off their faces, we agree to meet again in Central Park later in the day. And because it is Shabbat, and none of us use our phones for 25 hours, we simply fly by the seat of our pants and commit to our plans and show up. When we get to the park we talk to each other, because no one is working or texting or scrolling. It’s very bucolic, and yet it feels like an alternate reality every single Saturday, orbiting the rest of my social life.

So this is the scene of my “mom friends.” We all live in the same neighborhood, but do different things for work. Many of us didn’t know each other before mommy and me, and we all have lives and friends of our own, outside of our motherhood. Our WhatsApp chat errs on the side of purely logistical, confirming our weekly meetup or a sporadic special event—a Purim puppet show, a Hanukkah celebration.

I think this is what religion, as divisive as it can be, is meant to bring to our lives. A place to go with your kid every Saturday morning, the will to keep showing up, people who expect you to be there. And if you’re lucky, kugel.

#4022. Mysterious brain activity under anesthesia challenges what we know about consciousness

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Even when a person is fully unconscious under general anesthesia, their brain may still be doing something remarkable: processing language, recognizing patterns, and even anticipating which words will come next.

Researchers at Baylor College of Medicine have discovered that the brain retains surprisingly sophisticated abilities during anesthesia, despite the absence of conscious awareness. The findings, published in the journal Nature, challenge traditional assumptions about the relationship between consciousness and thinking. They could also provide new insights into how the brain processes memories and language, potentially leading to advances in technologies that help people communicate after neurological injuries.

"Our findings show that the brain is far more active and capable during unconsciousness than previously thought," said Dr. Sameer Sheth, professor and Cullen Foundation Endowed chair of neurosurgery and a McNair Scholar at Baylor. "Even when patients are fully anesthetized, their brains continue to analyze the world around them."

Recording Brain Activity During General Anesthesia

To investigate what happens inside the unconscious brain, Sheth, who is also a neurosurgeon at Baylor St. Luke's Medical Center, and his colleagues studied patients undergoing epilepsy surgery while under general anesthesia.

These procedures offered an unusual opportunity to examine the hippocampus, a brain structure deeply involved in learning and memory. Located within the brain's temporal lobe, the hippocampus helps organize information and plays an important role in forming new memories.

The researchers used advanced recording devices called Neuropixels probes to monitor electrical signals from hundreds of individual neurons in the hippocampus. These tiny probes can detect activity from many nerve cells simultaneously, providing a detailed picture of how groups of neurons respond to information.

According to the researchers, this technology had not previously been used to investigate this particular brain region in this way. It allowed the team to examine how the hippocampus responds to sounds and language while patients are unconscious and unable to report what they experience.

The first experiment involved playing a series of repeating tones, occasionally interrupted by a sound that differed from the others.

Although the patients were under anesthesia, neurons in their hippocampus responded differently to the unexpected sounds. Even more surprisingly, the brain cells became better at distinguishing these unusual tones as the experiment continued.

This improvement suggested that the hippocampus was showing signs of neural plasticity, the brain's ability to change its responses through experience. In other words, some of the basic processes associated with learning appeared to remain active even without conscious awareness.

The Unconscious Brain Can Process Language and Predict Words

The researchers then tested whether the unconscious brain could handle something more complicated than simple sounds.

They played short stories to the anesthetized patients while continuing to record activity from neurons in the hippocampus.

The results revealed a surprising level of language processing. Distinct patterns of neural activity contained information about different categories of words, including nouns, verbs, and adjectives. This suggested that the hippocampus was responding to meaningful features of language rather than simply detecting that sounds were occurring.

But the most intriguing discovery went a step further.

The researchers found that neural activity also carried information that could be used to predict upcoming words in a sentence. Even though the patients were unconscious, their brains appeared to anticipate how the language unfolding around them might continue.

"The brain appears to anticipate what comes next in a story, even without conscious awareness," said Sheth, who is also Director of The Gordon and Mary Cain Pediatric Neurology Research Foundation Laboratories within the Duncan Neurological Research Institute at Texas Children's Hospital.

This ability is related to a process known as predictive coding. Rather than passively receiving information, the brain continually uses patterns and previous experience to anticipate what might happen next. When listening to someone speak, for example, the brain can use the words already heard to form expectations about what is likely to follow.

Scientists have typically studied this kind of sophisticated prediction in people who are awake and paying attention. Finding evidence of it during general anesthesia suggests that at least some of the underlying neural processes can operate independently of conscious awareness.

"This kind of predictive coding is something we associate with being awake and attentive, yet it's happening here in an unconscious state," said Dr. Benjamin Hayden, professor of neurosurgery at Baylor.

What Does This Discovery Mean for Consciousness?

The findings raise important questions about what consciousness actually contributes to the brain's ability to process information.

Consciousness is generally associated with subjective awareness, including the experience of hearing, thinking, and understanding the world around us. Yet the study suggests that some neural processes involved in interpreting language and predicting future information can continue even when that awareness is absent.

This does not necessarily mean that the anesthetized patients consciously understood the stories being played to them. Instead, it indicates that the brain can carry out surprisingly complex aspects of language processing without a person being aware of doing so.

The distinction could help researchers better understand how consciousness emerges.

One possibility is that awareness depends not simply on whether individual brain regions remain active, but on how information is coordinated and shared across larger networks of brain regions.

Under this view, a structure such as the hippocampus might continue analyzing information even when the broader communication between brain regions needed for conscious experience is disrupted.

A Surprising Connection Between the Brain and Artificial Intelligence

The discovery also highlights an intriguing similarity between human brain activity and artificial intelligence (AI).

Large language models, the technology behind many modern AI chatbots, generate text by using patterns learned from enormous amounts of language data to predict what should come next.

The researchers observed a related predictive ability in the hippocampus, where neural signals reflected information about upcoming words even during unconsciousness.

Although biological brains and AI models operate in fundamentally different ways, both can use patterns in language to anticipate future information. Studying these similarities may help scientists better understand how prediction works in natural and artificial systems.

The findings could also have practical implications for brain-computer interfaces, which use signals recorded from the brain to communicate with external devices.

One potential application involves speech prosthetics, technologies designed to help people communicate when injuries or neurological conditions have impaired their ability to speak.

If scientists can reliably identify meaningful language signals in the brain, those signals might eventually be used to support communication technologies for patients who have lost speech abilities.

"Can we use these signals to deploy and run a speech prosthetic for some of the parts of the brain that are damaged by stroke or injury? These are questions that we can now consider in relation to this part of the brain," said Dr. Vigi Katlowitz, first author and a neurosurgery resident with Baylor.

Scientists Are Rethinking the Limits of the Unconscious Brain

Despite the striking results, the researchers emphasize that several questions remain unanswered.

The experiments were conducted under one particular type of general anesthesia, so the findings cannot automatically be extended to other anesthetic methods or states of unconsciousness.

It is also unclear whether similar language processing occurs during sleep or coma, two conditions that differ significantly from medically induced unconsciousness.

Another important limitation is that the researchers focused on the hippocampus. Further studies will be needed to determine whether comparable abilities persist in other brain regions and how these areas interact when consciousness is absent.

Nevertheless, the results provide compelling evidence that unconsciousness does not necessarily mean the brain has stopped performing sophisticated information processing.

Instead, the brain may continue analyzing patterns, responding to unexpected events, and anticipating language even when a person has no conscious awareness of the surrounding world.

"This work pushes us to rethink what it means to be conscious," said Sheth. "The brain is doing much more behind the scenes than we fully understand."
