#1248. Safe Word: The Best Jewelry Launches of October 2026

Safe Word is a series highlighting the latest trends in the jewelry market, keeping you up to date on all things that sparkle and shine.

October’s jewelry launches are proving that what a piece represents can be just as important as how it looks. From meaningful motifs to nature-focused materials, this month’s new arrivals are putting a deeper sense of purpose behind what we wear. First up, Mejuri adds Montana sapphires to “Salmon Gold”; John Hardy looks to Bali for “Modern Talisman”; Tiffany & Co. gives HardWear a colorful update; Buccellati revisits a century-old motif with the latest evolution of “Torsadé.” By Pariah plays with contrast in “Smoke and Light”; Asprey teams up with Silvia Furmanovich to reimagine rare jadeite; Kinn Studio celebrates nine years with “Dare to Dream”; Aurate taps stylist Molly Dickson for a vintage-inspired collaboration; Fie Isolde gives Art Deco an organic edge with “Déco”; and Julietta makes jewelry the outfit for holiday/resort 2027.

Together, October’s launches prove there’s more than one way for jewelry to tell a story.

For the rest of the month, we’ve got you covered on all the jewelry-related happenings worth knowing about.

Bolola Expands Its Bolo-Inspired World for Resort 2027

Bolola is embracing gilded excess for holiday 2026 and resort 2027. At the center of the collection is gold rutilated quartz, a translucent stone threaded with natural golden inclusions that feels equally festive for the holiday season and light and pared-back for resort. The brand also introduces the Delphine, a sculptural new silhouette inspired by the natural geometry of a nautilus shell. Rounding out the season is Bolola’s first collection of cocktail rings, bringing its signature bolo-inspired designs from the collar to the hand. Together, the new additions give the brand’s unique take on the bolo tie even more ways to shine.

Blue Nile is joining forces with Royal Asscher for a new collaboration centered on natural diamonds. As Royal Asscher’s exclusive licensed retailer in North America, Blue Nile will offer the historic house’s six patented diamond cuts alongside the new “Curated by the Asscher Family” assortment. The partnership also introduces “SIX by Royal Asscher,” a 14-piece collection spanning rings, earrings, pendants, and high-jewelry tennis styles featuring center stones from one to five carats. Drawing on six generations and more than 170 years of Asscher diamond expertise, the collaboration puts craftsmanship and the beauty of the cut front and center.

Spinelli Kilcollin Introduces the ‘Élan’ Collection

Spinelli Kilcollin is expanding its signature interconnected design language with “Élan,” a new collection of necklaces, bracelets, earrings, and rings. The assortment plays with linked circular forms, creating sculptural pieces designed to make an impact whether worn alone or layered with one another. The campaign brings that spirit to life through a cast of creatives, each wearing the collection in a way that reflects their own personal style. It’s a fitting introduction to a collection that makes connection feel entirely personal.

Zales Introduces the 26.2 Edit for the Marathon

Zales is taking fine jewelry for a run with “The 26.2 Edit,” a new collection inspired by the dedication and milestones behind marathon training. The 27-piece assortment spans tennis bracelets and necklaces, earrings, anklets, and pendants in silver, gold, and lab-grown diamond-accented styles, including new 13.1 and 26.2 pieces commemorating half- and full-marathon distances. To put the pieces to the test, Zales assembled a 16-person Running Squad led by Carl Radke and joined by Tayshia Adams and Chloë Lukasiak, who will train and run an upcoming marathon in the jewelry. Built around the brand’s “You Are the Occasion” message, the collection celebrates not just crossing the finish line, but every mile it takes to get there.

Prounis Steps Into High Jewelry With ‘Moderne’

Prounis is turning up the volume with “Moderne,” its boldest collection yet and first true step into high jewelry. Launching October 21, the cocktail jewels introduce larger scales, unexpected black-and-white palettes, and intricate combinations of multiple stones, while still holding onto the historical details synonymous with the house. This time, founder Jean Prounis looks especially close to home for inspiration, drawing from The Versailles, the glamorous 1930s New York nightclub once run by her great-grandparents. The result brings a little Jazz Age glamour into the present, marking an exciting new chapter for Prounis.

Buccellati Adds More Sparkle to Its ‘Torsadé’ Collection

Buccellati is putting a new twist on a century-old signature with the latest evolution of its “Torsadé” collection. First explored by Mario Buccellati in the 1920s, the twisted-rope motif has evolved across three generations of the family. Today, Andrea Buccellati pairs the house’s signature “rigato” engraving with curved bands of diamonds, bringing even more dimension and brilliance to the spiraling design. New diamond-set rings and earrings join necklaces and bracelets in expanded sizes, giving a historic Buccellati motif a fresh reason to shine.

#992. No-Knead Pumpkin Focaccia

📅 Oct 05, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

For those who fall into the pumpkin-should-be-savory camp, let us offer up a new recipe primed to become a seasonal staple: Pumpkin Focaccia. The iconic bread we know and love is ripe for the pumpkin treatment, lending it a golden hue and earthy sweetness. This version doesn’t require any kneading, making it straightforward enough to pull off in a single afternoon. You’ll perform a series of folds as it rises, simply pulling and stretching the dough to help it develop gluten. A showering of fresh rosemary and flaky salt on top is optional, but it takes crisp, plush focaccia from good to great. Serve it with a bowl of olive oil for dipping or a schmear of cream cheese to lean into the seasonal profile.

This recipe is part of Pumpkin Week 2026, Bon Appétit’s annual celebration of autumn’s most magical pantry staple.

Mix one ¼-oz. envelope active dry yeast (about 2¼ tsp.), 5 cups (625 g) all-purpose flour, 1 cup canned pumpkin purée, 1 Tbsp. Diamond Crystal or 1¾ tsp. Morton kosher salt, 1 Tbsp. light brown sugar, and 1½ cups warm water in a large bowl until a dry, shaggy dough forms. Cover with plastic wrap and let sit in a warm spot 15 minutes.

Place a small bowl of room-temperature water on work surface for easy reaching. Uncover dough and moisten hands in water, shaking off excess. Stretch 1 side of dough up and over to meet opposite side; turn bowl 90°. Repeat process 3 more times, turning bowl after each fold. (This constitutes 1 full fold.) If dough doesn’t always stretch to meet opposite side, just aim for the middle. Remoisten your hands and shake off excess water any time dough begins to feel sticky. Cover dough and let sit 30 minutes (set a timer). Repeat entire process 2 more times (folding and resting), about 1 hour more. By the last full fold, the dough should be significantly smoother and tighter.

Pour ¼ cup extra-virgin olive oil into a 13x9" metal baking pan. Lightly oil your hands and punch down dough, then transfer to prepared pan. Sprinkle with flaky sea salt. Turn dough salted side down to coat both sides in oil. Press into an even layer with your fingers. Cover with plastic wrap and let sit in a warm spot until puffy, about 45 minutes.

Place a rack in bottom third of oven; preheat to 450°. Unwrap focaccia and scatter rosemary leaves and more sea salt over if desired; drizzle with oil. Oil your hands and press fingers into focaccia several times to create dimples across surface, making sure to press fingers all the way down to the bottom of the pan. Bake focaccia, rotating pan front to back halfway through, until deep golden brown, 23–28 minutes. Transfer pan to a wire rack and let focaccia cool slightly in pan. Using a metal spatula, turn out focaccia onto rack and let cool completely.

Do Ahead: Focaccia can be made 1 day ahead. Store tightly wrapped at room temperature.

#980. Pumpkin Bolognese

📅 Oct 05, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Classic Bolognese, with its multi-hour cook time, can be truly remarkable, if you ever have a stretch to make it. In this version, a scoop of canned pumpkin purée is the head-turning ingredient that gives it full-blown flavor and body, without the wait typical of a nonna-style sauce. Lightly caramelizing the pumpkin in the pot along with tomato paste gives it a nutty, savory edge. Milk is the other surprise ingredient here, especially in the quantity of a full cup. Think of it as a tenderizer, making the final sauce soft and delicate. Pay attention to the order in which the ingredients appear—the milk goes in first with the wine to follow. If you switch the order, the acidic wine will curdle the milk.

This recipe is part of Pumpkin Week 2026, Bon Appétit’s annual celebration of autumn’s most magical pantry staple.

Heat 4 Tbsp. unsalted butter in a medium Dutch oven or other heavy pot over medium. Add 1 large white or yellow onion, finely chopped, 2 celery stalks, finely chopped, 5 garlic cloves, finely chopped, 2 tsp. Diamond Crystal or 1¼ tsp. Morton kosher salt, and ¼ tsp. freshly grated nutmeg and stir to combine. Cover pot and cook, stirring occasionally, until onion and celery are very soft, 8–10 minutes. Uncover and continue to cook, stirring occasionally, until onion and celery are golden around the edges, about 5 minutes.

Uncover pot and add 1 lb. ground beef, preferably 20% fat; season lightly with salt. Cook, stirring occasionally, until beef is very lightly browned but not crisp (it will still be pink in spots), 6–8 minutes. Pour in 1 cup whole milk and cook, stirring occasionally, until milk has evaporated and fat is pooling on surface of meat, 7–10 minutes.

Pour in 1 cup dry white wine and cook, stirring occasionally, until wine has evaporated, 6–8 minutes. Add 1 cup canned pumpkin purée and 2 Tbsp. double-concentrated tomato paste; season generously with freshly ground pepper. Cook, stirring often and scraping bottom of pot, until combined and pumpkin darkens slightly, 8–10 minutes.

Pour in 1 cup water, reduce heat to low, and cook, stirring occasionally, until mixture is saucy, 12–15 minutes. If meat seems dry, add an extra ½ cup water and continue cooking another 3–4 minutes. Taste sauce and season with salt if needed. Keep warm.

Meanwhile, cook 12 oz. fresh tagliatelle or pappardelle or dry rigatoni, in a large pot of boiling salted water. If using fresh pasta, cook about 3 minutes. If using dry, cook until very al dente, about 2 minutes less than package directions.

Using tongs, transfer pasta to pot with sauce. Add 1 cup pasta cooking liquid and 2 oz. Parmesan, finely grated (about 1 cup). Increase heat to medium, bring to a simmer, and cook, tossing constantly, until pasta is al dente and sauce is slightly thickened, about 2 minutes.

Transfer pasta to shallow bowls and sprinkle with more Parmesan.

#970. Pumpkin Enchiladas With Black Beans

📅 Oct 05, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Homemade enchiladas might sound like a laborious weekend project, but with a few clever tricks, this version made with black beans and pumpkin can be whipped up on even the most hurried of work nights. For starters, canned pumpkin creates the base of a quick sauce that requires no precooking. After 327 days, 17 hours and 30 minutes at sea, Dave “Dinger” Bell has completed one of the most ambitious ocean rowing expeditions ever attempted. The UK man rowed 28,700km solo and unsupported from the west coast of Peru to Kenya across both the Pacific and Indian Oceans without stopping.

He set off from Peru on November 9, 2025, armed with 350 days’ worth of food, and a plan for crossing two oceans that hinged on how he felt when he had finished his crossing of the Pacific Ocean and reached Australia. If in good shape, he would cross the notoriously tricky Torres Strait and cross into the Indian Ocean so that he could row to Mauritius, the original end point of the row.

One of the strangest problems came in the South Pacific. By April, he was making too much progress. If this kept up, he would reach the Coral Sea during cyclone season. For weeks, he had to reduce his rowing hours. For someone whose entire expedition depended on covering distance, weeks of forced inactivity became a major challenge.

“I was too quick, so I had to slow down,” Bell told the BBC. “To sit, mid-ocean, knowing you’ve still got seven, eight, nine months at sea but not be rowing was one of the hardest things, psychologically, that I’ve ever had to deal with.”

In May, he was finally able to row through the Coral Sea, although the wind almost constantly pushed him in the wrong direction. At one point, the waves were so large that he almost capsized. Water flooded into the boat, setting off his EPIRB, and he had to make some frantic InReach calls to assure everyone he was okay. By the end of the month, he could see Australia from his boat, a huge milestone in his journey. There was no question in his mind about continuing across the Indian Ocean, so he picked his way across the Great Barrier Reef and through the Torres Strait.

After making it through the Strait, he paused for a few days on para-anchor and gave himself some time to rest and sort out equipment and electrical issues. Bell then continued along Australia’s northern coast before striking out into the Indian Ocean.

The second ocean of his journey brought a whole different set of problems. In July and August, he seemed to switch back and forth between calm conditions and storms with large swells, heavy rain, and awful visibility. At the start of August, gale-force winds reached 30 knots, and a massive wave slammed into his boat. Water swamped the cabin through the air vents, soaking his sleeping cabin and triggering the emergency beacon. Bell contacted Coast Guard officials to alert them to a potential rescue. Luckily, the weather soon abated, and no intervention was required.

Bell spent several days, and once almost a week, riding out conditions on his para-anchor. “There is no prize for pushing your luck,” he later wrote on social media. “Sometimes the smartest thing you can do is stop, wait, and let the ocean have its moment.” When the weather finally eased, he discovered that the autopilot controls had stopped working. Still, he was able to continue.

All of this meant the planned finish location had to change. Bell had originally intended to end his row in Mauritius, but persistent southeasterly currents and weather made that impossible. Continuing to Kenya and mainland Africa added roughly 1,800km and three weeks to the expedition. As he was making such good time, he still had enough food and supplies for the additional few weeks the detour added to his journey. Even with the additional distance, he made it back to dry land well before his 350-day aim, and with 20 days’ worth of food left.

Bell managed to stay upbeat throughout the row, despite being completely isolated for almost a full year. He used podcasts, audiobooks, and voice notes to avoid constantly watching the clock, and relished any encounter with wildlife. At different points, he had birds hitch a ride with him and whitetip sharks following his boat for weeks, swimming on either side as he rowed.

The final approach was almost as strange for him as the beginning of the row. In the final few days of the row, he described his head as “all over the place.” After months of thinking purely about moving the boat forward and looking hours ahead, the prospect of getting back onto dry land was overwhelming. He described the prospect of stepping ashore, thinking about the practicalities of walking, sleeping in an actual bed, and dealing with people again as “bewildering.” Bell predicted “total sensory overload.” He said the last week on the ocean had been the calmest of his life.

When he finally arrived in Kilifi, he was joined by two small support boats, and a helicopter flew above him. On the beach, his family, friends, support team, and the local press greeted him.

“I’ve not stepped off that boat since the ninth of November. This is the first time I’ve touched land,” he told reporters in Kenya as he made his way onto land, slightly hunched over, but surprisingly stable for someone who hadn’t walked in months.

He has since updated followers that after a few days back “in the land of luxury of dry, clean clothes and a bed that doesn’t move,” he is doing well, can stand up straight, and walk without assistance.

For cooks and gardeners, colder weather is something of a second spring. It is our Greens Season.

In hot summer climates, there is no hope of growing crisp salads and chewy kales from June to August. And cilantro bolts while your back is turned. But as the nights grow longer and chillier, we can begin to look forward to a fall salad strategy, and order seeds, and dig, and wait.

Here are seven of our favorite autumn vegetables to start growing now:

Thanks to heirloom seed saving and clever breeding, lettuce is no longer just lettuce. There are dozens of cultivars from which to choose, Flashy Trout Back Lettuce (also known as Spotted Trout and Speckled Trout) is a charmer, with freckled pale green leaves and an open habit. Sow it every week for a succession of salads till frost.

Arugula’s peppery bite might be ubiquitous in supermarket clamshells by now, but nothing beats a spicy leaf picked straight from the pot on your terrace or from the row you dug three weeks earlier. Allowing your arugula or mustard plants to bolt is rewarding, too, because their buds and flowers are as hot as their greens, and perk up mild salads.

A microgreen is the immature form of any edible leaf, snipped while it has just two cotyledons or the first two true leaves to its name. These miniature leaves can be grown in windowsill trays if you are an apartment dweller, or in outdoor pots in full sun. Sow every week for repeated harvests. Collect them when their stems are about an inch long.

Dwarf kale is a container gardener’s delight, providing the full kale taste and health experience packed into a small and sturdy leaf which is mature before its full-size cousins. If you plant it in full sun, you might be lucky enough to be eating your crop well into the frosty weather—I have seen the plants withstand inches of snow and become sweeter in the process.

Chards and spinaches love cold nights and like kale and other cool greens can be harvested well after the first frost. Rainbow chard may taste the same as regular chard, but its prettiness factor is so high that it can be included as a fall crop for aesthetic pleasure alone.

Waiver: fall-planted peas will not set pods in cold climates. Instead, the goal is gather as many bunches of tender pea shoots as possible. The tender stems and leaves are as sweet and pea-ish as the pods and are wonderful in salads, or gently steamed and dressed with soy and sesame oil. The seeds must be soaked for 12 hours before planting, and full sun is optimal for the most prolific growth. Pinch out the tender growing stems as soon as the plants are about four inches tall, and keep picking until successive frosts close your pea season.

This is one bean that loves the cold. Sow favas (also known as broad beans) in late winter and in early fall. As with peas, you will not see pods form after a fall planting–the days are too short. The autumn  crop is grown for the tender leaves and stems only, and these can be harvested about three weeks after planting and for a month or more beyond. The youngest silky leaves make unusual salad ingredients and are good cooked, too.

This root vegetable works while the winter world sleeps. Plant parsnips straight into deep pots (about 16 inches or more) in mid-fall for February or March harvest. Their leaves will appear before winter, and over several months the sweet tap roots will grow fat for harvest. Pulling vegetables from your garden in the middle of winter is a bit of a party piece–plan a celebratory salad with quick-pickled beets and kale greens and apple, and invite your friends.

It is ironic that the herb so closely associated with hot climates and spicy food evolved in cool Europe, where it was largely shunned. Cilantro, which gets a bad rap for bolting in a heartbeat, grows best before and after summer. Spring and autumn are the best times to sow this pungent herb. It will withstand a couple of frosts before it gives up for good.
