#3712. Zone 2 Training Transforms Your Fitness and Health—Experts Explain How to Execute It

📅 Oct 06, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

We explain why the majority of your rides should hit this effort and how to make the most of the miles.

Even if you’re not completely familiar with your power or heart rate training zones, you’ve probably heard that you should spend a majority of your time in the saddle riding at an effort that allows you to chat with your cycling buddies. This intensity, known as zone 2 training, provides a bunch of payoffs in terms of fitness and performance.

Also referred to as the aerobic zone, conversational pace, or all-day pace—though those titles can be slightly misleading—this effort can get confusing to master.

To help you conquer it, we explain exactly what zone 2 training means, why it matters, how it can help your performance, and how to do it for maximum gains.

🚴🏽 Ready to take on your fastest ride yet? Our How to Ride Faster program will help you make it happen!

Considered the leading global expert on all things zone 2, Iñigo San Millán, Ph.D., has spent three decades studying the effects of training in this aerobic zone. The former cyclist has worked with clinical populations dealing with diabetes, and he’s working with the upper echelon of the cycling world.

When he spoke to Bicycling a while back, he was in the middle of a rainstorm in France after chasing Tadej Pogačar up a Tour de France climb. “Let’s be clear,” he joked. “A normal person would be in zone 5 trying to keep up with Pogačar in his zone 2.”

That’s the just beginning of the nuances around this aerobic zone that’s gotten so much press coverage in recent years. (This fact bemuses San Millán, as someone who spent decades toiling away in labs and in the field on this work before it went viral.)

Before we get into the how to do zone 2 training, let’s briefly dig into the science side, which explains why it’s so important.

San Millán’s original interest in zone 2 came when he realized that optimal cellular mitochondrial function (the powerhouse of our cells) is key for both health and performance.

While you can’t see your mitochondria making moves when you exercise, you can feel how well your muscles clear lactate that’s produced by more intense exercise. “When you don’t clear lactate correctly, it builds up in the blood, and that’s a sign that your mitochondria are not keeping up very well,” says San Millán.

He started to notice that training more in the aerobic-based zone 2—the zone where the body produces lactate but is able to clear it equally quickly—allowed athletes to better clear the lactate from their muscles when they did increase their intensity and lactate production. Essentially, training at this lower intensity allowed them to train harder at higher intensities.

The same effect applies to pro athletes and recreational cyclists: Training that aerobic zone where the body is burning fat rather than glycogen improves both performance and health outcomes, thanks to how it improves mitochondrial function.

What makes zone 2 training particularly interesting is that it’s one of the few training stimuli that’s as good for health and longevity as it is for performance. San Millán’s research has found that many clinical populations—those with certain cancers, type 2 diabetes, and other metabolic diseases—have significant mitochondrial dysfunction.

While exercise alone isn’t a cure-all, San Millán found that adding in a protocol of zone 2-based exercise led to significant health improvements for those populations. Meanwhile, it also makes top pros like Pogačar get up hills faster.

Because it’s become so popular, there are many misconceptions around zone 2 training.

For starters, there’s the question of “what is zone 2?” Most people will say it’s all-day pace, conversational pace, or refer to it as LSD (long slow distance). But often, the pace you’re riding at in zone 2 is a bit harder than you may think. “You should be able to hold a conversation,” says San Millán. “But it shouldn’t be easy to do so. If it’s easy to chat, that’s more zone 1 recovery versus zone 2.”

It’s also not that slow. Again, see Pogačar’s “all-day pace,” which is likely faster than most of us could do on our best threshold testing day.

On the flip side, popular health and wellness blogs who’ve honed in on zone 2 will talk about walking as a great way to get in some zone 2 exercise. That may work for someone who doesn’t regularly exercise, but if you are training regularly, you’ll need to make that walk pretty brisk in order to move from zone 1 training into zone 2.

In other words: Zone 2 is different for everyone. The most important thing is to pay attention to your own effort to make sure you’re hitting the right intensity.

Also, if you got interested in zone 2 for health reasons, you may think that it’s all that you ever need to do. However, San Millán is quick to point out that while it’s the building block for cyclists, you do still need to do intervals and harder efforts if you want to make gains in your cycling. After all, if you never go hard, why did you bother improving that mitochondrial function that allows you to push the intensity in the first place?

While zone 2 trains you to be efficient at burning fat, once you get above that zone, you start burning glucose for fuel—and that’s something you need to train your body for, rather than hoping that on race day, you’ll magically be really good at going hard after training solely in zone 2.

The ultimate test for finding your zone 2 pace is a lactate test, because zone 2 is where your body is clearing the lactate you produce during exercise, not letting it accumulate.

While in an ideal universe, San Millán would send you to a lab for lactate testing in order to find your zone 2 pace, you can also base it off of your functional threshold power or heart rate. (Use this chart to help determine your pace.)

However, San Millán believes that FTP tests aren’t a perfect power-setting option when it comes to zone 2. Use that as a starting point, but make sure the power or heart rate matches up with the correct feeling: Your zone 2 pace should be sustainable for a few hours, and you should be able to hold a conversation, though it shouldn’t feel easy to do so.

Kristen Legan, gravel cycling coach and winner of the 350-mile Unbound XL in 2023, knows a thing or two about spending a lot of time in zone 2. When you’re training for races like the XL, most of your time is spent in the aerobic zone, though as with racers like Pogačar, she does need to spend some time going really freaking fast.

She says that even if you aren’t riding for six hours, it should feel like you could if you wanted to. “Zone 2 is the pace that you can sustain for a really long time—as long as you’re eating and drinking!” says Legan. (Often, the reason zone 2 starts to feel harder as the ride gets longer is fuel-based, not leg- or lung-based.)

However, while this sounds like the ride should be easy, don’t confuse conversational with “easy pace.” “Zone 1 is a recovery or easy ride,” says Legan. “In these rides, you’re in easy gears, you’re really relaxed, and the goal is just getting your body moving. It’s not about pressure on the pedals. Zone 2 is a bit harder than that.”

“You can pay attention to your heart rate monitor or power meter to make sure you’re staying in the zone if you aren’t comfortable with relying on your perceived exertion,” says Legan. “But I think just trying to keep that conversational pace—testing out talking while you’re riding if you need to check in—is the best way to ensure you stay in the zone.”

To be quite honest, zone 2 can take some practice to perfect, considering it runs the fine line right between going too easy and going too hard. Don’t get discouraged if you struggle with it at first!

“It takes practice to do zone 2,” says Legan. “But the more you do it, it will become easier and easier to just fall into it rather than staying in the easier zone 1 or ratcheting up to zone 3 or higher.”

“As athletes, I think a lot of us hate that feeling of needing to shift down, because we just want to be pushing all the time, and we want to feel like we’re getting work done,” says Legan. “It takes mental practice to just be okay with not trying to speed up a hill.”

Don’t forget, outside influences can affect your zone 2 pace, too—it’s not a steady marker, says San Millán. In heat or at altitude, for example, you may see a 10 percent decrease in the power you’re able to hold while staying within your zone 2 heart rate, because the environment is also taxing the body.

“Ideally, someone would be training three to four days a week in zone 2,” says San Millán. “But these sessions don’t need to be as long as you might think. People always assume zone 2 rides need to be three or more hours to ‘count,’ but even 90 minutes in zone 2 will show good results.”

If you are hoping to do longer races, obviously adding more volume on some rides will be beneficial, but that 90 minutes can improve your mitochondrial function.

For most cyclists, Legan says that the breakdown of hours spent in zone 2 should be about 80 percent of time there, with 20 percent spent in intervals and recovery from said intervals. “Whether you’re a crit racer, road racer, or a gravel racer, that breakdown works—you just do different intervals in the other 20 percent of your time. But the biggest physiological boost comes from that time in zone 2,” she says.

When you’re on a zone 2 ride outside, don’t panic if you go slightly above or below your zone as you go up a hill (or descend down one). Get comfortable shifting into an easy gear and spinning away on hills to keep your effort as close to zone 2 as possible, but San Millán says that even if 80 percent of your ride is in zone 2, that’s a good ride!

Like most things, training in zone 2 takes time before you see major benefits. “You’ll start getting some adaptations after a couple of months if you’re training in zone 2 consistently,” San Millán says. But the real improvements come after a year of keeping your zone 2 training consistent, while still adding in intervals and high-intensity work occasionally.

“I compare training in zone 2 to planting a vineyard,” he adds. “In the first season of planting, you’re not going to have any harvest even if you do all the right preparation. You’ll see some vines, you’ll see that it’s coming, but you won’t have grapes yet. But the second year... that’s when you have that first harvest.”

#2441. That Stamp on Lumber Tells You More Than the Price Tag

📅 Oct 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Stop picking your lumber based on how it looks.

A lumber grader with almost 30 years on the job once watched a buyer reject five good boards for wane (bark or missing wood along an edge), then hand-pick five replacements. All five were below grade. Most of us choose lumber by the price tag and by eye, and the eye picks wrong more often than you’d think. The stamp on lumber tells you what the eye can’t. It says whether a board is rated to carry a floor, what decides how far it can span, and roughly how much shrinking it has left to do after it’s nailed in place.

I framed and renovated houses for years with Wargo Construction, and I didn’t really know what that stamp said until I read the federal standard behind it. It’s a block of black ink a foot or two from one end of the 2x4s, 2x6s and other framing lumber in the aisle, and it names the mill, the grading agency, the grade, the species and how wet the wood was when it was surfaced (run through a planer to its final size). A 2×4’s grade turns on what its knots, splits and holes do to its strength, a different question from the one behind plywood grades.

Ahead, you’ll find what each field on the 2×4 stamp means, which values it can hold, and what each one changes about the board you take home.

The 2×4 stamp tells you which grade a qualified grader, or an agency-approved grading machine, assigned the board under an agency accredited by the American Lumber Standard Committee (ALSC). That grade, with the species beside it, keys to the board’s design values (the published strength ratings that building codes are built on). Those values feed the span tables (the code charts that give how far a joist or rafter of a given size, species, grade and spacing can reach between supports).

The 2024 International Residential Code requires sawn lumber used for load-supporting purposes, meaning floor, wall and roof framing, to carry that stamp or a certificate of inspection.

The grader in that story is Pete Austin, Director of Quality at the Western Wood Products Association (WWPA), who has graded lumber for almost 30 years, 23 of them in sawmill production, and runs the association’s design-value testing for Western species. Most buyers never read past the grade, he says. They look at a board, see No. 2 and assume it will work. “I don’t think that they fully understand what it is they’re buying,” he says.

Paul Conway is Executive Vice President of the ALSC, the Standing Committee in charge of the federal lumber standard PS 20 and the body that accredits the grading agencies under it. He wrote that since a tree is a natural product, no two are alike. Grading is not an exact science. Agencies check a mill’s grading by the unit (the banded bundle of boards a mill ships as one lot), and due to a difference in opinions between graders, a unit is on grade when 95% or more of its pieces meet the stated grade. No lumber is permitted to be intentionally off-grade. “The grade stamp does not guarantee that every single piece of wood in a bundle is on-grade, but design values are developed to be intentionally conservative to account for the lowest quality pieces in a bundle,” Conway wrote.

By the USDA Forest Products Laboratory’s Wood Handbook, the published bending value starts from a strength near the bottom of the grade, one that only about 1 board in 20 falls below, and is then divided by 2.1 for safety and for loads that stay in place for years.

Every accredited stamp carries the same five fields in the agency’s own layout, and some carry a surfacing note beside them. Start with who made and graded the board, then read the three fields that say how it should behave.

This is the number, name or brand of the mill that made the piece. Three boards photographed for this article read 134, 167 and 338, all Stimson Lumber mills on WWPA’s list. The number won’t change what you buy. Read it and move on, because it only matters if a bad batch ever has to be traced back to where it was made.

The logo beside the mill number belongs to the agency that supervised the grading, not to the mill. ALSC’s January 2026 list, on alsc.org, carries seven U.S. grading agencies and ten Canadian ones, with a picture of each agency’s stamp for checking an unfamiliar logo. The ones you’re most likely to meet:

The Canadian agencies, where Spruce-Pine-Fir framing comes from, grade to the National Lumber Grades Authority’s rules and print their own logos. The grader is usually a mill employee, and the independence sits in the supervision. ALSC requires an agency to inspect each mill at least 12 times a year, and PS 20 bars accrediting any agency controlled by a producer whose products it inspects. Every agency’s dimension lumber rules have to conform to one National Grading Rule, so a No. 2 means the same thing under any of these logos. If the logo is one you don’t recognize, check it against ALSC’s list before you trust the grade printed beside it, because a logo that isn’t on that list isn’t an accredited grade stamp, and the board counts as unstamped (see the FAQ below).

The grade sets what’s allowed to be wrong with the board. The National Grading Rule for dimension lumber lists eight grades in three groups, with each group serving a specific purpose.

Stamps abbreviate them (SEL STR, 1, 2, 3, CONST, STAND, UTIL, STUD), and a combined grade such as “2 & BTR” means No. 2 or better, carrying No. 2’s design values.

Knots decide most of the grade, and where a knot sits matters as much as how big it is. A knot is where a branch grew out through the trunk, and the grain swirls around it instead of running the length of the board, so the wood there is partly cross-grain and the load piles up around the knot instead of passing through. A board carrying weight bends, stretching one edge and compressing the other; the stress is highest along those two edges and lowest along the centerline. So an edge knot costs far more strength than the same knot in the middle of the face. And a narrower board gives the same knot a bigger share of its width, which is why the allowances shrink as the board does.

The rule book turns that into numbers. On a 2×4, No. 2 allows a 1-1/4-inch knot at the edge and a 2-inch knot on the centerline, Stud allows a 1-3/4-inch edge knot, and Utility allows 2-1/2 inches anywhere on the face. Austin has measured the difference by breaking boards in WWPA’s test lab. A knot on the centerline leaves clear wood along both edges to carry the load, he says, so it takes far more force to fail.

At the rack, match the grade on the 2×4 stamp to the job as the list above gives it, and between two No. 2s, take the one whose knots sit away from the edges.

This names one species, or a group that shares design values, printed as an abbreviation. The groups you’ll meet most:

The species group sets the design values, so the same grade and size span different distances in each group, and the span tables in the residential code list Douglas Fir-Larch, Hem-Fir, Southern Pine and Spruce-Pine-Fir as separate rows. Canadian-graded Douglas Fir-Larch and Hem-Fir carry an (N) after the name and have rows of their own.

If your plans or a span table name a species, buy that species. When the store stocks a different group, read the row for that group on the stamp instead, which may mean a larger size or tighter spacing, or ask whoever drew the plans. And if nothing names a species, read the group off the stamp, not the shelf tag, and look up that row with the grade before you pick a joist size and spacing. The tables are in the residential code’s floor-joist and rafter chapters, which the International Code Council posts online. A tag reading “Premium Whitewood Stud” is store signage. “Whitewood” covers a family of pale softwoods, and the board underneath says Hem-Fir.

The code says how wet the board was at the planer, not how wet it is now. What you’ll see:

You’ll often see HT beside the code. It isn’t a moisture code but a heat-treatment mark, which says the board’s core was held at 56 C (about 133 F) for at least 30 minutes to kill insects, which is what export packaging needs. Most mills with kilns stamp everything KD HT, Austin says, because one kiln run earns both, and WWPA inspectors read the kiln charts and meter the boards every month. S-DRY usually means the board reached 19 percent some other way. One WWPA mill near Fairbanks, Alaska, dries its lumber in the yard over months and stamps it S-DRY.

The code matters because wood keeps moving after it’s stamped. It shrinks as it dries and swells as it takes on moisture, almost all of it across the width and thickness of the board and very little along its length. Conway puts the change at about 1 percent across the width and thickness for every 4 points of moisture. On the way from 19 percent down to the moisture of a heated room, that comes to about 1/8 inch on a 2×4 or 2×6 and about 1/4 inch on a 2×10 or 2×12.

Framing that does that shrinking after it’s nailed in place loosens around its fasteners and moves the finishes attached to it. Nail pops in drywall, cracks in plaster, open joints and uneven floors are the common signs of a frame that shrank too much after it went up. A board stamped S-GRN starts wetter, so it has far more shrinking ahead of it, and what it hasn’t dried off by the time it’s nailed in place, it loses in the wall.

Match the moisture code to where the board is going. For anything inside a heated house, buy KD or S-DRY. Buy S-GRN only where movement can’t hurt anything (temporary bracing, for instance), because it will keep moving.

The big-box chains often order No. 2 with in-house limits on wane and skip (a patch the planer didn’t touch), Austin says. Mills flag those orders with words like “Premium” or “Top Choice,” which ALSC allows only if they sit at least 6 inches from the grade stamp, and such a mark can’t borrow a grade name from the rule book the board was graded under. One mill had to drop an “APP” mark, Austin says, because APP is the rule book’s own name for an appearance-limited grade.

None of those words changes the grade or says anything about strength, and the same word on a shelf tag is the store’s own, not the mill’s. What the word buys you is fewer patches of wane and skip, which matters for looks and for nailing. A stamp can also carry a surfacing note beside the five fields, like the “1/4 EE” on the boards photographed here; it isn’t one of the five and doesn’t change the grade. Read past all of it to the grade stamp, and never take “Premium” as a strength claim.

So never rip down a joist, rafter or header to make it fit. Buy the size the span table allows instead, and if a ripped piece has to count as graded lumber, ask your local code official before it goes in the wall.

Often it’s the other way around. A board with clean edges and a large knot is weaker than a board with wane and no knots, because wane and skip are appearance problems that don’t cost strength, while knots, holes and splits do. When Austin breaks No. 2 pieces whose only defect is wane, they test stronger than pieces carrying a knot or a hole, “every time,” he says. “I’ll buy lumber with wane before I buy lumber with large knots all day long.” That fits where the wood comes from. Wane is usually the rounded outside of the log showing on the board, and the outer rings of a trunk are its mature wood, while the weaker juvenile wood grows in the rings nearest the center.

Sight down it, because nothing on the stamp predicts warp. The grade caps how much curve and twist a board can leave the mill with, more can develop afterward, and neither says anything about strength. Austin’s test lab takes in boards as wet as 30 percent moisture, stacks them with spacer strips between the layers and lets them dry to the lab’s 12 percent before breaking them. Some warp badly and others never move, he says. “There’s no way to put on a stamp what it can do.”

So sight down each edge and each face from one end and set aside any piece with a visible curve or twist. Then stack the rest flat where it will be used, with spacer strips between the layers, and give it time to take on the room’s moisture. Austin’s lab allows a couple of weeks for stock that comes in wet; kiln-dried framing has less distance to travel, but give it time all the same. Then sight it again before you cut, and set aside anything that moved.

Most recipes serve multiple people. In Me, Myself, and Dinner, each dish serves one and only one. That's you! Because whether you live solo (almost 30% of Americans do) or are enjoying a night to yourself, sitting down to a meal should be something to look forward to.

The farmers market is my biggest hack for solo cooking. Instead of committing to a three-pound bag of carrots, you can buy just one, and choose your produce based on beauty or mere curiosity. That’s how I came up with this fall salad, comprising a single head of Little Gem lettuce and some crunchy produce, including that carrot we mentioned, cucumber, watermelon radish, and an Asian pear. Slicing those elements thinly with a mandoline and throwing them in an ice bath for a brief soak helps keep everything cold and crisp.

The dressing is equally important. I start with Kewpie mayo for its richness and savory depth—it’s made with only egg yolks and contains MSG, giving it a fuller flavor than standard mayonnaise. White miso brings even more umami, while rice vinegar keeps things bright. The result is a creamy finger-licking dressing that clings to every bite. Serve with a fried egg (or your protein of choice) and a piece of fresh bread to scoop up the leftovers. Make a double batch of dressing for dipping crudités or marinating salmon, chicken, or roasted vegetables.

Combine 1 small head of Little Gem or other crunchy lettuce, leaves separated, cut into 2"–3" pieces, 1 rainbow carrot, peeled, very thinly sliced lengthwise into ribbons with a mandoline or vegetable peeler, 1 Persian cucumber, thinly sliced, preferably with a mandoline, ½ small watermelon radish, peeled, halved, thinly sliced into half-moons, preferably with a mandoline, and ¼ medium Asian pear, thinly sliced into half-moons, preferably with a mandoline, in a large bowl of ice water. Let soak 10–15 minutes.

Meanwhile, whisk ¼ cup extra-virgin olive oil, 2 Tbsp. seasoned rice vinegar, 2 Tbsp. white miso, 2 tsp. Kewpie mayonnaise, 2 tsp. pure maple syrup, and 1 tsp. toasted sesame oil in a small bowl until smooth and emulsified (or combine in a small glass jar, cover, and shake). Transfer salad to a shallow bowl or rimmed plate. Top with a fried egg and serve with country-style bread if desired.

The layout side of design is what most creators actually struggle with. Anyone can generate an image with a prompt, but composing that image alongside text, white space, and branding elements in a way that looks intentional is a different skill entirely. Claude Design handles that composition.

Have you ever had a termite infestation? You likely didn’t realize you did until it was too late. For months or sometimes years, you go about your life without the faintest idea that something is afoot, because after all, the house looks just fine from the driveway.

By the time you notice that sagging floorboards, the structure is already compromised.

Debt is that termite infestation. You don’t realize the damage it’s doing until it’s too late. It gnaws through your income, unnoticed, until the day a job change, a market downturn or an unplanned expense reveals just how rickety the foundation has become.

More than a third of Americans earning $200,000 or more say they’re living paycheck to paycheck. Nearly a third of six-figure earners describe themselves as financially stretched, struggling, or drowning.

Physicians are not exempt from this. A high income means nothing on its own. It can open up opportunities, yes but those options feel out of reach when debt takes away the majority of your paycheck.

Student loans and a primary mortgage are fixtures of adulthood. If you’re a physician and have never had to carry these, you’re extremely lucky. Or you know something I don’t— in which case, please share your secret with the rest of us.

The median education debt for the class of 2025 was $215,000— not including undergraduate loans. Many physicians carry this into their 40s and 50s.

Nobody becomes a doctor debt-free unless family money covers the tab. In return you get to enjoy a career in medicine earning good money with plenty of lucrative options for supplementing that income.

The mortgage on a primary residence is an equally unavoidable aspect of adult finances. In return you get a place to call home and an asset that (usually) appreciates. Mortgage is debt, yes, but paying it down builds equity that would otherwise go to someone else in the form of rent.

This type of debt cannot be categorized as termites. This is the foundation you build on top of and most physicians manage it fine once attending income kicks in. There are also ways to significantly ease the burden on this type of debt.

Credit card balances, car payments beyond what a reliable car would cost, a second home bought at the top of a market, an extravagant vacation you didn’t save for but financed instead— I could go on. These are all examples of bad debt. Not because they aren’t “profitable” but also because the ROI they provide is short-lived.

Having a lake house feels great but not if you have to put up with a toxic workplace in order to pay it off. Jetting off to Switzerland feels like freedom but not if you have to skimp on your 401(k) contributions for the following years in order to pay it off.

In the second quarter of 2026, the average APR on credit card accounts that carry a balance was 22.15%, up from 21.52% in Q1. Total U.S. credit card debt reached $1.26 trillion in Q2 2026, closing in on the all-time high of $1.28 trillion set at the end of 2025. Roughly 60% of cardholders carry a balance month to month rather than paying in full.

A $15,000 balance at 22% APR costs over $3,300 a year in interest alone, money that produces nothing, builds nothing and simply vanishes. Extended across a decade of minimum payments, the total interest paid can exceed the original purchase several times over.

A 43-year-old high-earner drowning in debt posted his story on Reddit. Let’s call him Bill.

“On the outside, I probably look like a success. On the inside, I feel like I’m slowly breaking — just getting through one day at a time,” he wrote.

Him and his spouse carry close to $1 million in combined loans. Both work high-paying jobs. On paper, they look fine. In practice, their special-needs son’s therapy costs $3,000 a month. Before his diagnosis, that $3000 was the extra income they had planned to put towards paying down debt. These are the kind of curveballs life can throw at you. Out of nowhere, unannounced— thwack.

Bill tried his hand at several side businesses which failed and added more debt on top. He describes feeling trapped in a career he doesn’t enjoy with no visible exit.

Close to $1 million is debt may sound insane but in New York, D.C. or California, that number is an easy target. HCOL is no joke and Bill’s situation isn’t unique. Debt can be enticing even to the best of us.

Bill’s failure stems from the fact that he had thought nothing would ever go wrong and he would be able to pay off

the debt from the extra income. It was that years of “should be fine” income had never been forced into a written plan, so a single disruption ate the entire buffer.

That’s the sinkhole version of debt. It doesn’t look like reckless spending from the outside. It looks like a normal life that happens to be standing on ground with nothing underneath it.

We love compound interest but not when it’s on the other side. Compound interest grows our savings the same way it grows balances— with indifferent efficiency.

Every month a balance carries over at 20%+ APR, that growth compounds against the borrower. Over 20 years, that comes out to 52.83x of the original number which could easily mean the difference between a paid-off house and a large credit card bill that never seems to shrink.

Every debt payment carries two costs. The first is obvious: the dollars leaving the account each month. The second almost never gets counted, because it never shows up anywhere: opportunity cost.

The cost of what you give up when your dollars go towards debt when they could’ve gone towards something profitable.

Once the balance hits zero, that monthly cash flow can now go towards investment instead of paying a loan servicer. It can now be invested in index funds, a retirement account or a rental property that produces income instead of consuming it.

Everyone takes stress as a given. As children we’re conditioned to worry about our homework and grades, as we grow older, we worry about fitting in, dating and winning the rat race.

The joys of parenthood are symbiotic with the worries of how to raise and protect our children in a world like ours. Being alive is, in essence, being stressed.

Debt adds in another layer of stress to all of that. But this is one we usually push away to the back of our minds so much so that it stops registering as stress at all.

Anyone carrying a meaningful balance knows what I’m talking about. Unexpected car repairs make us flinch. Due dates feel heavy but they arrive and we pay; then do it again the following month. Progress feels theoretical.

A systematic review of 39 U.S. studies found that debt is consistently linked to higher rates of stress and even suicide. The authors pointed to financial strain and the pressure of collection as the major drivers. Some debt, such as a mortgage, was labeled as “secure” which proves the point that not all debt is bad.

Another study reports that over-indebted consumers have lower life satisfaction and poorer health due to a diminished sense of control.

Eventually the discomfort becomes the baseline, and that’s when people take on more debt, because an actual plan to eliminate it no longer feels realistic.

Financing makes this worse by design. Nearly every major purchase now arrives with a monthly payment option built in, because a payment feels smaller than a price tag even when the total cost is higher.

47% of Buy Now, Pay Later users have been late on at least one loan in the past year. That percentage had been rising steadily, as it was 41% in 2025 and just 34% in 2024.

More alarmingly, 54% of consumers say that they wouldn’t be able to make ends meet without these loans. 29% say they use them to pay for groceries.

Financial well-being research consistently finds that experiences with debt and credit correlate directly with how secure people feel about their finances, independent of income.

So the relief once you’re on the other side? It’s exhilarating.

A Gallup study found that among recent graduates, only 25% of those with more than $50,000 in debt were thriving financially compared to 40% of debt-free grads.

Physicians who’ve finished paying off credit cards or student loans, especially after years of aggressive payments alongside PSLF, will know what I’m talking about. The financial change is expected but the psychological weight lifting is what often catches you off guard.

If you want to experience that relief sooner rather than later, stick to aggressive repayment and steer clear of financing anything marketed by its monthly payment instead of its total cost.

Debt is sneaky like that. It can make you feel like you’re living your best life, doing better than ever— doing better than those around you. It’s a costly little dog and pony show.

Home equity products are the easiest way to explain this. A physician spends years building equity in a primary residence, then starts seeing offers to unlock that equity through a HELOC (home equity line of credit) or cash-out refinance for a kitchen remodel, debt consolidation, etc.

The homeowner is essentially being gaslit into converting equity that already belongs to them into a new long-term loan that accrues interest all over again.

These ventures can make sense in rare, high-planned situations tied to high-returns but not in cases where the motive is discretionary. Saving for a large purchase takes longer than financing it yes, but it also provides security which a new, bigger loan just cannot.

No matter how fancy it looks. Debt servicers know how to market their products well. They disguise shackles as flexibility, but really they’re coming for the wealth you’ve already built while claiming to make more for you.

Your home equity is your net worth, not spendable cash. Unless “unlocking it” funds something that generates income or measurable value.

A fee-only advisor is your best friend when it comes to dealing with offers for HELOC or cash-out refinancing. Before you decide anything, compare the total interest cost to how much you could save in 12–24 months for the same purchase before signing anything. The number might surprise you.

Debt is not a moral failing. Nobody builds a career in medicine— or anything for that matter,  without borrowing something along the way. But building wealth that lasts (preferably for generations) requires you to differentiate between good and bad debt.

Student loans, a reasonable mortgage and car payments aside, if you find yourself dreading the monthly payments you know will eat chunks out of your salary, maybe a new plan is what you’re missing.
