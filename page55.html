#4395. Take it on the chin SatNad: Microsoft chief exec's pay slips 6% in record year

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Revenue up, operating profit up, 30 million paid Copilot seats locked in, 88 DCs added, $43.8 billion returned to shareholders

Sometimes you’ve just got to roll with the punches. Microsoft chairman and CEO Satya Nadella was knocked down recently: his total compensation for the corporation’s fiscal 2026 is 6 percent lighter than it was in the prior year.

The leader of the software and cloud biz pocketed $90.6 million, including a $2.5 million base salary, $77.6 million in stock awards, $10.3 million non-equity incentive plan compensation and $259,203 in all other compensation (chiefly security for personal travel, charitable gifts and pension contributions).

As Reg readers are no doubt be aware, stock vests at a different rate annually and in the latest full-year Nadella was awarded a lower figure, despite breezing through all of his financial and operational targets.

Revenue for Microsoft's fiscal 2026 ended June 30 was up 18 percent to $331.8 billion and operating profit was 18 percent higher - at $155.237 billion - than SatNad's $130.96 billion target. Together, these areas accounted for 70 percent of the bossman’s remuneration goals. Operational assessment including security, strategic transformation and culture made up the rest.

The executive pay scale was outlined in the Notice of Annual Shareholder Meeting and Proxy Statement 2026.

Other highlights in that financial year included a 27 percent bounce in revenue generated by the Cloud division to $214.4 billion. Microsoft added 88 datacenters to its portfolio, and had 30 million paid Copilot seats.

"The scale of adoption reflects growing customer confidence in Microsoft's ability to deliver enterprise-ready AI capabilities integrated directly into existing workflows. Microsoft also delivered 625 Copilot updates during the year, reflecting its rapid pace of innovation and commitment to customer feedback," Microsoft claimed.

"Large-scale enterprise deployments accelerated across industries, with the number of customers deploying Copilot broadly across their organizations continuing to increase as Copilot evolved from an AI assistant into a platform for agentic work."

GitHub captured 225 million users - 50 million of these used Copilot, with one in three "pull requests involving an agent." Industry-specific AI services expanded, such as Dragon Copilot for healthcare, now used by "100,000 healthcare providers to help automate administrative and clinical workflows."

The rest of the C-suite's compensation went in the opposite direction to Nadella, and saw their packages increase, though they aren't nearly as well off as the CEO, who got the top job in 2014. CFO Amy Hood, was awarded $33.7 million, up from $29.5 million. The beancounting bigwig returned $43.8 billion to shareholders in the 12 months.

Judson Althoff, CEO of Microsoft’s Commercial Business was awarded $33.4 million, and chief marketing officer Takeshi Numoto - the person that also works on product bundles - was awarded $15.7 million. President Brad Smith was compensated to the tune of $33 million.

Like we’ve said before, it’s tough at the top. Nadella’s pay was equivalent to the average pay ($215,057) of 420 Microsoft employees.

Still, the pressure of running one of the biggest organizations on the planet must be a big load to carry, especially when Microsoft is spending hundreds of billions on AI infrastructure and looking to find massive cost savings by erasing up to a quarter of the workforce.

The annual shareholder meeting is scheduled for December 8 when investors will get a chance to have their say on exec pay. 91 percent voted in favor last year so it seems unlikely any dissenting voices will be heard at the upcoming event.  ®

#3579. fiat’s tiny multiplina brings 1950s italian microcar design into the electric age

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

inspired by the 1956 fiat 600 multipla, the electric concept fits four passengers into a retro-inspired body at just under ten feet long.

Fiat has presented its Multiplina concept at the 2026 Paris Motor Show, introducing a tiny electric four-seater inspired by the original 1956 Fiat 600 Multipla. Measuring just over three meters (9.8 feet) long, the new concept revisits an idea that shaped some of the Italian automaker’s most inventive early vehicles: how much space can a car offer while taking up as little room as possible? With its rounded body, cheerful orange paintwork and contrasting cream roof, the Multiplina brings the familiar character of Fiat’s small city cars into a new generation of electric mobility.

The reference is the original Fiat 600 Multipla, which managed to accommodate six passengers within a body measuring only 3.5 meters long. Introduced seventy years ago, the unusual little people carrier challenged conventional automotive proportions by pushing the passenger compartment forward and making efficient use of its compact footprint. The Multiplina follows a similar approach, with an upright windshield and a short front end that give most of the vehicle’s length over to its occupants. Alongside the Multiplina, Fiat also unveiled its Multiplay concept in Paris — check it out here.

The Fiat Multiplina takes the shape of a small, almost continuous volume, with its cream-colored upper section wrapping around the windows and extending toward the front. Below, the orange bodywork has gently inflated surfaces and short overhangs. The wheels sit close to the corners, emphasizing the generous cabin relative to the vehicle’s size. Round, color-coordinated wheel covers reference Fiat’s earlier small cars, while the tiny rectangular headlights introduce a contemporary detail to the otherwise retro appearance.

A few recognizable elements connect the concept more directly to the 1956 Multipla. The horizontal lines across the front recall the original car’s distinctive moustache-like treatment, while similar ribbed detailing appears across the rear hatch. Vertical oval taillights sit at either edge of the body, leaving the rear surface largely uninterrupted. Along the sides, expansive windows reveal striped seating inside, and a rolled fabric roof adds another familiar touch from Fiat’s open-air city cars.

Despite its small proportions, the Multiplina accommodates four passengers across two rows of seats. It measures 1.4 meters (4.6 feet) wide and 1.53 meters (5 feet) high, dimensions that place it close to Fiat’s two-seat Topolino, although the additional length makes room for the rear passengers. The long side windows and forward-positioned windshield reinforce this emphasis on interior space. Even the car’s simple, upright silhouette contributes to the packaging, allowing the cabin to occupy a substantial portion of its overall volume.

The concept uses a fully electric L7 heavy quadricycle architecture, a European vehicle classification that places it between lightweight urban runabouts and conventional passenger cars. Fiat describes the proposed category as a Multi-Purpose Quadricycle, combining the compactness of a microcar with the practicality of four seats. The company has yet to announce the Multiplina’s battery capacity or driving range, although the concept is intended to offer greater versatility than the smaller Topolino.

The Multiplina forms part of Fiat’s broader approach to micromobility, which already includes the Topolino and the three-wheeled Tris. Within that family, the new concept occupies an interesting position. Its four-seat configuration makes room for passengers beyond the driver and a companion, while its dimensions remain suited to narrow city streets and limited parking space. Fiat sees potential for the vehicle in short journeys and urban commuting, where the size of a conventional car can feel excessive.

The Multiplina draws attention to how much can fit inside a small footprint, and how an approachable, slightly eccentric little car can still offer a practical response to urban mobility. For now, it remains a concept, with production details yet to be confirmed. Its proportions, however, make a convincing case for the continued relevance of the microcar.

#1096. The 10 Best Layered Hairstyles, According to the Pros

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When we’re feeling meh about our hair but don’t want to go too drastic and chop it all off (or go for the very tempting fringe), a stylist will usually recommend a layered haircut. Why? Beyond being universally flattering and easy to maintain, there are many variations to choose from. And we love options.

The best layered hairstyles should remove weight, add movement, and shape your overall look. Celebrity hairstylist Bobby Eliot says layers can create an airy, effortless vibe and are a great option if you want your hair to feel lighter. Becca Raziuddin, Mane ambassador and celebrity hairstylist, agrees and adds that layers can frame your face, highlight your favorite features, and remove unwanted parts without sacrificing length.

Below, Eliot and Raziuddin break down their favorite layers and 10 layered hairstyles for every hair texture. Save this for the next time you want to subtly change your look.

For fine hair, Eliot recommends going with minimal layering. “I generally find that keeping the layering to face framing and having minimal—if not any—layers throughout the back is generally best,” he says. “Minimal layering keeps the weight at the bottom, which gives the illusion of fuller hair.”

Raziuddin also loves soft, long layers for fine hair. “They create movement while keeping the ends looking full,” she says. “Too many layers can make fine hair appear thinner, so less is definitely more.”

Eliot says wavy hair depends on the shape you want and your curl pattern. He likes keeping your natural texture and putting layers throughout. “It creates a flowy airiness that can give wavy hair a lot of movement,” he says.

Raziuddin also likes long, blended layers for wavy hair to enhance your natural texture. She says they will also add definition without creating too much bulk. Eliot adds that blended layers can also benefit straight hair, since it’s easy to see every layer cut in straight strands. “Blending is key with straight hair,” he says.

For naturally straight hair, Raziuddin likes a butterfly cut. “It adds movement and dimension, keeping the hair from looking too flat or heavy,” she says.

Both Eliot and Raziuddin say natural curly and coily hair should pair with layers that give your natural texture a rounder shape. “I generally avoid anything that looks triangular or shapes that create extra width at the bottom,” says Eliot.

Raziuddin agrees: Look for cuts that create shape and let curls fall naturally. “The key is cutting with the curl pattern and shrinkage in mind to avoid unwanted volume or uneven lengths.”

According to Eliot, thick hair can handle many layers. The key is making sure they create movement. “Undercutting works really well for thick hair,” he says. “That’s where you take a section or a panel of hair and have another panel of hair lay over it. This can remove extra unnecessary weight, create the illusion of less hair, and also create movement.”

Eliot says that with short hair, you should lean into your layers to emphasize your cheekbones and other natural features. Raziuddin agrees, saying a layered pixie can add movement and dimension. Plus, it’s easy to style.

Another layer cut that Raziuddin loves for short hair is the textured bob. It adds movement and dimension and gives you that effortless feel.

For mature hair, soft, face-framing layers are always a safe choice. “They add movement and volume around the face without removing too much density,” says Raziuddin,“especially if the hair has become finer over time.”

Eliot recommends trimming layers every six to eight weeks to maintain their shape. Raziuddin adds that shorter, more structured layered cuts may need trimming sooner, so keep that in mind when getting your hair layered.

What are your best tips for caring for layers?

“It’s best to see a hairdresser who is knowledgeable in bone structure and knows shapes that are suitable for you specifically and your hair type,” says Eliot. I recommend always getting a proper consultation and showing images [of the layers you want and on hair] that are similar to your hair.”

Hydration is also key. Raziuddin recommends using something like The Mane Hydration Hug Leave-In Conditioner to keep your layered ends soft, smooth, and manageable. We also like the K18 HeatBounce Conditioning Heat Protection Spray to help protect layers from heat styling or the Briogeo Farewell Frizz Rosarco Milk Leave-In Conditioning Spray to keep layers looking smooth.

#2949. Week 18 on the CDT

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Elevation gain since last update: 4,028m/13,215ft

Total elevation gain so far: 117,110m/384,219ft (equivalent of 13.2 Everests)

The Land of Enchantment lives up to it’s moniker and has some amazing landscapes, geology, and history. El Malpais is a very unusual area of lava flow fields which the CDT passes through, tough on the feet but amazing to explore, particularly the caves that have been carved out by ice over the last few thousand years.

There is also real history in this area, with relatively untouched ruins of the Pueblo culture from around 1200AD that can be found not far from the trail.

With the terrain being so flat, the miles seem to come much easier here, and it takes no time at all to log 10 or 15 miles before lunchtime. A day of 25 to 30 miles is fairly relaxed, and I had my biggest day so far at 53 miles! Feet are doing well, a little sore and achy after a big day of walking on roads, but ready to go again after a good night’s rest. This means the remaining mileage to go is coming down rapidly, with less than 500 miles to go to the Mexico border.

The weather in this section has been very unpredictable, and not at all what I expected in New Mexico. Significant rainfall has turned many of the trails into streams, and the mud roads into giant slip and slides! Temperatures during the day are nice and warm, but we have also had a few nights where it’s dipped below freezing, waking up to ice on my tent.

One day in particular I found myself sheltering under a bush from a hail shower, while lightning strikes the hills all around me. Bright sunshine to the West, and a giant rainbow to the East, all at the same time.

Beyond the town dogs that are very protective of their territory, the wildlife in the desert is much more elusive. Butterflies float around the cactus plants, while insects scuttle around in the sand. There are also tarantulas wandering the trails, so I will not be cowboy camping in the desert!

At night, the calls of Cayotes float across the air, although I’ve yet to see them in person.

There are long stretches between towns and trails where the only option is a road walk. Sometimes these are little more than dirt tracks, sometimes proper highways with asphalt surface. Drivers are generally kind and slow down to move over when passing though, and the roads around here are very quiet, often only seeing a couple of cars an hour.

The long walks along these sections do get monotonous, particularly when they are so straight and flat, with little to see. The next section should get a little more interesting as we follow the Gila river, especially after the recent heavy rains!

#4089. When 'Someone' No Longer Means Human

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

We may soon need a follow-up question when somebody says, “I met someone.”

Once, the natural response might have been “Where?” In a not-too-distant future, it may be “Human?”

For most of history, that question would have sounded absurd. A face belonged to a person; a voice came from a body; conversation implied another consciousness somewhere at the other end. Technology mediated encounters while leaving the categories intact: humans on one side, human-made things on the other. That boundary is becoming less dependable. A machine can enter a factory on two legs, use tools designed for human hands, and work in spaces built around human geometry. Another machine has no body at all, yet can appear on any screen, remember earlier exchanges, respond to emotion, advise, reassure, coach, and flirt. One increasingly resembles us physically; the other socially. As both become more familiar, the consequential question is what repeated encounters with them do to our expectations of one another—and eventually to our sense of being human.

Humanoid robots borrow our physical form largely because the world already fits it. Stairs, shelves, doors, tools, and production lines are easier to navigate when a machine shares roughly our proportions. Progress is rapid, though the spectacle still outruns everyday deployment: the 2026 AI Index describes a field still defined more by industrial pilots and manufacturing ambitions than widespread use, while global data indicate that only about 7,000 humanoid robots were sold in 2025 for industrial and professional service applications. The direction of travel is striking; the present scale remains modest.

Digital humans need no legs. They include avatars, virtual influencers, synthetic presenters, conversational characters, and artificial intelligence (AI) companions whose social force comes from appearance, language, responsiveness, and, increasingly, memory. How people respond depends on much more than visual resemblance. Across 51 empirical investigations, perceived agency, embodied presence, and social role repeatedly shape whether a digital entity feels useful, credible, engaging, intimate, or unsettling. The crucial frontier, then, is not simply whether machines look like us. It is whether they begin to occupy recognizable human roles in our minds.

Human beings have always anthropomorphized. We name cars, plead with computers, and accuse printers of spite. AI gives that old instinct unusually persuasive material. There is an important distinction between human-likeness designed into a system and anthropomorphism supplied by the observer. A face, voice, or conversational style can invite the inference of intention, personality, or feeling; our minds complete the picture. Humanizing AI can make it easier to accept, while its effects vary with context and may gradually influence how we understand humanness itself.

Normality is the cause and consequence of small steps. The first conversation with a machine feels peculiar; the hundredth is habit. Novelty turns into utility, utility into habit, habit into social furniture. We do not have to believe a system is conscious for this migration to occur. We only have to start behaving around it as though some familiar social conventions apply. Already, trust, social alignment, task performance, and interaction experience seem broadly comparable between human-machine and human-human encounters. We can learn to coordinate with a machine in socially familiar ways without granting it the moral standing of a person. That gap—socially legible, functionally useful, yet morally different—may become one of the defining psychological spaces of hybrid life.

The influence runs both ways. Human-like machines enter our social world, and repeated interaction can also alter the standards by which we judge that world. A digital companion can be continuously available, patient, and calibrated to preference. Human beings cannot live on those terms. Real relationships contain interruption, disagreement, boredom, competing needs, and sentences we would rather not hear. Those frictions are important to who we are, and become. Friction conditions growth.

Identity itself is relational. Highly agreeable AI companions can foster self-reflection, affirmation, and exploration, while overly positive or incomplete feedback can also shape the self-conceptions users build. The concern becomes sharper when companionship and loneliness begin feeding one another. Over 12 months, data from more than 2,000 adults showed that weaker social connection predicted greater subsequent chatbot use, while greater social-chatbot use predicted more emotional loneliness on one measure. Without establishing simple causation, this does raise a question: If frictionless artificial relationships become an everyday benchmark, will ordinary human relationships start to feel needlessly demanding?

Thus far, humans still seem to prefer human empathy to artificial versions of it. Yet as artificial sociability improves, this distinction may become ever easier to overlook. The main risk may not be mass confusion about whether a counterpart is biologically human or software-driven.

Today's biggest danger might be the gradual recalibration of what we expect from relationships: instant gratification, endless patience, affirmation on demand, personalization without reciprocity.

When artificial companions become more accommodating, humans by comparison can begin to seem inefficient. Social norms are a result of accumulated habits of a critical mass of people.

One practical response is strikingly low-tech. As machines become better at reproducing the signals of humanity, we can become more deliberate about exercising the capacities from which those signals acquired their meaning.

Give another person something an artificial companion can reproduce in form but cannot personally risk: your attention. Listen beyond the moment when you already know what you want to say. Stay with an inconvenient difference. Call when messaging is easier. Help when there is no return. Be kind.

Being human is a biological fact. Being humane is a practice—can we make it the new normal?

Shopify’s Help Center states that the Merchant Admin knowledge base (Sales channels > Agentic > Knowledge base) is used to hold all non product related information an AI agent may need in order to answer customer inquiries regarding a specific store (e.g., Return Policy, Shipping Times, Brand Values). Information developed through a storefront scan will remain unpublished until the merchant decides to publish those items. A further explanation is also provided within the Help Center indicating that facts associated with the admin are published when Shopify creates them. Merchants are able to publish/unpublish all items at one time, a complete category or a specific field.

Merchants that have more than 1,000 facts in the Knowledge Base app did not have their facts transferred into the new database. The company is stating that these larger retailers must enter the facts they wish to retain on their own before Jan. 15, 2027. In addition, the company stated that it had sent email notifications directly to these merchants.

For smaller retailers, all facts entered/edited by merchants in the app were transferred automatically to the new database and published, and require no action by the merchant.

According to the Help Center, there is a maximum number of facts that can be held within the new page equaling 1,000 facts (both generated facts and facts written by merchants). Conversely, the changelog refers to this limitation as 1,000 additional facts.

The app’s metrics page includes a query log of customer queries submitted to AI-based shopping assistants, details on which agent responded to each customer’s question and whether or not the agent was able to answer it. The company’s Managing agentic storefronts webpage lists sales, orders, sessions and conversions by AI channel; additionally, a product search preview is available. However, neither this page nor the new Knowledge Base Help Page list a record of customer questions about the store.

Admin defaults to publish the store’s policies and contact details for agents to use when they need to find that out on their own using the Storefront MCP prior to anyone looking at this new page. The Help page for the older version of the app states that it enhances the accuracy of AI generated responses regarding the specific store; however, it does not impact how many times the store will be shown in an AI Platform result.

Because there isn’t a dedicated knowledge base category for cancellations, you can add any terms missing from a store’s policies as an additional fact. Hunt also recommends testing the policy tool with questions from buyers, a check that doesn’t rely on the old app’s query log.

Shopify’s developer resources indicate that the Storefront MCP tool (which is used for policies/FAQs) has been marked as deprecated, although still functional. They direct developers to Ask MCP. The ask_business tool provides answers to a buyer’s question based on a store’s knowledge base and policies. Shopify describes Ask in their documentation as a “draft” capability of the Ask MCP and notes the potential variability in availability of this feature across different stores.

The dinosaur market has produced another monster sale.

“Jodi,” a 67-million-year-old Nanotyrannus skeleton, sold Tuesday at Sotheby’s New York for $47.3 million, including fees, more than five times its $9 million high estimate. The skeleton hammered at $40.5 million, making it one of the most expensive dinosaur fossils ever sold at auction.

#4532. Revisiting Ecstatica - the pioneering survival horror that used its fancy ellipsoid graphics tech to fill its world with dongs

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There's a well-known phenomena in video games development known as time-to-penis. It's a somewhat tongue-in-cheek measurement of the time players, when given an exciting suite of new customisation tools, will take to create the first inevitable in-game dong. Ecstatica sort of implies it works the other way round too. That developers - when giddy with a new tech toy - can't always help themselves either. But maybe I'm getting a little ahead of myself.

Ecstatica, developed by Andrew Spencer Studios and published by the long-defunct Psygnosis, originally released back in 1994. It follows a bumbling traveller who unwittingly becomes embroiled in the fate of a demon-cursed medieval village after making a rest stop in search of water. It was heavily inspired by survival horror game granddaddy Alone in the Dark (and arrived a full year before Resident Evil), but while it adopted a similar fixed-camera approach to its adventure, it had some tricks all of its own.

Most immediately notable was the decision to build its pre-rendered backdrops and characters out of ellipsoids; giving the medieval village of Tirih and its surrounding environs a softer, more organic feel compared to the angular lines afforded by the limited polygons of the time. And even today its bold, vibrant, and decidedly spherical aesthetic is quite unlike anything else.

Ecstatica isn't, I should say, new to me. I first played it back on its original release. And it's a game that never quite left my mind; in large part, I'll admit, because of the relative proliferation of dick, boob, and bulge. Faced with the infinite potential ellipsoids could offer, Andrew Spencer Studios' urge to penis was perhaps inevitable, and, lo, the moment our tights-wearing hero dismounts his horses in the opening cinematic, neither his bulbous front bauble or two–giant-eggs-in-a-hanky posterior leave anything to the imagination. And if you opt for the alternative lady hero, well, she's got ample going on there too.

And so it continues: prancing fawns with mega-breasts, muscle gimps with proud chipolatas. Ecstatica really is a singular sort of horror game. But while the cheerfully perky genitalia is undoubtedly what my teenage brain chose to squirrel away in the memory vaults, I also had distant recollections of a game that was intriguing for other, more interesting reasons. So when SNEG - a publisher founded by GOG veterans and focused on reviving lost classics - announced it was re-releasing Ecstatica and its sequel in time for Halloween, I knew I had to go back.

And you know what? After spending a few happy hours revisiting Ecstatica these past few nights, I kind of feel bad I'd mostly condensed it down to its naughty bits (for starters, the pickles, plums, and melons aren't quite as frequent as I remember them). This really is a fascinating game, with a wonderfully, uniquely odd mood - playing something like a bawdy fairytale as it blends daft slapstick silliness with an effectively sinister undertone. This is a game where our hero will stop for an unprovoked pee in a bush or, after being turned into a frog, must seek a kiss from the busty woman who lives down the road. The surprisingly great voice acting, while scant, is pure British whimsy, but throughout the village: demonic destruction, gory evisceration, and crucifixion. And Ecstatica walks a tonal tightrope that somehow works.

It still looks fantastic in its own strange way too; its broad-daylight horrors are vibrantly hued, everything is exuberantly animated, and there's so much characterful incidental detail. As you roam from one jauntily angled screen to the next, searching for objects you'll need to progress - a bible dropped by a hanged priest you can read aloud at the monastery gates, or a flying broomstick you'll need to navigate a boobytrapped dungeon - you'll encounter ale-swigging bears, perpetually annoyed imps with tiny pitchforks, and peeved minotaurs. You'll trip on rocks and inelegantly face-plant; you'll smack your bonce on low ceilings; you'll excitedly put on a suit of armour only to discover it slows you to a comically useless crawl. And you can even kick a priest off the belltower if you're feeling particularly mean.

It's not your usual sort of survival horror, then, but it's got one hell of a trick up its sleeve: recurring enemies that'll readily give chase, notably including an unkillable werewolf that's essentially a precursor to the stalkers - think Resident Evil's Mr. X - beloved by modern-day horror games. You'll catch him peeking around corners as the camera switches to a new angle, he'll launch a surprise attack from the rooftops, or suddenly give chase if you disturb his murdering. And when that happens, he's relentless - the only way you'll lose him is to inflict enough damage he'll give momentary pause, or hide in a cupboard until he moves on. There's even a little stealth. It's rudimentary stuff by today's standards, not helped by the unwieldy combat and tank controls, but it still manages to muster a whiff of tension. And it's remarkable to rediscover something so pioneering in a title I'd mentally reduced to 'the willy game' - particularly when horror's better-known 3D stalker series, like Clock Tower, were yet to come.

Granted, Ecstatica has enough old-school jank that it's not an easy recommendation for modern players; from its wayward combat to its untelegraphed (though pretty amusing) sudden deaths. Or the inventory system, which demands you drop anything your two hands can't hold right there on the floor - so you better remember where you left it. And you're screwed if you accidentally place something in tall grass or just outside the fixed camera frame. SNEG's remaster, though, is decent, and an enjoyable way to experience this cult-classic curio.

And 'curio' is perhaps the best word to describe Ecstatica, with its pioneering survival horror spirit, its ellipsoids, and its unashamed love of slapstick, boobs, and dong. It's been an unexpected pleasure revisiting it some 30 years later, and I'm excited I'll soon have my very first brush with Ecstatica 2. All I know about the 1997 sequel is its hero isn't blessed with a prominent bauble from the off, so its time-to-penis is already looking like a longer one.
