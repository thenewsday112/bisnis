#126. How AI drones are influencing the course of the Ukraine war

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The roots of Russia's invasion of Ukraine go back decades and run deep. The current conflict is more than one country fighting to take over another; it is — in the words of one U.S. official — a shift in "the world order."Here are some helpful stories to make sense of it all.

A drone operator with the military call sign Professor operates a Lupynis-10-TFL-1 from The Fourth Law at a test site in central Ukraine. Paula Bronstein/for NPR hide caption

KYIV, Ukraine — Like many tech entrepreneurs in Ukraine, Yaroslav Azhnyuk turned to developing drone warfare after Russia's 2022 invasion.

Drones have transformed the way wars are fought. Azhnyuk says artificial intelligence is making drones faster and more capable.

"When the other side has orders of magnitudes higher capabilities, it gives you an asymmetric advantage," he says. "It can make you 10,000 times more effective."

Azhnyuk runs the Ukraine-based defense firms The Fourth Law and Odd Systems. They are among the top companies producing AI-powered drones in Ukraine. Dozens of Ukrainian military units already use their autonomous drone, called the Lupynis-10-TFL-1.

Russia is also deploying AI-enabled drones against Ukraine. AI warfare is part of a high-tech arms race that has many worried, including Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He has warned that AI could lead decisions on the battlefield as soon as next year.

Azhnyuk told NPR that he acknowledges the concern over destructive weapons but says it's not specific to AI.

"It has been with humanity since the invention of fire or steel," he says. "Any kind of tool can be used for good or for bad. And in the end, it's the human that decides how to use that tool."

Azhnyuk is 37, a gregarious computer scientist who hails from a family of scholars and scientists. NPR meets him in a conference room in Kyiv; for security reasons he asked us not to reveal much else about the location.

He worked for years in Silicon Valley and started several companies, including Petcube, which makes smartphone-powered cameras that use artificial intelligence to watch and even entertain pets while they're alone.

"These cameras allow pet parents to watch pets when they're not at home," he says, "and fling treats to them and shine a laser toy for them. We sold a million of these devices that are quite popular in the U.S."

After Russia launched its full-scale war, Azhnyuk, by then living in Ukraine, pivoted to the defense space.

"I was like, OK, I've got to move on from Petcube and do something to protect my country," he says. "And so now we're making not cameras that fling treats but cameras that fling explosives at people who came to occupy our land."

The cameras he's talking about are part of an image recognition system incorporated into first-person-view or FPV drones.

"We are leaders in autonomous drones and thermal cameras," he says.

A Lupynis-10-TFL-1 drone is seen in the air at a test site in central Ukraine. Paula Bronstein for NPR hide caption

The autonomous Lupynis TFL-1 drone combines a small, affordable FPV drone with an autonomous terminal guidance unit. That means the drone has an onboard computer and AI technology that takes control during the last 500 meters (or 0.3 miles) of the flight.

"But it's very different from, say, the kind of AI that sits on those data centers that power Claude," he says. "Or even the kind of AI that does self-driving for your Tesla."

Azhnyuk says this AI technology uses simple tools to recognize images and make smart software "which then allows you either to navigate autonomously without a human or to choose a target autonomously or to fly toward the target autonomously."

The Fourth Law's autonomous drone is named after the 20th century Ukrainian poet and political activist Anatoliy Lupynis, who endured decades of Soviet imprisonment and psychiatric torture. The company named all its weapons after Ukrainian intellectuals and artists killed or persecuted by Russians in the last century.

"It is important for us to help to resurrect those people," Azhnyuk says. "Their ideas are still with us. And now they're fighting back."

The Fourth Law teams test the Lupynis TFL-1 drones every day. We meet one team in an overgrown field outside Kyiv. They've set up a fold-out table near a copse of trees and a hornets' nest.

A slight, sandy-haired man stands up from the fold-out table and says he's the head of the test and integration team. He uses the military call sign Stab. The Fourth Law asked NPR not to reveal this location or the identities of those who work here for security reasons.

"So you see here is our camera, plus the microcomputer inside this 3-D model," Stab says. "All of the mind is here," he adds with a laugh.

The microcomputer inside the drone runs on a deep-learning neural network that uses hours of combat footage to recognize targets.

"Like vehicles, light-armored vehicles, tanks, and flying objects," he says.

The drone begins its flight guided by a pilot, who also asked us to use his call sign, Professor. He navigates the drone with a control stick. He selects a target and checks a video monitor. Then he sees a red flash.

The drone has locked onto its target: a dilapidated car an employee is driving along a dirt road. Professor lets go of the control stick. The drone flies itself to the car. The microcomputer inside constantly runs algorithms to keep it on course.

"See, Professor is doing nothing, and the drone is doing everything itself," Stab says.

On the battlefield, the drone would carry an explosive to hit the car. The test drone doesn't have one.

Stab says AI drones are useful because they're hard to jam. He also insists safeguards are in place so The Fourth Law drones won't go rogue. Humans select the target, he says.

Yaroslav Azhnyuk, the founder of the AI drone company The Fourth Law, previously worked in Silicon Valley and started several companies, including one that makes smartphone-powered cameras that use artificial intelligence to watch and entertain pets while they're alone. Paula Bronstein/for NPR hide caption

Back in the conference room, Azhnyuk says his company's AI-powered drones are not killer robots.

"They're very far from a Terminator kind of scenario, right?" he says. "These are just sort of calculators. Like they're no more likely for a Terminator uprising than a warehouse with artillery shells."

He does acknowledge, though, that AI is developing quickly. "It's not unthinkable," he says, "to imagine that some years in the future there will be drones that will be able to make their own decisions on designating targets or not."

That's the future. Right now he is focused on what Ukraine needs today.

"Our adversaries are using the very same technologies, and if they use AI for decision-making in an offensive scenario, and if we refuse to use AI in decision-making in a defensive scenario, we've lost, right?" he says. "Because their systems are capable of making decisions in microseconds, and human control takes minutes, hours and sometimes days."

If Ukraine loses, he says, "there's no way for us to argue about the ethics of AI."

The most important thing, he says, is don't die.

NPR's Polina Lytvynova contributed to this story from Kyiv.

#55. US judge narrows Macrons' defamation lawsuit against American podcaster

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A judge in the United States has ruled that French President Emmanuel Macron and his wife Brigitte ​could continue their defamation lawsuit against the companies of the American podcaster Candace Owens, but not against Owens herself.

The Macrons had filed a lawsuit in July 2025 against Owens and two companies, accusing her of helping fan online rumours about whether Brigitte is a transgender woman.

Owens, 37, an influencer with a large following on social media platforms, has claimed that Brigitte was a man.

Delaware Superior Court Judge Sheldon Rennie said in a ruling on Tuesday that Owens could not be held personally liable in the case.

But Rennie allowed the suit against two companies associated with her – Candace Owens LLC and GeorgeTom Inc – to continue.

Rennie said the Delaware court did not hold jurisdiction over Owens because her residence was in another state, Tennessee.

The ruling means that if the French first couple were to eventually win the case, Owens' personal assets would be shielded.

In their lawsuit, the Macrons said Owens ⁠touted ​false claims and conspiracy theories ​against them "to promote her independent platform, gain notoriety, and make money".

Rennie deferred judgment on Owens' motion to dismiss ‌the ⁠Macrons' litigation until he was briefed on how French defamation law and statutes of limitation intersect with US law.

"I am in the absolute best mood over this Macron dismissal," Owens said on X on Wednesday.

'Centuries of patriarchal history': why trans rumours are wielded against women

Brigitte Macron, 73, has taken to the courts in France to combat claims she was born a man.

Two French YouTubers, Natacha Rey and Amandine Roy, were convicted in September 2024 of spreading false claims after they posted a video in December 2021 alleging that Brigitte Macron had once been a man named Jean-Michel Trogneux – who is actually her brother.

Rey and Roy were ordered to pay €8,000 in damages to Brigitte Macron and €5,000 to Jean-Michel Trogneux.

The defendants were also given a suspended fine of €500.

Did French media silence enable Brigitte Macron fake news story to go viral?

Brigitte Macron had filed a libel complaint against the two women after their claim went viral, triggering conspiracy theories notably among the far right.

Roy, an online fortune-teller, interviewed Rey, a self-described independent journalist, for four hours on her YouTube channel in December 2021.

The women discussed the unsubstantiated rumour that Brigitte Macron had undergone gender reassignment, with Rey claiming to have uncovered a  “state lie” and “scam”.

The claim went viral just weeks before France's 2022 presidential election, spreading even to the US where Owen derided the first lady in a now deleted YouTube video.

U.S. officials have told several West Asian countries and regional allies to be prepared for potential renewed military action against Iran after next week's midterm elections in the United States — as well as the possibility that Iran will attempt to retaliate against them, according to two diplomats briefed on the matter.

Both of the diplomats spoke on the condition of anonymity because they were not authorised to comment about the sensitive warnings conveyed by U.S. diplomats and defence officials.

U.S. President Donald Trump has not made a final decision on whether to carry out new strikes against Iran, the diplomats stressed. The President, however, has repeatedly said that he is weighing ordering new military action once the critical November 3 elections in the U.S. are over.

Those threats and the fresh U.S. urgings for allies to prepare for potential retaliation are putting the region on alert that a return to major operations could now be on the horizon.

The White House and U.S. officials declined to comment.

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said on Wednesday (October 28, 2026) in a post on X that Tehran “continues to give diplomacy a chance to succeed” and is working with mediators to review ideas that Tehran included in a proposal floated last month — summarily rejected by Mr. Trump — that the Iranians still want to see used as the basis to return to a ceasefire.

Meanwhile, the United Arab Emirates Foreign Ministry issued a statement urging the sides to “exercise wisdom and pursue political solutions to help contain tensions” and prevent a return to full-scale operations in the war that began in late February.

The U.S. has told regional allies that the new round of military action could last for several days, but regional officials are bracing for Iran and its proxy groups to retaliate — leading to a fight that could last even longer, the diplomats said.

The allies have also been urged by U.S. officials to take precautionary measures in anticipation that Iran's response will include retaliatory strikes on energy facilities and other crucial infrastructure.

It's unclear how soon the U.S. military would be ready

While the U.S. has not carried out a strike against Iran since early September, the situation in West Asia has become more complicated.

The conflict has spread, with Iran-backed Houthi rebels carrying out attacks on Saudi Arabia's oil infrastructure and airports. A Saudi-led coalition has responded with its own deadly attacks on Houthi-held territory in Yemen.

The Wall Street Journal reported earlier on Gulf nations being briefed to be prepared for potential escalation by the U.S.

It's unclear how soon after Election Day that the U.S. military would be ready to potentially resume strikes.

The Pentagon late last month deployed the USS Theodore Roosevelt, along with a series of amphibious landing ships with Marines aboard — roughly 9,000 troops in all — from San Diego to the Persian Gulf to bolster the U.S. military in the region.

At the time, one U.S. official said this deployment could lead to an unusually high naval presence of more than 20,000 sailors and Marines, as well as hundreds of aircraft, in the region, which has only occurred a handful of times in the last two decades. The Navy had three carrier strike groups in West Asia in April for the first time since 2003.

Mr. Trump, who has focused in recent weeks on campaigning for Republicans ahead of the November 3 elections, has been evasive about what he plans to do next with Iran.

The war has proved to be a heavy weight on Republican candidates as they make their case to voters who are fatigued by rising gasoline prices, a byproduct of the war that's driven up costs across nearly every sector in the global economy.

The cost of fighting the Iran war to American taxpayers has grown to $47.1 billion, according to the latest estimate the Pentagon has provided to Congress.

While campaigning for Republicans, Mr. Trump has consistently assured voters that the war’s end is near, while also threatening that a new round of bombing could be coming once the election passes.

During a campaign stop in Lubbock, Texas, on Tuesday (October 27, 2026), Mr. Trump said a conclusive end to the eight-month-old conflict could be coming “in a couple of weeks, if you don't mind.”

“Somebody asked me before, when will the war in Iran end?” Mr. Trump said earlier in the same address. “I said, Whenever I want it to end.’ It’ll be very quick, but we have to have it end properly, with total and complete — a total statement, they will never have a nuclear weapon.”

Mr. Trump may be looking at a postelection timeline for possible action, but the region is on edge that Tehran could do something provocative before then — especially if they feel U.S. actions is imminent, said Jonathan Schanzer, executive director at the Foundation for Defence of Democracies, a hawkish Washington think tank.

“Why would they wait until after the elections here ... if they could initiate it and potentially gain the upper hand?” he said.

#729. Your fast guide to the US midterms and how they work

On November 3, Americans will head to the polls for midterm elections that could have big ramifications for the second half of Donald Trump’s presidency.

All seats in the US House of Representatives and a third of those in the Senate come up for grabs.

The results will determine whether the Democrats can neuter Trump’s agenda or if he will maintain his grip on America until the presidential election in November 2028.

The elections, which are on track to be America’s most expensive ever, are being fought on everything from the cost of living to the war on Iran, artificial intelligence and data centres, abortion rights, the immigration crackdown, and, of course, the president himself.

The US midterms take place on the Tuesday after the first Monday of November, as stipulated in a law passed 181 years ago. They are held midway between presidential elections to determine the make-up of Congress, which comprises both the House of Representatives (which has two-year terms) and the Senate (six-year terms).

The Trump administration narrowly controls Congress. In the House of Representatives, the Republicans hold a five-seat majority, with 219 seats (including an independent who votes Republican) against the Democrats’ 214. In the Senate, Republicans have 53 seats versus the Democrats’ 47 (including two independents who vote Democrat).

Come November, all 435 seats in the House (the lower chamber of Congress) are up for grabs. In addition, a total of 35 Senate seats (the upper chamber) are in play, which comprises a third of all Senate seats, plus two seats vacated by prominent Republicans JD Vance (who became vice president) and Marco Rubio (who became secretary of state). In addition to Congress, 36 out of 50 states will have gubernatorial elections on the same day.

To take control of the House, the Democrats need to pick up at least six more seats (218 seats in total); in the Senate, they need to win a net four seats (51).

Polling suggests Democrats are on track to win the House easily, and possibly the Senate.

That’s largely because the unpopular war on Iran and high inflation have pushed Trump’s popularity to the lowest point in his political career. Trump started his second term with an approval rating of 47 per cent, but the latest Reuters/Ipsos poll found his approval rating had slumped to 32 per cent. The same poll found 43 per cent of registered voters planned to vote for the Democrats in these midterms, and 35 per cent for the Republicans – the biggest lead for the Democrats this year.

The Democrats winning control of both houses would frustrate Trump’s agenda for the final two years of his presidency, plus revive the party ahead of the 2028 presidential campaign. “A win is now measured based on how many seats Democrats pick up rather than getting to a balance or status quo,” says Cory Alpert, who worked in the Biden-Harris administration.

Alpert expects young Democrat senator Jon Ossoff to beat Republican Mike Collins in the battleground state of Georgia, and Democratic nominee Roy Cooper to defeat Michael Whatley in North Carolina.

“I think races in Texas, Ohio, Alaska, and Michigan are all toss-ups, which in this environment probably favour the Democrat in the race. Florida, South Carolina, Kansas, Iowa and Nebraska are on my watch list for potential flips.

“If we see any of those flip on the night, it’s a Democratic wave election.”

Texas is a key battlefield, with former teacher and seminarian James Talarico hoping to become the first Democrat elected to the US Senate from the conservative state since 1988. Talarico, dubbed “Talafreako” by Trump, is up against the president-endorsed state Attorney-General Ken Paxton. “If Talarico wins, it’s a banner year for Democrats,” Alpert says. “If Paxton wins, the margin will tell us a lot.”

In the swing state of Michigan, epidemiologist and Democrat progressive Abdul El-Sayed – nicknamed “Mohamed” by Trump – is up against Republican Mike Rogers for a Senate seat. Emma Shortis, a senior researcher at The Australia Institute think tank, says former president Joe Biden’s support of the Netanyahu government in Israel is “one of the key fault lines of the Democratic Party. The result in Michigan will tell us a lot about the ideological make-up and political momentum of the party ahead of the next presidential election.”

It’s lawyers at 10 paces in Alaska, a state Trump won in 2024. This time around, Democrat Mary Peltola is up against incumbent Republican senator Dan S. Sullivan and a similarly named unaffiliated candidate called Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. There is also another long-shot candidate named Gerald Heikes.

Last week, the US Department of Justice filed a lawsuit requesting Alaska not to certify its Senate results if Peltola wins, arguing that having two Dan Sullivans on the ballot threatened the “integrity” of the vote count. Peltola’s team responded by asking a federal court to dismiss the Trump administration’s lawsuit, describing it as baseless and raising “profound constitutional concerns”.

Ohio is home to a hotly contested Senate race, with incumbent Republican Jon Husted neck and neck with Democrat Sherrod Brown. But it’s the battle for the Ohio governorship that has produced international headlines. In an attack ad, Republican Vivek Ramaswamy featured the estranged mother of his Democratic rival, Amy Acton, who was allegedly molested by her stepfather as a child. (The mother called her daughter a liar). The New York Times called it the “most ruthless ad of the midterms”.

Then there’s Maine, which has something of a maverick streak when it comes to politics.

The Democrats initially picked oyster farmer Graham Platner to take on veteran Republican senator Susan Collins, until an allegation of sexual assault (which Platner denies) killed his candidacy. Former Maine Senate president Troy Jackson is trying to snatch the Senate seat from his five-term Republican rival Collins.

#580. Iran targets art, sports events where women defy hijab rules

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As growing numbers of Iranian women appear in public without headscarves, authorities are pursuing organizers of cultural and sporting events over alleged violations of mandatory hijab rules.

Tehran’s prosecutor’s office announced this week that it had opened a case against the organizers of Tehran Art Fair, a privately run event held at the Iran Mall commercial complex from September 20 to 23, citing “failure to observe legal and religious requirements.”

The organizers have been summoned to court, while the fair’s ticketing website and social media account have been blocked on the prosecutor’s orders.

The announcement did not specify which activities at the event, which featured visual arts, music, theater and performance art, were considered violations.

Images and videos showed numerous female visitors without headscarves, as well as women performing without hijab.

In one performance, two women sat on the floor as red yarn was wrapped around them in a spiderweb-like pattern. Their bodies were then decorated with flowers before two other women cut the threads with scissors, freeing them.

Another video circulating on social media showed a woman in a long black dress that covered her body but left her head uncovered as she moved to music.

The action against the art fair came amid similar proceedings over Fitex Tehran, a sports equipment exhibition where women performed fitness routines and acrobatics to music without headscarves.

The judiciary announced Tuesday that an unnamed fitness blogger had been arrested on charges of “offending public sentiment” and “propaganda against the system.”

Videos of the performances, in which women wore long-sleeved tops and leggings but no headscarves, circulated widely on social media.

The prosecutor’s office ordered the event’s social media account closed for publishing “criminal content” and said an arrest warrant had been issued for one of the exhibition’s organizers.

The event drew criticism from conservative media. Tasnim, affiliated with the Revolutionary Guards (IRGC), condemned what it called widespread “deviations” at Fitex in an October 25 report and urged sports authorities and other institutions to intervene.

State broadcaster IRIB also described the event as an example of “deviance” and called for judicial action.

Other cultural events have faced scrutiny over the past year, including music and theater performances and Tehran Design Week, held at the University of Tehran’s College of Fine Arts.

In some cases, judicial intervention has concerned interactions between women and men rather than hijab violations.

Last month, those involved in the play Tehran, Paris, Tehran/Bridge faced prosecution after a scene showed a female actor briefly resting her head on the chest of a male actor playing her brother.

The prosecutor’s office described the actors’ behavior as “contrary to tradition and propriety.”

Authorities have also taken action against venues hosting events attended by women without hijab.

Earlier this month, the IRGC-linked Fars News Agency reported that a banquet hall in the religious city of Qom had been sealed on the prosecutor’s orders after the wife of prominent soccer player Ali Daei attended a private company conference there without a headscarf.

A judicial case was also opened against the conference organizers, and the prosecutor warned that everyone involved would face action.

Since the 2022 Woman, Life, Freedom movement, which followed the death in custody of Mahsa Amini, street-level enforcement of compulsory hijab has declined.

Increasing numbers of women now appear in public without headscarves, wearing clothing other than the long coats and chadors traditionally expected by authorities. Women also exercise in public parks without covering their hair.

But mandatory hijab rules remain in force, including in schools, government institutions and universities, while organizers of public events continue to face legal pressure.

Women’s rights activists and opponents of mandatory hijab argue that the growing visibility of women without headscarves does not amount to freedom of choice as long as legal restrictions remain.

The recent prosecutions show how authorities continue to enforce those restrictions through legal action against organizers, performers and venues, even as uncovered hair becomes increasingly commonplace in public.

Iran's two biggest soccer rivals met in their first women's derby on Wednesday, a historic occasion overshadowed by restrictions on male spectators and journalists and uncertainty over live television coverage.

Persepolis defeated Esteghlal 2-1 at Shahr-e Qods Stadium west of Tehran, marking the first-ever meeting between the women's teams of two clubs whose rivalry has dominated Iranian men's soccer for decades.

Fatemeh Rezaei and Somayeh Esmaeili scored for Persepolis in the first half before Niloufar Azimi pulled one back for Esteghlal after the break.

Female spectators were eventually allowed to attend following days of uncertainty and intervention by government officials. Men, however, remained barred from women's soccer matches under restrictions that also extended to journalists, photographers and technical personnel.

Tehran prosecutor Ali Salehi on Tuesday sent separate letters to Sports Minister Ahmad Donyamali and Iranian Football Federation President Mehdi Taj demanding that men be barred from women's soccer matches.

The restrictions covered male spectators, journalists, photographers, camera operators, security personnel and event staff. Salehi warned that prosecutors would take legal action if they were violated.

The warning followed reports of female spectators attending women's league matches without the headscarves required under Iran's mandatory hijab laws, as well as men being present at the games.

Prosecutors described the incidents as violations of Islamic regulations and public decency.

The Iranian Football League Organization had already issued a directive prohibiting men from attending women's soccer matches, including male media workers and club executives.

Farideh Shojaei, the Football Federation's vice president for women's affairs, confirmed ahead of the derby that men would not be permitted to attend.

The restrictions also raised practical questions about organizing the match, particularly the use of video assistant referee (VAR) technology, which relies on technical personnel and match officials.

The league had previously called for a male official to be removed from the VAR team for the derby, adding to uncertainty over how the technology would be operated.

The dispute over attendance came after a series of changes to the arrangements for the historic fixture.

Originally scheduled for Friday, October 23, at Kazemi Stadium, the home ground of Persepolis' women's team, the match was moved to Wednesday, October 28, at Shahr-e Qods Stadium.

The league organization cited anticipated demand from supporters and the need for better facilities, although the change also shifted the match from Iran's weekly day off to a working day.

Ticket sales were suspended twice in the days leading up to the game, while the final decision on whether female spectators could attend was referred to the Tehran Provincial Security Council.

At one point, there were concerns that the encounter could take place behind closed doors despite the anticipated interest from supporters.

Women were eventually permitted to attend following discussions involving Vice President for Women and Family Affairs Zahra Behrouz-Azar and Sports Ministry officials.

Government spokesperson Fatemeh Mohajerani also attended the match, according to Iranian media reports.

Women's soccer struggles for television exposure

The restrictions extended beyond stadium access, raising questions about how widely the first women's derby would be seen.

Iran's state broadcaster, IRIB, had announced no plans to televise the match, while reports ahead of the game indicated restrictions on broadcasts through the two clubs' own platforms.

The uncertainty contrasted with the men's Esteghlal-Persepolis derby, one of Iran's biggest sporting events, which regularly attracts large television audiences.

For the women's teams, limited broadcasting and media access also restrict opportunities to build a following, attract commercial sponsors and gain wider public recognition.

Female soccer players in Iran are required to wear mandatory hijab while competing and have long faced unequal access to sporting facilities and limited media exposure.

The first meeting between the women's teams of Esteghlal and Persepolis offered an opportunity to showcase their talents to a national audience. Instead, much of the attention ahead of the match centered on restrictions over who could attend, photograph or broadcast it.

A different picture elsewhere in the region

The restrictions surrounding Iran's first women's derby contrast with similar fixtures involving major soccer clubs elsewhere in the region, where women's competitions have attracted television coverage, government support and spectators of both sexes.

In December 2021, Turkish rivals Fenerbahce and Galatasaray held their first women's derby as a friendly match at Istanbul's Nef Stadium.

Organized as part of a campaign against violence toward women, the match was attended by male and female supporters, broadcast on national television and watched by Turkey's justice and family ministers.

Then-Justice Minister Abdulhamit Gul said afterward: "Whatever the result, women are the winners."

Saudi Arabia staged its first women's Jeddah derby between Al-Ittihad and Al-Ahli in October 2022, during the opening week of the country's Women's Premier League.

The match took place at King Abdullah Stadium, with spectators attending without gender restrictions.

Saudi women's soccer subsequently secured wider television exposure. Beginning with the 2023-2024 season, Women's Premier League matches were broadcast domestically on SSC sports channels and internationally through streaming platform DAZN outside the Middle East and North Africa.

Alia Al-Rasheed, head of women's soccer at the Saudi Football Federation, described the agreement as an opportunity to attract new audiences and inspire future generations of female players.

While Turkey and Saudi Arabia have expanded opportunities for women's soccer, Iranian female athletes continue to face mandatory hijab requirements, unequal facilities and restrictions on media exposure.

For Iran's women footballers, the first derby between the country's two biggest clubs was a milestone. But the restrictions surrounding it showed how far the women's game remains from enjoying the visibility routinely afforded to its male counterpart.
