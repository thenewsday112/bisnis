#1810. Tubi reveals ten shows led by TikTok stars as part of incubator program

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Earlier this year, Tubi announced its intention to launch original programs alongside some of the biggest stars on TikTok. Seven months later, that initiative is coming into focus.

The Fox-owned platform defined by its ad-supported video on-demand (AVOD) model has revealed the first slate of shows that will emerge out of its Creatorverse Incubator. That was the name Tubi gave to a partnership with TikTok built around long-form content. Now, as Tubi moves forward with its incubator, it is spreading its creator push across an eclectic variety of formats, genres, and tones.

The programs developed through the incubator include an animated superhero comedy (Lynnell Jones Jr.‘s Feeling Super), a gender-bending dating show (Kai Cameron‘s Kai Picks the Guy), a big-city-girl/small-town-boy love triangle (Yasmine Sahid, Alex Maystrik, and Pablo Hernandez‘s Never Have I Ever Spent the Winter with the Romano Brothers), a film-inspired horror show (Allie Tran‘s Scare Tours), and a Down Under travelogue (Marc Sebastian‘s A Great Australian Gay Sidequest). Among the ten shows greenlit by Tubi, 12 notable TikTokers are getting top billing.

TikTok helped Tubi identify creators who are ready to develop their own long-form shows, and it is expected to use its Spotlight product to promote the resulting Tubi originals. In a statement, TikTok Global Head of Entertainment and Microseries Dawn Yang highlighted the “unique perspectives” that will get serial opportunities through the Creatorverse Incubator.

“The creators we’re working with have built a following, a point of view, and a body of work that people come back for,” added Tubi VP of Creator Partnerships Kudzi Chikumbu, who is a former TikTok exec. “The Incubator is where we come in as their partner, giving them the creative freedom to grow what they’ve started. We’re proud to support them as they create their first TV shows, making ambitious work they may not otherwise be able to make and growing beyond the audiences and formats that had previously defined them.”

The Creatorverse slate covers a lot of ground, but as Chikumbu alludes to, the shows also target the categories that have become reliable audience magnets on Tubi. Horror, young adult content, and comedy have been recurring genres within Tubi’s previous creator initiatives, and the platform is doubling down on those popular genres.

Release dates for the Creatorverse shows haven’t been revealed, but don’t expect Tubi to stay idle as it rolls out its fresh slate of programming. While it turns creators into long-form showrunners, Fox is also looking for new ways to connect with rising influencers. For example, Fox-affiliated Red Seat Ventures recently announced a play targeting the newsletter industry, so Fox is committed to meeting creators wherever they want to produce their next piece of work.

#3687. 10 Art Shows to See in Los Angeles This November

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Yuntao Xu’s art show in a gas station, LA’s first gallery run for and by Black artists, iris yirei hu’s reliquaries for a dream, and April Bey’s alien tapestries of human life.

The art community in Los Angeles is digging deep into history this November, excavating and reframing it through novel juxtapositions and archival findings. Isabelle Frances McGuire tackles the Salem Witch Trials with a video game that bears uneasy relevance to our current age of creeping authoritarianism (just in time for next week’s midterms). Milano Chow draws on the archives of the LA Public Library to create ghostly architectural facades that seem at once familiar and foreign. At LACMA, an exhibition gives proper due to the city’s pioneering Brockman Gallery of 1967–90, which gave early support to scores of Black artists. And at the Brick, a retrospective brings together the two sides of Todd Gray’s long career: his commercial photos of musical acts and his post-modern tableaux, which themselves pull from archival images to construct new narratives.

Bel Ami, 709 North Hill Street, Suite #105 and #103, Chinatown, Los AngelesThrough November 28

Isabelle Frances McGuire plays with history, dusting off well-known figures and disrupting their iconic status to enhance their humanity. In Joy is a Puppet, she takes aim at the Salem Witch Trials, a dark period that has taken on increasing relevance as we slide deeper into fascism. The exhibition features a bronze statue, a trio of frogs, an assemblage of baskets, and an interactive video game in which the player takes on the role of a woman accused of witchcraft and must figure out their next move.

Vielmetter Los Angeles, 1700 South Santa Fe Avenue, #101, Downtown, Los AngelesOctober 31–December 19

Bahamian-American artist April Bey explores love, romance, and companionship in new fabric works on view at Vielmetter. These pieces represent the latest chapter in Bey’s Atlantica project, in which the artist assumes the identity of an extraterrestrial being and shares dispatches about human life with her home planet. Incorporating crushed velvet, metallic brocade, and sherpa fabric, the large-scale woven tapestries feature portraits that Bey took during a 2024 residency in the historically Black community of Orange Mound in Memphis, Tennessee, depicting scenes of queer intimacy within her opulent textile world.

Michael Kohn Gallery, 1227 North Highland Avenue, Hollywood, Los AngelesThrough December 19

Charles Arnoldi has been expanding the terrain of abstraction for almost six decades, from his early twig-and-stick constructions to his chainsaw-carved plywood works and hard-edge geometric paintings. String Theory presents paintings made between 2016 and 2019 in which networks of lyrical lines dance across the surface, their fluid energy contained by the rectilinear boundaries of the canvas. Balancing a sense of visual delight with formal complexity, these paintings are in dialogue with works by Brice Marden, Alexander Calder, and others, while branching out into compelling new directions.

Official Welcome, 672 South La Fayette Park Place, Suite 46, Westlake, Los AngelesThrough December 19

Iris yirei hu draws on dreams, ancestral knowledge, collective ritual, and elements of the natural world to ground contemporary issues within traditional frameworks. Unconscious Relics features new paintings, ceramics, and reliquary boxes adorned with images and objects that hold personal and cultural significance, originating from Tongva customs and Chinese herbal medicine. Juxtaposing these disparate symbols, hu layers various belief systems in order to create new mythologies for our current, troubled times.

Hollyhock House, 4800 Hollywood Boulevard, Barnsdall Art Park, Los AngelesThrough December 20

Milano Chow creates stark, haunting architectural facades and interiors using graphite, ink, and photo transfer. For the works in Fantasy Street, she ventured into the archives of the Los Angeles Public Library, pulling images of early-20th-century movie theaters, shops, and apartments to construct fictional buildings through ink, pencil, and photo transfer. The works are displayed in an unfurnished room in Frank Lloyd Wright’s Hollyhock House and mounted on folding screens designed by Chow, putting them in conversation with the historic home and blending fiction and reality. Alongside this exhibition, Chow curated The Lifted Veil, featuring surreal, dreamlike photographs by Deborah Turbeville from the 1970s through the 1990s that resonate with her own entrancing alienation.

Adams Square Mini Park Gas Station, 1020 East Palmer Avenue, Glendale, CaliforniaThrough January 8, 2027

Los Angeles is full of art spaces in unconventional sites — strip malls, storefronts, apartments —  and the Adams Square Mini Park Gas Station in Glendale fits right in with that tradition. Within this modest, window-lined box, architect and artist Yuntao Xu has created an installation inspired by the natural landscape surrounding the gas station that houses it. Composed of reflective fabric hung from the ceiling, fluffy textiles, and colored lights, Estranged Landscape echoes the forms of Southern California’s dramatic peaks and valleys with a campy, space-age spin.

The Brick, 518 North Western Avenue, East Hollywood, Los AngelesThrough January 16, 2027

Todd Gray’s photographic assemblages mix images of art, architecture, and the natural world in ambitious tableaux that lay bare the intertwining threads of colonialism and culture. Gray got his start as a commercial photographer, however, when he was just a teenager, photographing bands including the Doors, the Rolling Stones, and Led Zeppelin; shooting album covers for Gladys Knight and Bobby Brown; and even becoming Michael Jackson’s personal photographer. Church and State brings together these two bodies of work for the first time, alongside biographical ephemera illustrating Gray’s personal experience as a Black man coming of age in Los Angeles during the 1960s, ’70s and ’80s.

Fulcrum Arts, 544 North Fair Oaks Avenue, Pasadena, CaliforniaThrough January 23, 2027

Julie Tolentino’s wide-ranging practice incorporates performance, sound, installation, scent, and text. Fittingly, her current exhibition at Fulcrum Arts aims to “decenter the visual,” encouraging visitors to utilize their other senses, especially hearing. Eschewing a static presentation, this new installation includes cultured crystals, an auditory composition, and a performance-based drawing, creating an environment that will continue to change and develop throughout the exhibition’s run.

Los Angeles County Museum of Art, 5905 Wilshire Boulevard, Mid-Wilshire, Los AngelesThrough January 24, 2027

The Brockman Gallery was LA’s first major art gallery founded and run by and for Black artists, exhibiting the work of David Hammons, Ulysses Jenkins, Suzanne Jackson, Senga Nengudi, Betye Saar, and others early in their careers. Founded in 1967 by brothers Alonzo and Dale Brockman Davis shortly after the Watts Rebellion, the gallery served as a vital cultural and community hub in Leimert Park until its closing in 1990. This is the first large-scale exhibition dedicated to the gallery, tracing its history and legacy through over 60 artworks and archival material.

A Theater of Miracles showcases the influence of Old Master paintings in his work, including several at the Getty, such as Jacopo Bassano’s “The Miracle of the Quails” (1554), which will be on display at CAAM. With the release of Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim’s new documentary film, You Can See Everything, interest in Elizabeth Holmes has been on the rise. According to Variety, The Dropout—Hulu’s 2022 limited series starring Amanda Seyfried as Holmes amid the rise and fall of her biotech company, Theranos—saw an 186 percent increase in viewership in September ahead of the film’s release. Those numbers only continued to soar the following month. The week of the doc’s October 16 debut, viewership rose 92 percent compared to the week before, per Variety.

Fans are clearly hungry for more stories about Holmes, but does that mean The Dropout could return for a second season? Here’s what to know.

The Dropout has not been renewed for a second season and was billed as a limited series upon its release. The show’s creator and showrunner, Elizabeth Meriwether, has also expressed that she wasn’t open to giving the show another installment.

In a 2022 interview with Vanity Fair, Seyfried said that playing Holmes was “the best role I’d ever been given.” She added, “It would be another thrill of my life if I got to continue it a little bit...there’s so much more to say.”

In response to Seyfried’s comments, Meriwether said in an interview with Vulture that The Dropout had run its course. “She keeps saying that, and I keep telling her that I will not be writing a second season! She actually texted me this weekend like, ‘I hear you’ve started working on a second season.’ She’s messing with me a little bit,” she explained. “I would love to work with her and all the people who worked on this show again, but this story has gone as far as I want to go with it.”

In October 2026, Seyfried’s stance on a possible season 2 of The Dropout had changed. When asked during a red carpet interview on October 17 (the day after You Can See Everything opened in theaters) whether she was interested in another season or in meeting Holmes for a chat, she responded, “I don’t think so, but life is long.”

She added that she kept up with news about Holmes “on and off” since starring in the series. (Those who have seen YCSE will know what she really means here.)

#4380. Just to be clear: The most passive-aggressive email phrases, according to workers

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An overflowing inbox is stressful enough, but reading work emails with phrases like "Going forward" or "Not sure if you saw this" can send some workers over the edge, at least according to a new study.

ZeroBounce, an email validation platform, revealed the results of its survey of 2,000 American and European professionals on email vernacular. Specifically, the survey examined common email phrases and whether they are perceived as passive-aggressive.

The phrase that topped the list of most passive-aggressive email sayings? "Just to be clear." That statement was cited as the most passive-aggressive phrase by 21 percent of respondents, followed by "Per my last email" (14 percent), with "Kindly note" and "As previously discussed" tied for third place at 10 percent each.

Other hated sayings included "Just checking in," "Not sure if you saw this," "Going forward," "For future reference," and "Friendly reminder."

ZeroBounce also asked respondents what passive-aggressive phrases they still use in their workday. Seen as necessary evils, apparently, "Thanks in advance," "Following up," "Just checking in," and "Any updates" were at the top of that list.

"People may not intend to sound passive-aggressive when they write an email," Liviu Tanase, founder and CEO of ZeroBounce, said in a statement. "But some phrases have picked up so much baggage that the reader perceives light hostility. 'Just to be clear' is a perfect example. It sounds neutral, but more than half of the professionals surveyed read something else into it."

The survey also examined phrases that go beyond being irritating and are instantly stress-inducing. The most stressful email phrases are "Need this ASAP" (56 percent), "Can we chat?" (40 percent), "Action required" (34 percent), and "Let's take this offline" (27 percent).

Trump Says He Will Ban the Term ‘Artificial Intelligence’

President Trump wants to rebrand artificial intelligence, presumably to improve public perception of the data centers he wants to build everywhere.

“When will The Wall Street Journal start calling Artificial Intelligence (AI), a very bad and inaccurate name, SI (Super Intelligence), the name which virtually everyone else is using, or soon will be,” President Trump wrote on Truth Social today. “In short order, we will be officially BANNING the term, ‘AI/Artificial Intelligence,’ in all relevant documents, and everything else, due to its inaccuracy. Thank you for your attention to this matter. President DONALD J. TRUMP.”

Artificial intelligence (AI) comes up a lot here on PetaPixel. Nearly all modern cameras use some form of AI in their autofocus systems; photo and video editing software uses AI for all sorts of tasks; and, not for nothing, AI poses an existential threat to some creative professionals. If President Trump has his way, the term AI will go away, replaced by “SI,” or “super intelligence.”

While the President of the United States may have significant influence over how government documents describe certain things, including the “relevant documents” he mentions, he doesn’t have the power to determine “everything else.”   It’s also unclear precisely who the President means by “virtually everyone else,” as hardly anybody uses the term “super intelligence,” not even many tech executives with skin in the game.

While President Trump did sign an executive order last month directing the executive branch to use “SI” instead of “AI,” as The Hill reports, it has done virtually nothing to move the needle with the American public.

The President also described anyone who uses the term “artificial intelligence” as an “enemy,” claiming that the term “artificial intelligence makes intelligence sound fake.”

“It is not fake,” Trump continued. “It’s actually amazing.”

“Following the Historic White House Accord on Super Intelligence, where the World’s most important Technology Companies came together, and confirmed their responsibilities to the American People, I am announcing the formation of the Super Intelligence Force (SIF). The Super Intelligence Force is tasked with coordinating the effort of the Federal Government to ensure that America continues to lead the World in Super Intelligence, which many say is bigger than the Industrial Revolution, and the Internet, and will protect the interests, and improve the lives, of all Americans,” the President wrote on October 4.

Meanwhile, while AI gets a White House-approved makeover, Americans are growing more opposed to data centers, a hot topic ahead of next week’s midterm elections. Would people really be more in favor of SI data centers? Maybe if they were something really cool and fun like “super-duper mega ultra data centers deluxe 2.0,” then people would be happy to get on board.

#2897. Salkantay Trek: Machu Picchu and Much More

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

October usually spells the end of the alpine hiking season in my neck of the woods. But as the spooky season rolled around this year, I wasn’t quite ready to hang up my trail runners. Where could I still climb face-to-face with glaciers and not risk an unforeseen PNW blizzard (or a very foreseen PNW drizzle)? In that other hemisphere, of course!

And so I traveled south to Peru to hike the Salkantay Trek, a 35.7-mile route through the world’s longest and second tallest continental mountain range, the Andes. Starting near the former Inca capital of Cusco, the trail climbs up toward Salkantay Pass at over 15,000 feet above sea level before descending into Peru’s inland jungle region and ending at the legendary Inca citadel of Machu Picchu. This trek can be undertaken independently or with one of Peru’s many superb guiding companies. It takes from three days to over a week, depending on your desired pace and route. We went with a four-day guided and supported itinerary through Salkantay Trekking, since my partner is a big fan of glamping and not such a fan of camping. We spent three days in Cusco (elevation: 11,152 feet) to acclimate before starting the trek.

Peru is an enormous country – the third biggest in South America, more than triple the size of California. Getting from one place to another often involves waking up long before dawn to catch an epic bus ride. Thus we checked out of our hotel in Cusco around 4:30am to catch our shuttle to the trailhead at Challacancha. After a steep initial climb from the road, we settled into a stretch of trail that our guide described as “Peruvian flat” – gradually but steadily uphill. After a couple hours walking alongside a narrow aqueduct, which channeled glacial melt to the village of Mollepata and its surrounding farms, we reached the ascent to Humantay Lake. The ascent was an optional out-and-back (or more accurately, up-and-down) side quest from our trek, but you’d have to be crazy to miss this stunning turquoise lake. The Inca worshipped many mountains as Apus, living mountain spirits that provide life to surrounding communities, and this glacial waterfall-fed lake illustrated Humantay Mountain’s role as an Apu very clearly. Peru held elections just before our visit and the campaign posters were still everywhere – in Mollepata and along the roads near the trailhead, the slogans were all about water. “Water for All – Tourism AND Agriculture” was a common refrain. The blessings of the mountain were still well understood to be at the center of people’s lives.

The trail to and from the lake was bracingly switchback-free, which made it a great trial run for the ascent to Salkantay Pass that we’d have to do the next day.

We spent our first night glamping at 12,795 feet inside a cozy glass dome, with a view of the Humantay and Salkantay glacier-bejeweled mountains and of the brilliant milky starry night sky. Then it was time for another early morning, waking up before 5am to begin the ascent to Salkantay Pass. It was a beautiful, steady ascent, and when we arrived at the pass at 15,190 feet, our wonderful cook was waiting for us with a thermos of coca tea, which helped preempt any symptoms of altitude sickness. It was wild realizing that we were higher than the summit of Mt. Whitney. These altitudes were no big deal to the Inca who traversed this path over 500 years earlier, without the benefit of synthetic fabrics, nut butters, well-cushioned trail runners, etc. This was a trade route between different ecological and agricultural zones in the empire, between different cities and religious centers, and a place of worship to venerate the apu mountain spirit at a time when few peoples around the world were venturing so far above sea level.

From Salkantay Pass, it was a long, rocky, brutal descent – about 5,500 feet down, sharing a narrow dusty trail with the many mules who had packed our food and gear up the mountain. We routinely had to pull over the side of the trail to let a massive mule train pass us by – the largest we counted was over 30 mules. The ecological transition we witnessed was intense – from the drier alpine puna ecosystem of the high Andes, we descended into Peru’s inland jungle ecosystem. The forest grew thick, the bugs started biting, and the temperature and humidity rose quickly.

Upon finally reaching the village of Collpapampa, we checked into our most glam glampsite of all – the Mountain Sky Camp, where we stayed in glass domes with attached private bathrooms and hot showers (!). The restaurant in the campground even served delicious pisco sours, Peru’s signature cocktail and a welcome treat after 14.6 miles with a serious climb and a much more serious descent.

The fruit of all fruits … the one, the only, the custard apple / chirimoya.

After the first two fairly intense days, the trek mellowed significantly. We hiked out of Collpapampa at dawn, descending to the bottom of the scenic Santa Teresa River valley. We followed the river for a few hours, then stopped by a local fruit stand where we got to sample some fruits that don’t make it to typical U.S. grocery shelves, including lúcuma, pacay, and granadilla. It was here that I enjoyed my very favorite meal of the whole trail: a ripe custard apple, known locally as chirimoya. The juicy, fleshy white fruit tasted just like candy and was a great way to rehydrate after a few hours of steamy jungle hiking. Later in the day, we stopped by a coffee farm and got to sample Peru’s super-intense geisha coffee beans – much more potent flavor and caffeine punch than the standard arabica beans that we find in most U.S. coffee shops.

After hopping on a bus to the riverside dot on the map known as Hidroeléctrica (for the dam), we walked a few more miles along the Peru Rail train tracks until we reached Aguas Calientes, the base town for one of the wonders of the world, the Inca city-in-the-clouds of Machu Picchu.

It was another early morning to beat the crowds to the entrance to Machu Picchu. We made it to the entrance gate around 6am, and there was already a massive line. There is no low season here: visitors pour into this site all day, all year, from all over the world. But the crowds do nothing to diminish the magic of the ruins in the morning mist. We were astonished to learn that this citadel was inhabited for less than a century, completed around 1450 AD and abandoned by around 1530 after the Spanish invaded and toppled the Inca empire. It still isn’t clear why this stunning and seemingly impregnable citadel, built on a mountain ridge hundreds of feet above the valley floor, was so precipitously abandoned. As far as we know, the Spanish never made it here. There was no dramatic battle. Did smallpox or some other disease spread here, via Inca visitors from other towns that the Spanish had reached? Or did the residents heed the call to evacuate and join emperor Manco Inca in his last stand against the Spanish at Vilcabamba, about 40 miles away? Maybe we’ll never know. But the extraordinary craftsmanship of the Inca who built this place, and their knowledge of astronomy which informed the citadel’s design, will stand as an eternal testament to their civilization.

Peru is truly a hiker’s paradise – the eco-tourism sector is extremely well-developed, and you can find a broad range of guided and independent hikes throughout the gorgeous Andes. In addition to leading groups on the heavily trafficked Inca Trail and Salkantay Trek itineraries, our guide was preparing to lead a 7-day hike to Choqueqirao, another spectacular mountain complex of Inca ruins with much smaller crowds than Machu Picchu. The villages we encountered along our hike had a wide selection of accommodations and resupply options for independent hikers, as well as pay-to-use showers. And did I mention the dogs?! Everywhere we went in Peru, both in the cities and in the mountains, there were adorable, well-groomed, well-fed, well-socialized dogs of innumerable breeds ambling around the streets. We were especially delighted to encounter chakus, indigenous Andean herding dogs that were officially recognized as a breed during our visit in October 2026.  I couldn’t wait to return and explore more of this stunning country. In the meantime, I’d be searching the produce sections of Seattle’s mercados for some custard apples.

#1162. <b><strong>What Is RED-S? Doctors Explain the Condition—and Why Women Are at Higher Risk</strong></b>

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

When it comes to exercise, there’s one thing that’s just as important as the training itself: fueling. Having the right nutrients—and enough calories—to sustain your activity and recover from it are critical for staying healthy, feeling your best, and keeping your body functioning in tip-top shape. After all, fueling your body isn't just about performing well today; it's about being able to keep doing what you love for years to come.

Eating can sometimes feel like an afterthought, especially when you’re busy with training, but consistently underfueling can actually result in a condition called relative energy deficiency in sport, or RED-S for short. And women have a much higher risk of developing RED-S compared to men. In a 2026 study in the Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport, researchers found that 70 percent of high-level female athletes were at risk of developing RED-S.

Top-ranked tennis player Emma Navarro recently revealed that she is one of those athletes dealing with the condition. Earlier this month, she posted on Instagram, detailing her experience, which included extreme mood swings, brain fog, a weakened immune system, constant headaches, and bouts of dizziness. Exercises she could do with ease in the past suddenly felt impossible. She was also hungry, all the time. “I had been under-fueling for almost three years,” she wrote. “My body was fighting back any way it knew how.”

But, you don’t need to be an elite-level tennis player to experience the condition. Here’s what you need to know about RED-S, including how to spot—and stop—it early, according to experts.

Meet the experts: Elizabeth Matzkin, MD, is an orthopedic surgeon and chief of women’s sports medicine at Brigham and Women’s Hospital. Amy West, MD, is a sports medicine physician at Northwell Orthopedic Institute. Amy Stephens, RD, is a sports dietitian who specializes in eating disorders.

There’s a simple explanation behind what causes RED-S: It occurs when someone is not eating enough to meet the demands of their training—or even their body’s basic physiologic needs, says Elizabeth Matzkin, MD, an orthopedic surgeon and chief of women’s sports medicine at Brigham and Women’s Hospital. Your body needs energy not only to sustain you, say, on a run or in a weightlifting session, but it also needs energy to support bone health, hormone production, metabolism, immune function, and cardiovascular health.

When you’re consistently not consuming enough food to support your activity levels, over time, your body won’t have the energy it needs to support those critical functions, leading to a wide range of symptoms that define RED-S—which is more than medical jargon for undereating. It reflects what happens when your body is chronically underfueled and can lead to long-term health issues.

Anyone, from the elite athlete to someone training for their first half-marathon to the person increasing their lifting sessions in the gym while trying to lose weight, can be at risk of developing RED-S. Remember, the “r” in RED-S stands for relative; so it’s all about not meeting your energy needs, whatever they are.

When underfueling, in the short term, you may experience fatigue, impaired concentration, more frequent illnesses, poor recovery, and irregular periods or none at all, Dr. Matzkin says. When it comes to your sport, training and performance may suffer too. Low energy availability can make it harder to sustain a workout, decrease strength and endurance, or lead to muscle weakness or pain, says Amy West, MD, a sports medicine physician at Northwell Orthopedic Institute.

You’ll also be much more susceptible to injuries, particularly when it comes to your bones. This has impacts on the long term: “Adolescence and young adulthood are critical years for building peak bone mass, so prolonged low energy availability during this period may have consequences later in life,” Dr. Matzkin says. Down the line, that could mean osteoporosis. Meanwhile, missing your period for long periods of time can have impacts on future fertility, Dr. West adds.

Early stages of RED-S can happen pretty quickly, says Amy Stephens, RD, a sports dietitian who specializes in eating disorders. After just one day of underfueling, you’ll feel symptoms of “hanger”; after just a week, your body may stop building muscle and endurance. Then, she says, it could take six to nine months to fully recover.

RED-S is much more associated with women and girls compared to boys and men. Until 2014, RED-S was referred to as the “female athlete triad,” which highlighted the relationship between undereating, menstrual dysfunction, and decreased bone mineral density. While RED-S can happen to anyone, “female athletes are more vulnerable, especially in sports that emphasize leanness, endurance, aesthetics, or weight categories,” Dr. Matzkin says. (Think: Running, gymnastics, and diving.)

But, that’s not the only reason women may deal with RED-S more than men. There’s also the pressures that women face disproportionately to be thin, says Dr. West, and the fact that women are more likely to have eating disorders.

RED-S doesn’t happen all at once. Earlier, more subtle signs like fatigue and trouble sleeping pop up when a person has what docs call low energy availability, which occurs when they’re not eating enough to support their activity levels. As someone is continuously in a state of low energy availability, RED-S can occur.

Low energy availability and RED-S can affect almost every system in the body, but these are the areas you may most often see symptoms, according to the doctors.

If RED-S is suddenly on your radar now, don’t worry. You don’t need to completely overhaul your life or your training to avoid it. In fact, there are a few intuitive steps you can take to give yourself a good shot at fueling fully—no calorie tracking necessary. Here’s what you can do.

It’s so simple, but occasionally taking stock of how you’re feeling in and out of training can go a long way. The symptoms above are warning signs of not eating enough, but you should also know what a fully-fueled athlete feels like too. A well-fueled athlete has consistent energy all day, mostly only feels hungry around mealtimes, and doesn’t deal with brain fog, Stephens says. Their mood is stable, they recover well between workouts, and they sleep well too. It’s a good idea to check in and reflect on how you’ve been feeling lately—strong and energized and able to “kick” at the end of a workout, or sluggish and hungry, struggling to get in those last few reps.

Plus, if you wanted to dig even deeper, you can also get some more information from an annual blood workup with your doctor. Since RED-S affects so many systems in the body, Stephens says, you might see issues pop up across a range of tests, including your thyroid, white blood cell count, ferritin and iron, vitamin D, or complete blood count. If any of those are out of range, it could be due to underfueling, but they would need to be discussed with your doctor in the context of the rest of your health history and any other testing.

You don’t need to download a macro-tracking app or suddenly start counting calories to avoid RED-S. (For the record: Stephens doesn’t have her athletes do that.) Instead, focus simply on getting three meals per day, with your plate being about one-third protein, one-third carbs, and one-third vegetables each time. Most people don’t eat breakfast, Stephens says, and that’s often one of the quickest culprits when it comes to not having enough food in a day.

This is another simple reminder, but if you’re someone with a training plan on the horizon, keep in mind that what you’re eating now is not what you’ll be eating in the future, Stephens says. As plans progress and get more and more intense (you’re running longer distances or lifting heavier in the gym or simply adding another day of Solidcore per week), you’ll need to increase the amount of food you eat.

One helpful rule of thumb is that for every hour you spend working out, you should add on an extra 500 to 600 calories to your day, whether that’s with the help of snacks or larger serving sizes at meals, Stephens says. Then, anything in the 2-to-3-hour range will require in-session fueling, like gels on a long run.

If you’re noticing any of the above symptoms or suspect that you’re not fueling right, it’s a good idea to see a professional. Earlier signs like fatigue and poor sleep are easy to write off, but checking in and making sure they’re not a result of underfueling can keep low energy availability from developing into a full-on case of RED-S. In addition to setting an appointment with a doc, you can also try increasing your food intake by incorporating more snacks throughout your day or having larger portion sizes at meals. If you’re not already having a carb-rich snack before a workout, try adding that for some extra energy, paired with something high in protein and carbs afterward to support recovery.

Just keep in mind: It’s never too early to bring this up with a doctor or dietitian, whether you want help determining the appropriate calorie and macro goals for your workout demands or for them to evaluate symptoms you’re not so sure about. “Earlier evaluation is better,” Dr. Matzkin says. “We don't want to wait for a stress fracture—this means bone health has already been affected.” Ideally, you’ll talk with a doctor before reaching this point, but two symptoms that should send you to their office ASAP are frequent injuries, especially if you’re having a tough time recovering from them, and period irregularities, Dr. West says.

From there, treating RED-S is all about getting your body back to the right balance of energy intake to energy output and managing any injuries that may have arisen. You’ll likely need to work with a doctor and/or dietitian to help you get enough calories and nutrients and reduce your training demands while you get caught up. In addition to a sports medicine doc and/or dietitian, you may also work with a bone endocrine specialist (if your bone health has been affected) and/or a sports psychologist, Dr. Matzkin says.

Whether you are at risk for RED-S or not, consider this your reminder that fueling is critical for any active person. Being thoughtful about getting enough calories, carbs, and fat for energy, and protein for recovery is your best line of defense against the condition. While everyone’s needs will be different, a rule of thumb, per Dr. West: “Athletes often need more food than they think.”

#573. Young people trust TikTok doctors without checking their credentials

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Young TikTok users often trusted doctor-influencers more than their own physicians, and none of 26 interviewees had checked a license.

Raised on social media, young people are usually quick to catch an influencer selling something. Put that influencer in scrubs, though, and some followers stop asking questions.

In long interviews, young adults who follow doctors on TikTok often described them as more caring and easier to reach than their own physicians.

Not one had checked whether those doctors were licensed.

That trust, the researchers wrote, may give doctors on social media more pull over their followers’ health habits than an ordinary office visit has.

Suzanne Makarem, an associate professor of marketing at the VCU School of Business, led the work.

Her co-authors were Brittany Beck of Appalachian State University and Jie Fowler of Valdosta State University.

It rests on 330 TikTok videos and interviews with 26 young followers, so it shows how people think about these doctors. It doesn’t measure what they do.

“Young consumers are usually very literate when it comes to social media influencers and commercial intent. But when you have a physician on TikTok, that becomes something different,” Makarem said.

The team started with the doctors. They picked 11 U.S. physicians from industry lists of top doctors to follow on TikTok and confirmed every license through the Federation of State Medical Boards.

All were big accounts, averaging about 875,000 followers apiece.

Researchers collected every post those doctors made during one month in 2022.

Two years later, they went back to update the numbers. Less than half of the posts were about health. The rest covered life as a doctor, home life, and opinions.

Most were filmed at home, in a car, or outdoors, yet 58% of all posts showed the doctor in scrubs or a lab coat. Even in the personal posts, almost half still had the medical outfit.

“They’re not singing and dancing like many others, but they do share personal information,” Makarem said.

“And they’re likely to wear medical attire, even if they’re not talking about medicine. Like, they’re making a peanut butter and jelly sandwich in their kitchen, but they have a lab coat on.”

On average, posts showing the doctor in medical clothes got about 1,150 comments, against about 170 for casual clothes.

Videos shot in a hospital or clinic averaged about 62,000 likes, more than twice the figure for videos shot at home.

Dancing, acting, and trending TikTok music didn’t raise likes, comments, or views. The posts that drew more engagement were the ones where the doctor looked like a doctor.

TikTok’s design makes that harder to read. The app decides which videos people see, and a popular video gets shown to more people.

Likes and comments also miss a big part of the audience. Most of the followers said they didn’t comment or send messages to the doctors.

They saved videos to watch again, or sent them to friends and family, and none of that shows up in a public count.

For the second half of the project, the team held interviews in 2023. Each of the 26 conversations lasted 45 minutes to an hour.

Everyone interviewed used social media at least weekly and had followed at least one doctor for a year or more.

They were recruited through business courses at one Southeastern university and through referrals from other participants.

Asked how they knew their favorite TikTok doctor was real, most pointed to a bio, a name badge, scrubs, or a stethoscope. All 26 said they had never looked up the license.

“I asked, ‘Have you checked whether these physicians are actual physicians?'” Makarem said.

“And the response was, ‘No, but I’m sure. They’re wearing a stethoscope; there’s a diploma hanging on the wall; they have scrubs on. I don’t think they’d be lying about this.'”

For these followers, the outfit seemed to be enough.

Most of the followers didn’t see product mentions as ads. They read them as medical advice. When a doctor sold a product of their own, several took it as proof the doctor believed in it.

Even followers who noticed the money had an answer for it.

“If they do recognize the commercial intent, they say, ‘Well, they have to. If they could do it for free, they would,'” Makarem said.

Their own doctors were judged more harshly. Several described them as rushed and more interested in repeat visits than in preventing illness.

“Doctors primarily just want to see you again. But TikTok doctors … they have no interest in seeing you,” one follower said.

The followers said they took the advice seriously, from drinking more water to starting vitamins.

Makarem’s team named the pattern the mere social media presence effect. Simply being on the platform seemed to make these doctors look kinder, and harder to doubt.

“What a physician-influencer recommends may carry more weight than what their own doctor tells them,” Makarem said. “That should give us pause.”

This was an exploratory study, built to find patterns rather than prove causes. The followers’ reports of buying products and following advice are their own accounts, and nobody tracked what they actually did.

The doctors were all large accounts on a single platform, and the authors warned against treating 26 interviewees as stand-ins for a whole generation.

Researchers also didn’t check whether the doctors’ advice was accurate, so they can’t say how much health misinformation reached these followers.

For people who trust a doctor without checking, that’s the question that matters most.

Two fixes came from the authors. Platforms could offer verified badges for board-certified doctors, and young viewers could learn to look up credentials and spot paid promotions.

Next, they wrote, come surveys and experiments that test whether a doctor’s presence on social media changes what followers do.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Consumer Affairs.

#2720. As Rivers Swamped New Mexico, Raft Guides Paddled Through Flooded Streets to Rescue Stranded Pets

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Desert River Guides co-founder Cody Dudgeon stands holding a dog with another rescue volunteer (Photo: Courtesy of Ryan Dudgeon)

Ryan Dudgeon emerged from waist-deep quicksand, her raspberry-pink drysuit caked in desert mud. The Farmington, New Mexico, whitewater paddler towed a 14-foot inflatable raft through flooded streets with a team of fellow guides on October 23. The group was searching for pets left behind after historic flooding devastated the Four Corners region the night before, destroying homes and displacing thousands.

Using their swiftwater rescue skills and drysuits, Ryan and her volunteer team spent several days rescuing animals stranded in flooded homes, locked in corrals, or trapped in submerged kennels and cages.

“We are the only ones in the county qualified to do anything like this, aside from Search and Rescue,” Ryan, 41, told Outside over the phone. Ryan and her husband Cody, 46, own Desert River Guides, a rafting company that leads trips down the Animas and San Juan Rivers. The couple has lived in Farmington their entire lives and said they have never experienced flooding like this.

“We aren’t used to natural disasters here,” Ryan said. “None of us know what to do because we’ve never dealt with something like this.”

With guidance from the Farmington Regional Animal Shelter and the City of Farmington, the Dudgeons turned their commercial rafting warehouse and equipment into a temporary emergency animal shelter and holding area for lost pets after the floods.

“We have kittens and puppies all over the place,” Ryan said. “More and more are showing up now that the waters have receded and other people are finding lost animals.”

Across the Four Corners region, community members have been displaced and their homes decimated; rockslides have closed trails like Bridal Veil Falls in Utah, and Lake Powell has risen at least ten feet since the flooding began.

Ryan believes their guide shop will store animals—chickens, cats, dogs, rabbits, and more—for weeks to come.

Over the phone, Ryan described the heartbreak of watching floodwaters swallow her home on October 23. She knew she had to do something to help her community.

“We got in our dry suits, loaded up the van with the boat, and we went out there,” Ryan said.

The group paddled their raft through inundated neighborhoods and, where possible, walked the streets while towing it.

“People needed help,” she said. “People were scared. They left their homes, but they also left their pets locked inside their houses, which is why we went out.”

The Dudgeons rescued dozens of animals, pets, and livestock that were left behind. Farmington is an agricultural community with thousands of ranches and farms in the surrounding San Juan County. Ryan recalled trying to reach a goat farm and hearing the animals bleating in the distance. The team of paddlers was unable to reach the goats and, like many other livestock, the herd drowned.

“There were dead animals everywhere,” Ryan said. “People were calling us, and we went out with our boats and gear, and we spent two days trying to rescue animals in rafts and float people out. Just as much as we could help, just as much as we were allowed to do.”

Deceased pigs, horses and goats floated through the streets alongside housing debris and logjams.

Longtime residents like the Dudgeons compared the event to the historic 100-year floods of October 1911, when historic rains tore through the Four Corners region. The 2026 floods have been so bad that, Ryan said, rumors circulated through town that a nearby dam had broken. In reality, it was just flooding from swollen rivers.

Rivers are measured in cubic feet per second (CFS). One cubic foot is roughly the size of a basketball. The Animas River, for example, usually runs between 1,000 and 4,000 CFS, but on October 26 it was pushing 12,000 CFS—roughly that many basketballs pummeling through one point every second. The Dudgeons usually raft the nearby San Juan River at around 500 CFS. During peak floods, it surged to 33,500 CFS in Farmington.

Many residents have lost everything. A friend is staying with her now after the floodwaters inundated his house, she said. Now it’s filled with nearly one foot of mud.

“He can’t go home, and he doesn’t know what to do,” Ryan said. “He has nowhere else to go.”

Upstream, mobile home parks in Bloomfield and areas downriver through Shiprock and the Navajo Nation suffered severe inundation, with homes washed off foundations and submerged to the rooflines.

“Trailers were moved off the foundations and houses were taken downstream—they’re stuck on bridges or on the side of the river,” Ryan said. “They’ve lost everything. I can’t imagine how long this recon will take. It’s literally unbelievable.”

Floodwaters also left behind thick layers of contaminated river mud that make re-entering inundated homes nearly impossible, Ryan said. Sewage treatment plants have been flooded, further contaminating waterways in the area.

“None of us know what to do because we’ve never dealt with something like this,” Ryan said.

Shortly after the Dudgeons opened their warehouse to the animals, officials placed the shop under an immediate flood watch around 10 P.M. on October 23.

“We moved out all of our rafts onto the bus, and started moving animal crates in,” Ryan said. “And then all of a sudden, after we got everything perfectly set up, officials called us around 10 P.M. on a Friday night.”

Her friends and colleagues called and showed up to help move the animals and their things out of the building to another on higher ground.

“It was just the most incredible community involvement, and I will never be able to thank these people enough,” Ryan said. “It was just the most incredible community involvement, and I will never be able to thank these people enough.”

The following morning, she said up to four inches of rain fell within ten minutes, flooding the town.

“I have my whole life in that building,” Ryan said. “Rain was just pouring down the hills, all toward our shop and river. The river was breaching its banks.”

For now, the warehouse houses not only rescued animals but also those keeping them safe. People stay at the warehouse, sleeping on the guide company’s camping gear, using the pool table and grill, and cooking breakfast on the camp stoves.

“It’s a rafting company, and now it’s a rafting company turned into an animal shelter,” Ryan said.

Ryan, an elementary teacher and parent of two, said her next job is to be a face of hope for her classroom and the two children she and her husband share.

“Everybody in the community has come together. I’ve never seen anything like this or experienced anything like this,” Ryan said. “Tomorrow, everybody’s going back to school, and as a fourth-grade teacher, I’m just going to try to make my kids’ lives feel as normal as possible.”

Though she hasn’t convinced Cody to adopt any of the displaced animals, she said the couple did get a chance to paddle the Animas River at its record-breaking flow, just for fun.

“I will tell you, best float of my life,” Ryan said.
