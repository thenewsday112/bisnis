#2959. You Won’t Be Able To Spend Your Money When You’re Old

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you wait too long to start spending your money, you will have wasted many years of unnecessary work, stress, and worry. You'll have traded your best decades for a number on a screen that eventually gets passed to someone else.

After another summer living with my parents, I'm even more convinced about 45, my recommended start of decumulation. Not because the math changed, but because I watched the alternative up close for two summers in a row.

Here's the realization: you won't be able to spend all your money when you're old. Not “you shouldn't.” You won't be able to. The ability and the desire itself disappears.

And when I say old, I'll peg that age at 65+, the traditional retirement age when corporations no longer deem you productive enough to keep around.

If you want to get a glimpse of how you'll be when you're old, simply spend more time with your parents when they're old. After all, children are highly influenced to follow in their parent's footsteps in many stages of life. Spending time with your elderly parents will give you a clue as to how you’ll be when you’re their age.

My parents used to love going on cruises to various parts of the world. It was one of the ways they enjoyed their wealth in retirement.

Then COVID hit and their cruising enthusiasm never came back. When I asked them why they don't go anymore, they said they can't be bothered. They also don't want to risk getting sick.

So I offered to pay. A nice cruise, balcony suite, any duration, any location. My treat.

They declined. This makes me sad as I fondly reminder seeing the joy on both my parent's faces every time they went to the buffet.

The single largest barrier to spending money, the money itself, was removed entirely. And they still said no.

Upon second thought, maybe money isn’t the biggest hurdle since they have pensions. It’s simply just their lack of interest and their desire to stay healthy.

One of my favorite childhood memories was going to Hanauma Bay with my father. We walked down and up the hill no problem. We even ran into Robin Williams. But when I decided to take my own kids for the first time this trip, I already knew he wouldn't want to come, so I didn't even ask.

Instead, he enjoys hours of free TV programming at home. My mom does too.

So I feel great knowing I bought them the largest new TV that would fit on their stand. For over a year, they had been watching a blurry TV, and just chalked the image up to poor eyesight.

But even the finest 60″ TV from a retailer now costs under $1,000. So cheap for years of entertainment.

Not one to give up easily on finding fun things my father can do with us, I first got him to play Monopoly Junior with my son and daughter. That worked until my boy lost interest after the first game. Then my daughter chucked a game piece on the ground after losing a second time. Monopoly Junior is almost entirely luck, so it stops being fun pretty fast.

The second evening, I switched to chess, a game my father used to play constantly. He was enthusiastic, and so were the kids. One of my fondest memories is playing him about 10 years ago after snow bowls for dessert. This time it was my dad versus both kids. They had a lot more fun, even though they still got crushed.

Each game cost about $20 at Target. They will be played dozens of times.

That's the entertainment budget of two people in their 70s and 80s with no debt and pensions. Board games and a TV antenna, not even cable. The rest of their spending is food, taxes from required minimum distributions, house maintenance, and property taxes.

The travel and the big-ticket experiences are simply gone.

Here's the part that makes it worse. My parents have been frugal their entire lives.

They didn't become careful with money after they retired. They were careful at 30, at 40, at 50, and by the time they hit their 70s, thrift wasn't a strategy anymore. It was part of their DNA.

We spend so much time talking about how compounding works on the asset side. Nobody warns you that it also works on the behavior side. Forty years of telling yourself “no” builds a muscle that gets very hard to relax.

You can hand a lifelong saver a spreadsheet showing they can safely spend $50,000 more a year and they will still hesitate over a $12 shave ice. The number doesn't matter. The wiring does.

This is the cruel joke of the FIRE movement that nobody puts on a chart. The exact discipline that gets you to financial independence is the thing that prevents you from enjoying it.

I have friends worth eight figures who still won't upgrade their 11-year-old car with electrical gremlins. They aren't being smart. They're being trapped by a version of themselves from 1998 who genuinely couldn't afford it.

When you're younger, it's easy to say your life will look different from your parents' when you get to their age. Then you catch yourself doing the exact same things.

I remember when my dad was 49, because I was 17, finishing up my senior year of high school and getting into a fair amount of trouble. He worked a regular job and came home with my mom around 5:30 or 6pm.

So we're different in that respect. I stopped working a day job five years before we had our son.

But I forgot that my dad and I used to play golf together whenever I came home to Honolulu. That hasn't happened in about 10 years. He lost interest and physical mobility. There was no longer enough time or motivation to get out there.

Here's the thing. I don't find golf that fun anymore either after being infatuated with the sport in my 30s. I only go when someone invites me to a corporate outing or their club, which is only two or three times a year. I'd rather play pickleball and tennis, but my body is wearing down too with increasing injuries.

Since graduating from college in 1999, I've never gotten into the habit of watching more than two hours of TV a day. Most days I watch none, just short video clips online. After all, I cut cable, remember this year. A long time ago I decided producing content was more fun than consuming it. It was also more profitable.

But if I don't end up watching a lot of TV as my cheap entertainment, I'll probably just play pickleball, go to the beach, or go hiking instead. Surprise surprise, all of those are free.

So when I was facing a liquidity crunch and my wife wanted us to vacation in Las Vegas, I decided to YOLO. I wasn't going to let my silent stress keep us from a memorable experience.

My parents drive a 28-year-old car. That means my dad has been driving the same vehicle since he was 54 and I was 21. At 49, I'm not that far behind. I've had my 2015 car, bought a year old, for 10 years now.

I could easily see myself keeping my next car until it disintegrates. The thrill of owning a fancy new car died for me around age 32. Now I want reliable, safe, and roomy enough for a family of four. Once both kids are out of the house, I'm not sure I'll need to buy anything nicer. I only drive 6,300 miles a year as it is.

Finally, my parents live in a paid-off home my grandparents built in the 1980s, after tearing down the previous house that was infested with termites. The house is too big for them, but it's great when we visit, especially now that I've finished remodeling the two-bedroom in-law unit.

Unless they want to move into a two or three-bedroom condo with amenities, they aren't spending money on property either.

We found our forever home in San Francisco after climbing the property ladder for 20 years. We won't be upgrading if we stay in the city. If we move to Honolulu, we might buy a cheaper house since prices are more affordable there.

Hmm. Almost every single line is me becoming my father, just on a 33-year delay.

Ages 65 to 70 is not that old, assuming your health is intact. But I've clearly watched my parents slow down after 70. Maybe there are being extra cautious due to the high cost of eldercare. I need to ask.

Besides yummy food and fitness, there is genuinely nothing money can buy them that brings more joy. The only thing I came up with is hiring a reliable house cleaner once a month for $500. And even that would come out of their cash flow, not savings or investments.

Which means the only way my parents will ever decumulate their wealth is through a bear market. A decline that provides them nothing in return. That's it. That's the exit.

Their money will not be spent. It will simply be transferred, either to the market on a bad year or to me and my sister.

But, I don't want it. I have my own money. I built my own thing specifically so I would never have to think about theirs. Every dollar they don't spend is not a dollar I'm quietly rooting for. It's a massage they didn't get, a cruise they didn't take, a business class seat they suffered through coach to avoid.

I'd trade the entire remainder for two more good years of them enjoying themselves.

So when I look at my life at 49, and at their lives after retiring in their 60s, it becomes obvious that there's only about a 15-20-year window to spend money intentionally.

You don't want to spend heavily before 45, because there's too much life left and probably dependents to care for. But after 45, it's go time. That window closes faster than anyone expects, and it closes from the inside.

If you FIREd well before 65, ironically, you may need to spend even more aggressively. You accumulated enough capital to retire early in the first place, which means you benefited more from the bull market than the average worker who can't live off investments alone.

A 4% or even 5% safe withdrawal rate has simply been too conservative since 2012. Your net worth is far higher than when you first retired.

After observing my parents in detail for two straight summers, I'm going to work harder at consumption spending.

I tried my best with the $40,000 earmarked for the Summer YOLO Fund and failed spectacularly, spending only 20% of it. But I still spent more than I would have without setting an intentional target, so the exercise wasn't a total waste.

I also used the remaining funds to invest in Mezzi, the AI investment advisor I think is incredibly helpful.

America generally thinks spending money on private school is a waste. I don't, so that expense is covered. My home is set for now, though I'm considering something nicer in Honolulu.

What's left is upgrading my car, eating absurdly expensive food I don't need, and donating more to charity.

I've also got to stop treating investing like a luxury expense. Investing has been one of my biggest hobbies for the past 30 years, and it's almost impossible to stop. It brings me so much joy!

Finally, I'd love to live long enough to see and help take care of my grandchildren. Hence, more money to physical and mental health.

Ironically, my parents seem happy. Or maybe the better word is content.

If you asked them whether they're missing out on anything, they'd look at you like you asked whether they're missing out on scuba diving. Not at all.

Maybe I'm projecting. Maybe I'm looking at their life through the eyes of a 49-year-old with a functioning body and a to-do list, and mistaking a calmer life for a lesser one.

Or maybe contentment is just what a lifetime of frugality feels like from the inside, and there's no way to tell the difference between peace and adaptation from the outside.

For myself, the lesson is simpler. Front-load the spending after age 45. Do the trip while your knees work. Buy the thing while you still want things.

Because the money will absolutely still be there at 75. You're the part that won't be.

#3978. Modern neuroscience is rediscovering a surprising idea freud had 130 years ago

📅 Oct 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

What if the human brain works in ways that Sigmund Freud began describing more than 130 years ago? A new scientific analysis suggests that some of his ideas about the mind share surprising similarities with one of the most influential theories in modern neuroscience.

Published in the scientific journal Entropy, the paper explores connections between Freud's original theories, later developments in psychoanalysis, and a modern approach to brain research known as the prediction paradigm.

This approach describes the brain as a prediction machine. Rather than simply responding to information from the outside world, the brain is constantly anticipating what will happen next. It then compares those expectations with incoming information from the senses and works to reduce any differences between what it predicted and what actually occurred.

Scientists believe this ongoing process plays a central role in how people perceive their surroundings, make decisions, behave, and regulate emotions.

Researchers Erik Stänicke, Bendik Hovet, and Line Indrevoll Stänicke, together with colleagues, argue that this modern understanding of brain function has important parallels with ideas that psychoanalysts have been developing for more than a century.

"For over 130 years, psychoanalysis has developed psychological theories about how predictions take place at a subjective level, which cognitive neuropsychology is now studying at a physiological level."

How Freud's Ideas Connect to the Predictive Brain

Although neuroscience and psychoanalysis approach the mind differently, the researchers suggest that both may be describing related processes.

Neuroscience examines the physical mechanisms behind thought and behavior, often using mathematical models to explain how the brain processes information. Psychoanalysis, which grew out of Freud's work, focuses more on unconscious mental processes, emotional experiences, and the ways past relationships influence present behavior.

In other words, one field investigates what happens inside the brain, while the other explores how those processes are experienced by the individual.

A particularly interesting connection involves the psychoanalytic concept of projection and the neuroscientific concept of prediction.

In psychoanalysis, projection involves attributing feelings, intentions, or qualities to someone else. The researchers suggest that this resembles how the brain uses previously learned expectations to interpret new experiences.

"When we attribute qualities, intentions or feelings to other people, our brain shapes our experience of the world in line with established expectations," says Stänicke.

For instance, someone who has repeatedly experienced rejection may become especially sensitive to signs that other people are rejecting them. Even an ambiguous remark or facial expression might be interpreted negatively because past experiences have shaped what the person expects.

Over time, these expectations can influence how someone approaches unfamiliar situations and forms new relationships.

"This corresponds to the neuroscientific distinction between changing one's own predictions, perceptual inference, and the attempt to make the world conform to them, namely active inference."

Perceptual inference refers to the way the brain interprets sensory information and updates its expectations in response. Active inference, by contrast, involves taking actions that help bring experiences into line with predictions or reduce uncertainty.

Together, these concepts help explain why people do not always experience the world as it objectively is. Their expectations can influence what they notice, how they interpret events, and how they respond.

Why the Brain Holds On to Negative Expectations

The researchers believe the similarities between Freud's ideas and modern neuroscience could offer valuable insights into mental health disorders, particularly those involving deeply established patterns of thinking and behavior.

Both approaches portray the mind as constantly seeking stability and predictability. This tendency is related to homeostasis, the process through which a system maintains a relatively stable internal state.

In the predictive brain model, reducing uncertainty is an important part of maintaining that stability. The brain relies on familiar expectations to make the world easier to understand, even when those expectations are inaccurate or no longer helpful.

"Psychoanalysts refer to the tendency in the mind to recreate familiar relational patterns, even when these are poorly adapted," says Stänicke.

This could help explain why people sometimes repeat harmful patterns in relationships or continue interpreting situations negatively despite evidence that challenges their assumptions.

"Rigid and persistent symptoms, such as paranoid ideas or an internalized critical voice, may be stable but not very flexible prediction models," says Stänicke.

"For example, there may be people who automatically expect criticism, rejection or hostility from others, and therefore interpret situations through this filter despite the fact that reality does not warrant it."

Such expectations can become deeply entrenched because they make experiences feel familiar and predictable. Even when the resulting interpretation is distorted, maintaining an established mental model may feel less uncertain than changing it.

According to Stänicke, this offers a possible explanation for why some psychological symptoms can persist for years and why changing longstanding patterns of thought and behavior often requires considerable time.

The connection is particularly interesting because it suggests that some mental health difficulties may involve more than consciously held beliefs. They may also reflect deeply learned expectations that automatically shape emotional responses and interactions with others.

How Psychotherapy Could Help Change Deeply Rooted Patterns

The researchers also highlight an important distinction between what people consciously believe and what they have learned through experience.

"In addition, both models give us insight into how parts of our expectations of the outside world are not only anchored cognitively, but in procedural memory that is expressed in relational ways of being," he says.

Procedural memory is a type of long-term memory involved in learned habits and skills. It allows people to perform familiar actions without consciously thinking through every step.

In the context of relationships, the researchers suggest that a similar form of learning may help explain why certain emotional reactions and patterns of interaction become automatic.

Someone might intellectually understand that other people are not necessarily hostile or critical, for example, yet still instinctively respond as though rejection is inevitable.

These expectations are not simply thoughts that can be changed through reasoning. They can also be embedded in habitual ways of reacting to people and navigating relationships.

This distinction may help explain why psychotherapy sometimes needs to address personal relationships and emotional experiences rather than focusing only on conscious thoughts.

"Therefore, psychotherapy sometimes has to work relationally. For example, new experiences in the relationship between therapist and patient can gradually help to change entrenched relational patterns."

Through repeated experiences that challenge familiar expectations, patients may gradually develop different ways of interpreting situations and relating to other people.

Bringing Freud's Psychoanalysis and Neuroscience Together

The researchers argue that connecting psychoanalysis with modern predictive brain theory could benefit both fields.

Neuroscience may provide a biological framework for understanding processes that psychoanalysts have long described through subjective experience. At the same time, psychoanalytic theories may offer more detailed explanations of how expectations are experienced emotionally, interpreted personally, and expressed through relationships.

The proposed similarities do not establish that Freud's broader theories were correct. Instead, they suggest that two very different traditions of studying the mind may offer complementary ways of understanding some of the same psychological processes.

By examining both the brain's physical mechanisms and the individual's inner experience, researchers hope to develop a more complete understanding of human behavior and mental health.

"Bringing these two fields together can open up for a more holistic psychology, in which both neurological mechanisms and subjective experience are included. In this way, we can understand subjectivity in a more scientific manner."

#3999. Scientists discover a natural compound that could protect the brain from too much salt

📅 Oct 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Consuming too much salt can affect more than blood pressure. Growing evidence suggests that excessive salt intake may also interfere with memory, thinking, and emotional well-being. High salt (HS) consumption is already a major public health concern because of its connection to hypertension, but its potential effects on the brain make finding ways to reduce the damage even more important.

Researchers in Japan have identified a promising possibility in LPC70, a compound derived from soy. Previous research found that this ingredient could reduce high blood pressure and cognitive problems associated with a high-salt diet (HSD) in mice. The latest study takes a closer look at how it works, revealing that the compound influences important chemical signals in the brain and kidneys.

How a Soy Compound May Counter the Effects of Excess Salt

The research centers on prostaglandins (PGs), signaling molecules made from a fatty acid called arachidonic acid (AA). These substances act much like local hormones, helping regulate essential functions throughout the body.

In the cardiovascular system, prostaglandins help control blood pressure by balancing the widening and narrowing of blood vessels. Their effects in the brain are more complicated. Depending on which receptors they activate, they can trigger signals that either protect nerve cells or contribute to harmful processes, potentially affecting memory, thinking, and emotional responses.

LPC70 is a form of soy lysolecithin in which lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) accounts for more than 70% of the phospholipids. Although earlier experiments showed that LPC70 could ease salt-related hypertension and cognitive difficulties, scientists had not fully understood the mechanisms responsible, particularly its influence on prostaglandin signaling.

To investigate, a research team led by Professor Akihiro Mouri and Dr. Hisayoshi Kubota, working with Dr. Kazuhiro Kagotani, examined how LPC70 affected these signaling pathways in mice consuming a high-salt diet.

Dr. Kubota, the study's first author, played a central role in the experiments. He assessed blood pressure, monitored behavioral changes, and analyzed molecular activity associated with prostaglandins. His work helped clarify how LPC70 produces different effects in the organs involved in salt-related cardiovascular and cognitive problems.

Prof. Mouri and Dr. Kubota are affiliated with the Department of Regulatory Science for Evaluation and Development of Pharmaceuticals & Devices at Fujita Health University Graduate School of Medical Science, as well as the International Center for Brain Science (ICBS) at Fujita Health University in Japan. Dr. Kagotani is affiliated with Tsuji Oil Mills Co., Ltd, in Japan and the Tsuji Health and Beauty Science Laboratory at Mie University, Japan.

The findings were released online on May 14, 2026, and published in Volume 197 of Neurochemistry International on July 1, 2026.

"We wanted to understand the role of LPC70 in coordinating changes in PG signaling across organ systems," mentioned Prof. Mouri. "Our study originated from a collaborative research project with Tsuji Oil Mills Co., Ltd, a company that develops naturally derived food ingredients," he added.

Soy Compound Improves Blood Pressure and Memory in Mice

As expected, mice that consumed a high-salt diet developed elevated blood pressure. However, the consequences extended beyond their cardiovascular health.

The animals also became less socially interactive and had difficulty recognizing objects they had encountered before, indicating problems with memory. Interestingly, their general movement and anxiety-like behavior showed no substantial changes. This pattern suggests that excessive salt selectively disrupted more complex cognitive and social functions rather than causing widespread behavioral impairment.

When researchers supplemented the animals' diets with LPC70, several of these effects were reduced. Blood pressure improved, and the mice performed better on tests of social interaction and object recognition memory.

These improvements prompted the researchers to examine what was happening inside the animals' kidneys and brains.

Different Protective Effects in the Kidneys and Brain

In the kidneys, a high-salt diet increased the expression of two components involved in prostaglandin signaling: cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and the PGE2 receptor EP3. Both may contribute to the development of high blood pressure associated with excessive salt consumption.

LPC70 reduced these salt-related increases, suggesting that the compound helps correct changes in kidney signaling that can interfere with normal blood pressure regulation.

The researchers found a different response in the prefrontal cortex, a brain region essential for thinking, memory, and social behavior.

Excess salt reduced the expression of the PGD2 receptor DP1, which plays a role in protective and anti-inflammatory signaling in the brain. LPC70 restored expression of this receptor without changing DP2, another receptor associated with potentially harmful effects on nerve cells.

This distinction is important because it suggests that LPC70 does not simply increase or suppress all prostaglandin activity. Instead, it may selectively favor signaling pathways that help protect the brain, potentially explaining the improvements in memory and social behavior observed in the mice.

A Surprising Change in Chemical Signaling

The team also discovered an unexpected change involving arachidonic acid, the fatty acid the body uses to produce prostaglandins.

Mice consuming excess salt had lower levels of circulating arachidonic acid. Yet when they received LPC70, levels of prostaglandins derived from this fatty acid, including PGE2 and PGD2, increased.

This seemingly contradictory result suggests that LPC70 may help the body use available arachidonic acid more efficiently to produce prostaglandins under high-salt conditions.

Taken together, the findings reveal a coordinated but highly selective response. In the kidneys, LPC70 appears to suppress signaling changes associated with hypertension. In the brain, it restores signaling linked to protection against inflammation and damage.

The compound's effects therefore depend on both the organ involved and the specific prostaglandin receptors being regulated.

Could Soy-Based Ingredients Help Protect Against Excess Salt?

The results suggest that LPC70 could eventually become a useful functional food ingredient for reducing some of the health consequences of excessive salt intake.

Its ability to improve blood pressure and cognitive performance in mice raises the possibility that it could support cardiovascular and brain health, particularly among people whose diets contain high amounts of salt.

However, the findings come from animal experiments, and further clinical studies are needed to determine whether the same benefits occur in humans.

"Our study elucidates LPC70's role in PG signaling, laying the groundwork for future clinical research and dietary interventions aimed at reducing hypertension-related cognitive decline and promoting healthy aging globally," concluded Prof. Mouri.

The research provides a clearer picture of how a soy-derived compound can influence the body's response to excessive salt, opening new possibilities for nutritional approaches to protecting long-term health.

#1648. Psychologists Say THIS Is Why You’re So Drawn to Antique Decor and Old Places

Here's some justification for your flea-market habit.

For many of us, spending an early Saturday morning at an estate sale or touring a historic place such as a house museum is a form of self-care. Feeling immersed in a time-worn setting and among the fixtures of a bygone era is a great comfort, even if it’s hard to put your finger on exactly why.

This ineffable feeling of comfort and belonging actually has a name: place authenticity. It’s a socioecological concept that every vintage lover should know about. We asked a psychologist who studies place authenticity what its characteristics are and how we can recreate it in newer settings that haven’t yet been touched by the patina of time.

"Place authenticity refers to the unique essence of a place that makes it feel genuine, alive, and connected to its history, culture, or community," explains Ashley Krause, M.S., a doctoral student in the Department of Psychology at the University of Florida and the co-author of a study on this concept.

Place authenticity intersects with personal authenticity (that is, the sense of being true to oneself), which may account for those feelings of engaged enjoyment in places whose unique look and feel are singular and irreplaceable. After all, they're one of a kind—just like you.

"In one of our studies, we asked participants to recall a time when they felt connected to a particular place. What we found was that the authenticity of the place participants described was linked to deeper emotional and psychological connections," says Krause, emphasizing the interdependence of personal and place authenticity. "A place can become a source of continuity, stability, and identity, offering refuge from the homogenized spaces that often dominate modern life."

Note that place authenticity is also a subjective experience. Krause's paper on the concept posits that two people may have entirely differing views on the assumed authenticity of a place—and the same person may even perceive the place as more or less authentic in differing circumstances. For example, that record store in your neighborhood that you deem the epitome of authentic spirit may be seen by another person as an upstart that replaced the authentic mom-and-pop shop they remember occupying the space previously.

When you experience an innate sense of belonging somewhere—even if you've never stepped foot there previously—it's pretty safe to assume that there's something that feels authentic about the setting to you. Unless you are incredibly conscious of the effect a space has on you, it's easy for this feeling to sweep over you as a general feeling of inexplicable calm. #3988. Magic mushroom ingredient takes the fight out of aggressive fish

📅 Oct 18, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Psilocybin, the psychedelic substance found in more than 200 species of mushrooms, appears to have an unexpected effect on aggressive fish. Most mushrooms containing this compound belong to the gilled mushroom genus Psilocybe. In mammals, psilocybin interacts with serotonin receptors in the brain, influencing emotions and behaviors such as mood, appetite, and aggression. However, scientists still know relatively little about how the substance affects social interactions among animals.

To investigate this question, researchers in Canada examined the effects of psilocybin on the amphibious mangrove rivulus fish (Kryptolebias marmoratus). Their findings, published in Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience, suggest that even a brief exposure to a low dose can significantly reduce aggressive behavior without disrupting normal social interactions.

"We show that an acute, low dose of psilocybin significantly reduces activity and aggressive attack behavior during social interactions in adult mangrove rivulus fish, a species that is naturally highly aggressive," said first author Dayna Forsyth, a research associate and former MSc student at Acadia University in Nova Scotia.

"These findings provide the first evidence that psilocybin can selectively reduce escalated aggression in a vertebrate model without suppressing social interaction," added senior author Dr. Suzie Currie, a biologist at The University of British Columbia.

Why Scientists Studied Aggressive Mangrove Rivulus Fish

Mangrove rivulus fish are naturally combative, particularly when they encounter another member of their species. Their aggressive responses are easy to recognize and measure, allowing researchers to detect even small changes in behavior.

These fish also have an unusual reproductive ability. They can fertilize themselves, producing offspring that are genetically identical. This makes them especially useful for experiments because scientists can minimize genetic variation and more confidently attribute behavioral differences to psilocybin rather than inherited traits.

For the experiment, the researchers worked with three genetically distinct lines of fish bred in the laboratory. One group received psilocybin, another provided the fish used to provoke social interactions, and the third helped researchers measure how much psilocybin entered and accumulated in the animals' bodies.

The researchers first established a baseline for each fish's behavior. They placed a fish into a tank already occupied by another fish, with the two separated by a fiberglass mesh barrier and an opaque cover. The mesh prevented physical contact while allowing the fish to see and smell each other once the cover was removed.

After allowing five minutes for the fish to adjust to their shared environment, the team removed the opaque cover and recorded their interactions.

The following day, 24 hours after the initial observation, the researchers placed the same fish in water containing dissolved psilocybin. Following 20 minutes of exposure, they returned it to the tank with the same fish it had encountered previously. The opaque cover was then removed again so the researchers could observe any changes in behavior.

Magic Mushroom Compound Reduces Aggressive Attacks

The results revealed a striking change. Fish exposed to psilocybin moved around less and displayed fewer bursts of aggressive swimming than fish that had not received the substance.

To assess these changes, the scientists measured overall activity (time spent moving) and several signs of aggression (including swimming bursts). These rapid bursts are particularly important because they indicate that an encounter is escalating toward an attack, even when the fish cannot make physical contact.

"Swimming bursts are high-energy attack behaviors that represent an escalation of aggression towards the stimulus fish without making physical contact," explained Currie. "Other types of aggressive behaviors, like head-on displays, are more about communication and social assessment and require very little energy."

Interestingly, psilocybin did not suppress every form of aggressive or social behavior. While the fish became less likely to engage in intense, energy-demanding attacks, their less physically demanding social displays remained largely unchanged.

"Psilocybin's calming effect appears to selectively reduce energetically costly, escalated behaviors while lower-energy social display behaviors remained largely unchanged," said Forsyth. "This suggests that this compound can selectively dampen escalated social conflict rather than shutting down behavior altogether."

The compound also reduced general activity. When placed alongside another member of their species, fish treated with psilocybin spent less time swimming than untreated controls.

Together, these findings suggest that psilocybin may influence specific aspects of aggressive behavior rather than simply making animals less responsive to their surroundings.

What the Findings Could Mean for Psychedelic Research

The researchers believe animal studies like this one could eventually help scientists better understand how psychedelic compounds influence behavior and identify possible directions for therapeutic research.

By examining which social responses are most sensitive to psilocybin, researchers may be able to uncover biological mechanisms that are difficult to investigate directly in people. Such insights could ultimately inform studies of potential clinical applications.

However, the team emphasized that the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a treatment for aggression in humans. The experiment involved fish, did not evaluate any clinical intervention, and cannot establish whether similar effects would occur in people.

There are also important questions about how long the calming effect lasts. The researchers examined only single doses and short exposure periods. They did not investigate repeated treatments, long-term behavioral changes, or whether the fish might adapt to the substance over time.

Additional experiments will be necessary to determine whether psilocybin's ability to reduce aggressive attacks can persist beyond the brief period examined in this study.

"Future studies can build on this work to explore how psilocybin alters neural signaling, which serotonin pathways are involved, and why some aspects of social behavior are affected while others are not," concluded Currie. "These are questions that are difficult or impossible to answer directly in humans."
