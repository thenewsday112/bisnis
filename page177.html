#2542. Flu season is early this year. Here’s how to actually protect your family, according to experts

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For real advice, we talked to two physicians who've spent this fall fielding questions from worried parents.

By Sara Goldstein Updated Oct. 16, 2026

It’s almost that time of year when a small cornucopia of medicine takes up residence on the kitchen counter and the house becomes a chorus of coughs and sneezes. As a parent, you know the particular dread of seeing the school nurse’s number flashing on your phone, because you know it’s only a matter of hours until the whole family is flat out.

Unfortunately that call may be right around the corner: Flu season is also arriving months earlier than usual this year.

Experts say it’s a good idea to prepare now before the first fever strikes. Most of what actually protects a family is simple, though, and you don’t need a cabinet full of products or a nursing degree to do it well.

For real advice, we talked to two physicians who’ve spent this fall fielding questions from worried parents. Tracy Tyson, MD and Brittany Odom, MD, are both board-certified pediatricians and moms themselves. Here’s what they want you to know about how to protect your family from the flu this season.

Yes, in parts of the country. “We have started seeing influenza earlier this year, and nationwide reports are also showing an earlier flu season,” Dr. Tyson says. The picture is uneven, though. “Nationally, the latest CDC report shows flu activity is still relatively low but increasing, with the highest activity on the West Coast,” Dr. Odom says, and she’s already seeing plenty of cases in her area. Regions surge at slightly different times, she says, reason enough to get ahead of it now. “You don’t need to wait for flu to fill your child’s classroom before getting them protected.”

Get it as soon as it’s available, both doctors say. “I recommend getting it in September or October, ideally by Halloween, for everyone 6 months and older,” Dr. Odom says. November isn’t too late, she adds, but she wouldn’t wait on purpose, since protection takes about 2 weeks to build and some kids ages 6 months through 8 years need 2 doses at least 4 weeks apart.

“It isn’t ever too late, but the earlier, the better,” Dr. Tyson says. The flu and COVID shots can be given at the same visit, with no required spacing. “As a mom, I know how quickly something on the calendar can get pushed back,” Dr. Odom adds. “So if you have the opportunity now, take it.”

Either one works, if your child is eligible, both doctors say. The shot is approved for children 6 months and up. The nasal spray, or flu mist, is an option from age 2, with limits. “Children ages 2 through 4 with asthma or wheezing in the past year shouldn’t receive the nasal spray, and older children with asthma or other medical conditions need an eligibility review,” Dr. Odom says. Dr. Tyson draws the same line for the mist. Past that, it comes down to your kid. “My priority is an appropriate vaccine that your child actually receives,” Dr. Odom says.

The shot lowers the odds of catching the flu and softens its severity when it breaks through. Dr. Tyson likes an old health-department line. “Taking flu from wild to mild is exactly the right way of thinking,” she says, estimating it prevents flu in up to 60% of cases. It targets influenza specifically, not the parade of other bugs your kid hauls home from daycare. “A sick day after vaccination doesn’t mean the vaccine had no value,” Dr. Odom says, since even a vaccinated child who gets the flu is less likely to end up seriously ill.

If one kid goes down, the rest of the house doesn’t necessarily have to fall like dominos. You absolutely can limit the spread, Dr. Tyson says. The moves that help are unglamorous. Wash hands, stop sharing cups and utensils, cough into an elbow, and separate sleeping spaces where you can. Vaccination cuts household spread too.

You don’t need to disinfect every surface. “Don’t put all your energy into wiping down the house while overlooking the air everyone is sharing,” Dr. Odom says. Crack a window, run a HEPA air purifier, or run the HVAC fan while someone is sick. Masks help during close contact, though never on a child under 2.

And it’s not a failure if another family member gets sick.. “Viruses are sneaky, it happens,” Dr. Odom says.

Keep a few basics on hand, Dr. Odom says: a thermometer, an oral rehydration solution like Pedialyte, saline drops or spray, and an age-appropriate fever medicine with a dosing syringe. An at-home combination test (which generally covers flu A and B, COVID and RSV in one swab) can also help you tell the flu from the other bugs going around. Care itself is mostly rest, small frequent sips of fluid and saline for congestion. For babies, keep offering breast milk or formula. Honey can soothe a cough for kids 1 and older, but never for a baby under 1.

For fever and aches, acetaminophen helps, and ibuprofen is fine from 6 months, though skip it if your child is dehydrated or vomiting a lot, Dr. Odom says. Dose by weight and use the syringe, not a kitchen spoon, she adds, and never give aspirin to a child with flu.

On how high is too high, the number matters less than you’d think. “There isn’t a level of fever that is unsafe per se,” Dr. Tyson says. “It’s more about the length of time and the symptoms with the fever.” Call the doctor for a baby under 3 months at 100.4°F or higher, and for an older child whose fever repeatedly tops 104°F or won’t come down with medicine. The goal, Dr. Odom says, is a child comfortable enough to drink and rest.

The most vulnerable people in a house can’t protect themselves, so the job falls on everyone else. Babies under 6 months are too young for the flu vaccine, which makes vaccinating the adults and older kids at home especially important, Dr. Odom says. Keep sick visitors away, improve airflow, and for newborns, the advice is obvious but still worth stating since the desire is strong: “No kissing the babies!” The flu shot is safe in any trimester of pregnancy and protects the baby after birth too. For anyone pregnant, immunocompromised or caring for a newborn, don’t wait out a symptom or a known exposure. Call your provider promptly about treatment or prevention.

Asked what they most wish parents knew, both doctors have the same advice: reach out sooner. “You don’t have to wait until your child looks terribly sick to call us,” Dr. Odom says. “Flu has treatments, and timing matters. Asking for help early is part of taking good care of your child, not overreacting.”

#2293. France’s Riots Echo 1968. But Macron Is No de Gaulle

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

On March 29, 1968, at the height of leftist protests crippling France, President Charles de Gaulle boarded a secret flight to a French military base in Germany. France was teetering on chaos, and the iconic French leader wanted to know whether the army would remain loyal.

There are strong similarities but also differences with the present situation in France, whose streets are now engulfed daily in violent riots that are spreading to other European countries.

Clement Liebard, president of La Cocarde Etudiante, a conservative student group fighting the Left to keep schools open, emailed me one difference with ’68: Trade unions are less engaged now than they were back then. That reflects the trend of industrial workers being less and less interested in pursuing societal change.

But some elements of today’s protests are integral to France. The French character can be explosive, which means that France periodically succumbs to massive street violence, and this violence can topple not just governments but entire societal systems.

De Gaulle was despondent when he landed at the base in Baden-Baden. According to Gen. Jacques Massu, the commander of French forces in Germany, de Gaulle said to him, “Everything is ruined, the communists have caused a total paralysis of the country. I command nothing anymore. I’m going to quit.”

Massu, who revealed this exchange years later, well after de Gaulle’s death from a heart attack in 1970, said he reassured his president that the military was behind him in case he needed to use it to quell the unrest. He urged him to return to Paris.

That de Gaulle did. The next day, back in Paris, de Gaulle resolutely told his countrymen: “I will not resign.” He dissolved the National Assembly and called for snap elections. A massive demonstration in his favor took place on the Champs-Elysees, and weeks later the Gaullists triumphed in the elections, re-establishing de Gaulle’s authority.

President Emmanuel Macron, however, is no Charles de Gaulle. The former is a weak man leaving behind him a failed economy and a country gripped by riots; the latter led the Free French during World War II.

Another difference in today’s riots is the participation of Islamic demonstrators, who can be seen waving Palestinian and Algerian flags and who have forged a “red-green alliance” with communists, also coordinating the riots. We see this alliance here in the U.S. as well.

France in the 1960s had a Muslim population of just over 1%, nowhere near the 10% of today. And today they are highly concentrated in the urban areas where the riots are taking place. In Paris it is close to 20%, and in Marseille, just under 30%.

But when you hear today’s rioters chant slogans from ’68, you realize that the similarities are numerous, too.

De Gaulle was well aware back then that street violence could lead to change in France, having himself returned to power in 1958 after demonstrations over the war in Algeria shook France, and he was asked to form a government.

The turmoil proved fatal for the Fourth Republic, with its unstable coalitions, and led to today’s Fifth Republic with its strong presidential system, initially under de Gaulle.

The ’58 crisis is not called “the Algiers putsch” for nothing; constitutional niceties were kept as the National Assembly invested de Gaulle as president and authorized him to write a new constitution. But it was street violence that delivered the death blow. Massu himself seized government buildings in Algiers and formed a “Committee of Public Safety.”

And then there was that other massive street uprising, the French Revolution, which ended Europe’s old order and rewrote borders, politics, and society, and the street rebellions of the 19th century. Baron Georges-Eugene Haussmann, Napoleon III’s prefect, had parts of Paris demolished and ordered the construction of the wide, straight boulevards we admire today—to make it easier for troops to shoot protesters.

Today, street violence is helping the Right electorally, just like the Revolution led to Napoleon and ’58 to de Gaulle. Marine Le Pen, leader of Rassemblement National, is poised to win the upcoming presidential elections, defeating by a 67-33 margin Jean-Luc Melenchon, the communist leader of La France Insoumise. Melenchon is expected to face Le Pen in the second round of voting in May.

RN is described in the media as “far right,” but this is wrong. It is a strong supporter of French identity, and Le Pen has responded to the riots by saying, “I will immediately conduct mass deportations. Mass immigration has been a disaster to France.” Liebard tells me that the riots and LFI’s involvement give Le Pen “the perfect arguments.”

However, Liebard adds that the riots help Melenchon mobilize his electorate and train his activists. “He will have the largest activist striking force to occupy the field during the weeks leading up to the election,” he wrote to me.

Melenchon has already promised to install a Sixth Republic with a new Marxist constitution if he is elected. Let’s hope France finds a way to save herself and not export her revolution to the rest of Europe—as it did during the period known as “the Terror” in the 1790s.

Well before they utter their first words, infants as young as six months can learn to sign. Baby sign language is thought to reduce baby’s frustration levels, boost self-esteem, and bolster language development. And you don’t need to be an expert. Begin with three to five signs, saying the word as you sign it. Encourage other caregivers and siblings to join in. Then repeat, repeat, repeat. Your little one’s motor skills may not allow them to perfectly mimic the sign, so watch for hand gestures that indicate she’s trying, usually within two months of being introduced to it. Here are some great baby sign language starter signs.

Show your little one the sign for a person they know, like mommy, daddy, brother, grandma, or baby. It doesn’t matter if you plan to use mommy, mama, mother, or mom; the sign is the same — using an open palm with fingers spread, tap your thumb on your chin. For “daddy” and its variations, use the same hand position; only tap the forehead. Remember to point to the person as you sign.

Food is a great motivator. Teach your little eater the sign for their favorite food or the ones for eat, milk, or bottle. When Baby’s plate is empty, “more” and “all done” allow them to set the pace for the meal. To sign “more,” press the thumb and fingertips of each hand together, and then tap the fingertips of the two hands together several times. Look for baby to put their fists or palms together as they start to get the hang of this sign.

Animal signs are fun because they often mimic some aspect of the animal. Baby will love learning the signs for her beloved pet or for the animals around the neighborhood. Dog, cat, fish, and bird are all good candidates. You can even have a favorite stuffed animal act out the motions for its sign. The baby sign for “dog” is patting your hand to your hip, as if calling a dog. This is a slight modification from the American Sign Language version, which includes a snap that is too difficult for emerging motor skills.

It’s never too early to introduce your little bundle to being polite. “Please” is a great word to start with. Try this technique when it’s clear your baby desires something specific, like a toy or a drink, and he’s not upset or in any distress. Say that you will get it and ask if they can say “please,” demonstrating the sign as you do so. Then give them the item, regardless of whether they do the sign or not. Eventually the concept will begin to sink in, and they’ll be doing it on his own. With a flat hand and thumb extended, sign “please” by moving the palm of your hand in a circular motion in front of your chest.

Use the repetition of baby’s routine as an opportunity to teach her the signs of daily life. Learn “sleep” for naptime and bedtime. Let baby know it’s playtime by signing “play,” and then show her the sign for whatever you are playing with, be it a ball, blocks, or a book. The sign for “bath” mimics the act of washing. To do this sign, simply move both your fists up and down in front of your chest as if scrubbing it.

#2322. Why You Should Pay Attention to Diamond-Shaped Signs on Trucks

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Those metal diamonds on the back of trucks serve a purpose. Learn how hazmat placard holders work and what an empty one actually means.

Drivers have a lot to pay attention to on the road. They watch their speed, check their mirrors, and keep an eye out for sudden stops. But one detail most drivers probably haven’t given much thought to is the diamond-shaped designs on the back of trucks, even after seeing them countless times.

No, they aren’t decorations. It’s a small detail that serves an important safety purpose, especially if there’s an accident involving dangerous cargo. Learn how the holders work, what the warning signs mean, and why some appear empty.

They’re called hazmat placard holders. These metal frames hold warning signs that identify certain hazardous materials a truck might be carrying. The bare diamond in the photo is simply a holder. Its lines and clips don’t send any special message to other drivers.

Truck operators leave the holders attached and change the signs depending on their cargo. Some hold removable placards, while others have several signs that drivers can flip between. Labelmaster, a company that makes flip-placard systems, explains that drivers can change the warning without replacing the holder.

The placards use colors, words, and symbols to identify different hazards. Red often indicates flammable materials, while a black-and-white sign marked “CORROSIVE” warns about chemicals that can cause damage. Other signs identify dangerous gases, explosives, or toxic substances.

“Placards serve as an easily identifiable, visual source of information used by millions of workers in the transportation, public safety, and hazmat industries,” according to the U.S. Department of Transportation’s Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA).

These warnings help emergency responders recognize potential hazards during an accident or spill. Some placards also display numbers that identify specific hazardous materials.

An empty holder may mean the truck doesn’t need to display a warning for its current shipment. Trucking companies leave the frames attached because their vehicles might carry different types of cargo from one trip to the next. An empty holder doesn’t necessarily mean the truck isn’t carrying hazardous materials, though. Federal regulations don’t require placards for every hazardous material or quantity.

When trucks do need placards, PHMSA requires them on both sides and both ends of the vehicle. Carriers must also keep the signs secure, readable, and visible. Federal regulations also prohibit misleading signs that resemble official hazmat placards. The rules specifically mention “Any sign, advertisement, slogan (such as ‘Drive Safely’), or device” that could be confused with an official warning placard.

A bare metal diamond, however, isn’t a hazard warning by itself. It’s simply a holder for the appropriate sign when the truck needs one.

#4029. Yale scientists discover how stress changes the brain to make memories stronger

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Stress does more than influence how we feel. It can also change what we remember, particularly when an experience is emotionally intense or personally meaningful. Scientists have long known that cortisol, a hormone released during stressful situations, can strengthen emotional memories. What has been less clear is exactly how this happens inside the brain.

Researchers at Yale University have now uncovered important clues. Their study reveals that cortisol strengthens emotional memories by changing how brain networks involved in emotion and memory function and communicate with one another. The findings offer a deeper understanding of why certain experiences leave such powerful impressions while others quickly fade.

"We all experience stress, and my lab is interested in understanding how stress can be helpful," said corresponding author Elizabeth Goldfarb, an assistant professor of psychiatry at Yale School of Medicine and of psychology in the Faculty of Arts and Sciences.

The findings were published in the journal Science Advances.

Previous research involving both humans and rodents has shown that stress and cortisol can improve memory for emotional events. Scientists also know that certain brain regions, including the amygdala, play an essential role in this process. The amygdala is particularly important for processing emotions and identifying experiences that may be significant.

However, understanding precisely how cortisol strengthens memories has been challenging. Many brain regions participate in several mental functions, making it difficult to determine whether the hormone is influencing emotional responses, memory formation, or both.

"Forming memories for emotional experiences involves different processes in the brain: first, perceiving an experience as emotional or intense, and second, encoding that experience into long-term memory," said Goldfarb, who is also a member of Yale's Wu Tsai Institute, an interdisciplinary research initiative that explores cognition.

The researchers wanted to examine these processes separately to determine how cortisol affects the brain's ability to recognize emotional significance and preserve an experience as a lasting memory.

Watching the Brain Form Emotional Memories

To investigate, the Yale team conducted an experiment in which participants took a pill containing either hydrocortisone, a form of cortisol, or an inactive placebo. They then viewed a series of pictures while undergoing functional MRI (fMRI) scanning.

This imaging technique measures changes in blood flow and oxygen levels associated with brain activity, allowing researchers to identify which areas of the brain are more active during different tasks.

As participants examined the pictures, they reported their emotional reactions to each image. The following day, researchers tested how well they remembered what they had seen.

Each person completed the experiment twice, once after receiving hydrocortisone and once after receiving the placebo. The study used a double blind design, meaning neither the participants nor the experimenter knew which substance had been administered during a particular session.

This approach allowed the scientists to compare how the same person's brain responded with and without the additional cortisol.

A Five-Second Look at Brain Network Activity

One of the study's most important advances came from the way researchers analyzed the brain scans.

Scientists often study a measurement called functional connectivity, which describes how closely activity in different parts of the brain fluctuates together. When two regions show synchronized patterns of activity, it can indicate that they are participating in related processes.

Traditionally, examining these relationships requires several minutes of brain imaging data. The Yale team instead analyzed functional connectivity during individual trials lasting only around five seconds.

Using these brief snapshots, the researchers tested whether patterns of brain activity could predict two different outcomes: how emotionally intense participants found a picture and whether they would remember that picture the following day.

The approach worked for both. By identifying patterns associated with emotional intensity and memory formation separately, the scientists were able to examine how cortisol influenced each network and the interactions between them.

This gave the researchers a more detailed picture of the changing brain activity associated with emotional memory formation.

Cortisol Changes How Brain Networks Work Together

First, cortisol improved participants' ability to remember emotionally significant experiences, confirming what earlier research had suggested.

Second, and more importantly, the hormone strengthened emotional memory through distinct changes in the brain networks responsible for processing emotion and forming memories.

In networks associated with emotion, cortisol produced more consistent patterns of activity and increased their engagement. This suggests that the hormone helped the brain respond more strongly and reliably to the emotional significance of an experience.

In memory networks, cortisol had a different effect. It made their activity patterns more specialized for emotional information, suggesting that the brain was adjusting how it encoded emotionally meaningful experiences.

The hormone also increased coordination between the networks involved in emotion and memory. In other words, cortisol appeared to help these different brain systems work together more effectively when processing experiences that carried emotional weight.

Together, these findings suggest that stress strengthens emotional memories through several interacting brain mechanisms rather than through a single region or process.

Why Emotional Experiences Can Be So Memorable

The results help explain why stress does not necessarily improve memory for everything. Instead, its benefits appear to be selective, making experiences that feel particularly intense or meaningful more likely to be remembered.

The researchers believe this reflects the brain's ability to adapt to stressful situations by prioritizing information that may be important.

"Stress responses are fundamentally adaptive and can help you form strong memories -- but this is specific to experiences that you find to be emotionally intense or meaningful," Goldfarb said. "This is in part related to your brain increasing engagement of networks that track emotional intensity. So, if you are stressed out and trying to learn something new, it might help to think about exciting elements or strong feelings connected to what you are learning."

The findings also point to a potentially useful insight about learning. Connecting information to something exciting, personally important, or emotionally engaging could make it easier to remember, particularly during stressful situations.

Rather than simply disrupting memory, stress can sometimes help the brain identify which experiences deserve special attention and strengthen the networks involved in preserving them.

Researchers hunting for the wreckage of Amelia Earhart’s plane have followed new clues to a remote Pacific atoll, inhabited by giant coconut crabs so large and active at night that the team sleeps aboard its ship for safety.

In 1932, the American aviation pioneer became the first woman to fly solo across the Atlantic. Five years later, she and navigator Fred Noonan disappeared while attempting to circumnavigate the globe. After leaving Papua New Guinea on July 2, they failed to reach Howland Island, their planned refueling stop. The official account is that navigation and communication problems caused their Lockheed Electra to run out of fuel and crash into the ocean. The disappearance has produced decades of speculation about what happened.

Amelia Earhart beside her Lockheed Model 10-E Electra. 1937. Photo: National Portrait Gallery

One long-standing hypothesis, advanced mainly by the International Group for Historic Aircraft Recovery, is that the pair reached Nikumaroro (then known as Gardner Island), about 350 nautical miles southeast of Howland, landed on the reef, and later died as castaways.

A 17-person team from the Archaeological Legacy Institute and Purdue University arrived at the uninhabited atoll — part of Kiribati — this week after a 1,500km voyage on their vessel, according to the BBC and Purdue University updates. They are investigating the Taraia Object, an elongated shape in a lagoon first identified in satellite imagery in 2020 and visible in aerial photos dating to 1938.

Some researchers view it as a possible remnant of Earhart’s plane. Others consider it more likely a fallen tree or other debris. This marks the first authorized visit to the island in seven years, and the first to the Taraia Peninsula in more than 15 years.

The uninhabited Nikumaroro Island in the Pacific Ocean. Photo: Wikipedia

#3453. That Mysterious Bike Creak Is Driving You Crazy—Here’s How to Check Your Bottom Bracket

📅 Oct 16, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Simple checks to pinpoint bottom bracket creaks before they become bigger repairs.

As cyclists, we hear a lot of sounds when riding. Usually, the noises come from chirping birds, the wind rustling leaves, or your bike’s tires rolling over your favorite trail. But sometimes the noises sound bad and come from your bike, and those noises can range from annoying to anxiety-inducing as you ponder the cause.

Bicycle components are moving parts that wear out and sometimes need adjustment. These squeaks, creaks, clicks, and other strange sounds are often a sign that something on your bike needs repair or replacement. And if your bike seems extra noisy, it might be time for some routine maintenance.

Noises on bikes are sometimes frustrating and mysterious. It’s often a challenge to figure out exactly where the sound originates—especially if it’s hard to reproduce when not riding. When I ran a shop, we would have customers come in and they would be certain that a noise was coming from one spot—only to find the culprit be somewhere else. Many times a noise would originate at the cranks or bottom bracket, so that’s what we’ll focus on here.

It’s worth checking on the lowest hanging fruit before we dig into the bottom bracket. The most common noisemakers are loose parts or bearings requiring adjustment, cleaning, or replacement. Performing regular preride bolt checks helps keep components tight and noises at bay. Several spots on your bike use bearings to rotate smoothly, including your headset, bottom bracket, hubs, and suspension linkages on mountain bikes. Keep these bearings properly adjusted to cut down on squeaks and knocking sounds.

Before checking if the noise originates from your bottom bracket, check that the crank bolts (which affix the crank arms to the BB on many bikes) are sufficiently tight. You might hear a clicking when these bolts loosen. And if a crank bolt entirely backs out, it can cause a crankarm to fall off while riding or damage your crank or frame. Grab a torque wrench and tighten the bolts to the manufacturer’s recommended specification.

After you are sure the noise is not from a loose crankarm, stand next to your bike and grab both brakes. Then, put your foot on the pedal closest to you and press inward. If the bike still creaks or ticks when you put weight on the pedal, the problem likely comes from your bottom bracket.

If the clicking sound isn’t from your drivetrain or pedals (see below), your BB may be loose, or the bearings may be worn. Many bikes use a non-adjustable cartridge or press-fit BB that must be replaced when the bearings wear out. Some systems (such as SRAM DUB and Race Face Cinch) use a preload ring to add preload to the bottom bracket bearings. These rings can loosen and cause a click while pedaling. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions to tighten it back up.

If you don’t hear anything, the problem is likely the bearings in your headset, hubs, or suspension parts. Bearings can wear out after a season, especially if you live somewhere wet or sandy. Your local bike shop can help you order and install new ones.

It’s also possible you simply need a new bottom bracket in the bike. Bottom bracket standards are a deep rabbit hole and can feel overly complex if you aren’t familiar with the system. Different frames and different cranks require different bottom brackets. Before searching Amazon for the best deal on bottom brackets, consult the technical specifications for your specific bike and component group or visit your local shop for direction.

I have seen bottom bracket issues actually cause issues on carbon frames as well. I’ve seen this specifically on older carbon frames with press-fit bottom brackets that were not maintained over time. All the bikes that I saw frame issues from bottom brackets fell into the same category: older carbon road bikes with press-fit bottom brackets that were not maintained for years. Eventually the customer would hear a noise, but keep riding. Then that noise would turn into knocking in the crank. By the time they got to our shop, the space in the frame for the bottom bracket was no longer a perfect circle. That circle shifted into an oval over time—which more times than not resulted in a new frame being needed. If you have a creak in your bottom bracket, get it checked out (or inspect it yourself) before it becomes something bigger.

In most gardens, October is a pivotal transition period for perennials as they go from active growing and flowering to winter dormancy. As early-season bloomers, irises can look pretty tired by fall, but that doesn’t mean they don’t need care.

October iris care is all about preparing plants for dormancy and ensuring they’ll emerge healthy and flowering next spring. From preventing rot to managing pests and diseases, good care now ensures healthier growth next year.

Here, we’ll focus on October chores for bearded irises, the most commonly grown type, with notes on how care differs for Siberian and Japanese irises. Essential fall care includes trimming foliage, cleaning up around plants, dividing as needed, and managing mulch appropriately by iris type.

Depending on where you live and garden, some or all of your iris foliage will be giving up the game by now. Much of the foliage will be yellow or dying. Use clean, sharp shears to remove spent leaves. For precise trimming around healthy foliage, try the Fiskars Titanium Micro-Tip Pruning Shears, available at Home Depot. Their narrow blades make it easier to snip away dying leaves without damaging neighboring growth.

In general, trim established plants back to about six to eight inches (15 to 20 cm). Leaving leaves intact can cause winter damage that collapses onto the rhizomes, increasing the risk of rot.

Leave any healthy green leaves intact, as they are still photosynthesizing and gathering energy for the plant to use next year. In milder climates, you might still see a lot of green leaves, so you’ll need to delay trimming until the plant has more yellowing leaves.

It’s very important to remove debris from around and under your irises before winter. Remove all fallen and dead leaves, stems, weeds, and other plant material and compost it, or, if diseased, dispose of it in the garbage.

This chore is crucial because leftover material can increase the risk of plants getting diseases or being infested with pests later. The iris borer lays eggs in dead foliage, which overwinter there and hatch in the spring. Removing debris also opens up airflow, reducing the risk of fungal infections.

If iris borers have been a problem in your garden, make a note to tackle them again in spring. A spinosad-based insecticide, such as Bonide Captain Jack's Deadbug Brew, available on Amazon, can help control newly hatched larvae before they burrow into the plants. Apply when new iris shoots reach around 4–6 inches tall (10–15 cm).

Bearded irises store energy in fleshy rhizomes near the soil surface. They may rot if moisture levels are too high. It’s a good idea to check for and manage any areas of rot on rhizomes now, so they don’t get worse over winter. Brush back the soil from the tops of the rhizomes and inspect them for spots that look soft, mushy, or dark, or that have a bad smell.

Use a sharp, clean garden knife to cut out rotten areas until you reach healthy, firm rhizome tissue. A tool such as the Fiskars Big Grip Garden Knife, available at Amazon, is also handy for carefully lifting affected rhizomes to inspect them more closely. Disinfect the blade between cuts to avoid spreading infection. If a rhizome is severely rotted, it’s usually best to remove and discard it.

If you find a lot of rot, check drainage around the plants and make sure water isn’t pooling. Japanese and Siberian irises also grow from rhizomes, but these are generally less exposed than those of bearded irises. You can still check around the crowns for signs of rot or damage caused by excessive moisture.

Fall is generally a good time to divide bearded irises. However, October might be too late in some areas. In milder climates (zones 7 to 9), you can divide irises now. In colder areas, October is likely too late to move irises and expect them to establish before winter.

If you are dividing an iris now, lift the clumps carefully. A sturdy digging tool, such as the Radius Garden Root Slayer Shovel, available at Home Depot, has a serrated blade that makes it easier to dig around dense, established clumps. Take care not to damage the rhizomes as you lift them. Separate the rhizomes, ensuring each one has a healthy fan of leaves. Plant the divisions in suitable areas right away, making sure they have good drainage and that the rhizomes remain near the surface. Siberian and Japanese irises are best divided in spring.

If it’s too late to make divisions now, you can still plan ahead by identifying large clumps that could benefit from a spring division. Target bearded iris clumps that are spreading into dense patches, have a bare center, or have not produced as many flowers as in previous years.

Label or photograph the clumps you plan to target in spring, so you don’t forget. It’s not always easy to identify the overgrown irises in spring. The best time to divide next year is after flowering.

A little fall care goes a long way toward establishing healthy, flowering irises in spring. It’s easy to forget these early bloomers come fall, but don’t make the mistake of ignoring them. Irises need fall care to ease their way into winter in good health.

Bearded irises are different from many perennials in that they generally should not be mulched over in winter. The rhizomes need to remain near the soil surface and slightly exposed to air and sunlight to stay dry and avoid rotting. Never cover them in heavy layers of mulch or compost.

If you have very cold winters, you can apply a thin layer of more breathable mulch to prevent frost heaving. Use pine needles or clean straw, like CleanStraw Longleaf Pine Straw Mulch from Walmart. Other types of irises can benefit from a more typical layer of mulch to retain moisture and as protection against cold.

Growth is slowing and stopping this time of year as irises prepare for winter dormancy. This means they no longer need fertilizer. Stop feeding irises now to avoid promoting fresh new growth that will only expend energy and freeze in winter.

You can also slow down your watering routine as temperatures drop, especially if your area is getting more rain. Rather than following a fixed schedule, check the soil every few days for moisture and water as needed. A Yamron 4-in-1 Soil Moisture Meter, available at Amazon, can help you monitor moisture levels and avoid overwatering. Newly transplanted divisions will need more consistent moisture as they establish roots, but take care not to leave the soil waterlogged.

Thanksgiving falls reliably on the fourth Thursday of November, year after year. However, if you want your home filled with vibrant blooms right as the turkey hits the dining table, the most important month to give your Thanksgiving cactus (Schlumbergera truncata) love and care is October.

Despite its name, the Thanksgiving cactus (Schlumbergera truncata) bears little resemblance to desert-dwelling cacti like the towering saguaros of the American southwest. It’s got no spines at all and wouldn’t survive a day in a hot, dry desert. These plants are tropical epiphytes, native to the coastal rainforests of southeastern Brazil. Its flattened, arching green stems feature pointed, claw-like serrations along the segment margins, giving rise to its popular nickname, the claw cactus.

But the true magic of this plant lies in its vibrant flowers, erupting in radiant shades of red, magenta, soft pink, coral-orange, pure white, and gold. Yes, the plant typically blooms from mid-November to early December. Still, you only get those pretty blooms next month if you devote a little time to their care this month. By making a few simple adjustments to light exposure, night temperatures, watering and feeding now, you guarantee a breathtaking holiday display that lasts for weeks.

October is a transitional bridge for indoor houseplants, as outdoor temperatures decline and home heating systems kick on across most northern regions. For your Thanksgiving cactus, this month represents a delicate shift, as active leafy vegetative growth halts and reproductive bud development takes over. While vivacious cultivars like White Christmas, Samba Brazil and Madame Butterfly offer distinct bloom colors and stem habits, they all share a few key care needs.

If your plant spent the summer vacationing on a shaded porch or patio, early October is the time to bring it indoors before night temperatures drop below 50°F (10°C). Depending on your USDA hardiness zone, the next few days are critical. While southern areas may have a little latitude, in chillier northern areas a couple of days of sharp temperature slides can have a massive impact.

A quick check of holiday houseplants now prevents environmental shock when transitioning them indoors. However, even if your cactus has spent the summer indoors, this is still a vital time to make adjustments. Before you get stuck into key container care such as light exposure and watering, it’s a good idea to invest in a soil probe that can give you a reliable idea of light and moisture levels. I’d recommend getting hold of a diagnostic tool like the Raintrip 4-in-1 Soil Meter from Amazon, which can give you a solid heads-up about where your plant is at before you start.

Controlling light exposure in October is the single most important factor for triggering heavy flower bud formation. Thanksgiving cactuses are short-day (long-night) plants. During the day, position your cactus in a location that receives bright, filtered indirect sunlight. An unobstructed east or north-facing window sill is ideal. Avoid harsh, direct afternoon sun through south or west-facing glass, which scorches the flat green stem pads and turns them a stressed, purplish-red color.

Daytime light is only half the equation. Long dark nights are what induce flowering. To initiate flower buds, these plants require 12-14 hours of uninterrupted darkness every night for roughly 6 weeks. Even tiny bursts of artificial ambient light from living room lamps or passing streetlights can reset the plant's internal clock and stop bud formation. If needs be, gently move your cactus into a dark closet or cover it with a breathable cardboard box every evening from 6pm to 8am. Once tiny buds lock in at the tips of the green segments, you can place it back in indirect sun and leave it there for days and nights. In fact, it’s best not to move the plant after budding.

Alongside long nights, cool ambient air temperatures also help to initiate those all-important flower buds. Maintaining cooler indoor conditions during October reassures the plant that its natural blooming cycle is underway. The ideal temperature range for setting Thanksgiving cactus buds is between 55-65°F (13-18°C) at night, warming slightly to 65-70°F (18-21°C) during the day. If your plant is kept in a heated living room that stays at a constant 72°F (22°C), bud formation will be sparse or severely delayed.

If outdoor night temperatures in your region remain between 50-60°F (10-15°C) without dropping toward freezing, leaving your pot in a sheltered outdoor spot can work wonders. Alternatively, move the plant to an unheated bedroom, enclosed sunroom, or bright basement window well where nighttime temperatures run cooler. It’s well worth investing in a digital thermometer to help keep track of fluctuations. The AcuRite Digital Indoor Thermometer & Hygrometer, available from Amazon, helps you monitor daily high/low room temperatures and ambient air humidity.

As fall days shorten, your Thanksgiving cactus's metabolic rate slows. Although native to rainforests, these plants grow attached to tree branches, so their roots are susceptible to root rot in soggy potting mix. In October, shift to a conservative watering routine. Allow the top 1-2 inches (2.5-5cm) of potting soil to dry out between waterings. When you do water plants, soak until moisture drains from the bottom, and empty the drainage saucer 15 minutes later so roots never sit in stagnant water.

You also need to pause feeding. High-nitrogen feeds force the plant to push out weak, soft foliage segments rather than flower buds. So stop all feeding in October and November. You can add a diluted liquid fertilizer in late winter after flowering finishes. Additionally, pay attention to indoor humidity as household heating switches on. Maintain an ambient relative humidity of 40-60% by placing the pot on a shallow tray filled with pebbles and water, ensuring the pot bottom sits above the water line.

Placing your cactus alongside other moisture-loving tropical houseplants like ferns or calatheas helps buffer indoor humidity drops caused by household furnaces. You can also boost humidity levels with a reliable plant mister. The Gradvis Plant Mister from IKEA offers excellent pump action at a reasonable price. By establishing a consistent routine, you give your plant the precise signals it needs to set buds on stem tips.

By late October, hopefully your efforts will reward you with tiny, jewel-like flower buds forming at the tips of the arching leaf segments. Once these buds appear, the plant enters its most sensitive phase. The primary threat to your upcoming holiday show is a frustrating phenomenon known as bud blast, where tight, healthy flower buds suddenly turn yellow, shrivel, and drop off the stems overnight.

Bud blast is usually caused by sudden environmental changes or physical disruption. Selecting a permanent, draft-free location for your cactus before buds form is vital. Keep the pot far away from forced-air heat vents, cold window drafts, exterior doors, and direct fireplace radiation. You can pick up under-door buffers like Under-Door Draft Stoppers from Lowe’s, which protect your sensitive indoor plants from drafts.

Most importantly, avoid rotating, moving, or repotting the plant once flower buds exceed a quarter-inch (60mm) in size. Even rotating the pot by 180 degrees alters the direction of light, causing the plant to release ethylene gas that severs the delicate stems attaching the buds to the leaf tips. Set your plant in its holiday display location, then step back and let your cactus get settled (and hopefully bloom merrily).

Pests love this plant’s fleshy stem segments. Catching infestations now ensures your holiday blooms aren’t ruined by unsightly damage. Inspect your plant once a week under good light, checking the upper and lower surfaces of the segments, stem joints, and soil line. If you do catch pests, it’s best to do so before you start to see those flowers emerging.

Mealybugs are among the most common pests, appearing as cottony masses tucked tightly into the joints between plant segments. Scale insects present as raised brown bumps along stem edges, which scrape off easily with a thumbnail. Spider mites leave dusty stippling across stem surfaces and webbing in leaf crotches, thriving in dry indoor air. Fungus gnats show up as tiny black flies fluttering around topsoil, indicating that the potting mix is too wet. Aphids chow down on tender new growth and flower buds, sucking sap and causing buds to drop prematurely.

Address any pests before full flower buds open. Gently wipe down infected leaf segments using a soft cotton swab dipped in 70% isopropyl rubbing alcohol. Alternatively, spray the foliage with a gentle, organic insecticidal soap or pure cold-pressed neem oil drench.
