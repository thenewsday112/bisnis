#4159. What Happens When Sixth Graders and Elders Learn Together

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

In Saskatoon, Canada, a group of sixth graders spend their school year outside their normal classroom and at the Sherbrooke Community Centre, engaged in conversation and activities with the long-term care residents who live there. One day, they might be found journaling, another learning new technologies, or writing limericks with the “elders,” many of whom have dementia or other conditions. Or they might be gardening, helping out at the center’s gift shop, or working on an art project.  “From my work with elders, I learned stories, games, and how to become a better person,” said a student named Mya. It even relieved despair for one resident, Jodi, who said, “I went from a daily dread that I was still alive and breathing . . . to anticipat[ing] an awesome day that was full of life and joy.” Many extracurricular activities, sports, meetups, and other community programs bring people of similar ages together: children with children, teens with teens, adults with adults, seniors with seniors. But new research on an intergenerational program known as iGen suggests that these initiatives may be especially beneficial to our health and well-being. Finding meaning in connection For the study, the 25 participating students—as well as three elders and eight long-term care staff members—completed a variety of surveys. At the end of the day, they rated how meaningful their interactions with different groups of people were, how much fun and positive and negative emotions they had, and the quality of their physical health that day. They also reported how many kind and helpful actions they performed, and how strong their social connections and sense of belonging were. Everyone completed surveys for 10 straight school days in late September and early October, and then again in June. The results suggested that people were experiencing meaningful interactions between generations, and highly meaningful interactions with their peers of similar ages. While people didn’t become happier, healthier, or more kind overall over the course of the program, the researchers did observe changes on a daily basis. On days when people had more meaningful interactions, they tended to feel better, more connected, healthier, and kinder. This was true for both interactions with people the same age (like students with students, or elders with elders) and intergenerational interactions—although the benefits seemed more pronounced between generations than with peers. This link appeared even stronger toward the end of the program than near the beginning. In June, having meaningful interactions through iGen seemed to have an even bigger impact on people’s well-being than it did in the fall. The researchers, led by Jason D. E. Proulx, suggest this might be because students had deepened their relationships with elders by that point, or they were savoring their last few weeks together. The length and depth of this program—spending whole days together over 10 months—may have contributed to how powerful it was, the researchers suggest. Although this research only reflects one program, with a majority of white participants, it offers promising evidence that programs like this can help young and old alike, and comprise a valuable part of students’ education. Other research finds that interacting across generations can improve mental health and cognitive function in older people, and a sense of purpose for all of us. “Programs like iGen may be one effective and scalable intervention to foster more inclusive, meaningfully connected, and happier communities,” write Proulx and his colleagues. Mya’s mother Tanya said that she “built long-lasting friendships with the residents . . . but also learned the value and appreciation of life.” “It took those sixth-grade kids to bring life back into focus for me,” said Jodi, who had thought her life was over and sometimes wanted it to be. “iGen saved me.”

#4157. What Happens When We Stop Trying to Win a Difficult Conversation?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

It’s become a common story: Divided by politics, my father and I grew more and more distant, and after a while we could not talk about much without tension rising between us. He passed before that distance was ever repaired. I did not have the tools in time. I kept wondering why this happened so easily, to families and friendships and people who had once known how to be in the same room together. Then I went to graduate school at Colorado State University. I became a graduate teaching assistant and facilitator for the Center for Public Deliberation (CPD), which became the most fulfilling part of my time in graduate school. Part of what drew me to this work was my experience with my father, though our story is one many people will recognize. In recent years, political division has worked its way into kitchens and living rooms across the country, coming between people and making once-ordinary conversations hard to have. You may have felt it at your own table. When I found the CPD, I found the tools I had needed so desperately and had not known existed. These were not techniques for winning an argument or even holding your ground. They were ways of reaching the “why” underneath another person’s perspective, the experiences and stories and information that had built it. The goal, I discovered, should never have been agreement. It was understanding, the point where someone’s view makes sense to you as something a person could reasonably arrive at, even when reaching that point is hard. That was exactly what my father and I had never had a way to give each other. It showed me the path I wanted to take in life. Even beliefs that look irrational on the surface usually trace back to a real concern or value underneath. Understanding that root does not mean every belief holds up equally against facts. Some do not. What it means is that curiosity about the person’s underlying concern is what makes deep value-based conversation possible. You can respect where someone is coming from and still hold the line on what is true. The CPD is hired by cities and local organizations across Northern Colorado to bring community members together around shared challenges. Rather than holding traditional town halls, where people often talk past one another, the CPD designs events that foster collective wisdom: the recognition that the knowledge of a group is far more powerful than any single person’s alone. What I loved most was who filled the room. A retiree and a college student, a small business owner and a recent arrival, people from across the political spectrum and from neighborhoods that may rarely interact. The issues were as varied as the people: housing, how communities care for their aging members, student mental health, and, with the local newspaper, misinformation and political polarization. Whatever the topic, the aim was the same: to give a group what it needs to work through a shared problem, together. As a facilitator, my role was to help people, using techniques like uncovering the value tensions underneath their disagreements. This is more delicate than it sounds. When people argue, they tend to argue about conclusions, but conclusions are not always where the real disagreement lives. More often we hold many of the same values and simply weigh, rank, and apply them differently, shaped by what our own lives have taught us. Take accessory dwelling units (ADUs), small additional homes built on existing properties. One neighbor might prioritize privacy, worried that an ADU next door changes the quiet street or brings people they don’t know closer to their daily life. Another might prioritize freedom, believing homeowners should have the right to build on their own land as they see fit. Both values can be held by reasonable people. Neither is wrong. The disagreement isn’t really about ADUs. Instead, disagreement lives in which value, privacy or freedom, someone weighs more heavily when the two come into tension. Naming the value underneath a position does two things at once: It shows the group where the actual tension sits, and it makes the other person’s view legible as something a reasonable person could hold. Once you can see that, movement becomes possible. We were not necessarily searching for common ground that already existed. But, rather, we were building it together. At some point, tools stopped being something I reached for and became something I embodied in everyday life. I noticed it less as a single moment and more as a pattern showing up everywhere. I found myself curious in interactions that used to pass by unnoticed, wanting to know more about people for no reason other than wanting to know them. When someone said something I disagreed with, my first instinct stopped being to argue. It became asking what shaped that belief, what experience or value sits underneath it, digging past the kind of surface-level exchange most conversations settle for. Curiosity tends to be contagious. When I show someone true interest, they often show it back. What started as one person asking questions turns into two people learning from one another. That only works, though, if it stays voluntary. Some people are private, and that is their right. I am not asking anyone to disclose more than they want to. If someone wants to share, I listen. I ask. I learn. It stopped being a skill I used and became a way I move through the world. Long after the events ended, I would catch myself in a heated debate at a dinner or with friends, reaching for the why underneath what someone had said instead of what I deemed to be disagreeable or flawed. I stopped needing to win. I started assuming the person across from me knew something I did not, and that searching to discover their reasoning was the most respectful thing to do. The harder lesson was patience, and most of it was with myself. Some conversations made me frustrated. Some I simply got wrong. The discipline was never to pretend otherwise but to notice the mistake and repair it, and then to extend that same grace outward. Other people arrive carrying their own frustration and stumble in their own ways, and they need the room to learn that I needed, too. Sometimes the best thing I had to offer was curiosity. It was a single conversation across difference that left someone feeling heard, which can feel rarer than it should. That curiosity follows me well beyond any event, into encounters that have nothing to do with policy. In an old mining community in Wyoming that had largely emptied out, I stopped to talk with a man who made pottery. The town was the kind of place where the buildings tell the story before any person has to, where you can read the initial boom and the migration in the empty storefronts and homes. We sat together while he told me what it had been like to watch his town change, the prosperity that came and went, the quiet the exodus left behind. He spoke about staying when others left, about his craft, about what it means to keep making something with your hands in a place that feels as though some had forgotten. I was not there as a researcher and I had no agenda. I simply wanted to know, and so I asked, and he told me. I think about that conversation more than he would ever guess, and it gave me something no book could impart. This is what curiosity does. It lets you hear about other people’s lifetimes rather than just their opinions, and it asks something of you in return: the willingness to admit that your own understanding is incomplete, and that other people’s stories and experiences are part of what fills in the gaps. This requires us to choose curiosity over certainty, again and again, even when certainty would be easier. I do similar work now at Unify America, helping design and support civic assemblies that bring everyday people together to address shared problems. The timeline is slightly longer than those evening events in graduate school, but the ethic is the same. What moves me most still happens in the small moments of connection: people who would never have crossed paths in ordinary life, of different ages and neighborhoods and political realities, sitting down together over weeks and slowly getting to know one another. They share meals. They learn the names of each other’s kids. They discover, often to their own surprise, that a person they assumed was their opposite carries many of the same worries and hopes they do. I have been in the room while it happens, and you can feel the shift when it comes. The distance that seemed permanent reveals itself as circumstantial. I don’t say this to suggest that dinner-table tension is the whole story. Many people are living through a period of real precarity right now, for reasons that differ sharply depending on who you ask and what news you consume. Some people are afraid for their safety. Some are afraid for their livelihoods. Some are afraid of being unheard or unseen in a country that no longer feels familiar to them. Whatever the reason, whether it belongs to you, your neighbor, or a stranger you pass on the street, it comes from the same place. We are all moved by our hopes, our dreams, our values, our experiences, our fears. That’s part of what makes this work harder than it looks from the outside. You cannot ask someone to be curious about a neighbor’s perspective while pretending the stakes are small. The fear is real, even when two people in the same room are concerned about two completely different things. Deliberation does not erase that concern or resolve it. But it does something else that matters as much. It reminds people that they are not just living alongside one another, they are co-creating the communities they share. That means everyone has a seat at the table, not to win an argument, but to build something together that helps all of us navigate life the best way we know how and become a little more of the people we hope to become. Belonging grows out of that kind of collaboration. It is hard to build when we default to blaming our neighbors for our differences, and it gets easier when we choose curiosity instead, again and again, until it becomes a way of being with each other rather than a technique we reach for. The same attitudes carry weight beyond any single conversation. When people practice curiosity and patience with each other, they build the capacity to do something bigger together: to take part in shaping the decisions that govern their shared life. Civic processes work best when the people inside them can actually hear one another, weigh trade-offs, and hold a disagreement without needing to end it in conquest. That capacity does not appear on its own. It is built the same way any skill is built, through patience, in rooms like the ones I have described, and in the ordinary conversations that happen everywhere else. The more of us who carry these attitudes in public life, the more capable we become, together, of building a collective vision of what it means to participate in a shared civic life. This is why I do this work. Not because I am trying to solve polarization as an abstract problem, but because I know what it feels like when understanding breaks down between people you care about, and what it feels like when it can be repaired. Every assembly, every conversation, every moment of curiosity is a chance to help others experience what I am still learning myself: that we do not have to choose between our perspectives and our relationships. We can seek to understand one another not out of self-interest, but because human experience is rich beyond measure, and we are all a little incomplete without each other’s stories.

#4155. How Can We Make Relationships the Heart of Schools?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Ask any educator today, and they’ll tell you that relationships across the board are strained in schools. Our educational landscape is rife with sociopolitical pressures: political polarization, disagreements over the teaching of race and LGBTQ+ rights that lead to restrictions on addressing particular topics, curriculum debates and questions about the effectiveness of inclusive education that undermine inclusive curricula and social-emotional learning (SEL). Other concerns that impede supportive school climates include health and safety crises such as the pandemic, and student mental health concerns that are manifesting in dramatically changing student behavior. And the list goes on. What are educators doing to address these increasingly challenging pressures? What practical strategies are they implementing to rebuild positive, relationship-centered classrooms? Amid these pressures, many educators are creating hopeful new ways of rebuilding school climate, as we learned through our research. This article highlights promising, relationship-centered practices that educators are using right now to foster belonging, productive classroom discussions, conflict resolution, and emotion regulation for both students and themselves. Connection before correction: Cultivating a sense of belonging Sarah, a fifth-grade teacher we encountered in our research, notices that Maeve often acts out on test days. As a math test is about to begin, Maeve becomes disruptive—bothering classmates, crumpling papers, and making accusatory comments. Sarah can either immediately correct the behavior or first connect with Maeve. She chooses connection. Sarah pulls Maeve aside and asks how she’s feeling about the test. Maeve admits she’s nervous and afraid of failing. Sarah listens, reassures her that mistakes are part of learning, and shares that she gets test jitters, too. Feeling seen and supported, Maeve calms down. Only then does Sarah briefly address the earlier behavior, recognizing it was rooted in Maeve’s anxiety. According to a survey our team administered to nearly 100 educators, teachers are utilizing practical ways to prioritize connections through strategies such as warm individual greetings when students enter the classroom, empathy and validating feelings, sharing personal stories about their lives, and showing sincere interest in students’ lives. By actively listening, using reassuring words and facial expressions, and recognizing student hobbies and activities, they establish common ground for building caring relationships. Saying something as simple as “I understand how you feel” can build a bridge. Research indicates that when students are in a state of “belonging uncertainty,” they may question the quality of their social connections, directly impacting their motivation. A compromised sense of belonging can cause students to interpret tasks as more difficult than they are as they align the challenge with a feeling that they simply don’t belong. Connecting before correcting can bolster students’ view of problems as more temporary and typical than permanent and unsolvable. Empowering student voice Soon after the murder of George Floyd in June 2020, numerous graduates from a suburban high school reached out to their hometown school district’s curriculum office. Across their college classrooms, cafeterias, and dorm rooms, discussions about race in modern America were intense. All of the alumni who reached out felt ill-prepared to engage in such discussions. The director of curriculum shared this feedback with the secondary social studies and English language arts (ELA) teachers. The teachers responded with fear and concern. They were worried that facilitating classroom discussions around controversial and emotionally charged topics, such as race, would lead to microaggressions, hurt feelings, and repercussions for teachers as a result. Classroom discussion driven by student voice, where students share their ideas and learn from one another, can sometimes be counterproductive. Research indicates that teachers require explicit professional learning to design and sustain structured student voice practices, including strategies for eliciting authentic input, facilitating deliberation, and engaging students as co-designers of the practices. To achieve this, the secondary social studies and ELA teachers received training in creating structured environments for discussion using evidence-based best practices:Co-create classroom norms: Let students set the norms and rules for class discussion. Scaffold respectful civil discourse: Structured techniques like Yes-No-Maybe and Respectful Debate teach students to articulate opinions, listen deeply, and reflect on others’ ideas. Practice audience-focused communication: Students tailor their messages for different audiences—classmates, teachers, administrators, or community members. This framework validates all students’ voices and choices and tends to increase their internal motivation, while maintaining a safe and structured environment. Structured, student-driven open forums—such as Socratic seminars, student-led meetings, and restorative circles—help formalize respectful exchanges and cultivate trust so students feel safe to take intellectual, social, and emotional risks, all of which are foundational conditions for productive discussion in and out of the classroom. Co-regulation as the foundation for student-centered discipline After a peer conflict in algebra class, a middle school student visited the counselor’s office, displaying signs of emotional distress—rapid breathing, tense shoulders, and defensive responses. Instead of asking questions about the situation or reinforcing expectations about appropriate behavior, the counselor sat next to the student, and in a calm tone of voice said, “It looks like you’re feeling frustrated, so let’s slow things down and take a few deep breaths together.” After the student’s breathing slowed and their tense shoulders softened, the counselor asked several open-ended questions to help the student reflect on and make sense of the experience and determine a clear plan of action to repair the peer conflict. Self-regulation involves a wide range of skills, including emotional awareness, self-soothing, and flexible thinking. Students learn how to self-regulate through co-regulation, the bidirectional process between a child and adult, in which a calm and caring adult provides the environment and scaffolds for the child to regulate their emotions and behavior during stressful situations. When a child experiences feelings of anxiety or overwhelm, their nervous system enters “fight or flight” mode, which may manifest as an emotional meltdown or withdrawal. Through the calm and steady presence of an attuned adult modeling regulation techniques, the balance of their nervous system can be restored. One of the counselors we surveyed draws upon the 3 Rs from psychiatrist Dr. Bruce Perry with her students:Regulate: Help the student to use exercises such as deep breathing or rhythmic movement to reach a calm state. Relate: Connect with the student through active listening and empathy to understand their perspective. Reason: Invite the student to engage in reflection and problem solving. Perry’s steps are grounded in neuroscience, acknowledging that the lower brain must be regulated first before students can access higher brain regions, such as the cortex, to carry out activities like reasoning. Over time, engaging in co-regulation with caring adults fosters whole-brain integration, reduces the long-term impacts of stress, and helps children to build resilience by internalizing emotional regulation skills through safe, supportive relationships. Restorative practices to build accountability After two students got into a heated argument during a group project in a seventh-grade social studies lesson, one student slammed her laptop and said, “That’s it! I give up! You do everything your way.” The other student rolled her eyes and continued typing. Rather than sending the students to the main office or school counselor, the teacher invited them both into the hallway for a restorative conversation. She asked them each to share their perspectives and then to consider who was affected by their actions. After each student had an opportunity to share, she guided them toward repair and resolution by asking them, “What can you do to improve the situation?” and “What do you need to do that?” Within five minutes, both students verbally agreed to apologize to their fellow group members, as well as to clearly define everyone’s roles within the group. The restorative conversation allowed the girls to resolve their conflict, strengthen their relationship, and shift the focus from blame to accountability. Restorative practices like this conversation consist of formal and informal processes that help to resolve conflict, foster relationships, and build a sense of community. Numerous studies find that exclusionary forms of discipline, such as suspensions, have detrimental effects on students, including heightened mental health challenges, increased misbehavior, and lower academic achievement. Restorative practices offer a relationship-centered alternative that empowers students who have committed wrongdoing to take full accountability by understanding how their behavior affected others, acknowledging the harm it caused, and taking meaningful action to repair the harm. Whether used formally through restorative circles and conferences or informally with restorative conversations and talking about feelings, when students are exposed to restorative practices, research finds they show benefits such as experiencing decreased bullying and violence, improved mental health, fewer suspensions and expulsions, improved behavior, and restored relationships. One school administrator we interviewed said, “I have found that schools who use restorative practices encourage stronger bonds and relationships between teacher and students and between students themselves. This sense of community is a vital facet of a positive school culture and climate.” Routines to promote educator wellness and connection Just before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic, the emotional labor demands on teachers increased to levels unseen in history. In an effort to mitigate the emotional distress of his teachers, one principal asked each staff member to give a positive shoutout to a colleague in the first few minutes of every staff meeting. This routine did lift the mood momentarily and promoted greater positivity, but there was still a great deal of emotional turmoil under the surface that was unaddressed. Just as students benefit from adult co-regulation that scaffolds their ability to regulate their emotions in stressful situations, so do teachers. An effective co-regulation strategy that works for principals aiming to co-regulate their staff’s emotional distress involves adult modeling and scaffolding of emotional regulation strategies. In the midst of COVID, the principal implemented a three-minute routine at the start of each staff meeting by modeling a strategy called structured SELf-Questioning:I do: The principal models via a think aloud, asking and answering out loud two questions (“What am I feeling?” and “What can I do to self-calm?”) We do: Staff ask themselves the same questions, write down their answers, share in pairs, and share out with the whole group if time permits. You do: Teachers replicate this in their classrooms with their students. As the emotional challenges facing schools intensify, the steady ability to navigate one’s own emotions while helping others do the same is the key to a principal’s success. Providing teachers with evidence-based adult SEL experiences—such as those embedded into staff meetings or professional development—can meaningfully improve educator well-being and promote greater engagement. Abundant research finds that SEL training and SEL teaching have major benefits for both teachers and students. Reclaiming relationships as the heart of schooling The pressures facing today’s schools are real, complex, and often overwhelming. It is becoming increasingly clear that meaningful change does not need to start with expansive policy revisions or punitive approaches; it begins with relationships. Across classrooms, counseling offices, and staff meetings, educators and administrators are choosing connection over correction, regulation over reaction, and restoration over exclusion. They are prioritizing belonging, elevating student voice within classroom environments, engaging in co-regulation, utilizing restorative practices, and tending to educator wellness. While these approaches are not quick fixes, they are grounded in research, brain science, and the lived experiences of educators who understand that behavior is a form of communication. When students and educators feel seen and heard, they take more academic risks; and when conflict is approached with repair instead of punishment, relationships thrive. Rebuilding school climate is not about returning to what used to be; it is about shaping how we will move forward, intentionally. And these educators and administrators remind us that the path forward is always better with connection.

#4153. Is Talking or Texting Better for Disagreements?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

During a period of his life when he publicly shared political opinions, Dylan Marron encountered a lot of online hate. The old comments section in his YouTube channel contains horrific, dehumanizing comments like “You should kill yourself.” Imagine receiving a comment like that—how would you respond? A possible—and tempting—response is to volley the vitriol right back, wishing death in return, giving back the hatred that had been communicated to you first. After all, in certain corners of the online world, many people do exactly that, publicly engaging in rhetoric so toxic and disturbing that it sends chills down your spine. But Marron did not behave like most people. Instead, he did something that most people would probably question. He reached out to the person who wrote “you should kill yourself” and asked if they would have a spoken conversation with him. As a psychologist who studies conflict conversations, this is the sort of incredible psychology study that I wish I could run—but no Institutional Review Board would ever approve. What happened when Marron reached out to his online haters is not what you might have expected. First, you might expect that none of the commenters would respond, let alone agree to have a conversation. But instead, dozens of them agreed to talk to Marron (and even be part of a later-produced podcast that he created, aptly called “Conversations With People Who Hate Me”). Second, even more shocking than their willingness to talk, the conversations were sort of…good. Over the course of the conversations (some lasting up to an hour), both communicators experience moments when they acknowledge each other’s viewpoints, moments when they not just listen to each other but seem to understand each other, even moments when they possibly find just a tiny bit of common ground. They almost never reach real agreement, but they also don’t sound like they hate each other by the end, much less want to kill each other. By inviting his haters to a spoken conversation, Marron did something brilliant. He removed them from a context that is fundamentally dehumanizing—an anonymous, text-based, engagement-optimized, algorithm-driven environment—in other words, an online comment section. The very design of most online platforms brings out the worst in us, eliciting a level of vitriol rarely observed in the offline world. Having a spoken conversation allowed Marron and the person who thought they hated him to re-humanize each other, recognizing and consequently treating each other as people who have faces and families and rich, complex opinions instead of as nameless adversaries defined by idiotic, strawmen positions. Marron’s case study illustrates something I’ve been researching for almost 20 years now: why certain types of conversational structures—specifically, spoken versus written conversations—seem to fundamentally change the constructiveness of communication. In 2017, I published a paper called “The Humanizing Voice.” It posited that hearing what a person says, compared to reading even the same words in text form, can humanize the communicator. For example, in one experiment, hearing a political opponent’s statement caused observers to attribute more “uniquely human” traits (e.g., greater rationality and thoughtfulness) to their opponent than reading the opponent’s same words. As another example in a different experiment, hearing a script spoken by a voice actor, compared with reading the same script, made observers assume the original creator was more likely a human than an AI program.  In my latest published research, my coauthors Burint Bevis and Mike Yeomans and I put the “humanizing voice hypothesis” to a different type of test. Rather than having observers form evaluations of previously recorded statements that they either heard or read, we asked real political opponents to have hundreds of live conversations. By changing the medium of the entire conversation (from speaking to writing, for instance), we could test how the medium influenced both which language people used—language production—as well as the way people’s opinions were understood—language consumption. Changing the conversation medium also let us directly test how constructively disagreement conversations unfold, specifically how much communicators feel understood and how much conflict they experience. Before we collected real conversations, we first examined people’s preferences and predictions. What we found underscored the appeal of the online comments section. In a survey of 200 adults, respondents strongly preferred to write to a disagreeing stranger than talk to them (84% vs. 16% preference, respectively). Why? They thought it would be more comfortable and easier, and also lead to better conversations (e.g., less conflict). We think these predictions—and everyone who believes writing vitriol in online comment sections will be a good use of time—are mostly wrong, based on the experiments we ran next. We conducted a series of experiments at universities across the U.S. to test the real effect of conversation medium on disagreement conversation outcomes. Our final analysis set for our paper included 1,842 conversations. In all experiments, communicators were randomly assigned to either speak or write to each other, typically for short (e.g., nine-minute) conversations about their topic of disagreement. For example, in one experiment, we identified some topics that would be controversial among a sample of UC Berkeley students (e.g., California government reparations for slavery). We first asked participants to tell us their own opinions on the designated topics, then assigned them to talk or write to a disagreeing partner, and finally asked them in private surveys how the conversations went. The talkers reported having more constructive conversations than the writers—specifically, greater understanding, less conflict, more liking and positive impressions, and more openness to changing their attitudes toward their partner’s attitudes. They didn’t even have to see each other to accrue the positive benefits of speaking; talking to each other without video on sufficed. We found that the effect of medium persisted even when we made the conversations a bit shorter or longer (e.g., six minutes or 12 minutes), changed their synchronicity (enforced pauses or no pauses), and changed it from a dialogue to an exchange of monologues (only one chance each to communicate). Further, these other structural changes (conversation length, synchronicity, and interactivity) did not have as much impact as the conversation medium on the reported constructiveness of the ensuing conversation. We partnered with an organization called “Bridge USA” to run conversation events on multiple college campuses, including Minnesota State University, Mankato, and Arizona State University. We selected a topic that would be polarizing for the community (e.g., for Arizona State University, we selected the U.S./Mexico border wall being built) and advertised the events as opportunities to discuss this topic. We randomly assigned attendees to sit at a “speaking” or “writing” table, where they either spoke or wrote with a partner about the topic. After each short conversation (six to 10 min), attendees completed a survey about their experience and then rotated and moved to another conversation. Most attendees had at least five conversations during the event. The conversation events showed the same results: Speakers reported having more constructive conversations, liking each other more, and finding more attitude alignment, than writers. This was even true when they largely agreed at the outset. At Arizona State University, we also asked communicators to predict their partner’s actual attitudes after the conversation ended, and speakers were more accurate than writers. This suggests that they didn’t just feel more understanding in the spoken than written conversations, they actually had more understanding. To understand changes in the production of language, we analyzed the laboratory conversations for which we had clean recordings (699 in total). The structure of language among pairs who had been assigned to speak was very different than pairs assigned to write. For instance, speakers produced shorter, quicker exchanges than writers, with more overlapping words. They also tended to use less formal and more agreeable language. Particularly interesting to us was that speakers used more receptive language—language that signaled their openness to opposing views, like more subjectivity (e.g., “in my opinion” or “I believe”) and fewer negative emotions (e.g., “this is terrible”)—than writers did. We found that the more receptive language in the spoken (versus written) conversations was also associated with more constructive outcomes, like greater reported understanding in the post-conversation surveys. Marron understood the intuition of my results before they were published. Not only did he avoid the ultimately unconstructive allure of getting into online arguments, he leveraged the humanizing value of a spoken conversation. By engaging constructively with his antagonizers, he lived the research while providing a powerful example for all of us. Let’s follow Marron’s example and try living the research. The next time a disagreement is brewing, instead of replying over text, try talking. Ten minutes of speaking could save you a lifetime of misunderstanding.

#4151. The Ups and Downs of Communal Living

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you’re struggling with something, chances are the answer in some way, shape, or form is community. Or at least that’s been my experience in my 46 years on this too-often-lonely planet. When I got confused about where to send my kid to school, I found a ragtag group of parents all over the country who were committed to being a force for integration. When I fell down the hell that is our elder care “system,” I found solace in day programs, support groups, and all other manner of collectivizing. And when I was pregnant and in my late 20s, trying to figure out how to be a grown-up, you better believe I jumped on the chance to live in intergenerational, interfaith cohousing with some intentional community veterans. I lived there for over a decade and it completely transformed how I understand abundance, cooperation, and, yes, the many uses of homegrown kale (true to stereotype). So, of course, I am over the moon about a new anthology, Living, Together: Reimagining Community in the Age of Disconnection, just out from Beacon Press (who also published my book, Do It Anyway, back in the day). Living, Together features a few of my favorite contemporary thinkers on interdependence, including Rhaina Cohen (author of The Other Significant Others) and, as mentioned, Dani McClain. Here’s an interview with the anthology’s editor, Samantha Paige Rosen. Courtney Martin: You begin the book talking about your own pleasurable experience of living with your parents as an adult—something that grew out of crisis, but has become a choice. It seems that in the case of multigenerational households, part of the challenge is rewiring adult relationships and not falling back into early conditioning. How did you do this rewiring when you all lived together as adults? Samantha Paige Rosen: For me and my parents, I think a handful of different things occurred. First, some of the rewriting began before I moved in with them. I lived with them for nine months when I was 24 after I returned from the East Coast and was considering a career shift. I stayed with them during the summers I was in graduate school for the next couple of years, and I did the same for most of my college summers. All that time, they were seeing the adult I’d become and I was seeing them with emerging young adult eyes. I think this cemented the adult-level friendship between us so that by the time I moved in at 29, after which I ended up staying for six years, we could build off of the rewiring we’d already done. Another piece is communication. I love talking about my feelings and my perspective, and I want my loved ones to feel comfortable doing the same. So as much as my mother likes to joke that I talk everything to death—and I know I have to pick my moments; not everyone is receptive at every moment—I attribute the way we understand each other as adults to my communication skills and my parents’ openness to that, much of the time. What that meant was that when there were instances over the six years I lived with my parents where my mom doted on me like she did when I was young, or my dad was a little too opinionated about my whereabouts, I could remind them, Thank you, I love you, and, as an adult, I’ve got this! If they’d make an assumption based on how I acted when I was younger, I’d call them out on it, nicely, and let them know that the thing they’re saying isn’t true of 30-something me. Of course, this goes both ways, and I let my parents know they could call me out, too. I worked hard to make sure I was seeing my parents as the fully realized adults they are and not just my mom and dad. At the same time, I’ve always said that neither of my younger sisters could have lived with my parents as adults, but I obviously could. I don’t always need to be in charge, and I don’t mind a little doting! My sisters prefer to run the show and feel strongly about their independence. So perhaps there was an element of luck, personality-wise, too. For readers who live in multigenerational households and have the difficulties around rewiring, I’d say tell and show your loved ones the ways you’ve changed—and often—and ask them to do the same. Communication is everything (and when that doesn’t work, demonstration is). And are there minor aspects of falling back into early conditioning that wouldn’t be so terrible to embrace? CM: It seems like one of the biggest barriers for so many people to live more creatively and interdependently is cost. Cohousing, for example, is notoriously expensive to create and is still too elite a movement, as a result. What did you learn about the role that finances play in our ability to, as the subtitle says, reimagine community? SPR: I relate to feeling priced out of communal living. I remember my college friend, Francesca, sending me this article about cohousing when I was at my most overwhelmed and depressed living alone, about six months before I ended up moving in with my parents. I had never heard of cohousing; she saw I needed communal living before I did. I remember feeling enraged, honestly, because she was like, “This is what you need!” and I felt like, Why did you make me aware of something I clearly needed but couldn’t afford?! The subtitle of the article was literally “Where Responsibilities Are Shared And Life Is A Little Less Lonely,” and I was so lonely, overworked, and just kind of crushed by the weight of life. But a few weeks into living with my parents, I realized I was living communally, just in a different way. And now, I live alone in a townhouse and my parents live across the parking lot. We have dinner at least twice a week, we share groceries, we pet-sit for each other; I take my cat there in a backpack every day to play with their cats. Some people might say I live alone, and in some ways I do, but I can still reap some benefits of communal living in an informal way and that I can afford. I’m not going to pretend that finances don’t offer access, or at least a wider ability to reimagine community. But only a few of the stories in Living, Together feature forms of intentional community that can have a high price for entry, like cohousing. That’s by design: I wanted readers to feel like they had many different models for getting their needs around communal living, and even community outside the home, met. In the book, Amanda E. Machado and Rhaina Cohen both share stories of renting homes with friends, and Amanda’s essay, in particular, talks about the cost-sharing element of that approach. Essayist Elizabeth Hart Bergstrom lives in a separate space above their parents’ house, and Dani McClain shares the house she grew up in with her mother and daughter. Gabrielle Korn has a house with an attached studio apartment she rents out, and also offers for free to writers seeking short residencies. Suanne Carlson shares a glimpse into the nomadic life, which I think more people are going to be forced to do if this housing crisis continues; for Suanne, living out of a vehicle is a choice that allows her to travel, feel at home in nature, and get together with other nomads who see home and community the way she does. Tiffany Harris writes about how she made a career out of how much she liked having roommates by supporting programming for Jewish people in their 20s who want to share living space and traditions with other young Jewish people. Simone Gorrindo writes about having an interdependent relationship with a neighbor when they were both new military wives and were incredibly lonely. All of these forms of communal living have pluses and minuses. There’s no perfect way to live, and when you’re interdependent with others, there will always be friction. If you want the kind of communal living where you have completely separate space from other people, then, yes, it could be more expensive. (Although not necessarily: tiny house communities? Fourplexes? Accessory dwelling units? Separate apartments on the same floor as loved ones?) But there are so many other ways to live communally if you think outside formal intentional communities and realize you can…make up whatever you want! [Here are some resources on making cohousing more affordable.] CM: What was the biggest surprise of working on this anthology? SPR: Working on the anthology further broadened the way I defined communal living. I really have come to believe that communal living is any way a person is regularly interdependent with others around their home base, sharing time, resources, and offering a sense of safety. Maybe it’s several generations of a family living within walking distance to one another, or neighbors who deliberately make time to see each other most days, share groceries or meals, and offer rides to doctors’ appointments. But to me, communal living is less about the roof you have over your head and more about how intentionally interdependent you are with others. Reading the Q&A with Hank Gamel and Fran Biederman in Living, Together, where Fran, who is 91, talks about all the different ways she and her neighbor support each other got me thinking about this. So did Kristen Arnett and Simone Gorrindo’s essays. Kristen’s friends, her chosen family, all live within a short drive from each other. They celebrate every milestone together and jump at the chance to offer help, like inviting those who don’t have power during hurricane season to the house of whoever does. Simone’s neighbor taught her how to drive and helped Simone furnish her house. For a period of time, they had regular dinners, watched each other’s kids, and Simone even slept at her neighbor’s house on anxious nights while their husbands were deployed. CM: What is one thing you have changed in your own life because of reading this book? A new tradition with your parents? A new way of using space? SPR: I’ve become more flexible about the way I think about home, community, and my own future. As I write in my introduction to the book, I bought pretty hard into the American dream as a kid, including the idea of a “forever home.” I think I was seeking a feeling of safety, and the finality of a “forever home” seemed to offer that. But reading my contributors’ stories, seeing their openness to different configurations of housing and home, and the way those changed as their needs did over time, has taken the pressure off of finding that one ideal living scenario. I no longer feel like I’m searching for a place to settle; I just feel more settled within myself. And it couldn’t have happened at a better time, because home and housing is so tenuous now for so many of us. It feels freeing to have my attitude be that right now I live in a two-bedroom townhouse across the parking lot from my parents. It meets a lot of my needs, but not all of them. And when I’m missing having people in my immediate space, or there’s another reason this doesn’t work as well anymore, I tell myself that this living scenario can be for me until it isn’t. When my needs change, I can adapt to them. Someday, I hope to live close to a bunch of artists who also raise kids communally, in whatever form of housing that takes. But not today, and that’s fine! #4149. Eight Fun Ways to Keep Your Summer Alive

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Backpack-and-pencil time is just about here, but it’s not too late to treat yourself to a healthy dose of summer joy.   There’s no need to feel bad about stretching that vacation feeling out as long as you can. These eight mini-vacations for the mind and spirit require no suitcases to pack (and unpack), no expensive gas tanks to fill, and no big credit card bills to pay. Not only are many of our favorite summery luxuries simple and inexpensive, research suggests they just might be good for us, too. Before you swap the flip-flops and tanks for boots and sweaters, take a moment to seize some feel-good fun. Catch a concert You don’t need expensive tickets to the hottest concerts of the summer to reap the happy rewards of listening to live music. “Collective effervescence”—the feeling of meaning and closeness with others that we experience in a positive group activity—is key to a concert’s feel-good benefits, researchers say. While listening to songs at home is fun, too, the “sacredness and connection” of collective effervescence is rooted in the togetherness of a crowd at a concert, and the uplift can continue for as long as a week after. Free and cheap local live music options abound in many areas, whether it’s an old-time swing band in the gazebo in the park, a vocalist on the patio at a nearby café, or a full orchestra playing to a grassy outdoor amphitheater. Bring a blanket, let the kids run if you’ve got any, and soak in that sweet collective effervescence. Set your sights on water—or even jump in A beach or lake vacation may not be in the plans this summer, but you can still treat yourself to a walk next to a stream or a paddle in a nearby pond. Research finds that people who regularly spend time in natural environments like parks, woods, and beaches tend to have better physical and psychological health, and water offers specific benefits to well-being. According to new research from 19 countries, water-centric “blue spaces” like rivers, lakes, and oceans bring us feelings of restoration, calm, and greater mental and emotional energy. Being near water is itself beneficial, but swimming or even wading boosts the restorative effects. My own summer favorites are regular evening walks around a small local lake and—if I can make it work—getting out on a stand-up paddle board at least once a summer. Happiness! Look up at the night sky Research on the benefits of nature has generally focused on daytime hours, but that’s beginning to change. Researchers have created a “night sky connected index” (NSCI) to measure the effects of outdoor time at night, especially looking up at the sky, and they found that people with a higher NSCI tend to have “greater mental well-being and happiness.” Looking at the night sky is also linked to feelings of awe, according to the same study, and experiencing awe can help reduce negative thinking and boost our happiness. August is Perseids meteor shower time: Every year, across the Northern Hemisphere, thousands of meteors may be visible as they streak across the night sky. As a child, my siblings and I would often sleep in the backyard in mid-August and happily count meteors while we lay on our backs in our sleeping bags. My backyard sleeping days are behind me, but I admit that lately adulthood has been so busy that I haven’t even looked up at night. Time to find the magic again. Enjoy a meal with others—outdoors No matter what outdoor activity you choose, getting outside in green space specifically (even parks and street greenery count!) is beneficial. But you can boost your outdoor fun—and health—even more by inviting others to share a meal together. Recent research finds that eating with others makes people feel more happiness and less stress, pain, and sadness that day compared to days when they only eat alone. You don’t have to host a dinner party or spring for an expensive restaurant meal. Greater Good reader Catherine Michaud shared her simple, satisfying summer recipe with us: “Find a local park with good shade trees. Bring a book, a blanket, a friend (or three), lawn chairs, picnic, and board/lawn games.” Count us in. Put up your feet while your kid climbs a tree Speaking of kids: As a kid of the 80s, I climbed countless trees with friends and siblings. But I am having trouble remembering a time when my own kids clambered up branches just to see how high they could go. A recent Australian paper reviewed many studies about adventurous play and “overwhelmingly” concluded that children engaging in risky outdoor play showed better overall mental health and social abilities and were less prone to anxiety. “Risky outdoor play” generally includes activities involving high speeds or heights, play with dangerous tools (saws, knives) or near dangerous elements (fire, water, cliffs), rough-and-tumble play, and activities in which the child could “disappear” out of adult sight. This list may make some of us (including me) a little nervous, but maybe we could at least let the kids help build, light, and tend the fire for some late summer s’mores? The authors of the study specifically caution against helicopter parenting; it’s key, they say, that the kids learn to evaluate risks themselves. This isn’t a permission slip for parents to neglect safety, but it is a reminder that this summer it’s OK for parents to turn down the worry-meter, skip the hovering, and instead sit on the patio with a book or a friend while the kids take in a joyful climb or a bike race down the driveway. Watch sports for the win This year’s World Cup excitement is over, but the sporting fun doesn’t need to end: People who watch sports, either live or on TV, tend to show a number of health benefits. A new study published in the journal Nature found that watching live sports reduces the stress hormone cortisol and increases the social-bonding hormone oxytocin. So, whether it’s an evening of Little League or the Big Leagues, and whether you’re in the middle of a cheering crowd or cozied up on the couch during a thunderstorm, take a break from the end-of-summer rush and relax into a game, win or lose.  Apple-pick your way to less stress Greater Good reader and mom Aja Russo loves taking her kids strawberry picking every year in early summer. But if you missed strawberry picking time, and you didn’t plant a garden this year (raising my own hand), it’s not too late. Start reaping the benefits of gardening at a pick-your-own farm. Research finds that even short periods of gardening can lead to feeling calmer and more relaxed. What to pick? Peach picking is still going strong in many areas, and apples aren’t just for autumn—popular varieties are ready for picking in August in much of the United States. If the climate where you live doesn’t cooperate with picking fruit right now, you can still exercise your green thumb and boost well-being by tending a plant indoors. Indulge in ice cream Yes, we said what we said. Most of us intuitively know that eating ice cream makes us feel good, but researchers were surprised when decades of observation found a lower rate of cardiovascular disease among diabetic people who ate a small amount (about half a cup) of ice cream a few times a week compared to diabetic people who never ate ice cream. Now, scientists caution that this doesn’t mean that ice cream is a health food, but it certainly suggests that we don’t need to heap a topping of guilt onto our occasional cool treats. There’s still plenty of summertime left for a sunset trip to the local ice cream parlor with friends or family. Yum! If you live in a place where the autumn chill will soon set in, now is prime time to revel in long evenings, soft temperatures, and as much summer well-being as you can seize. As the famous song goes, “Summertime, and the living is easy.” And healthy!

#4146. When Scientists Admit Mistakes, That Means Science Is Working

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

When the first cases of COVID-19 were discovered in a hospital in Wuhan, I had already studied the U.S. public health response to seven epidemics and pandemics. I am no stranger to the role of politics in pandemic response. Still, the recent Senate hearings grilling Dr. Anthony Fauci alarmed me. Fauci is a physician and immunologist who served as the director of the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases and as chief medical advisor to President Trump during the period of Covid-19—who has subsequently become a scapegoat for those who didn’t like the restrictions that came with the pandemic. As Senator Rand Paul put it, based on the evidence-free conspiracy theory that Dr. Fauci funded research that led to the pandemic: “One million Americans died from COVID and not even an apology from Dr. Anthony Fauci.” While we might recognize such claims as preposterous, it would be a mistake to treat these hearings as mere political theater. Those who are treating errors in the understanding of Covid-19 as criminal threaten one of the most important public goods we have during an infectious disease crisis: the willingness of experts to publicly admit mistakes. Dr. Fauci has openly acknowledged missteps, communication errors, and evolving judgments in responding to the COVID-19 pandemic—an honesty that is not matched by his critics. Instead, his honesty has been leveraged by them to sow doubt in science itself. But the reason we turn to experts during a crisis is not because they are omniscient. Of course they aren’t. It is because they learn faster and better than non-experts. Learning, however, is a naturally error-prone process. And learning when a novel infectious disease is fueling a global pandemic is particularly so. One can hope that scientists responding to such a crisis will be able to reduce uncertainties in a linear and predictable fashion. But that is not the norm. As a social scientist, if I discover something new, when I present my findings to my colleagues, the most likely result is that they will tell me all the ways they think I might be wrong. My job is to prove that my findings hold even after considering their counterarguments. Skepticism is essential in the process of separating fact from fiction. We have more confidence in results that have survived such pressure-testing. Experts at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) during the pandemic received just this type of skepticism from other experts. And yet much of the political commentary surrounding these normal and essential controversies cited the evidence of controversy as proof the CDC was flailing. This approach treats science as though the discovery of new facts unfolds like a treasure hunt where the first person to find the treasure opens the lid and everyone can see the treasure with their own eyes. In a treasure hunt, discovery and validation are essentially the same thing. But science does not work this way. Take the discovery of HIV. Dr. Luc Montaignier identified the virus under an electron microscope in February 1983. Uncertainty surrounding whether the virus was causative or opportunistic led to more than a year of additional research, culminating in four papers published by Dr. Robert Gallo and his colleagues in 1984, that affirmed Montaignier’s finding. According to Montaignier, it was one of these publications, in particular, that “convinced the scientific community that this new retrolentivirus was the cause of AIDS.” This example shows something that is true across the sciences. New discoveries that eventually become accepted as scientific facts can linger for an extended period as mere “claims” while uncertainties are addressed. Critics argue that the scientific uncertainty around the discovery of the AIDS virus was unnecessarily prolonged and cost lives. If moving quickly on yet-to-be-validated evidence is the right approach during a pandemic, note that Fauci and his public health colleagues are currently being attacked for doing exactly that. Public health experts believed at the outset that COVID-19 was like SARS, with transmission most likely to occur late in the course of infection. Hence the guidance that directed the public and health care workers to wear masks when caring for patients already showing symptoms. Mounting evidence of asymptomatic infection eventually proved that COVID-19 transmission was very different from SARS, leading to new masking guidance. When should public health experts act on early, but unvalidated, evidence versus taking the time to iron out uncertainties? While it may look obvious in hindsight, there is no magic formula that can sort out in advance when the better course will have been to slow down and validate versus act on plausible, but still uncertain, claims. Climate scientists would almost certainly never argue their experience in the public eye has been a picnic. But they had one big advantage over those trying to guide the response to a pandemic driven by a novel infectious disease. Climate scientists had decades—as opposed to only weeks or days—to conduct research before they were asked to explain what they knew to the public. In fact, climate scientists did not always expect warming to result from burning fossil fuels. Up until the 1950s, the prevailing wisdom was that the oceans would absorb excess carbon dioxide so that there would be minimal if any warming. Roger Revelle and Hans Seuss, who together discovered the error, both received awards for their research. When a novel infectious disease comes to town, the first cases detected are rarely the actual start of the outbreak. There are no tests to make a positive diagnosis. Some of the suspected cases turn out to have other causes. People who are infected are overlooked. Data collected at the outset is patchy and error-prone. As the public health community pivots to emergency response footing, they begin to collect data more systematically. Even so, new data can rapidly overturn early assumptions. The case definition for COVID-19 was updated almost weekly during the first 10 weeks of the pandemic. Monday morning quarterbacking is easy. Try advising countries on how to manage a growing pandemic while the ground is moving beneath your feet. As the United States’ performance during the COVID-19 pandemic is reviewed, even those who are not engaging in political grandstanding tend to reduce the effort to a scorecard that tallies the times experts were right versus the times they were wrong. Even if the reversals are few in number, they are barely tolerated. We usually give examinations to people after they have had time to learn and master the material. What is the right metric to use for public health experts who are trying to inform us when the answers are still being discovered? Speaking of treasure, I think we were lucky during COVID-19 to have public health experts who were willing to admit mistakes as soon as they were discovered. Things will go markedly worse for us if, during the next pandemic, they are less forthcoming. An earlier version of this article first appeared on MS NOW.

#4144. Do You Compare Yourself to Your Partner? It’s Not All Bad

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

After venturing through the wasteland of modern dating, it would be natural to harbor disdain for social comparison. Dating apps require you to stand out, which means you can’t just be yourself; you need to sell yourself as more appealing than the 50,000 sexy singles in your area. Putting yourself on display and knowing you’re being compared to others can feel exhausting. But once you’ve swiped right, matched, and established a lasting romantic relationship, surely the social comparisons are all in the rearview mirror…right? That couldn’t be further from the truth. Research suggests we compare ourselves to romantic partners more than anyone else in our lives—on average several times per day. In my own studies, I’ve endeavored to unpack these daily partner comparisons to understand: What kinds of people make partner comparisons, and why? The answer, it seems, depends on the type of comparisons partners are making: either based on our unique abilities, or on the opinions we hold. The good news: Comparisons can offer a learning opportunity and even bring us closer together as partners. Who’s doing better? Many partner comparisons revolve around who is better at a given activity—also known as “ability-based comparisons.” These comparisons can range from trivial (Who’s the better bowler?) to weighty (Who’s the better parent?). In my latest study with collaborators Robin Edelstein and Stephen Garcia, we studied patterns in who makes more of these ability-based comparisons. We surveyed over 1,100 U.S. adults in romantic relationships, asking them questions about how regularly they compare themselves to their partners. For instance, one question asked them, “To what extent do you pay a lot of attention to how you do things relative to how your significant other does things?” Participants also completed measures of self-esteem, relationship quality, and demographics. The results were clear: People who were younger, those with lower self-esteem, and those in shorter and less satisfying relationships were particularly likely to make ability-based comparisons. Men in relationships with men also reported making more of these comparisons than any other gender pairing, but we only surveyed 37 men in same-gender relationships, so take this finding with a grain of salt. Does my partner think differently? A second type of comparison involves comparing judgments and attitudes—known as “opinion-based comparisons.” In these comparisons, people evaluate their own opinions by comparing with those of their partners. For instance, before enthusiastically locking in a major purchase, a partner might wonder, “Would my partner consider this a good deal, too?” Similar considerations may arise in the contexts of voting, child care, or anywhere judgments lead to decisions. When it came to who was most prone to making opinion-based comparisons, our results flipped: These were most common among people in longer and more satisfying relationships. The equity-assessor and the self-improver Taken together, these individual characteristics paint two portraits of people who frequently compare themselves to their partners. The first portrait is that of a young, insecure partner who is in a newer relationship and unsure whether that relationship will work out. Think Bella Swan from Twilight who worries endlessly about whether Edward is too good for her. And, indeed, when we asked participants why they made partner comparisons, two of their motives included assessing equity and predicting the relationship’s trajectory. For instance, participants grappled with questions like, “Am I doing for her as she is doing for me?” The second portrait is that of someone in a long-term relationship who respects the knowledge and expertise of their partner and seeks to harness it as a tool for growth and decision making. For this one, think Leslie Knope from Parks and Rec who regularly compares her own judgments to those of her long-term work wife, Ann Perkins, as a means to make better decisions. This theme, too, emerged from our participants. For instance, one participant lovingly wrote about how his partner outshines him in most areas, and this dynamic continually inspires him to grow. Understanding me and you Yet partner comparison isn’t limited to these two portraits. Whereas these portraits describe those who compare most frequently, we found most people make some comparisons sometimes. What motivates occasional comparers? The two most common motives for partner comparison were striving to gain a deeper understanding of oneself and one’s partner. With respect to self-evaluation, one participant shared that they compare regularly to their partner because comparison helps them benchmark their progress toward goals. Want to know if your cooking class has paid off? One of the best ways to evaluate your growth is to see if your latest dish can delight your family in the same way that your partner’s cooking always does. Partner comparisons can also help us understand our partners’ quirks, what we call partner evaluation. For instance, one participant shared how she compares her upbringing to that of her partner, which helps her understand why her partner thinks, feels, and behaves so differently than she does. Partner comparison is a tool It’s no secret that social comparison can sting. No parent wants to hear, “I like Mom better,” and no newlywed wants to hear, “How did you land such a catch?” We find in our studies that partner comparisons are often involuntarily thrust upon people who would have preferred not to be compared. It can be true what they say, “Comparison is the thief of joy.” But comparison does more than push our emotional buttons. Partner comparisons help us assess our relationship and calibrate our behavior, such as how we divide household labor. They allow us to make important decisions, like who should take ownership over responsibilities. They serve as sources of growth, and they teach us about ourselves and our partners. Comparing yourself to your partner isn’t necessarily a sign that something’s wrong. Rather, partner comparison can be a handy strategy for making sense of your social world. The key is to use it as a tool that stays in its place, rather than letting it undermine your self-worth or relationship. Serena D’souza contributed to this article.

#4142. How Your Imagination Can Work for You or Against You

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

I have a pretty vivid imagination, sometimes for the good, sometimes not. I love it when it helps me to create new stories or songs, or when I can daydream about fun times ahead. But it can also lead to fear or worry, like when I imagine something terrible befalling the people or places I love. Is there some way to nurture the positive aspects of imagination while minimizing the problematic? According to Cassandra Vieten, director of research at the Arthur C. Clarke Center for Human Imagination, there is. In her new book, Imagine That, she explains imagination’s importance in our everyday lives—a way to understand our past, navigate the present, and explore future possibilities. If we learn more about its inner workings, she argues, we can use it more consciously and deliberately to create the kind of life we want. “Imagination is something we all possess, something we can all practice, for free and with equal opportunity,” says Vieten. “When someone develops this capacity, they gain something almost magical: the ability to see multiple futures simultaneously and navigate toward the preferred ones.” Imagination as part of everyday life Many of us tend to conflate imagination with creativity, thinking that being able to imagine new ideas is the same as bringing them to fruition. While the two may indeed be related, this is far too limited a view of what imagination can be, writes Vieten. “Imagination is an essential, foundational capacity that allows us to simulate reality and transcend it to explore possibilities beyond the known,” she says. In our everyday lives, imagination is what helps us make predictions as well as come up with novel (or creative) ideas. For example, we might imagine how stopping for a coffee on our way to work will help us focus (simulation imagination, based on past experience). But we might also use our imagination to transcend what’s known and come up with a totally new innovation or product idea once we arrive at the office (generative imagination). “Imagination weaves throughout the fabric of our cognitive, sensory, and relational lives—you are constantly speculating, visualizing, rehearsing, wondering,” she writes. These types of imagination show up differently in the brain, writes Vieten. Simulation is primarily centered in the default mode network (DMN), a part of the brain that remains active when we aren’t engaging in a specific task. The DMN is where we tend to mull over future interactions, plans, or options, often subconsciously (or even unconsciously). Generative imagination, on the other hand, comes by freeing ourselves from the DMN’s limitations (or, some would say, tyranny). If we can get out of the everyday chatter and let our minds truly wander freely around disparate parts of the brain, making new neural connections, we have a better chance of generating something truly unique. “Rather than simulating or iterating, comparing and contrasting, generative imagination is focused on combining, reconfiguring, or transcending existing knowledge to generate novel outputs,” writes Vieten. Either way, imagination isn’t frivolous, she writes; it’s important for our everyday functioning as well as creative self-expression. It helps us solve problems, while also expanding possibilities of what could be, giving us “the gift of wonder, whimsy, and surprise, the whoas and the wows of our lives,” she writes. Imagination’s darker side Imagination can sometimes be problematic, though. For example, we might imagine that our spouse is mad at us and worry about what might happen when she gets home from work when, actually, she’s not mad at all. This can cause us needless worry, not to mention potentially trigger a fight. We can also imagine that other people are unpleasant or even dangerous based on untested biases. These can be formed in part via our imagination, which tries to prepare us for unknown encounters, often behind the scenes at an unconscious level. “We regularly imagine entire narratives, scenes, and interactions without intending to or even knowing we’re doing it,” writes Vieten. Our imagination is often busy filling in the missing parts of our memories and perceptions, which are by nature incomplete. Memories, rather than being etched in our minds, are recreated (and often changed) each time they’re recalled. Similarly, we can’t perceive everything at once, so our imagination completes the picture for us, adding interpretations, too. This means it’s easy to corrupt memories or miss details of a situation—which can sometimes lead to problems (think eyewitness accounts of crimes, for example). Understanding these darker sides of imagination can help us tame them somewhat, says Vieten. “You can practice bringing greater attention to what you are imagining, recognize it as imagination, and decide whether it holds useful information, is irrelevant, or is actually causing harm.” How to increase your imaginative capabilities While the book covers the neuroscience behind imagination, it’s also about how to harness our imagination more effectively. Your personal imagination style may depend, in part, on your genetics (such as neurodiversity), culture, and life experiences. Understanding this can help you figure out where you might want to try moving the needle one way or the other. To assess your own imaginative strengths and weaknesses, here are some key imagination modes Vieten outlines in her book. 1. Receptiveness. “How willing you are to imagine—and how receptive you are to whatever bubbles up.” If you are high in receptivity, you may find it easy to do guided visualizations, for example; if low in receptivity, you may be less willing to imagine unknown scenarios. 2. Presence. How much do you embody your imagined experiences? If you tend to be absorbed, immersed, and emotionally involved in imagined scenarios, you’re high in presence; if you tend to observe these imaginings from a distance, you’re less so. 3. Embellishment. “How vivid, detailed, and elaborate people make their imaginings.” Those high in embellishment can imagine intricate scenarios with much more detail, clarity, nuance, and complexity. Those low in embellishment might be more “big picture” folks, who may not be able to conjure up details as much. 4. Originality. How much your imagination is fueled by what is or could be, based on reality, versus how much it veers from that and is based more on fantasy and novelty. 5. Temporality. How much your imagination is associated with time (in other words, assessing the past or projecting into the future) versus how much it is agnostic around time (not based on any particular time). (Vieten admits the science on this mode is not completely settled, but says there appear to be some differences in how people engage with time while imagining.) Each of these modes has pros and cons, she argues. For example, being high in receptivity may mean you’re more cognitively flexible and open to new experiences, but can also mean you’re overly compliant and easily manipulated. Similarly, the upside of being high in originality is that it can fuel advancements in art and science, though it can lead to boredom with everyday life. Considering my own strengths and weaknesses, I noticed that I seem to be high in receptivity and embodiment, but low in embellishment and originality (and mixed when it comes to temporality). Recognizing this makes me think about paying more attention to details in my environment and playing around with more fantastical ideas, especially if I’m wanting to fuel my creativity. To that end, Vieten provides avenues for stretching our imagination in different ways. Some ideas for accessing different aspects of imagination deliberately include rehearsing something before doing it, brainstorming ideas, engaging all of our senses when imagining something, reframing an idea from a different perspective, and freewriting. Vieten also suggests creating environments conducive to being more spontaneously creative. Setting time aside to daydream, being in solitude where your mind can wander, taking walks in the woods to de-stress, or, possibly, taking psychedelic drugs (in a safe, controlled environment) can all help us disengage our DMN and allow creative thoughts to enter more freely. “One of the key ways to make imagination welcome is to remove the practical pressures of the world,” she writes. Beyond self-help Imagine That is full of suggestions for unleashing your imagination, all based on the emerging science of imagination that Vieten and her colleagues (and others) are exploring. But while the book reads mostly as a self-help guide, there’s also an important message about why using our imagination is beneficial to society as a whole. Too many of us are experiencing the hopelessness and despair that come with being alive right now, writes Vieten. It’s too easy to get lost in the negative news cycles and focus on all of the problems in the world, making it more difficult to imagine anything better for our collective future. Learning how to engage our imagination more readily can create a sense of hope in our current situation. Not only can it bring more joy and aliveness to our individual lives, countering malaise, it can also lead to new ways of collaborating with others and innovating to solve societal problems, thereby providing a better life for everyone. “So many of the issues facing us locally and globally are rooted in our collective inability to imagine new solutions, alternative futures, creative pathways forward that could build a world that promotes thriving for all,” writes Vieten. “My deepest hope is that [you] take a deeper dive into the science, the practice, and the magic of imagination and [see] how it can re-enchant your life and our world.”

Movie date nights have long been a ritual of romance, from first dates to married couples’ standing nights out. You can probably name movies that leave you feeling warm, hopeful, excited, stimulated, and sometimes a little more in love with your partner. But sometimes, the movies we watch become more than that—they become conversation starters, litmus tests, or unexpected mirrors of our own relationships. These nine movies expose the underbelly of romantic relationships: the resentments, fantasies, secrets, compromises, and alternative lives that can coexist alongside love. Watching them runs the risk of cracking open a door that would be better kept closed, for the longevity of your relationship. Last month’s The Invite, starring and directed by Olivia Wilde, tops our list of nine movies you might want to watch without your partner—or at least be very sure you know what conversation you’re signing up for in the post-screening debrief. Each movie asks a question that might be safer left unanswered. 1. The Invite (2026) What happens when you say the quiet part out loud? The Invite turns the dinner-party setup into an examination of everything couples are usually too embarrassed, jealous, or afraid to say to each other. Angela (Wilde) and Joe (Seth Rogen) portray a married couple whose relationship has become strained by years of resentment and disappointment. When Angela invites their upstairs neighbors, Piña (Penélope Cruz) and Hawk (Edward Norton), over for dinner, what begins as an attempt at connection becomes a much more revealing conversation about sex, attraction, love, and what people want from their partners, when they’re being honest. Don’t cross the line. Indecent Proposal can turn a hypothetical into an uncomfortable inventory of your partner’s values—and your own. Unfaithful (2002) Can anyone be tempted, and can one handle the consequences of infidelity? Unfaithful, starring Diane Lane and Richard Gere, is a movie about a married suburban woman who meets a younger man and becomes increasingly drawn to him. There is simply attraction, followed by curiosity, a series of small choices that gradually lead down a road from which there is no return. Some people believe that infidelity happens only to certain kinds of couples—unhappy couples, disconnected couples, couples whose marriages were already in trouble. In fact, that is part of what sucks you into Unfaithful: Richard Gere’s character is a quintessentially good husband—a devoted father, attentive partner, and decent man. “I often find that the affair is a form of self-discovery. A fantasy can remain a fantasy. Marriage Story (2019) Can you love someone and still need to leave them? Marriage Story begins with a husband and wife listing the things they love about each other. It’s tender enough to feel like the opening of a great romance, but the film quickly reveals a more complicated reality. Inspired by director Noah Baumbach’s own divorce, the story explores what happens when the things we love about our partner begin to pull us in different directions. If you dare to watch this film with your partner, you’d be wise to think twice before sharing each other’s deep, dark secrets.

#4138. Three Qualities That Can Make You an Inspiring Leader

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Imagine you’re on a plane and suddenly hear an explosion. The whole plane rattles, and you see the left engine is on fire. After another loud noise, you realize there’s a hole in the side of the aircraft. As air rushes out, the plane is rapidly descending—over 20,000 feet in less than five minutes. If you faced what passengers of Southwest Airlines 1380 experienced, you would probably do what they did: frantically write goodbye messages to your family and loved ones, thinking that these are your last moments on Earth. But on that flight, the pilot, Captain Tammie Jo Shults, came on the intercom and said 10 simple words: “We’re not going down; we’re going to Philly.” The passengers would later talk about how those 10 words were transformative, turning their panic into possibility. By sharing those 10 words, Captain Shults was helping her passengers make sense of their experience and reframe the dramatic descent, from “plummeting to Earth” to a planned and orchestrated path to safety. As Captain Shults expertly landed the plane, she calmly and peacefully talked to air traffic control. After landing, she got up and went row by row, looking every passenger in the eye to make sure they were OK. People talked afterward about her words, her stature, and her empathy as she walked down that aisle. Contrast Captain Shults’s experience with another captain who also dealt with a massive hole in his vessel, Francesco Schettino, captain of the Costa Concordia. Headed out on a seven-day cruise in the Mediterranean, the ship got too close to shore. It’s debatable why that happened: Captain Schettino claims he was saluting mariners on the shore; prosecutors claim he was trying to impress his mistress who was on board. But what isn’t in dispute is what happened next: The rocks tore a 115-foot hole in the side of the ship, which caused it to eventually capsize. Before calling the Coast Guard, Captain Schettino spent time getting his story straight. The crash had caused an electrical blackout, but he was going to try to say that the blackout caused the crash. He tried to pin the responsibility on the helmsman. It took him almost an hour after the crash to sound the evacuation siren. While people were frantically trying to find safety, Captain Schettino was nowhere to be found. Finally, the Coast Guard discovered him in a lifeboat—though Captain Schettino claimed that he “accidentally” fell into the lifeboat. But he also somehow fell out of his captain’s uniform. He was sitting in a lifeboat watching his boat sinking, hiding in civilian clothing. You can hear the outrage in the Coast Guard’s voice when he says in an audio recording, “I’m gonna make you pay for this.” And Captain Schettino did pay. He was sentenced to 12 years in prison for manslaughter and abandoning ship. These two people faced very similar situations. They were both captains, dealing with holes in the side of their vessels, but how they responded to their crises was completely different. Captain Shults helped people make sense of their experience. She was courageous, competent, and calm in landing the plane, and she compassionately connected to each of her passengers. In contrast, Captain Schettino caused the accident, refused to take any responsibility, and did not take any action to help his passengers save their lives. As a result of his selfish negligence, 32 people died, and numerous people were injured. Why do some leaders create feelings of inspiration that percolate a wellspring of hope and possibility in others, while other leaders create seething cauldrons of infuriation and resentment? I’ve asked this question to tens of thousands of people across the globe, and here is what I learned about what makes a leader inspiring. Three aspects of good leadership My first big idea is that “inspiring” and “infuriating” leaders exist on an enduring continuum that is made up of three universal factors:Being a visionary, how we see the world Being an exemplar, how we are in the world Being a mentor, how we interact in the world We can inspire people in how we communicate, how we present ourselves, and how we interact with others—through our words, our actions, and our interactions. My second big idea is that these are the three factors of inspiring leadership because each one fulfills a fundamental human need. Being visionary fulfills the fundamental need for meaning and understanding. That’s what Captain Shults provided when she said, “We’re not going down; we’re going to Philly.” She helped people make sense of their experience. Being an exemplar fulfills the fundamental need for protection and passion. Captain Shults courageously protected her passengers with her calm and competence. And being a mentor fulfills the fundamental human need for belonging and status. That’s what Captain Shults offered as she walked down the aisle: She made her passengers feel recognized and seen. By surveying tens of thousands of people across the globe, I have found these three dimensions to be universal. There is not a single inspiring characteristic that is mentioned in one country that’s not mentioned in the dozens of countries in the world where I have collected data. Visionaries Effective visionaries not only offer a clear perspective on the world, but they also make their vision easy to follow and understand, and so engaging that it captures our attention. The easiest way to make something easy is to make it simple. I call this the Inception Rule, after the movie Inception, where Leonardo DiCaprio tries to plant an idea in someone’s head during a dream. He discovers it’s not about how deep you go; it’s about how simple the idea is. As a leader, you want to constantly be thinking how to reduce your ideas to their simple core. Another way that we can make things easy to understand is to repeat them, the way a chorus repeats in a song. The more people hear it, the more they will remember it. Let’s consider Zoom’s vision: Delivering Happiness. It’s simple, and bubbly. But that vision is also deeply practical—it helped Zoom’s employees efficiently make high-stakes decisions during the chaos of the COVID-19 pandemic. As millions of people began using the product for the first time, Zoom needed to decide on its product’s baseline level of functionality. Too many features would overwhelm people, but too few features would stymie people. They needed the Goldilocks baseline of features to deliver happiness. Similarly, delivering happiness led Zoom to invest heavily in customer service. Most of us know how much an hour-long wait for customer service can destroy happiness. So, Zoom hired many agents so wait times would be as short as possible. To make our visions more engaging, we can use visual language. Andrew Carton, an associate professor of management at Wharton, did a study where people were told that a company’s mission was to either “make our customers satisfied” or “make our customers smile.” Then, he had people create a product. When they were working under the more visual vision (“smile”), they created more engaging products. How to become more visionary: We can inspire people to be more visionary by having them reflect on their values. In one of my studies, we worked with a Swiss employment agency. When people came in to register for benefits, half of them were asked to reflect on their values, why those values mattered to them, and how they demonstrated them. For example, my own values include generosity, courage, and kaizen (a Japanese word for continued improvement). Two months later, the people who had reflected on their values were twice as likely to have a job. The effects were so profound that the employment agency asked us to end our study and give everyone the reflection activity. Exemplars To be a great exemplar, the first thing you want to do is to consistently embody your vision; don’t be a hypocrite. As former CEO of PepsiCo Indra Nooyi once noted, “Don’t ask someone to do something that you wouldn’t do [yourself].” Inspiring exemplars are calm and courageous protectors, like Captain Shults. And they show passion. When you ask someone to talk about a passion of theirs, their eyes light up, they smile a big beaming smile, they speak quickly and dramatically use their hands, and they lean in as if telling you a secret. When we are passionate about something, it’s revealed in our eyes, our mouth, our voice, and our bodies. And when you listen to someone’s passion, you smile, your eyes light up, and you feel the energy inside of you. In my research on Dragon’s Den, which is a variant of Shark Tank, when entrepreneurs expressed more passion—in their eyes, their voice, their hands—they were dramatically more likely to receive funding. You can’t inspire others if you aren’t inspired. How to become a better exemplar: Think about the moments in your life when you felt the most powerful and in control, your best self. Hundreds of studies around the world have used this technique that my colleagues Deb Gruenfeld, Joe Magee, and I created. We’ve even found that people are more likely to be selected for jobs if they’ve engaged in this reflection. Mentors Consider this proverb by Confucius: “Tell me and I will forget, show me and I may remember, involve me and I will understand.” When you involve people, they feel seen and heard and they feel like people, not objects. Inspiring leaders empower others everywhere across the globe. Even in cultures that are less individualistic than the United States—like Thailand or Saudi Arabia—there’s nothing more infuriating than when a boss micromanages you, rather than involves you or trusts you. Inspiring leaders also elevate others. They share credit rather than steal credit, which is one of the most infuriating things that can happen at work. Finally, inspiring leaders empathize with others. They do what Captain Shults did, in making sure each of her passengers were OK, versus only thinking about themselves, like Captain Schettino. How to become a better mentor: Reflect on the perspectives of others. My colleagues and I did a study with medical students who were tasked with diagnosing what was wrong with patients. Before meeting their patients, half the students were told to take the perspectives of their patients and to think about a recent time when they had engaged in perspective taking. Ultimately, the patients were more satisfied, trusting, and likely to follow the advice of the doctor who had been reminded to engage in perspective taking. How to become an inspiring leader My third big idea is that inspiring leaders aren’t just born; they’re also made. Because there is a universal set of inspiring attributes and behaviors, they can be practiced, nurtured, and developed. My fourth big idea is that when you’re a leader, you don’t have the option of not having an impact. Your behavior is going to inspire or infuriate. I call this the “leader amplification effect.” Everything you say and do as a leader gets amplified. Your criticisms become humiliating criticisms, but your compliments become glorious praise. My fifth big idea is that you can REIP what you sow. This phrase, drawn from the Bible, suggests that if we put inspiring energy out in the world, we receive inspiring energy in return. But my REIP is a homonym of reap, and it stands for reflect, emulate, intend, and practice:Reflect on your own inspiring and infuriating experiences as a leader once a month. Emulate: Identify the inspiring people in your life and think: How could I emulate them? Intend: Turn these reflections into an explicit intention. What specific behavior can you commit to enacting or implementing? Practice: Turn your intention into a daily practice. My final big idea is that inspiring leaders think like architects. Like an architect who designs buildings to produce particular reactions and interactions, leaders design policies and practices that lead people down the inspiring path, even when they’re not present. So instead of just changing individual behavior, you can think about: How can you change a broader dynamic? Thinking like an architect moves you from viewing a situation as a person problem—What’s wrong with this employee?—to start to think about what’s wrong in their environment, and how you can redesign that environment to help the employee be the best version of themselves. Here’s one practice that Joseph Stagliano, president of NBT Bank, designed to inspire those around him. Although he has over 1,200 employees, he sends a birthday greeting to every single person. That sounds impossible, but he does it in 10 to 15 minutes over coffee every day. The message is simple yet personalized. He showed me an example that said, “Hey Monica [pseudonym], Happy birthday! I hope you had a great weekend? How did track and bowling go?” Although his message was short, Monica’s response was extensive, overflowing with information and enthusiasm. I shared that idea with the CEO of another company, and then a month later, he told me he started doing something similar. Every morning over coffee, he reached out to one person in his organization, and he expressed either gratitude or praise. He said he did this to put a skip in their step, but they ended up sending him such glowing replies that it put a skip in his step. The CEO reaped the inspiring energy he sowed. I had lived in this building on and off for over 15 years. I was mortified. How could it be that I had nobody in my building to help with such a small task?” The invisible cost of a new address Moving ranks among the most stressful life events a person can experience, on par with divorce and job loss. But the stress isn’t just logistical; it’s psychological. Moving severs the informal ties and daily routines that quietly anchor our sense of who we are and where we belong. The result is a predictable but rarely named experience: not just loneliness, but identity dislocation. The question isn’t only “who do I know here?” but “who am I here?” To understand the scale of that loss, consider what a typical adult social network requires to build: roughly 200 hours to develop a close friend, 90 hours for a friend, and five hours of repeated interaction for each of the weak ties (neighbors, familiar faces, community contacts) that make daily life feel anchored. A typical adult carries around 1,300 hours of accumulated relationship time. When you move, you leave all of it behind and start from zero. This is also, paradoxically, what makes the first year after a move one of the most powerful windows for connection. Disruption breaks old habits and makes people unusually open to forming new ones, a phenomenon researchers call the habit discontinuity effect. People who have just moved are more likely to join things, try things, and reach out than they were six months earlier at home. What closes that window isn’t lack of desire. Research by Nicholas Epley at the University of Chicago finds that people systematically underestimate how much strangers want to connect and overestimate how awkward the attempt will be. Both the newcomer and the potential neighbor hold back, each assuming the other isn’t interested. Both tend to be wrong. And the effects of getting it right last longer than most people expect. She remembers her name, Mrs. Fisher, decades later. Creative experiments welcoming people across the country What would happen if welcoming became a civic practice instead of an individual virtue? This year, the US Chamber of Connection recruited over 250 people across 25 states to run creative experiments that aimed to help others feel more welcome in their neighborhoods. They were trained on the science of connection and worked together across the country to support each other. We call them the Welcome Committee. For many of the volunteers, they signed up because they had an experience like Liz. One shared that “when we arrived to our new town we felt welcomed, surprised, and alive through these touches, and wanted to build more.” Or as another volunteer explained, “people are still welcoming me and I still feel at home here. So it gave me this strong sense of duty to pay that forward. I wanna create spaces where people can land and feel comfortable.” For others, it came from an experience more akin to Darilys’s. “I would have loved to find this kind of thing when I moved,” shared one early volunteer. “I’ve been in my neighborhood and city since 2019 and have yet to make close friends in the neighborhood or city where I live. It feels scary and uncomfortable to get out there. But I’m realizing the huge impact of not having close friends. I’m ready to make the effort. Because if I’m feeling this way, I can’t be the only one.” They each began by inviting a neighbor for a conversation. They shared their stories of their neighborhoods growing up. Dreamed about the ideal neighborhood together. Explored the Six Points of Connection, a clear and actionable checklist for individuals and leaders to define and strengthen social bonds. And then they explored how they might together make the neighborhood more connected. They ran some creative experiments. Lindsay turned their suburban garage door into a community message board. Kaitlin created a traveling tin of homemade baked goods that moves neighbor to neighbor down the street, each recipient required to bake something and pass it on to someone new. Sarah hosted a gathering for other people named Sara(h) in her town. Courtney started flyering the neighborhood to organize a dog gathering in someone’s backyard. Nithya left a flower and a handwritten note for each of the five nearest neighbors inviting them to coffee. Many of the volunteers nostalgically tried things they remembered from their childhood or had seen in a movie. Kate moved an iron chair and table to her front step and started having her morning coffee there facing the street. Jon made lemonade with his kids and set out donuts for anyone walking by. Chet started introducing himself on his daily dog walks. Anne organized a multi-household yard sale across eight houses. And many of them hosted potlucks and block parties. They all expressed knowing “how meaningful it can be to walk into a new space and find someone who is genuinely glad you are there.” And they wanted to make people feel that way. Four ways to support newcomers As the volunteers conducted their experiments, they began to share their findings. They mirrored much of what we have learned from social scientists over the last 50 years. Four themes emerged that help to inform how we can all do more to help new residents, and any neighbor, feel more welcomed. 1. The first step is the most important. Stop playing “invite chicken.” Many of our volunteers shared a version of the same experience: sitting at home hoping someone would call and invite them to hang out, attend an event, or even just watch Netflix together. They assumed that the people they wanted to call were probably busy or uninterested. Those people were likely sitting at home thinking the same thing. Nobody called. Everyone stayed alone. Call it invite chicken: a standoff where everyone loses despite everyone wanting the same thing. The barrier isn’t reality; it’s anticipation. Someone just has to go first. And sometimes, when we do break the game of chicken, we don’t get the response we wanted. When Hazel hosted her first neighborhood coffee gathering, one neighbor texted that he was too nervous to come. She didn’t give up. She texted back encouraging him. He came. At one of Elizabeth’s gatherings in San Diego, a man had been dragged along by his wife, who promised they’d stay just five minutes. He was the last to leave. We are poor forecasters of our own social experiences. People overweight the discomfort of initiation and underweight the pleasure of connection. The host who sends the follow-up text, the wife who insists on five minutes, isn’t being pushy. They’re correcting for a cognitive error the reluctant person can’t see from the inside. 2. We are more motivated working together. Partner with someone. Erin hosted a neighborhood gathering in New York City and reflected afterward on what she’d do differently. Her top lesson: “I appreciated the help I did get from neighbors and wished I’d had a co-planner to share more of the work with.” This is consistent with what research finds: People are significantly more likely to initiate and sustain kind and helpful behavior when they’re doing it alongside someone else. A partner doesn’t just make the work easier; they make follow-through more likely, because the social cost of backing out now involves someone else. Several Welcome Committee volunteers named the US Chamber of Connection cohort itself as the thing that got them out the door. Not the training, not the tools, but the fact that others were doing it, too. 3. We gain comfort with repeat exposure. Build a rhythm. Hansen has been hosting a monthly porch gathering (coffee, bagels, same spot) for two years. Recently, he watched neighbors spontaneously make plans to go cycling together and quietly organize to look out for a boy on the street whose father is dying of cancer. Neither of those things was planned. They happened because the same people had been attending his events and they had built comfort with each other. Hansen didn’t suggest they help the boy but had created the environment for it to be possible. Research finds that repeated contact with the same faces makes us like and trust them more, independent of what’s said. Those repeated encounters build weak ties, which are how people find help in a crisis, how trust spreads beyond close friends, how a community starts to look out for itself. The first gathering gets you in the door. Showing up again is what builds the neighborhood. 4. Spontaneous actions of connection are powerful. Look for opportunities. The best welcoming often isn’t planned. When a neighbor left cookies on Kaitlin’s porch with a note that just said “happy spring,” the gesture was small, spontaneous, and agenda-free. It didn’t just make her smile; it made her want to pass something on. Walking through her neighborhood, Annie spotted a moving van, walked straight over, and introduced herself as a Welcome Committee member. The mover, who didn’t even live there, told her he wished he could move into the community. Another volunteer simply invited people they saw running to join their running group. They were seen and welcomed. What these moments share is spontaneity and clarity of intent. Research finds that unexpected gestures create a positive disruption that formal invitations don’t. They bypass the social calculation people run when a stranger approaches, and they signal warmth without asking for anything back. Research finds that being noticed is the precondition for feeling like you belong. And having a clear role removes the ambiguity that makes first moves feel risky. When Annie said “I’m a Welcome Committee member,” her motives were transparent before she said another word. You don’t need a plan. You need to look up. Connection is a lifestyle—a rewarding one Giving to others produces more sustained well-being than spending on yourself. The helper’s high is real. Welcoming a newcomer isn’t just a form of service; it’s one of the highest-return social investments you can make. Liz knows this firsthand. The woman who arrived in the Bay Area not knowing a soul, wondering if anyone would notice if she disappeared, is now the person who creates spaces for others to land. “People here took a chance on me,” she says. “So it gave me this strong sense of duty to pay that forward. I want to create spaces where people can land and feel comfortable.” All friendships begin the same way: by talking to strangers. Someone just has to go first. Years later, Liz became the person who notices.

Working with Fabrizio and her team, students created and conducted a nationally representative survey of 1,610 U.S. adults that oversampled Gen Z to provide a more granular look into their political attitudes and opinions. Fabrizio, who has taught the course for three years, has found that working with students surfaces concerns that standard polling may miss. “If we want to know what Gen Z’s political beliefs and attitudes are, we should let our students, as members of Gen Z, use their intuition to decide what matters in a survey,” Fabrizio explained. Working in small groups, students were allocated several minutes of survey time to explore topics such as the political influence of online content promoting masculinity—aka the “manosphere”—the religious revival in Gen Z’s lives, and their beliefs about the American dream. Fabrizio guided students step by step through survey design, showing them how to craft research questions, field the survey, analyze data, and, finally, present their findings. Students also examined the academic literature on polarization’s effects in American politics, such as how it contributes to political gridlock, drives support for political violence, and erodes trust in American institutions. “There are many legitimate divisions in politics over competing policies and values, but polarization often drives us apart unnecessarily,” said Fabrizio. “Young Americans will have to find their own ways of bridging partisan differences and overcoming misconceptions about one another. For her research project, Galperin examined the role of social media in inflating Gen Z’s hostility toward people from the opposing political party, a phenomenon political scientists call “affective polarization.” “We thought Gen Z would be more polarized and misinformed because we get so much of our news on social media compared to older generations,” Galperin said. Taken together, actively using social media seems to harden Gen Z’s feelings more than it distorts basic facts. We have a long path ahead,” said Urogdy, who said he is optimistic. #4132. The Overlooked Ways Love Shows Up in Urban Spaces

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Over the past two decades, my doctoral students and I have focused on studying love, care, kindness, and compassion in urban settings. Why? Because social science research on life in America’s cities is desperately incomplete. Much like the vision of urban life that permeates most of American popular culture, social science research imagines urban areas as sites of overwhelmedness, disengagement, social and moral crisis, threat, and violence. But, as someone who has lived and worked in large urban centers—New York City, Chicago, Detroit, Istanbul—I know firsthand that urban centers are also sites of human virtue. People in urban contexts, including those living in high-stress, oversurveilled, underresourced communities, draw daily from deep wells of love, compassion, empathy, kindness, and generosity in the service of caring for each other. From holding doors open for one another, to offering seats on buses and trains, to stopping to help with directions, to paying unexpected compliments, city-dwellers create environments that are saturated with powerful but easily missed acts of care.  My former students and I have been eager to understand how love, generosity, and care manifest and operate in the hyperdiverse, densely populated, loud, fast-paced complexities of urban spaces. Through surveys and interviews, we have sought to understand how adults residing in big cities define love, how they see love being expressed, and their views of the cascading effects of giving and receiving care, generosity, and love.  We have learned that the decision to express love and care for others in large cities is not simply a moral or emotional decision. We offer two examples of the lens needed to notice the presence of love in cities. First, we have learned that identities matter in experiences of compassion, love, and care between strangers. Urban dwellers’ identity (for example, race or gender), lived experience, context, familial, cultural, and legal systems’ realities work together to shape decisions about whether to extend acts of love to people you do not know. For example, Kehinde, a 26-year-old African American man living in a large, densely populated city, described his experience of giving and receiving kindness, care, and love in his city as shaped by how he navigates other people seeing him as a Black man. “I will see folks all the time that may need a hand with their bags or crossing the street or just little things like that. And I take a second, hesitate,” Kehinde says. “I don’t want to scare them. I don’t want to come off as a threat.” To get through these moments, Kehinde describes engaging in a practice of analysis and shape-shifting. Before he steps in to offer help, he assesses the environment and changes the way he carries himself so that people will perceive him as a possible helper rather than as a threat. He says: “I smile a lot more. My voice might change a bit.” He might shrink his body and keep his head down, to avoid seeming intimidating. After helping someone, he moves along without lingering. Reflecting, he acknowledges not being his full, true self in these interactions. “I have to realize I’ve been socialized—made to believe that this is what I need to do in order to navigate the situation.” Kehinde’s story highlights the point that identities matter in economies of care. The images and stereotypes that we construct about people come at a cost even in moments of compassion. Stereotypes affect who we perceive as deserving of care, concern, and protection. Stereotypes also inform our expectations about who can be trusted to help. Stereotyping is not unique to urban settings. But because urban settings tend to be especially diverse spaces, they create abundant opportunities to study how identities and the social scripts we hold affect the way that strangers care for each other across lines of difference. What Kehinde and others bring to the fore is the reality that the targets of stereotyping are aware of the ways that they are perceived. They learn that to be seen as helpful rather than as threatening, they need to read situations correctly, make adjustments to their approaches (e.g., keep “appropriate” distance), and exit situations strategically. Targets of stereotyping must strike a balance between their own deep-seated motivations to behave caringly and their desire to stay safe. For individuals like Kehinde, miscalculations can be costly. If, in the effort to behave lovingly and compassionately, someone is misidentified as a threat, that can lead to a range of consequences from being rebuffed and embarrassed to becoming the target of violence, arrest, or death. The end result is that people consciously or unconsciously learn the rules of engagement around helping strangers. A second lesson that has emerged from studying love in urban settings is that love lives in the built environment of cities. I highlight this lesson in my Positive Psychology in Urban Settings classes. Cities are often saturated with murals, billboards, and symbols that convey messages of love, solidarity, and belonging. As residents move through neighborhood streets, they may encounter signs prominently posted in shop windows or near the entrances of religious institutions that remind them that “Black Lives Matter,” “Love Is Love,” or “Immigrants Are Welcome Here.” People display rainbow flags and national flags from apartment windows, on cars, and in other spaces, declaring both their own identities and pride for and solidarity with those identities. The architecture of urban spaces may also play a role in expressions of love and care. In many urban neighborhoods, porches, stoops, and front steps serve as places of connection and care. Simple greetings (e.g., “Morning, baby!,” “You doing good?”) voiced by neighbors sitting on their steps serve as ways of spatializing love, care, and connection in urban landscapes.  I return here to my initial point. Our studies of urban areas are desperately incomplete and will remain incomplete until we more fully examine how various forms of human goodness—including love and care—flourish in urban settings. Likewise, our understandings of love and care will remain underdeveloped until we explore how these experiences emerge in the most densely populated, diverse, complex social structures in which people live. We will truly understand love when we understand how it is titrated through place, context, history, culture, religion, structures of power, identities, and a host of individual and relational factors. Urban centers give us powerful opportunities to know love.

#4128. Can Artificial Intelligence Predict the Right Partner for You?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Finding love is big business (a multi-billion–dollar industry), and artificial intelligence is transforming that business. Dating apps are increasingly outsourcing user matching to AI. Recent examples range from Overtone, an AI-powered matchmaking service started by the former chief executive officer of Hinge, to SciMatch, a dating app that aims to use AI to detect compatibility from a single selfie. But can AI actually help people find love? The promise of AI is to go deeper and end the days of endless swiping and unreturned messages. Now people can talk to AI about their hopes, dreams, and ideal partner preferences and AI can scan the pool of dating app users to find candidates who fit those criteria exactly. AI can also track behavior in the app to learn what people really want (something behavioral scientists call revealed preferences). As a relationship scientist, I see both peril and promise in this new generation of dating apps. Despite long-heralded promises by dating app companies that their apps are based on relationship science, the truth is that there is no strong foundation of science upon which to build these matching algorithms—relationship scientists do not know what makes two particular people compatible with each other. Not yet, anyway. Sure, we know some of the factors that generally predict liking—physical attractiveness, positive personality traits, similarity, familiarity, reciprocity—but we have yet to predict with the kind of certainty dating apps might like to claim whether any two people will hit it off before they even meet. So if a robust science of compatibility doesn’t exist yet, what is the AI matching based on? One of the first AI dating apps, Fate, uses “similarity and reciprocity of personality,” according to an interview with the founder. That sounds reasonable, and research from Amanda Montoya and her colleagues looking across 313 different studies of similarity and attraction does suggest that similarity may predict attraction—before people meet. Once people experience even a brief interaction, similarity has much less, if anything, to do with how much two people like and are attracted to each other, as seen in an analysis of actual and perceived similarity of personality traits in potential partners, by Sarah Humberg and her colleagues. A systematic inquiry from my research lab, led by Annika From, also yielded little evidence that romantic couples are happier if partners are more similar to each other. What about “reciprocity of personality”? If this phrase refers to the idea that two people may have complementary personalities that meld well together, this theory of compatibility, unfortunately, has even less empirical support. Perhaps it is not too concerning that we lack a strong, agreed-upon scientific basis for predicting compatibility. After all, one of the great promises of AI is that it will learn as it goes, based not just on what a user says they want, but how they actually behave (who they swipe right on, whom they actually chat with after matching). Emerging evidence from different fields suggests that AI may be able to detect complex patterns that humans have been unable to discern. In the medical field, AI has been used to create more precise and accurate predictions that are aiding diagnosis and treatment. From weather prediction to fraud detection, the use of AI has enhanced forecasting. So maybe it can do the same for our love lives? If the problem has simply been that we have not been able to identify the complex patterns that make up human compatibility, then AI may, in fact, be the answer. If, however, the problem is that humans themselves are too complex, then AI may not get us any closer to being able to forecast love. What do I mean by this? Well, researchers have found that people will list their preferred partner traits before taking part in a speed dating event, only to have all those preferences go out the window when they actually interact with people. In my own work, I have discovered that people will dismiss another user’s dating app profile for reasons like “same name as my brother” and “bad haircut.” Yes, they will rule someone out as the possible love of their life because of something as irrelevant as their name (give them a nickname!) or hairstyle (arguably our most changeable physical characteristic). Why do I bring up these examples? They raise the question of whether AI even has the right information to rely on when gathering data based on people’s stated preferences or swiping behavior. So what data might AI need? In my lab, we hope to find some answers to this question with a series of studies that aim to combine dating-app data with real-life interactions. This project will allow us to see what chemistry looks like online and offline, uncover who has online chemistry that translates into real-world sparks, and identify the factors that make chemistry more likely between two people (if there are any meaningful predictors). The truth is, researchers at the forefront of this work do not always agree on whether forecasting love is even possible. While many relationship scientists have devoted their careers to testing the complex ways in which personalities and preferences interact to predict compatibility, they have arrived at different conclusions. Some researchers believe it will be possible to build an algorithm to predict compatibility; these researchers would likely see the promise in AI. On the other hand, there are those who have concluded that compatibility is a dynamic process that evolves as two people interact with each other and cannot be predicted ahead of time. For these researchers, finding love is less about the perfect match and more about being in the right place at the right time and then building love together through shared moments, from special nicknames and in-jokes to regular rituals just the two of you share. With the research projects in my lab, we hope to test these theories by having people meet different numbers of times. We’ve seen from our early work that pairing two random people together does not typically produce high levels of chemistry. That instant attraction seems to be relatively rare; but what if they met repeatedly? Could chemistry develop? Does it need some sort of initial spark to set it off, and can we create that spark with the right equipment, such as a well-placed question or activity that brings two people together? In other words, is chemistry possible with anyone if the right conditions were met, or is there some, as yet undiscovered, set of requirements for two people to be able to fall in love? I don’t know the answer to these questions yet, nor does the field of relationship science, although many have theories. I see AI as both an exciting tool that might help us answer this question as well as a technology that people looking for love should employ with caution. When used as a way to explore and test models of compatibility, AI may yield invaluable insights. But we must always remember that AI is trained with specific beliefs about what makes two people compatible. If researchers do not yet know what makes two people compatible, then letting AI do all the work to vet potential partners means you are only going to see the people that AI thinks are right for you, while likely remaining in the dark about how AI decided they’re a good match. This gap in knowledge may keep you from meeting someone unexpectedly compatible with you. An alternative approach to dating is to think about it as a time for exploration. Instead of only connecting with people who have similar facial structures or reciprocal personalities, try dating people with all kinds of different experiences, personalities, beliefs, and desires. If you go this route, Paul Eastwick offers a valuable piece of advice in his recent book Bonded by Evolution: for those who have serious dealbreakers (e.g., you know you want children, have strong religious views, or do not eat meat), go ahead and surround yourself with people who share those views. This will help you avoid facing potentially difficult lifestyle tradeoffs in the future if you do find yourself falling in love. But don’t eliminate someone just because of a bad haircut or a shared name. Instead, be open to exploring and then see for yourself who you connect with and who you do not. You might end up being surprised.

Parenting a teen can sometimes make you nostalgic for the early years, when you could still scoop up a frustrated toddler and redirect her with bubbles. In the teen years, sweet hugs can provoke eye rolls; a well-intentioned “How was your day?” may be misconstrued as meddling. That’s just your teens becoming independent. Decades of research finds that portraying adolescence as a period of “storm and stress” is not only misguided, it’s harmful. In fact, developmental science shows this time of life is better recast as “the breakthrough years.” According to research on over one thousand adolescents by Ellen Galinsky, author and president of Families and Work Institute, there are key messages that teens want to get through to adults: Teens want adults to seek to understand their development and listen and talk with them and not at them. They want grown-ups to stop stereotyping them and appreciate that they’re trying to understand themselves and their needs, too. Finally, teens want adults to know that they are drawn to learn skills like goal setting, perspective taking, collaborating, problem solving, and meaning making. None of this is easy, of course. Along the way, tough situations crop up, and it’s hard to know what to do. Here are common questions, with answers backed by scientists and their research. Q: My 16-year-old doesn’t seem to be motivated to do anything beyond hanging out with friends and playing video games. When I bring up the prospect of college, he says he doesn’t care. What should I do? A: As parents, we want to set our high schoolers up for success. But, at that age, young people are still trying to figure out who they are, what interests them, and what their future looks like. “Identity development and purpose formation are underway, but rarely fully formed by high school,” says Kendall Cotton Bronk, professor of psychology at Claremont Graduate University.  Not finding any answers yet is expected. “Parents need to remind themselves that where their child is at this moment in time isn’t necessarily where they will be in the future,” says Bronk. To help teens along, encourage them to try different activities at school and in the community, whether it’s, for instance, robotics, the debate team, chorus, improv, or the school paper. Leave it to them to select what to try. Don’t do it for them. Don’t judge their choices. “While it’s not appropriate for parents to be too directive, they can help their teenager reflect on what they liked and didn’t like and what felt meaningful and not so meaningful about their various experiences,” says Bronk. “Having someone to debrief with can help them deepen their reflection and more quickly identify a purposeful direction.” Research suggests that when teens are guided by their own interests, free of external pressures, they’re more likely to take charge of their own career development. But when parents are overly involved in their adolescents’ lives, says Bronk, young people don’t have an opportunity to cultivate a sense of agency, and this can make career development planning a big challenge. Instead of trying to manage their path, says Bronk, parents should simply “be curious.” Ask your child: What interests you? What feels satisfying for you? “This kind of questioning can scaffold and deepen their young person’s reflections, and reflection is a key component of figuring out what kind of path a young person wants to follow,” she says. “Usually, what holds their interest is something they’re good at and care about, and that allows them to make a meaningful difference in the broader world,” she explains. Taking advantage of volunteer opportunities can help young people see themselves as useful and discover careers that might interest them—for instance, helping out in a food pantry, animal shelter, a kids sports organization, or hospital. Encourage your teen to ask people he knows about their jobs, what they entail, and what they studied to get there. Finally, don’t assume that your child’s lack of direction is apathy. Whatever their interest is, “work with it, not against it,” says Bronk. “At the same time, be patient. It may just be the case that they haven’t figured it all out yet. Their timetable may not be the same as yours, but they will eventually get there. Remember, this isn’t a race.” Q: My 15-year-old seems to be living a separate life. He spends most of his time in his room and has dinner more often with his friends than with us. How can we encourage him to be a part of the family? A: Seeking independence (while still navigating being dependent on adults) is one of the primary tasks of adolescents as they transition to becoming more self-sufficient into early adulthood. “I try to get parents to understand that it’s not about them,” says Laurence Steinberg, author of You and Your Adolescent. “The parents didn’t do anything wrong; the child isn’t rejecting them. This is a sign they’re growing up normally. It’s partly wanting to make their own decisions and have their own life, but it’s also wanting emotional independence—to show that they are more than their parents’ kid.” Chances are a closed door doesn’t mean trouble. Just as most adults don’t want others listening in on their calls, teens don’t want that either. They’re not distancing themselves from you, but simply distancing themselves from being a child. “There’s nothing the parents can do to stop this from happening,” says Steinberg, a professor emeritus of psychology and neuroscience at Temple University. “And they shouldn’t try.” That said, spending time with family is also important. To suggest that, say something like, “We want to spend more time with you, but we also know that spending time with friends is important. What’s the best way to catch up with you?” By making the planning of it a collaborative process, your child is more likely to stay engaged. Research suggests that they may even find the activity more enjoyable. Only rarely is a shut bedroom door cause for concern—for instance, you notice other behavioral changes, such as a drop in grades, a dwindling interest in his usual in-person activities. If you suspect an issue, let your child know that you’re there for him, and that he can get professional help, too. One last note: If your child is spending most of his time in his room online, setting limits on its own may not be enough, says Jacqueline Nesi, author of the Techno Sapiens newsletter and associate professor of psychiatry and human behavior at Brown University. You’ll also want to discuss with them what you want for him and what he wants. The evidence also supports “co-viewing” or “co-using” technology with your kids, whether that’s a video game, TV, or an AI chatbot, says Nesi. Using tech together can spark conversation and help parents better understand their kids’ digital lives. That said, it’s still important for you to protect their sleep and to make sure that they live a life in person. Again, to get their buy-in, decide on screen-time parameters, and replace screen time with in-person activities he might enjoy, such cooking a favorite meal together or an outing with family and friends. Q: My 17-year-old daughter recently suffered a breakup. How can I help her feel better without intruding? A: Even if a relationship doesn’t look like a monogamous romantic relationship in adulthood (it may be short, or the partners may not talk to each other very much), these breakups can still be painful. So always take your teen’s romantic activities seriously, as well as the emotions they are experiencing following a breakup, says Emily Bibby, a researcher at the Relationship Development Center at Stony Brook University. Be compassionate, validating, and nonjudgmental. Let her know that she is having a normal reaction to a difficult situation. Allow space to grieve and vent. “Listen hard to what your child is thinking and feeling,” says Barry Farber, professor of psychology and education at Columbia University Teachers College. “Ask what they’ve learned from the relationship. Framing the relationship as a learning experience, as opposed to a failure, can help nurture resilience and personal growth, and can help her feel more in control and more hopeful about future relationships.” As Farber and his coauthors point out in a recent study, while time doesn’t necessarily “heal all wounds” (because memories and thoughts can linger), it almost always “softens the blow” when other strategies are also employed, such as talking with a trusted friend or family member. While the study involved mostly white, female adult subjects, the same principle generally applies across ages, genders, and ethnicities. Letting your child know that things will get better can help. Trying to convince a teen to do anything is difficult, says Farber, so it’s better to simply listen, and then only encourage as opposed to insisting or dictating. Encourage her to engage in activities she enjoys, whether it’s treating herself to an afternoon manicure, going to a concert, or spending time in nature like hiking and walking on the beach. Encourage her to avoid all contact with her ex, both in person and on social media. If one person believes it’s really over, a clean break is usually best, says Farber. Any surge of adrenaline that happens when you see your ex in any form is only temporary, and only makes it harder to get over the breakup. If sad feelings are impairing day-to-day life—she’s not seeing friends, she’s failing her classes, there’s a dramatic change in eating or sleeping—have her consider seeking help from a mental health professional. “Therapy normalizes the experience,” says Farber. “It helps people find a new narrative when they’re stuck.” Therapy also gives people an opportunity to share their feelings in a private space and learn evidence-based emotion-regulation skills to improve their mental health and functioning, says Bibby. If your teen is debilitatingly depressed or harming herself, then advise that she get professional help as soon as possible, such as from her school psychologist, her primary care doctor (for a referral), or a therapist you think highly of. To help their teen navigate relationships in general, parents can model healthy relationships with not just a partner, but also friends and family members. Teaching your teen effective communication skills and boundary setting early on, in the context of friendships and family relationships, can prove helpful once your teen starts dating, says Bibby, who recently coauthored a research review on romantic relationships and mental health in adolescents. And as for parents of teens who are just becoming interested in dating, it’s helpful to talk about what healthy relationships look like, emphasizing the importance of mutual respect. “Thinking through what they would do [in different dating scenarios] and how to navigate different challenges that may arise can build confidence when they start dating,” says Bibby. Q: My 14-year-old has found herself in a friend group that spends a lot of money on expensive clothing and electronics. We don’t approve of these teens and don’t share their consumerist values. How do we prevent our child from becoming materialistic, too? A: You can empower your teen to make thoughtful, confident choices about money, friendships, and the pressures they face. Try leading with connection rather than control: Talk openly about your family’s values, help them practice setting boundaries, and remind them they do not need to match their friends’ spending to belong or feel worthy. Expensive tastes and social media pressures can be hard to ignore, but your teen can learn to make choices that reflect what matters most to them. People turn to materialistic things to fill a void, but that’s only a temporary fix, says Lan Nguyen Chaplin, a professor of integrated marketing communications at Northwestern University, and an expert in children’s and teens’ consumer behavior. “They end up in a vicious cycle of wanting and needing more to fill the same void because material things cannot fill real voids.” In her research exploring materialism in a group of 12- to 18-year-olds, she reported that those with high self-esteem were less likely to have materialistic tendencies. “Parents and peers [are] a source of emotional support and psychological well-being,” she writes. “Supportive parents and peers boost adolescents’ self-esteem, which decreases their need to turn to material goods to develop positive self-perceptions.” To that end, start at home and help your kids feel good about themselves. Gratitude journals can be a good way to curb materialism, but, of course, asking a teen who is new to journaling to start one can feel like a homework writing assignment if it’s yet another thing they are required to do. Rather, approach it with a light touch. Model gratitude by sharing with your teen who and what you’re grateful for, keep a gratitude journal yourself, or ask them what the high point of their day is. It also helps to stop tying accomplishments to material things. So, instead of, say, rewarding a good report card with money or sneakers, celebrate the hard work that was put into achieving the good grades over a fun experience together like a movie night at home. Most of all, spend time with your teen in lighthearted and playful ways, no matter how short. Even a 10-minute car ride can be a good opportunity to connect with them, and also reinforce the good things they have going for them, Chaplin says.

#4124. As People Live Longer, How Can We Live Better?

When the Stanford Center on Longevity launched in 2007, founding director Laura Carstensen spent a lot of time correcting misperceptions. “The initial response people had was, ‘Like the Methuselah Foundation? You are going to extend life?’” Carstensen was clear that the center’s aim was not to increase lifespans—which had nearly doubled during the 20th century—but rather to build societies that accommodated that new and unprecedented reality. Another persistent misperception was that “longevity” is a euphemism for aging and that the Stanford Center on Longevity was a center on aging. As Carstensen noted often, “Longevity is not synonymous with aging. It is a metric of length.” The center’s interest is in the challenges and opportunities that longer lives present. While the center is keenly interested in older people, in Carstensen’s view, “longer lives have implications for every stage of life, and the roots of many problems faced at advanced ages have their roots much earlier in life.” The center’s mission is to use longer lives to improve quality of life at all ages. In 2019, under Carstensen’s leadership, the center launched the New Map of Life initiative, designed to rethink the human life course in response to skyrocketing life expectancies, and it has trained nearly 40 postdoctoral fellows, mentored by faculty affiliates spanning multiple disciplines, about the ways in which longevity is transforming societies, cultures, and economies. After nearly 20 years, Carstensen, the Fairleigh S. Dickinson Jr. Professor in Public Policy and a professor of psychology at Stanford, will step down this summer from her role at the Stanford Center on Longevity to pursue a new project alongside her research and teaching. I spoke with Carstensen about what she’s learned along the way and what’s next. This interview has been edited for length and clarity. Karen Breslau: What’s changed since the center’s early days? Laura Carstensen: I hope it’s clearer that the aging process has not changed much, if any, genetically over the last several thousand years. Rather, longevity—the length of life—increased, and that has enormous implications for nearly all aspects of our lives. The modifiability of aging, on the other hand, is also clear. Even though we’re essentially the same physiological beings that our ancestors were, we changed the world in ways that are allowing us to live healthier and longer. That’s why we are confident that we can build a world where the majority of people live well for a century. KB: You are on the scientific advisory council to the Health District of Singapore, which is building the Queenstown Health District, a very intentional all-ages community that is incorporating principles from the New Map of Life. What are you seeing? LC: The Singapore government has given a group of scientists the freedom to design a neighborhood in ways that support long-term health and intergenerational connections, continued financial security, and exercise. They’re thinking about all the factors that contribute to long, healthy, and engaged lives and literally programming them into the community. What’s really cool about the Queenstown Health District is that it can provide a proof of concept that social and physical designs can improve long life. Already they have built parks and playgrounds with swings for the kids and balance beams for older residents, to provide shared spaces where different ages interact. When we advisers fly in from different countries, we’re starry-eyed at what they can do. Singapore can do things like this in part because they’re smaller, and their government can act quickly. For example, the government recognized about a decade ago that people weren’t saving enough for retirement. So they flipped a switch and said, “OK, everybody’s putting in more money into their retirement account.” KB: If you had to give a grade to where the U.S. is in implementing the New Map of Life principles, could you take out your red pen? LC: Compared to some countries, like Singapore, I’d give the U.S. a C-minus, and I am used to grade inflation! The federal government has done little to improve longevity. Unlike the Singapore government, which can require people to save more for retirement, for example, the U.S. cannot quickly implement such programs. On the other hand, in part because the government contributes less, we see a lot of innovation coming from the private sector. We have entire industries offering new products and services. So I’m optimistic about what the U.S. can contribute to longevity. At a global level, it’s fabulous to think about the different approaches that can come from the public and private sectors in a range of countries. KB: Longevity has gone mainstream and led to billions of dollars in market interest. How has that changed the center’s work? LC: It’s true that vast investments are going to longevity. Most of these funds are directed at ways that individuals can live longer. There isn’t, in my experience, much philanthropic interest in this space aimed at helping people live better as we live longer lives. I am discouraged that we are sleepwalking through this seismic demographic shift without investing more in the opportunity to rewrite the life scripts that have guided life and imagining ways that we could use them to improve life. It’s also discouraging that as longevity has entered public consciousness, much of what we hear about are pitches for questionable products that supposedly make people live longer. There are so many snake-oil salesmen in this space that it’s hard for consumers to distinguish what is evidence-based and what is not. KB: As you often say, transitions are a feature of longer lives. What’s happening next for you? LC: I’m going to focus more on research and teaching, but first I’m going on sabbatical and look forward to having more time for the things I love—my garden, being at home, sitting in the sun, going for walks. I want to take Pilates lessons. I’m going to take a break in my schedule to say, “How do I create a healthy lifestyle going forward and build the rest around it?” While I hopefully will have a more relaxed schedule, during my sabbatical I’ll be developing an online course called The Fundamentals of Longevity, which will tell the story of longevity and the potential to live longer, better lives, a change we really need in the world. The course will be accessible far beyond Stanford, so that we can help frame the conversation around longevity and help people appreciate the extraordinary promise of science, technology, and social change to improve life. There is a crisis on the horizon if we don’t change the way we live. We have the opportunity to change our lives for the better. We have opportunities no previous generation of humans in history has had, and we need to start implementing them. This story was originally published by Stanford Center on Longevity. #4120. You Might Be Experiencing Rustout, Not Burnout, at Work

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Some forms of distress at work are easy to recognize because they interrupt someone’s daily performance and the work of those around them: For example, a person on your team becomes visibly exhausted, they are irritable, or they’re frequently absent. But other forms of distress are harder to see precisely because the work continues uninterrupted. It’s common to talk about burnout at work and to recognize it in others and in ourselves. But a new paper of mine with David S. Smith examines a less visible and less understood form of disengagement at work: rustout. Rustout, as we studied it, often involves being underutilized, intellectually stagnating, and feeling a deep-seated sense of unfulfillment. It can arise when people feel their work no longer offers enough meaning or challenge to keep them intellectually and creatively engaged. But because it’s different from burnout, the solutions are also different—both for individuals and for organizations. Rustout vs. burnout I’ve presented this research at numerous conferences, and each time I have been approached by professionals who say, “That’s what I’ve been going through, but I never had a name for it until now.” These people, young and older, are committed to their work, they are hardworking, and they haven’t stopped caring. It is often the case that they care very much, and they meet expectations set before them. They are often the ones who are called upon because they are reliable and highly conscientious. If you look around your workplace, you probably know many people who fit this bill. The problem is not a loss of effort on the part of the individual; it is a breakdown in the relationship between effort and meaningful work. From my study of this topic, rustout is really about the decline in professional vitality, when a person’s capacities are not used in ways that feel meaningful to them. In our research, participants described experiences shaped by administrative overload, reduced autonomy, and a misalignment between their professional aspirations and assigned tasks. Many people highlighted feeling professionally stuck: “Sadly, the elements I love about the job . . . are often overshadowed by the other administrative aspects of the role,” “I feel like I’m feeding the machine,” and “I’m a square peg in a round hole.” They highlighted a sense that the work they were doing no longer drew sufficiently on their talents: “The better you are at it, the more of the administrative side of the job you get. It’s lacking opportunities to be creative.” Rustout can be quite psychologically disorienting. From the outside, a person may appear successful and competent. There is no question about their productivity; they meet set targets, but could do so “with [their] eyes closed.” However, they may feel increasingly detached from the work that once animated them. Their distress isn’t dramatic; it’s a slow process of psychological erosion. In many ways, rustout overlaps with burnout, but they are not the same. Burnout involves prolonged negative stress that isn’t adequately managed. It’s about too much—working too much, caring too much, too much pressure, and too little time to recuperate. In contrast, rustout is associated with too little positive stress or challenge over time, too little stimulation, and limited opportunities for individual growth. However, while burnout is part of organizations’ mental health literacy, rustout rarely gets a mention. And this is problematic—both for the individual and the organization. “I often feel I have produced nothing at the end of the week, and there is no sense of development,” one person said. Some will stay in their roles out of a sense of duty (and for other practical reasons like salary or seniority), but others will choose to leave. Another said, “I am functioning at my job, but I’m not thriving, and I am looking elsewhere.” Why don’t we talk about rustout? Rustout often hides behind competence, and the reliable person is often given more of the same work. The person who does not complain is assumed to be coping and doing OK. The person who stabilizes others and is a good team player is rarely asked whether they themselves feel developed or challenged. One person said, “I have no experience of ever having a conversation with anyone around it. No line manager ever named it, it has never been mentioned in a communal setting or in a one-to-one setting. . . . No one has ever asked, ‘Do you feel fulfilled or do you feel you have opportunities to use your skills and talents in a meaningful way?’” It’s easy to see how doing overtime and being dependable can become a trap. Eventually, the employee becomes valued less for what they might become and more for what they can continue to absorb—they won’t “rock the boat” by opening the can of worms that is their dissatisfaction. “You put up, and shut up,” one person said. Meanwhile, many workplaces are organized to detect individual or team shortcomings (or failure) more readily than stagnation. If someone stops performing, the problem becomes visible. However, if someone continues to perform while becoming inwardly disengaged, the organization may see no problem at all. And if anyone notices their dissatisfaction, they may not openly discuss it, as another of my interviewees reported. “It suits everyone not to talk about it; when everyone is working and doing their jobs—then don’t disturb anything. Everything is cosy.” How rustout arises Participants experiencing rustout described barriers that limited their ability to use their strengths or pursue professional growth, which are particularly important for having a sense of meaning and fulfillment at work. For many people, their working life gives them a sense of identity and purpose, but when work no longer offers that sense of meaning, their relationship with the job can become fractured or feel “off.” In some cases, they can’t put their finger on what is happening, and that might be why I often hear after presentations, “I never had a name for it.” Naming rustout won’t solve the issue, but it can help the person to understand why they feel underutilized or dissatisfied. Naming rustout can help people understand this isn’t a personal failure but an experience that is shaped by the design or conditions of work (much like burnout). It is also shaped by the culture of work, particularly when there is a resistance to change or a failure to see the value in helping people to flourish. From my discussions with participants and colleagues about rustout, it is clear that it isn’t a pre-retirement phenomenon. It seems to arise for those who are experienced, perhaps mid-career, and those who have been in their role for a long time. One participant explained, “I have been doing [this role] for years. There must be better ways to organize things to ensure the expertise of experienced staff is used in more effective ways.” Others said, “It’s the seven-, eight-, nine-year mark when there isn’t an opportunity to be stimulated,” and “It’s the seven-year itch, you’ve been doing something creative and then you reach a point where you are doing the same thing on repeat.” In such ways, we can’t dismiss rustout as a consequence of aging in the workplace. It can happen whenever people are pigeonholed into a system that no longer offers them meaningful development. Although some repetition and routine are an expected part of every job, there should also be a sense that your abilities and talents matter in your role. That there is a future and something exciting to work toward. What to do if you are rusting out The language of burnout has helped many people name distress that had previously been normalized or dismissed. It has also helped organizations take workplace well-being more seriously. But any dominant mental health vocabulary can become limiting when it starts to stand in for the whole field of experience. If burnout is the only category available for occupational distress, then recovery is often imagined in terms of rest and resilience. They may be necessary, but they are not always sufficient. A person experiencing rustout may not simply need less work; they may need different work: work that restores their sense of intellectual engagement and curiosity. As Maya Angelou once said, “We aren’t designed to be static”—that could apply to all aspects of life, including the workplace. If you are experiencing rustout, it would be good to do an inventory of what is happening for you. Do you feel fulfilled in your role? Do you have opportunities to use your talents? Do you gain some sense of meaning from your role? Are there ways to seek renewal in your work? What might this look like, and can you have this conversation with your line manager or boss? For organizations, the questions are also direct. Look around your team. Are you giving your most dependable employees opportunities for development? Do you continually lean on their “safe pair of hands” because it is easier than asking someone else to learn the ropes? Are you willing to put the conversation about professional dissatisfaction on the table? How can you help your team to flourish? That is the unsettling truth at the heart of the rustout. A person can be conscientious yet disengaged, reliable yet underused, competent but unfulfilled. They can be good at their job and still feel themselves receding from it. The silence around rustout allows it to thrive, which is why I often hear the same response after conference presentations: “I thought it was just me.” I think there is a particular loneliness in that sentence, the loneliness of continuing to do the work, while privately wondering why it no longer feels like yours.
