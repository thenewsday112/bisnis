#60. From workshops to the catwalk - why mended clothes are going mainstream

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Alice Brown uses felt animals to cover holes and stains on her clothes

If Alice Brown is browsing in second-hand shops and finds a jumper with a hole, that won't stop her buying it.

"I see it as challenge," says the 27-year-old forest ranger from Lincolnshire.

She is a champion of the growing fashion for eye-catching repairs.

Over the last year she has developed her own unique style, needle-felting cute animals and insects to cover moth holes, stains and other imperfections, winning her a huge following online.

"The natural place for decoration is in the centre at the front. If the hole is under the arm or on the shoulder, it forces you to think about the design," she says.

Often dubbed "visible mending" the trend is spreading from hobby crafters and community workshops, even drawing in fast-fashion brands and reaching the designer catwalks this year.

Some mends are designed to be eye-catching

At one Saturday morning workshop in Brighton, Jayne Eagle shows her students how to attach a patch of fabric behind a tear so the cat-face design peeps through.

Then she instructs them in stitching techniques inspired by the traditional Japanese methods Boro and Sashiko. Instead of matching the thread colour to the garment, they make the mend highly decorative using geometric patterns in large white stitching.

She also offers classes in darning using contrasting vibrant colours that celebrate, rather than disguise, the repair.

Jayne Eagle sees repair as an "act of rebellion" against fast fashion

One student, Nicola, has already used her newfound skills to stitch over sunscreen marks on a T-shirt "making a feature of it" and is now delighted that people "stop seeing the stains and just see the decoration".

Another student, Lisa, says her mother's generation would never have wanted the mends to show. "Now part of it is being proud of what you've done," she says.

Lisa bought a second-hand jacket online, but snipped the fabric with the scissors when she was opening the package. She managed to patch it in a way that "looks intentional".

"I was actually pointing it out to people," she laughs.

Lisa says she's so proud of her mends she wants people to notice them

In the past mending was primarily about saving money, by wearing clothes for longer, rather than buying new. But visible mending is about celebrating that choice.

The trend took off about six years ago, Jayne says, and has boomed since.

An era of "throwaway" fast fashion from the 1980s onwards undermined the tradition of looking after and mending your clothes and people lost the skills, she says.

The revival of interest feels to her like a backlash against overproduction in the fashion industry and the damage it causes: the pollution, carbon emissions, water use, and poor labour conditions.

So perhaps it is surprising just how popular mended style is proving among retailers, with brands from Ralph Lauren to ultra-fast-fashion firm Shein currently selling stitched and patched garments, albeit in both of those cases brand new off the peg.

Other High Street chains are adopting a more authentic approach, offering in-house repairs and workshops.

"We want to give customers practical ways to extend the life of the clothes they already love, rather than simply replacing them," says Uniqlo's head of sustainability, Maria Ledous.

Uniqlo offers decorative mending using Sashiko techniques, in keeping with the brand's Japanese heritage

Primark, often seen as a fast-fashion retailer due to its lower prices, has also joined the trend.

"We don't think that affordability and durability should be opposites," says Vicki Swain, the brand's longevity lead.

Outdoor brand Finisterre says around a fifth of the mends it provides for customers are designed to be eye-catching, while premium artisan clothing store Toast says around a third of customers go for that option.

Part of the "magic" is that customers can bring in a garment and "choose what they'd like it to become next", says Toast chief executive Suzie de Rohan Willner.

Toast has a team of 14 in-house "repair artists" providing a range of creative mends

Toast's repair services are free, but off-the-peg "renewed" pieces fetch higher prices than the originals to reflect their unique character.

Levi's, which boasts probably the longest tradition of being fixed up by its fans using anything from rhinestones to fringed cuffs, is rolling out a workshop programme this year.

The firm says its research shows younger customers in particular want to repair more but don't know how.

For now, Levi's' workshops focus on traditional mending skills, such as sewing on a button and hemming, rather than visible mends, and at a recent launch event in London, that seemed to be what its Gen Z participants were looking for.

"I'm from East London. If you have holes in your clothes people are like 'Ha ha! You're poor!'," says 16-year-old school student Bianca.

At a workshop run by Levi's, Nicholas (r) says he prefers mending not to show, while Bianca (centre) says people still see it as a sign of poverty

Theo, 19, says he'd rather learn how to mend without it showing.

"I would want [the jeans] to look how I originally bought them," he says.

He has a side hustle selling clothes at car boot sales and thinks traditional repair skills would make it more lucrative.

His friend Nicholas, also 19, says most people want to look "fresh".

"No-one wants to look like they came out of a dumpster," he says.

Still, Paul Dillinger, Levi's head of global design innovation, thinks more creative repairs will win them over as they pick up more skills.

"No-one has time for that perfect 'straight from the shop' thing. That's not cool.

"People crave agency," he says. "Mending is a demonstration of humanity: it's not a mustard stain, it's a memory of a great day in the ballpark."

#251. Bos BI Soal Isu Rupiah Bisa Rp20.000: Kami Jaga Stabilitas!

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Jakarta, CNBC Indonesia - Gubernur Bank Indonesia (BI) Destry Damayanti buka suara merespons kekhawatiran nilai tukar rupiah yang disebut-sebut berpotensi menembus level Rp20.000 per dolar Amerika Serikat (AS).

Sebelumnya, Utusan Khusus Presiden Hashim Bidang Iklim dan Energi Hashim Djojohadikusumo mengingatkan rupiah bisa melemah hingga ke kisaran Rp 20.000 - Rp 21.000 per dolar AS jika aliran modal asing terus keluar akibat polemik pasar modal di Indonesia.

Destry pun menegaskan, tugas utama BI adalah menjaga stabilitas nilai tukar rupiah. Namun, pergerakan mata uang Garuda tidak hanya ditentukan oleh kondisi domestik, melainkan juga dipengaruhi berbagai faktor eksternal yang berada di luar kendali bank sentral.

Menurutnya, jika melihat fundamental ekonomi Indonesia, kondisi dalam negeri relatif baik dibandingkan negara-negara lain yang menjadi pembanding atau peer group.

"Di Bank Indonesia kan tugas kami menjaga stabilitas rupiah. Jadi kalau kita lihat pergerakan rupiah kan memang sangat banyak faktor yang mempengaruhi. Tapi kalau kita lihat dari fundamental ekonomi kita, sebenarnya kita dalam kondisi yang relatif oke dibandingkan dengan peer group kita," ujar Destry kepada awak media, Jumat (30/10/2026).

Destry menjelaskan, pergerakan rupiah saat ini juga merupakan bagian dari fluktuasi yang terjadi di pasar. Sebab, nilai tukar dipengaruhi oleh dinamika permintaan dan penawaran (supply and demand), serta persepsi pelaku pasar.

Dia mencontohkan, perkembangan kebijakan suku bunga di Amerika Serikat dapat langsung memengaruhi pergerakan mata uang di pasar global. Ketika muncul perkembangan bahwa The Federal Reserve (The Fed) tidak menaikkan suku bunga, pasar merespons positif dan penguatan terjadi di tingkat global.

"Kalau kita lihat hari ini juga penguatan terjadi di global. Oh Amerika enggak naikkan suku bunga, dampaknya ternyata langsung positif. Jadi banyak faktor yang di luar kontrol dari kita," jelasnya.

Meski demikian, Destry memastikan BI memiliki komitmen untuk terus menjaga stabilitas rupiah. Menurutnya, stabilitas menjadi prioritas agar iklim ekonomi tetap kondusif dan sektor riil dapat tumbuh serta menciptakan lapangan pekerjaan.

"Fokus di kita itu adalah memang menjaga stabilitas. Jadi stabilitas itu adalah pertama yang harus kita jaga, supaya tercipta iklim ekonomi yang kondusif untuk nantinya ekonomi di sektor riil bisa tumbuh dan bisa membuka lapangan pekerjaan," tegasnya.

Di tengah tekanan terhadap rupiah, Destry belum memberikan sinyal bahwa BI akan menaikkan suku bunga acuan. Dia menegaskan, keputusan kebijakan moneter akan bergantung pada perkembangan data ekonomi atau data dependent.

"Nanti kita lihat perkembangannya seperti apa," ujarnya.

Destry menjelaskan, BI memiliki beragam instrumen kebijakan untuk merespons dinamika ekonomi dan pasar keuangan. Dengan demikian, suku bunga bukan satu-satunya instrumen yang dapat digunakan bank sentral.

Instrumen tersebut antara lain insentif untuk menekan biaya lindung nilai atau hedging, kebijakan makroprudensial, hingga Sekuritas Rupiah Bank Indonesia (SRBI).

"Karena kita punya kebijakan kan banyak ya instrumennya. Enggak hanya suku bunga aja. Kita punya kebijakan insentif untuk biaya hedging. Untuk yang moneter kita punya kebijakan makroprudensial. Kita punya instrumen SRBI, jadi banyak. Jadi bukan hanya suku bunga yang jadi kebijakan kita," papar Destry.

#12. WeWork CEO explains how a dress code and RTO flexibility helped change the culture at the once-troubled company

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For WeWork's CEO, Fridays are for sneakers. Sometimes.

At Business Insider's The Long Play event on Thursday, John Santora told Deputy News Chief Cadie Thompson that he rebuilt WeWork in part by establishing a sense of trust and decorum among employees.

In pursuit of that goal, Santora instituted a dress code for employees, but also gave them one day a week to work from home.

"I didn't have the ability to go watch my son play a 3 o'clock baseball game," Santora said, adding, "We have to give our employees that flexibility to do all that."

Santora, formerly the chief operating officer at Cushman & Wakefield, a real estate firm, became WeWork's CEO in 2024 as the once-buzzy office startup emerged from a disastrous Chapter 11 bankruptcy filing.

Santora had a short time to decide whether to join the faltering company, he recalled. And it wasn't exactly an easy choice.

"I was kind of at that point of, 'Should I retire?'" he said. "My daughter Nancy said, 'Dad, you know we love you, but when you have too much time on your hands, you're a pain in the ass.'"

Since those deeply red days in 2019 when WeWork's valuation plummeted from $47 billion to roughly $8 billion before it was bailed out, the company has stabilized. It posted eight quarters of EBITDA profitability and expects a fourth straight quarter of cash-flow profitability.

In 2025, it reported about $2.3 billion in revenue.

Though it hasn't hit the valuation it once had, the fact that the company could be revived at all is remarkable. WeWork, founded in 2010, rents out office space, spruces it up, and parcels it out to freelancers, startups, and other businesses.

Its first CEO and founder, Adam Neumann, made bold pronouncements that WeWork would "change the world" and "elevate the world's consciousness" by renting out office space to startups.

But after a bungled IPO, it became clear to the new stakeholders that a different type of leader was sorely needed.

As it turned out, Santora was that leader.

Having worked for 47 years at Cushman & Wakefield, Santora had the real estate experience that Neumann lacked, albeit without the magnetism of a Silicon Valley whiz kid.

"From a leadership standpoint, I'm a very structured, disciplined person," he said. "I'm a suit and tie, buttoned-up real estate person."

Santora recalled an early trip to one of the offices, where he discovered employees wearing jeans, pajamas, slippers, and shorts.

"I grabbed my head of HR," he said. "See that? I don't want to see it anymore."

Santora, who at one point described himself as an "old chubby guy from Staten Island," didn't just force employees to wear sports coats to important meetings. In his words, there was some "give and take."

"I don't look at what time they leave, what time they come in," he said of his employees. "As long as they're getting their work done and they're keeping their clients happy."

And Santora allows himself one exception to his suit-and-tie persona.

"Fridays, I'll actually sometimes wear sneakers, depending on what my schedule is," he said.
