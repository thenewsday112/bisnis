#890. The hidden treasure in discarded wearable and fast tech

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sales of fast tech like mini fans and disposable e-vapes, as well as wearable health tech such as heart monitors and smartwatches are soaring. They're creating a new e-waste problem but are full of hidden treasure.

From mini fans and the cheap chargers to smartwatches bought as part of a New Year's health kick, some 44% of Europeans own at least six "fast-tech" gadgets. And more than half say they keep unused and broken e-waste items languishing at home or throw them in the regular trash instead of recycling.

That's according to a new report by Brussels-based e-waste association WEEE Forum. It describes fast tech, which is booming, as charging and lighting devices under €10 (circa $11), high-tech items like power banks and fitness trackers, and gadgets and lifestyle appliances like cheap mini blenders and fans, as well as single-use electronic vapes.

"So, these electronics are a bit like fast fashion," Magdalena Charytanowicz, WEEE Forum's Communication Manager, told DW.

Nearly 40% of buyers recalled discarding a fast-tech item without using it at all or within a month of buying it, and nearly 60% said they had used a product for no longer than six months. Yet the vast majority of respondents were generally satisfied with their gadgets despite short-lived functionality.

This suggests consumers accept the "fast-tech bargain" of poor quality that "does the job for a while" and is easily replaced — similar to fast fashion — according to the report.

About 62 million tons of electronics are discarded worldwide annually. "That's the Great Wall of China of e-waste that is produced every year," noted Charytanowicz, adding that the figure is set to grow to 82 million by 2030.

In Europe, roughly 40% of electronic waste is recycled, yet the rate worldwide is just 17%. This means valuable materials are lost, while electronic waste ends up polluting the environment.

In 2022, a million tons of critical raw materials were embedded in the 10.7 million tons of e-waste generated across Europe, including manganese, gold, copper and aluminum.

These materials are at "the center of geopolitical tensions," Charytanowicz said, referring to escalating global tensions over access to rare earths and other critical minerals.

"Even the smallest" devices are worth recycling if they contain a circuit board or battery, she said.

Groups like WEEE Forum say more awareness raising is needed on how to dispose of cheap electronic devices, and that recycling should be made as easy as possible.

But the best way to reduce a product's environmental impact is to extend its lifespan through better design and repair, as recycling raw materials is energy intensive.

Charytonawicz said clearer, harmonized EU rules now under consideration could help improve e-waste collection. The bloc's 20-year-old directive is "not really adapted to the changing reality," she said.

Another growing area of e-waste is digital healthcare. While devices such as diabetes sensors and blood pressure monitors are "transforming healthcare," many are designed to be disposable.

A study published in Nature in December 2025 found sales of wearable healthcare electronics, for example, could reach 2 billion devices annually by 2050, and generate a million tons of e-waste.

Jeremy Faludi, associate professor of sustainable design engineering at TU Delft in the Netherlands, says making health tech — particularly digital devices used in, or issued by hospitals — recyclable or reusable comes with some "much bigger challenges" than normal electronics.

That's in part due to the potential for patient contamination, particularly with digital surgical devices that come into contact with blood or other bodily fluids.

"The way that a lot of things are designed now, you can't recover the stuff because it can't be easily sterilized without the electronics," said Faludi.

He worked on the Digital Health in Circular Economy (DiCE) project, an EU-funded initiative, to help redesign digital healthcare devices to "recover critical materials and reduce environmental impacts without compromising patient safety."

A modular design, for example, allows electronic components to be separated from the parts that need to be replaced between patients, making devices easier to reuse.

The four-year DiCE project, which included citizens, hospitals and manufacturers across nine EU countries, found reusable ECG cables — which connect patients to heart-monitoring equipment — cut environmental impacts by up to 81-94%, for example. The sets are usually designed for single use.

Rules around medical waste can also make reuse and recycling more difficult, particularly when requirements differ between countries and often come down to perceived rather than actual risk, Faludi added. He says some 85% of hospital trash is not infectious but everything ends up in the same bins. Better sorting as well as EU-wide regulations around treating non-infectious hospital waste would help here.

Like with other tech, consumer health devices such as blood pressure and glucose monitors often risk being forgotten in drawers or ending up in the wrong waste, simply because people do not know how to dispose of them, said Charytanowicz of WEEE Forum, which is also involved in DiCE.

Pilot schemes in Belgium, Spain and Slovenia that offered free postal returns and collection machines in public places found that more than 60% of people were willing to return unwanted devices if it was a straightforward process.

"What's really important is the convenience so that it can be integrated in your daily routines," said Charytanowicz, adding that "nudges" such as messages that recycling a device could help the health of another person motivate people to "do the right thing."

#885. FIFPRO report highlights growing pains in women's football

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Expected global revenue of over $1 billion and new faces at the U17 World Cup show women's football is booming, but a recent report also highlights need for better structures globally.

At first glance, women's football is healthier than ever. A recent report by FIFPRO, football's global players' union, said worldwide revenue is expected to reach over $1bn (€894m) in 2027. The World Cup in Brazil, a first in South America, will be the last with 32 teams and is expected to be the most watched and biggest revenue-generating women's tournament ever.

The good news continues with the U17 Women's World Cup in Morocco, where the tournament is being held every year for five straight years. Germany, France, Spain, Brazil, the United States and defending champions North Korea are all at the 2026 edition of a competition whose reputation continues to grow.

"You can just see where the game is going, which is so exciting," US head coach Ciara Crinion told FIFA. "The level has just gone up. We have to continue to stay with that and compete as well. [The U-17 Women's World Cup] almost feels like that first stepping stone in a major tournament for young players, and now you see how serious it is taken."

Puerto Rico, Australia and Argentina are making their debuts. For Puerto Rico, qualification is historic, as no football team in the men's or women's game has ever reached a World Cup tournament before.

"We've embraced this idea of being Puerto Rico's first team to qualify for a World Cup," head coach Andres Mirabelli told FIFA. Alongside national development plans, player recruitment was aided by a diaspora of nearly four million in the United States, perhaps the strongest place for women's football in the world. According to FIFPRO, the regional football average salary in North America is $5,940 (€5,547) per month, which is far higher than any other continent.

But even for the handful of players who make it out of these youth tournaments to a professional career, creating a living out of the beautiful game remains a real challenge. The aforementioned FIFPRO report details that many professional female players across the world barely earn minimum wage, with some even falling below it.

The report, which sourced its data from player surveys across the world, found that only 66% of professional players are paid for 12 months a year. The global average salary is $2,600 (€2,300) a month, but the range varies drastically across the world. In Eastern Europe, for example, that average is $880 per month. In Africa, it is $432, but in North America it is just under $6,000.

"I think what you also find in the women's game is the gaps are not just between the top five leagues and the rest. There is a gap there, but even within leagues themselves [there is one]," FIFPRO head of research and strategy for women's football Frankie Brown told DW.

"In some of the other leagues you're dealing with teams that are operating professionally, so will be paying their players on a reasonable wage, and playing in the same league as them you'll have players that are on amateur contracts and not being paid at all."

With economic and job security an issue for many, the report reveals that psychological stress factors are now the second main reason many consider leaving football.

"This is one of the most concerning parts of the report," Alex Culvin, the director of women's football at FIFPRO, said in a statement.

"Of the 49% of women’s football players considering leaving the sport early, 46% cited their mental health as the reason. In interviews, players say the sport's growth brings new pressures — including extra workload and social media abuse — but that clubs do not have the necessary safeguards in place to support these changes. We want clubs and national associations to provide mandatory mental health support focused on wellbeing, which is independent of sporting performance objectives."

FIFPRO's report is asking FIFA to set a so-called floor for the women's game so that minimum standards in key areas such as contracts, pensions and health can be enforced. Perhaps inspiration can be taken from the labor deal the WNBA agreed in 2026. It was long-negotiated deal that ultimately sent a message to female athletes everywhere to push for better pay, conditions and a greater share of the revenue they help generate.

"It is positive that average salaries are increasing for some players and that more are earning a satisfactory wage, but it’s important to note that there is a huge disparity in pay across regions," said Culvin.

"There is also an imbalance in conditions, not only across the globe but also within regions; in Europe, for example, the difference in standards between the east and west is enormous. This is a gulf we need to work hard to narrow; the growth in the most advanced markets must not be completely isolated from the rest of the industry.

With high hopes for the 2027 World Cup in Brazil and recent moves to increase professional football domestically, Germany is aiming to become an example for structural improvement in women's football. Hosting the Euros in 2029 certainly gives them an incentive to improve standards.

While salaries are in the upper tier — in the 2023-24 season, a women's Bundesliga player earned an average base salary of around €48,000 per year — the process of structural development in the country is still in its infancy. A new German football association rule shows that even basic professional structures are still being established. Only since the 2025–26 season have teams in the Women's Bundesliga been required to employ assistant coaches, goalkeeper coaches and athletic coaches on a full-time, professional basis.

"The facilities and the staff members," Nicole Anyomi told DW, were a factor in her making a recent move to London City Lionesses. "We have 30-plus staff members here whereas in Frankfurt we had like seven or eight. You can learn from each person and there is something new to learn every day. I think that's how you develop as a player faster."

Ultimately, the faster the game grows, the greater need to close the gaps.

"Despite playing on different continents, players are connected by their precarity," Culvin said. "The redistribution systems in women's football need to be more effective, touching more players and supporting a more sustainable ecosystem."

#878. Do non-German nationals commit more crimes than Germans?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

People without German passports appear more frequently in German police crime statistics than German nationals. A new study explains why.

Every year, when the Federal Criminal Police Office publishes the police crime statistics, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) — a party that is, in part, right-wing extremist — pays particularly close attention. They are looking for crimes committed by refugees, migrants and foreigners living in Germany.

But studies by media scholars show that 95% of the AfD's press releases focus on non-German suspects, while crimes involving German suspects are almost completely ignored.

However, the common narrative of the AfD — that non-Germans are inherently much more violent — is simply not true.

In a new research paper, Susann Prätor, a sociologist, criminologist and professor at the Lower Saxony Police Academy, has taken a very close look at crime data in Germany, together with her colleagues from the University of Zurich and the Lower Saxony Criminological Research Institute.

She looked not only at officially reported crime — that is, all offenses that appeared in police crime statistics — but also at unreported crime: These are all offenses that are not reported or detected, and therefore do not appear in any official statistics but are identified through representative surveys.

"According to police crime statistics, people who do not have German citizenship commit crimes more frequently than German citizens. However, studies into unreported cases show that non-German nationals are twice as likely to be reported. Differences between the two groups are also evident in crimes that go unreported, but they are significantly less pronounced," Prätor told DW.

Crimes committed by foreign nationals are therefore twice as likely to appear in police crime statistics as crimes committed by Germans.

These statistics record all reported cases and do not specify whether the allegations prove to be true.

When the statistics are adjusted for the bias in the reporting of crimes, foreigners are no longer 2.6 times more likely to commit crimes than Germans, but only 1.7 times more likely. This figure is also consistent with the results of studies on unreported juvenile violent crime. According to these studies, foreign youths are about 1.6 times as likely to commit violent crimes as German youths.

Other factors affecting crime include age and gender. Young men make up a larger proportion of the foreign-born population, and young men are the group most prone to criminal behavior across all societies.

According to Prätor, "categories such as being young, male, poorly educated and having a history of violence are factors that increase the likelihood of violent behavior. A certain concept of masculinity also plays a role, wherever that may come from. But we do not see culture as a factor, contrary to the prejudice that masculinity and violence play a greater role in other cultures."

Prätor points to a survey on attitudes toward violence. According to the survey, 5% of German youths view violence positively. Among those without a German passport, the figure is 10%.

The criminologist has identified a crucial factor: "Family upbringing plays an important role in shaping attitudes toward violence, because these attitudes are formed in the home. If I experience violence in my own home, I will have a different relationship to violence than if I hadn't experienced it," said Prätor.

Germany is, however, on the right track, says Prätor, even if it doesn't always feel that way. "Fifty or sixty years ago, parental violence was much more common in child-rearing than it is today. Acts of violence committed by young people are also far less frequent than they were in the 1990s or the early 2000s."

Prätor emphasizes that it is important to bring objectivity to the debate. A good illustration of this is a look at police records of serious violent crimes — such as aggravated assault, manslaughter, or murder. "Of every 100 German citizens, 0.2% commit violent crimes; among non-German nationals, the figure is 0.6%."

However, Prätor points out that "more than 99% of the people living here in Germany — whether of German or non-German origin — the overwhelming majority in this country do not commit any serious violent crimes at all."

Prätor also trains prospective police officers. She wants to send the message to policymakers that they should place greater emphasis on the positive aspects of migration. She argues that the debate on immigration is far too negative, largely due to an overly narrow focus on police crime statistics. The vast majority of non-Germans integrate exceptionally well, she says.

She wants the government to invest more money in prevention.

"By starting as early as possible with children and adolescents: strengthening social skills as early as preschool, building on that in school and responding when incidents of violence do occur. It's also important to invest in infrastructure and recreational activities in certain regions. After all, a key factor in youth violence — regardless of background — is that young people lack a meaningful daily structure," she said.

#871. Nord Stream: Germany puts suspected Ukrainian pipeline saboteur on trial

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

A Ukrainian citizen faces court in Hamburg, accused of attacking the Nord Stream gas pipelines in 2022. It raises questions about Kyiv's possible role in the explosions. German support for Ukraine is also under scrutiny.

The aerial photos made headlines worldwide: On September 26, 2022, massive quantities of methane gas bubbled on the surface of the Baltic Sea near the island of Bornholm. Unknown people had blown up three of the four sections of the Nord Stream 1 and 2 gas pipelines from Russia to Germany.

The attack, which occurred seven months after Moscow launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine, meant the end of a mega energy project between Germany and Russia in which both sides had invested billions, and which secured affordable Russian gas for Germany. However, the flow of gas via Nord Stream 1 had been restricted or stopped several times by Russia since the Ukraine war began; Nord Stream 2 was not yet operational.

From October 14, Ukrainian citizen Serhii K. is facing trial before a court in the northern German city of Hamburg. He was arrested in Italy in August 2025 on a European arrest warrant and later extradited to Germany. This week he must answer for the attacks before the Hanseatic Higher Regional Court. He is accused of being an accomplice to a war crime by attacking civil energy infrastructure, disruption of public services, causing an explosion and destroying structures.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has repeatedly said that Ukraine was not involved in the 2022 attack on the Nord Stream underwater gas pipelines.

"Ukraine has never officially taken part in any operation related to the blowing up of Nord Stream," Zelenskyy told reporters again in August in response to a question about the ongoing investigation.

The fact that the accused is Ukrainian is especially politically sensitive. Germany continues to support the country after it was attacked by Russia: militarily, financially, with humanitarian aid and by taking in about 1.3 million refugees.

Critics of Germany's support for Ukraine, especially the right-wing extremist Alternative for Germany (AfD) and the left-wing populist Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) want to improve relations with Moscow, resume energy deliveries from Russia – including via a repaired Nord Stream pipeline — and stop supporting Ukraine. Some of them have practically delivered their verdict on Serhii K., although the trial has not yet begun, and assume that Ukraine's political or military leadership was behind the attack on the pipeline.

Sahra Wagenknecht, former leader of the BSW, told news agency Reuters in 2025 following Serhii K.'s arrest: "It is totally absurd that Germany spends many billions on aid for Ukraine but never demands an explanation from [President Volodymyr] Zelenskyy. Questions should also be raised about possible compensation."

Alice Weidel, co-leader of the AfD, has shared similar sentiments. In early 2026, she asserted that the German government must seek financial repayment from Ukraine. If the AfD came into power, she would demand Kyiv compensate Germany for blowing up the pipeline and pay for its repair. According to her, a country that does such things is "no friend of Germany." She has, however, failed to provide proof that Ukraine was behind the attack.

In an interview with public broadcaster Deutschlandfunk in 2023, German naval commander Göran Swistek pointed out that, given the technical, financial, and organizational effort involved, “there is likely a state-run structure behind it.” In his opinion, a group of amateurs could not have carried out the act.

However, he considered it unlikely that this state could be Ukraine, since Ukraine, after all, would not want to jeopardize German support. At the time, Swistek thought a false lead pointing to Russia was possible.

The German-Russian Nord Stream project had many opponents from the beginning, including Poland, the US, the Baltic countries and — especially since Russia's full-scale invasion — Ukraine. That is why investigators always ask: Who benefits from the explosion?

Henning Hoff from the German Council on Foreign Relations (DGAP) thinks it is important to shed light on the background context.

"By halting supplies or using the threat to do so as leverage, Russia exerted immense pressure on Germany at the time, and gas markets went haywire," he told DW. "The explosion thwarted Russian attempts to extort Germany, prices for natural gas sank. I think one could say: The explosion was in German interests."

But what will happen if the Ukrainian Serhii K. is found guilty and a connection to the military or political leadership of Ukraine is proven? Could the German government still continue its current course regarding Ukraine?

The German government faces a dilemma: On one side, judicial independence must be preserved and consequences for the sabotaging German infrastructure must be pursued. On the other side, the German government wants to prevent Russia from instrumentalizing the case. However, pressure on Germany would increase enormously, also from within the country.

Roderich Kiesewetter, a lawmaker with the ruling center-right Christian Democrats (CDU), said there is no reason to deviate from the current course of support, even in the case of involvement by Kyiv: "Irrespective of the outcome, no consequences at all are appropriate, because our support for Ukraine is carried out in the interest of our own national security and stems from a sense of responsibility for a free Europe."

The debate around support for Ukraine is escalating in Germany, regardless of the court process. Public backing for Ukraine is decreasing, especially for deliveries of weapons.

The AfD gained significant support in three state parliament elections held in eastern Germany during September. The party could potentially supply the state premier in Saxony-Anhalt. The AfD has also led nationwide polls for months, far ahead of the conservative CDU of Chancellor Friedrich Merz.

In addition, the AfD and BSW have drawn a connection between planned government spending cuts on social services and aid to Ukraine.

Wagenknech told a campaign event before the state parliament elections that the federal government was "Sinking billions upon billions into the black hole of Ukraine, while in Germany, in our schools, the children are no longer properly learning reading, writing and arithmetic." Tino Chrupalla, co-leader of the AfD, raised the question of whether citizens were prepared to spend so much money on a non-EU and non-NATO country.

Given very expensive energy costs, it is not only the AfD and BSW who suggest repairing Nord Stream and receiving new deliveries of gas from Russia. Some CDU politicians favor this too, such as Saxony state premier Michael Kretschmer. He views a repaired gas pipeline as a possible bargaining chip for talks with Russia. Kretschmer's position, however, contradicts the federal CDU party line and European Union sanctions policies.

His party colleague Kiesewetter considers it dangerous: "Russia has always used energy as an instrument of geo-economic extortion. It would be extremely dumb if we were to again learn nothing from history and cling to a special path with Russia. That would fuel mistrust among our partners in Europe. I can only advise everyone, including people from the CDU, to keep their distance from such calls, because they are against German interests."

Hoff from the DGAP also rejected positions like Kretschmer's. "That is an illusion. There is no returning to the time before Russia's war. The supposedly cheap Russian gas, which has cost Germany dearly, will never flow again, if only because Putin has no interest in it doing so. It serves only as an instrument of propaganda for polarizing German politics and weakening support for Ukraine."

In any case, the outcome of the trial in Hamburg will undoubtedly influence the public debate about further support for Ukraine.

#865. Trump's diesel deal with Russia is unlikely to significantly lower global fuel prices despite expectations of rapid relief

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As the world's largest diesel exporter, the US benefits from high prices. At home, however, consumers are feeling the strain of rising living costs. Why Donald Trump's deal with Russia is unlikely to change that.

With or without a deal with Russia, diesel prices on global markets are unlikely to fall significantly anytime soon.

That is the assessment of experts interviewed by DW regarding the latest fuel-supply agreement between US President Donald Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin.

"The main reason for high diesel prices is the war in Iran and the simultaneous disruption of shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab al-Mandeb Strait," said Samina Sultan, a foreign trade expert at the German Economic Institute (IW) in Cologne.

The situation has led to production outages and made fuel shipments "more expensive, less secure and more difficult," she said.

Trump's deal with Russia also has an additional geopolitical dimension, Sultan said: "It causes the wars in Ukraine and Iran to become completely intertwined."

Things are looking up, though. Roughly 13.5 million barrels of crude oil are once again flowing through the Strait of Hormuz a day, according to shipping and commodities analytics firm Kpler.

But for refined products such as diesel, volumes remain far lower at around 677,000 barrels per day, compared with 3.6 million barrels before the war.

Carsten Fritsch, a commodities analyst at Commerzbank, also attributes the price increases to the wars.

"If nothing changes in Ukraine and the Middle East, I see very few ways to resolve these supply bottlenecks," he said.

In a social media post on October 9, Trump announced that he would temporarily suspend some sanctions on Russia and begin importing Russian diesel.

He wrote "diesel prices for Americans and, indeed, the world, will be COMING DOWN, IN RECORD NUMBERS, AND FAST!" in a mix of lowercase and all caps.

Russia confirmed the arrangement. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov told Russian state news agency TASS on October 13 that supplying diesel fuel was "in the interests of Russia, the United States and global energy markets."

Diesel prices in the US and many EU countries have risen sharply over the past 12 months.

According to the US Energy Information Administration (EIA), prices in the US have climbed by about 60% and now stand at $6.30 per gallon, equivalent to $1.66 (or €1.49) per liter of diesel.

But even if Russia were able to flood global markets with cheap diesel, prices would not fall nearly as much as they recently rose, something all of the experts contacted by DW agree on.

Many refineries around the Gulf region have suffered disruptions or damage, while transporting fuel via shadow fleets and securing insurance remains costly, cumbersome and risky.

Another key shift is that Russia is no longer the world's largest diesel exporter. In fact, it is facing its own fuel shortages at home.

The US is now the world's biggest diesel exporter. In 2025, it exported an average of 1.26 million barrels of diesel per day, accounting for roughly 15.4% of global diesel exports.

At the same time, Russia fell to second place with a 9.5% share, and that was before major Ukrainian drone attacks on its refineries.

Besides the new US-Russia diesel agreement, Trump also recently floated the idea of US diesel export restrictions. But the idea was abandoned because it could lead to even higher diesel prices in the country, according to Samina Sultan.

"There simply is not enough diesel storage capacity in the United States," Sultan said. "Production would have to be reduced, and then prices would rise again."

If the US were to impose export restrictions on diesel, buyers in Latin America and the European Union would be hit particularly hard. Since Russia invaded Ukraine, EU countries have increasingly imported diesel from the US instead of Russia because of sanctions.

"An export ban merely redistributes the shortage; it does not solve it," said ING chief economist Carsten Brzeski.

"Global prices would continue to rise, while refineries would scale back production as margins shrink," he added.

Export restrictions aside, few people consider that the US-Russia diesel deal will have any big impact.

According to calculations by the platform The World Data, the promised shipment of 2.2 million barrels of diesel from Russia would not even cover a single day's US diesel demand of roughly 4 million barrels.

Even if all of the diesel volumes announced by Trump were combined, totaling 35.8 million barrels, they would only cover about nine days of US domestic demand.

"I CANNOT OVERSTATE HOW MUCH OF A NOTHING BURGER THIS IS," Canadian oil market analyst Rory Johnston wrote in an all-caps post on X shortly after the announcement.

Russia would normally export almost 10 times that volume if its refinery fleet was not being targeted by Ukrainian drone attacks, he added.

Canada also supplies diesel to the United States. Roughly 80% of all US diesel imports come from its northern neighbor.

Given Trump's verbal attacks on Canada and the ongoing trade dispute, analysts view the prospect of Canada supplying significantly more diesel to the US as highly unlikely.

In Saxony-Anhalt, the AfD and BSW are joining forces to push for negotiations with Moscow. What is driving this rapprochement?

The state parliament of the eastern state of Saxony-Anhalt is calling for an immediate end to all German financial and military aid to Ukraine. The motion was passed during a special parliamentary session with the votes of the far-right populist Alternative for Germany (AfD) and the Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) which combines left-leaning economic positions with right-wing populist positions on immigration. Both parties are urging Saxony-Anhalt's current state premier Sven Schulze, of the center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU), to advocate in the Bundesrat, Germany's upper house representing the federal states, for peace negotiations with Russia.

However, as the federal states have no specific powers in the field of foreign policy, the motion will have no direct impact on Germany's Ukraine policy.

Speaking on behalf of the BSW in the state parliament, legislator Claudia Wittig called on the premier to take action: "It is your responsibility to finally use every available avenue to work for peace."

She was sent to the women's concentration camp at Moringen in Lower Saxony, where she remained until May 1935.

After her release, she continued her anti-Nazi activities despite the increasing danger, facing persecution both as a political dissident and as a Jew.

In these underground circles, she met Pieter Siemsen (1914–2004), and they remained a couple until he emigrated to Argentina in 1937. The only known surviving photograph of Eva comes from his personal archive.

She had a daughter, Tana, in 1939. The father's identity is unknown, however. The child was placed in a Jewish orphanage in Berlin and deported in 1942 to the Auschwitz-Birkenau extermination camp, where she was murdered.

Meanwhile, because she was Jewish, Mamlok was forced to perform compulsory labor at the Berlin firm F. Butzke Schrauben-Industrie in 1940. There she met other women who shared her opposition to Nazism.

Together they formed what later became known as the "Eva Mamlok Group," an informal resistance network of Jewish and non-Jewish women. They distributed anti-Nazi leaflets and wrote slogans critical of Hitler.

They also shared banned books. "They were books that are today part of Germany's educational canon," from Thomas Mann to Lion Feuchtwanger, said Weltz-Rombach. That the Berlin Central and Regional Library (ZLB), Germany's largest public library, stands on the new Eva-Mamlok-Platz is a fitting symbol of this act of resistance and the importance of books, she added.

Mamlok and others in her group were arrested for distributing this forbidden literature. On January 13, 1942, she was deported from Berlin to the Riga Ghetto in occupied Latvia.

According to Inge Berner's testimony, Mamlok remained active in the resistance there too. For example, she arranged for a contact still in Berlin to send her a cake with a miniature camera hidden inside it to document persecution in the ghetto.

In October 1944, she was sent to the Stutthof Nazi concentration camp, where she died in December of that year.

Blücherplatz was named after the Prussian field marshal Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher, whose most renowned achievement was leading his army against Napoleon at the Battle of Leipzig in 1813 and the Battle of Waterloo in 1815.

Several places, including streets and parks, have been named after him in Germany.

"There is no shortage of Blücher memorial sites," said Weltz-Rombach. "And perhaps it's also time to put an end to this old Franco-German enmity — which is, of course, inherent in the name Blücher, given its Prussian tradition — and to establish, in its place, a different form of remembrance."

The square also happens to be the site of the Carnival of Cultures, a multicultural street festival and parade held every year to celebrate Berlin's global diversity.

Having first heard about Mamlok when a Stolperstein commemorating her was laid in 2011, Weltz-Rombach has been researching her biography for the past few years. The Berlin-based filmmaker, producer and curator has also been sharing the resistance fighter's story through various projects, including an exhibition in 2024 and a podcast series with young Berliners.

Since Mamlok was only a teenager herself when she became politically active, the workshop participants can easily relate to her fate.

These teenagers learn about Mamlok's experiences at the actual locations where she was active, helping them imagine what it would be like to be arrested and imprisoned for six months without trial for doing things that normally wouldn't be considered crimes — such as simply expressing an opinion publicly or laying flowers at a commemorative event, explained Weltz-Rombach.

"Above all, she is, of course, a role model — an example of how one would like to act oneself, and how courageous one would like to be."

"[Women's] resistance was often of a more everyday nature, rather than planting bombs," said Weltz-Rombach. Their stories offer examples of "forms of resistance that have only recently received more attention." Other examples include a 2024 exhibition and the launch of a database dedicated to female resistance fighters.

Even though many of the eyewitnesses are now dead, Weltz-Rombach believes there are still many more of these stories that deserve to be brought into the spotlight: "There are definitely many names we do not know, that are waiting to be further researched."

#751. The Bundestag has debated the genocide Germany carried out in Namibia a century ago. But will it spur progress on addressing colonial crimes?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Opposition parties have forced a debate in the Bundestag on the early-20th-century genocide Germany carried out in Namibia. Will this finally spur progress in addressing colonial crimes?

Gaob Daniel Timotheus Frederick was sitting in the front row of the visitors' gallery in the Bundestag this week. Wearing a suit and hat, he had traveled all the way from Namibia to see this parliamentary debate unfold. For almost 120 years, his family had been waiting for the return of human remains of their ancestor, Cornelius Frederick (sometimes spelled Fredericks), who had led the Nama resistance against German colonial rule and died on Shark Island in Germany's first concentration camp.

In the name of his great-grandfather and as a member of the Nama Traditional Leaders Association (NTLA), Frederick was there to demand an "unconditioned apology" from Germany.

Sitting next to him, in uniform and white gloves, with a carved lion on the handle of his walking stick, was the head of the Ovaherero Traditional Authority, Paramount Chief Mutjinde Katjiua. Speaking before the Bundestag debate, the chief said: "We call upon the Bundestag to recognize unequivocally the genocides committed against the Ovaherero and Nama peoples."

Many people wonder why the German parliament hasn't already done this — and why the government has not yet issued an official apology. There is a draft joint declaration with Namibia in which the German government acknowledges that "from today's perspective, [it] would be called genocide." But there is no German-Namibian reconciliation agreement. The evidence is overwhelming: From 1904 to 1908, the German Empire waged a war of extermination against the Indigenous people of southwestern Africa, including the Ovaherero (also known as the Herero), the Nama, the Damara and the San. At least 100,000 people were killed, and just as many displaced. Many were subjected to forced labor and forced prostitution. The land seizures and expropriation carried out at the time continue to affect their descendants today.

Jürgen Zimmerer, a professor of history at the University of Hamburg whose research focuses on Africa, told DW that the Bundestag and government lack the will to confront this era in German history. "There are Bundestag resolutions on the Armenian genocide, one on the Holodomor [in Ukraine], and one on the genocide against the Yazidis [by the "Islamic State" in Iraq]. But there is no recognition of the genocide against the Herero and Nama by the German Bundestag." If there were any political will to do so, Zimmerer said, it could happen very quickly.

The socialist Left Party initiated the Bundestag debate by drafting a resolution calling for full recognition and compensation in collaboration with representatives of the descendants. The goal: "restorative justice." Paramount Chief Katjiua has rejected the existing agreement between the Namibian and German governments. "Development aid cannot substitute for comprehensive reparations," he said.

Since 2021, the governments have been negotiating a joint declaration that pledges €1.1 billion ($1.24 billion) in reconstruction aid to Namibia over 30 years. Once the agreement is signed, Germany's president is set to issue a formal apology.

But the Foreign Ministry warned: "Legal claims to compensation cannot be derived from the recognition of genocide. With these negotiations — which are unique worldwide — Namibia and Germany have broken new ground and are pioneers in confronting their colonial past."

Political scientist Andreas Mehler, the director of the Anton Bergstraesser Institute at the University of Freiburg, said the government's plan sounded like "checkbook diplomacy."

Zimmerer also said there was a financial calculation: "If we were to recognize an obligation to provide reparations to the families of those killed — given the millions of victims of World War II, the victims of the Germans — it would ruin us."

Four lines in the 144 pages of the coalition agreement of the current German government — between the conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU)/Christian Social Union (CSU) and the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) — summarize the extent of the government's commitment to reconciliation with Namibia.

"We will intensify our efforts to come to terms with colonialism," the text reads. "This includes transnational research into artifacts and the restitution of cultural property in dialogue with the countries of origin. Particular attention will be paid to establishing a dignified memorial site and the repatriation of human remains."

Compared with the efforts to address colonialism led by the SPD-led government from 2021 to 2025, "the issue has lost visibility," said Alexander Stroh-Steckelberg, a professor of political scientist at the University of Bayreuth who specializes in Africa.

Zimmerer has an even more critical take: "I wouldn't even call it a stalemate anymore. I believe this government is rolling back the progress that has been made in terms of raising awareness of colonial injustices."

Much remains to be done, as far the descendants of the Ovaherero are concerned: The remains of tens of thousands of people are believed to still be in Germany. Gaob D. Timotheus Frederick and other descendants of the victims have been fighting to heal these wounds for generations.

In March, Germany established a coordination council, headed by the Foreign Ministry, which stated that the government did not wish to "dictate to the countries and societies of origin which cultural artifacts and human remains they should reclaim, what priorities they should set or how restitutions should be handled."

Serap Güler, a state minister in the Foreign Ministry and the chair of the coordination council, defends the government's actions so far. Recounting her own official trips — she has already been to Togo, and next week she plans to travel to Rwanda and Burundi — she said: "Everywhere I go, I receive recognition for our sincere and trusting approach to the past — as well as for our determined commitment to a future of freedom, security and prosperity."

Zimmerer said Germany "has earned a reputation as a country that takes a critical view of its own history and learns from its mistakes" — which gives it "enormous cultural capital" on the world stage. But he is concerned that this capital is currently being squandered.

The government's memorial policy does not appear to address colonialism. Zimmerer even detects an attempt to "marginalize German colonial crimes" in the recent proposal by Minister of State for Culture Wolfram Weimer to create a dignified memorial site in Bremen. He notes that victim groups have not been included in Weimer's proposal and said the colonial capitals of the German Empire — Berlin and Hamburg — must also become sites of remembrance.

Ninety-five percent of the colonial troops set sail from Hamburg's Baakenhafen, including Commander Lothar von Trotha, who issued the orders to exterminate the Ovaherero and Nama. According to Zimmerer, the fact that a new opera house — rather than a memorial site — is being built at Baakenhafen constitutes a staggering case of "art-washing of history." Mehler also hopes that a productive debate on memorial sites will still take place and that, overall, "many more places will come to terms with colonial history."

Meanwhile, Gaob D. Timotheus Frederick continues to call for a collective effort to come to terms with the past: "Let us give opportunity to the affected communities that are being represented by us to have a seat on the table to discuss with them the rest of the way."

#739. China's influence in the gaming industry is growing

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Home to more than 700 million gamers, China is the world's largest gaming market — but it's also one of the most tightly regulated. The effects reach far beyond China, impacting players around the world.

In a dark underground hideout, dim light spills from a handful of oil lamps. A comrade fiddles with a rifle. A red flag hangs on one of the roughly hewn stone walls, bearing a hammer and sickle on a white star — the symbol of the ChineseWorkers' and Peasants' Red Army. Weapons are scattered on the ground. One is picked up, and soon the action moves into the snowy forest, where gunfire erupts among the trees.

Such a scene could fit in a new installment of "Call of Duty," the popular first-person shooter game that transports players to battlefields past, present, and future. But it is actually from "The Defiant." The game is currently being developed by the Chinese studio Hoothanes Technology in Hangzhou. It tells the story of Chinese forces fending off the Japanese army in the 1930s and 1940s.

Developer Connor Xu tells DW the weapons featured in the game are unique. They were used only in Asia and are therefore unfamiliar to many Western players, he says. The same goes for the story, which Xu says has never before been told in a video game.

"The Defiant" is also designed to appeal to an international audience. In just a few years, China has emerged as a major player in the global gaming industry despite strict regulations, censorship and a former ban on video game consoles.

More than 700 million people in China regularly play video games. According to market research company Newzoo, most consider gaming an important part of their lives. Many use games to stay in touch with friends.

"Games make life more interesting," developer Xu says. "I can try different roles, different stories, different characters, because we only have one life to live, but we can play a role in another story. That's really attractive, I think, to Chinese users."

People in China primarily play games on PCs and smartphones, while consoles are much less common. There are historical reasons for that: While gamers in Europe and North America debated whether PlayStation or Xbox was the better console in the early 2000s, the devices were not officially sold in China between 2000 and 2014. Chinese authorities said the restrictions were intended to protect children and teenagers from becoming addicted to gaming.

Minors in China are currently allowed to play online games for no more than three hours a week. There are no such limits for adults, but gaming is subject to strict controls. Anyone who wants to play must register using their real name and a personal identification number. That effectively allows the state to track who plays what and when, as well as whom they communicate with.

There is also a gray area that the Chinese government appears to tolerate. Some players log in using VPNs, giving them access to games that are not officially available in China. Some younger players use their parents' accounts to get around the limits on gaming time.

Despite its strict controls on underage gamers, China has become increasingly receptive to video games in general, having recognized the industry's enormous economic potential. Early on, games released for Chinese players often appeared to be copies of Western titles. At the same time, Chinese tech companies such as Tencent and NetEase invested in numerous Western gaming companies.

One distinctive feature of the Chinese market is its licensing system. "Unlike a lot of the other big video games markets in the world, China has a tightly regulated and licensed official video games market," George E. Osborn tells DW. The journalist advises NGOs and governments on video games and spoke at the Gamescom congress. In his book "Power Play," he describes how authoritarian states use video games to pursue political objectives.

The rule for all games in China is simple: No license, no sales. "That system essentially forces developers who want to release their video games to go through multiple layers of approval from China's Ministry of Propaganda, Ministry of Culture," Osborn says.

Games are prohibited from containing material that threatens China's unity, sovereignty or territorial integrity, among other restrictions. Pornographic content and gambling are also banned. Games may not incite crimes that threaten national security or spread information authorities believe could disrupt public order.

"The whole point around this criteria is that it's deliberately designed to be pretty vague," Osborn says. Whether a game violates the rules is a matter of interpretation. Sometimes games are altered even after release. In the 2025 action role-playing game "Wuchang: Fallen Feathers," for example, enemies based on famous Chinese figures from the Ming Dynasty suddenly could no longer be killed. Instead, they would collapse from exhaustion.

The Chinese action role-playing game "Black Myth: Wukong" became an unexpected international hit in 2024. By then, it was clear Chinese developers could do more than cater to the domestic market — they could create successful games for a global audience as well. The game was inspired by the 16th-century Chinese literary classic "Journey to the West."

Osborn sees nothing wrong with bringing Chinese culture to audiences around the world. The question, he says, is how much the state actually intervenes in the games themselves. "I think in general, video game players are actually quite deeply skeptical of these kinds of things. They are generally very good at spotting when someone might be selling to them or someone might be propagandizing. And so I think if it's too obvious, it won't work," he says.

Because of the licensing system, Western developers also have to comply with government requirements if they want to sell their games in China. And they do want to, Osborn says, because the market is too big to ignore. "But I think when you start thinking about the potential impact this has on creative standards around the world, I think it's really important that developers ask themselves the question of what will we or won't we compromise on here?"

He fears the real danger lies in self-censorship. That could lead developers, for example, to leave queer characters out of their games. That would represent a major step backward, he says, because games help many people explore their identities.

China is not the only country where games are modified to comply with national regulations. In Germany, for example, symbols that violate the country's constitution, such as the swastika, may not be shown in games unless the game in question explicitly contributes to the country's culture of remembrance. Depictions of extreme violence also have to be removed from German versions.

Glorifying violence is prohibited in China, too, yet Xu's team is still allowed to develop a war game. How does this add up? Xu explains it this way: "I think in this game, the most important thing is not violence. The most important thing is to tell history."

Xu says the team is committed to delivering a historically accurate product. Whether it succeeds will be for historians to judge.

#728. How big is Russia's threat against Germany?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Experts believe that, so far, Germany has been ill-prepared — both militarily and mentally — for Russian provocations. They also warn against the idea that ending support for Ukraine would make Germany safer.

Warnings keep mounting about Russia's threat to Germany. On October 12, Sinan Selen, president of Germany's domestic intelligence agency, the BfV, said that Russia's covert attacks and destabilization efforts against Germany and other European countries are "not a temporary development but a long-term hybrid attack."

Even if the war in Ukraine were to end, said Selen, the Russian threat would not disappear. "For Russia, this is about completely redrawing the map of Europe and redefining its own role within Europe and in global politics."

He added that Russia's aggression would continue to be felt not only in Ukraine, but also in the Baltic states, in Poland and in Germany.

German Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt has described the threat situation as one in which "our country is not at war, but we are the daily target of hybrid warfare." Russia's goal, he said, is not to defeat Germany militarily "but to subdue us politically and socially."

Security experts, however, are not so sure that Russia is only interested in exercising political and social influence. Last year, political scientist Carlo Masala told the political talk show Maischberger that "Russia is preparing for a major war." And he didn't mean just a cyberwar. Inspector General Carsten Breuer, the Bundeswehr's highest-ranking officer, agreed.

For now, though, Russia remains tied up in its war against Ukraine. According to NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, there is no immediate military threat to NATO territory. But according to military experts, European intelligence agencies, and the German Defense Ministry, Russia will have the personnel and equipment to wage a conventional war against NATO by 2029. The Russian defense industry is running at full speed, and Moscow plans to expand its armed forces to 1.5 million soldiers.

At the moment, Russia has other objectives, believes Stefan Meister, an expert on Eastern Europe at the German Council on Foreign Relations (DGAP): "I don't think Russia has any interest in a direct military conflict with NATO right now. It wants to test NATO and also display its capabilities," he tells DW.

An example of Russia's hybrid war might be the failed attack with an explosive-laden drone against a Ukrainian transport aircraft at Leipzig-Halle Airport in August. Soon after, the German government stated that it was certain Russia was behind the attack.

Security expert Nico Lange told DW at the time that Germany was very lucky the explosives did not detonate: "Honestly, it was pure coincidence and not the result of German defensive measures."

As for why Germany was targeted specifically, Lange said: "I believe that Germany has always been a primary target of Russian influence and hybrid warfare. Russia believes that Germany is the key to European decision-making. Germany is now leading the way in supporting Ukraine."

Moscow swiftly responded to the German government's accusation with an angry denial. Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov said it was "the beginning of a real war," though there was no immediate further escalation.

Nevertheless, there is always the risk that things could spiral out of control following a Russian provocation and subsequent response, according to Stefan Meister: "Accidentally shooting down an aircraft that they were trying to intimidate would trigger a counter-reaction. But I don't believe that Russia is currently aiming for a direct war but rather for provocations, polarization, and sowing uncertainty among the population."

Not everyone shares the assessment that Russia poses a threat to Germany — some believe that the threat can be averted.

Despite the war in Ukraine, politicians from the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) and the populist left-wing Alliance for Social Justice and Economic Reason (BSW) want to improve relations with Russia, resume Russian energy supplies and end support for Ukraine.

AfD co-leader Tino Chrupalla said in early September, "People are fed up with this escalation against Russia, with the billions being squandered there on a system that is actually funded by German taxpayers' money."

The message appears to have resonated among the population, especially in eastern Germany. The AfD saw strong gains in all three state elections in September. In Saxony-Anhalt, it became the strongest party by far and may even run the government there soon, which would be the first time that a far-right party has held power in Germany since the end of WWII.

The CDU's security policy expert Roderich Kiesewetter disputed the claim made by the AfD and BSW that ending aid to Ukraine would mean greater security and more money for German schools and infrastructure: "Spending on education, social policy, and infrastructure is only possible if we live in freedom and democracy — and, above all, in peace and security. Anyone who believes that we'll have more money for social programs if we stop supporting Ukraine and let Russia have its way is either gravely mistaken or is making themselves a tool of Russian propaganda."

The perceived security threat has led the German military, the Bundeswehr, to fast-track significant modernization through substantial investment. But it is struggling to find enough recruits for its planned expansion. Many young Germans have taken to the streets to protest against plans to reintroduce conscription. Defense Minister Boris Pistorius faced criticism from inside his own party, the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD), for saying that Germany must become "capable of waging war." Many found the statement to be too militaristic.

Is Germany prepared for a military threat? "Not at all. Germany is not well prepared militarily or in the civilian sector, and the population isn't mentally prepared for it either," said Meister. "Just look at the demonstrations against conscription and the military that continue to take place, even though Germany needs a draft. For decades, we've grown up in this culture of pacifism, in a comfort zone. I believe there is very limited societal resilience, but above all, we are not at all mentally prepared for war."

Chancellor Friedrich Merz and his conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) are under increasing political pressure — especially from the far right — to scale back support for Ukraine. But Merz never tires of emphasizing that Ukraine will continue to receive support under his chancellorship, calling it a matter of Germany's security. He recently visited Kyiv once again and, together with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, surveyed the destruction caused by Russia.

But this also means that the threat from Russia will continue and even intensify, says Stefan Meister: "I believe we will see a massive increase in attacks such as the one in Leipzig, of provocations involving drones. We will see attacks on infrastructures and possibly also on specific individuals within the military-industrial complex. I believe this is the real, concrete danger facing Germany in the coming months."
