The iPhone Duo is now in Apple stores, even if most buyers won’t get theirs until January. Here’s how to make the most of a hands-on demo.

The iPhone Duo went on sale Oct. 23, and anyone who didn’t preorder the device on Oct. 16 is now looking at a delivery date in late January. Apple stores have demo units on display, though, so you can spend time with Apple’s first foldable long before one could arrive at your door. A few minutes is enough to check what a spec sheet can’t tell you: how the hinge feels, how visible the crease is, how the phone sits in your hand folded and unfolded and how apps behave when you open it up. Here are six things worth testing on your next visit to an Apple store.

Here’s what should you be looking to do when you actually pick up the iPhone Duo. Because while the foldable phone might have a fun, unique design, you may run into things you’re unfamiliar with — or even dislike — when using it day to day. You’ll want to be confident that the device is a good fit for your needs before plunking down $2,000 or more for the folding phone.

We’re going to break down a series of things you should check out when you finally get to play with it at the Apple Store.

When the Duo was first revealed, Apple showed off a very intriguing animation when you unfold the it. Leave it to Apple to make a function so simple look so aesthetically pleasing.

It’ll likely be the first thing you notice when you first open the Duo up to see its inner display, and there’s a good chance you’ll continue to open and close the device to see the effect. At the end of the day, it’s a parlor trick — but a very good one, adding delight to ordinary use.

Foldables, well, fold, and they typically come with creases. The Duo has a minimal crease and a matte display, both of which are wins. That said, even the most noticeable creases tend to “disappear” when you’re viewing content — you just get used to them while watching TV shows or YouTube videos. The Duo’s minimal crease should be even easier to ignore.

And let’s not forget about the matte screen. Several foldables ship with glossy displays, but the iPhone Duo will have a less shiny screen, making it easier to view in bright environments without annoying reflections.

The outer passport-size display on the iPhone Duo may be a little awkward for watching videos because of how it crops content to fit the new aspect ratio. Still, it’s handy to prop the phone up in tent mode and watch shows that way. You also have a large inner display to work with, but because of its passport-style design, even that screen has a different aspect ratio than the widescreen format people are used to on regular phones, so there will likely be some letterboxing in videos.

Try watching something with the phone set up in both ways to see if it bothers you enough to dissuade you from buying the phone.

Again, it’s not an iPhone Duo issue but a foldable phone issue — the Samsung Galaxy Z Fold 8 is the same size and format. The aspect ratio of the display might crop the top or sides of videos, so it’s worth trying out if you expect to watch a lot of full-screen videos.

Like videos, apps on foldables can sometimes appear a little weird, and that could be the case with the iPhone Duo‘s aspect ratio. When you’re at the Apple Store, check out how your most-used apps look and feel on both the inner and outer displays, and make sure you rotate the inner screen to see how apps behave in this orientation, too — that wide screen could be great for watching vertical videos or scrolling through social media.

If some apps, such as Instagram, look a little different or are wonky, it’ll hopefully be just a matter of time until the developers make an update that optimizes the experience for this new design. Keep in mind that you can also have two apps open onscreen at once, making it easier to multitask.

If you want the best camera experience on an iPhone right now, you should be looking at the iPhone 18 Pro or Pro Max, but that doesn’t make the Duo a slouch for shooting photos, either.

Yes, the Duo is missing a third camera (telephoto), and if that’s important to you, then you probably shouldn’t even be considering Apple’s foldable. But still, if you’re going to shell out this much money for a new phone, test out those cameras — even if it’s in a controlled environment like an Apple Store.

Another thing to check out before you pull the trigger is your selfie shots with this new phone. There are two main ways to do this: with the phone closed, holding it up in the regular way you’d take a selfie using the outer screen’s 12-megapixel front-facing camera, or with the inner display open and shooting with the twin 48-megapixel rear cameras (the outer screen will show a preview of the shot).

There’s also a front-facing camera underneath the inner display, which is mostly for video calls — and which we suspect would produce the least-impressive shots from the device. Try them all and see what you think of the pics. And since you’re at an Apple Store, make sure you delete them afterward.

The odd but interesting aspect ratio of the front display on the iPhone Duo may be a little awkward for typing, so it’s best to do a typing test when you have one in front of you. While the inner display will provide a roomier and more comfortable typing experience, you probably won’t want to have to open up the Duo every time you want to shoot off a quick text to someone. If you have bigger hands, typing on the front screen could be a bit of a nightmare.

Typing one-handed on the small cover screen should be manageable, but try it out to see if it works for you. Some may find it convenient; others may find it cumbersome.

And when you’re typing on the inner display, try the Duo in both portrait and landscape orientations to see which feels better. You can also choose between a split keyboard and larger keys. It’s thoughtful of Apple to offer options that let you tailor the typing experience to your preferences, but you may find that none of them suit your style.

#1178. What Lies Beneath the Noise

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

In 1980, I made the first big error of my political-writing career by not trusting my intuition. As we approach historic midterms, I won’t make the same mistake.

Some days, I wonder how I ended up in this fun-house mirror of a timeline. I came to understand about midway through the 2016 general-election campaign that almost everything I knew about American politics was wrong. When the results from 2024 began to roll in, I decided that whatever I still thought I knew about American politics was also wrong. Which prompted me to recall one of the worst mistakes I ever made in this plague-ridden craft of mine.

It was 1980, and I was covering the presidential campaign between Ronald Reagan and Jimmy Carter for The Boston Phoenix. (While I am not at the moment dead, Reagan, Carter, and the Phoenix all are.) As part of our coverage of the homestretch, the newspaper rented me a car and sent me on the road to take the temperature of what was still charmingly called the New Deal coalition. I was to drive to various stops throughout the newly deindustrialized Midwest and see how the children of the people who’d benefited from the New Deal itself were doing. My first stop was in Youngstown, and the first—and last—answer was “not well at all.”

I stopped by a steelworkers union hall to talk to the president of a local and to some of the members. On the way, I passed closed steel mills, vast and faceless and empty beyond empty. Bits of rusted metal flaked off the plant buildings and drifted on the breeze like dirty snow in October. The crowd in the union hall was sparse and grim. The first thing I noticed was that every bit of furniture was marked with numbers written in chalk. Every chair. Every desk. Every file cabinet. I asked the local president whether they were moving. He said no. That Saturday, they were going to have a giant garage sale, and those numbers were the prices on the various items. They were selling off the union hall piecemeal. This should have told me something. In fact, it did, but I forgot about it.

The situation didn’t get better on the rest of the trip. I drove from Youngstown to Toledo, up to Flint, and finally to Grand Rapids, where I saw President Carter visit a local library. He sat in a chair that was much too small for him. And I stood in a narrow aisle taking notes and feeling as if I had walked into one of those miniatures that Toni Collette makes in Hereditary before she loses her head. Everything around me looked small, including the president of the United States. This should have told me something. In fact, it tried, but I was deaf to the obvious.

When I finally got to Milwaukee, I settled into an empty classroom at my old journalism school to write my magnum opus. I thought about the people selling off the union hall in Youngstown and about the weird dollhouse town meeting in Grand Rapids, and I pounded out the following passage on a borrowed gray Royal upright:

“The New Deal is as dead as the steel industry. Jimmy Carter is going to lose, and he is going to lose in a landslide. It is entirely possible that he will fail to receive 100 electoral votes.”

At the time, all the elite opinion agreed that the electorate was “undecided.” The overwhelming consensus was that the American voter was a muddled, disillusioned mess, liable to jump in any direction, including over a cliff. And I walked right into a rookie mistake. I removed my “landslide” theme and went with the “muddled America” theme. On election night, I gathered with my fellow alternative journos at a friend’s house. It was the angriest collection of sullen souses you’ve never seen. The results rolled in, and it plainly was a late-arriving catastrophe for the Democratic party. Carter lost state after state. He even lost Massachusetts. Giants of the Senate—Frank Church, George McGovern, Gaylord Nelson—all sank out of sight. And all night long, that lost paragraph rang like a gong in my memory.

I’d seen it. It was right there in front of my eyes. It was there in the chalked-up office furniture and in the shrunken presence of the incumbent president, who I’d once written would not get 100 electoral votes and ended up with 49. Not that my readers knew any of this, because I had lost faith in my own observations and my own instincts. I learned a valuable lesson that night: Trust what you know and what you see, because sometimes huge changes are under way, beneath all the vain noises that signify nothing at all.

Our revels now are ended. These our actors,

—Shakespeare, The Tempest, Act IV, Scene I

The 2026 midterm campaigns, which may indeed be the country’s last exits from an onrushing nightmare, have entered the realm of the spirits. Phantom issues abound. White House aides in the trunks of cars. With which podcaster have you appeared, and why haven’t you denounced them yet? Dead campaigns haunt the living ones. These are things that will lose all substance in our politics sometime on November 4.

Over the past few months, the Democratic party, the wind at its back to begin with, has demonstrated a remarkably energetic intellectual and ideological diversity. New candidates with a startling variety of appeals to a startling variety of constituencies have popped up everywhere from Alaska to Florida. Money is flowing to all of them for different reasons. Ordinarily, this would be a cause for institutional joy, if not perilous giddiness.

However, in the realm of the spirits, nothing is as it seems. This diversity of appeals to diverse constituencies scares the daylights out of some Democrats. It’s expressed as a party wrestling with internal contradictions and with a collision of generations. But I would argue that it really represents the last, fading tremors of the fear that entered the Democratic party when it awoke the morning after that dark day in 1980 and realized that its New Deal coalition had collapsed under its feet without anyone noticing.

Out of this came the ceaseless pursuit of white working-class men who were as gone forever from the party as their factory jobs were from places like Youngstown. The Republicans offered these folks someone to blame, which is precisely the crack cocaine they were seeking. Unfortunately, these also were the most reliable voters the Democrats had. The involuntary flinching reflex among the national Democrats proved very hard to unlearn. The available evidence so far this year is that the long process may have finally come to completion.

The Democrats come into November with a variety pack of very good candidates. In Massachusetts, Senator Edward Markey has fended off another challenge. Markey entered Congress in 1976, which was 13 years before James Talarico, the senatorial candidate in Texas, was born. Sherrod Brown in Ohio is trying to return to the Senate, while Abdul El-Sayed up in Michigan is trying for the Senate for the first time.

And then, all the way out in the 14th Congressional District of California, a woman named Aisha Wahab has replaced the disgraced Eric Swalwell in the House of Representatives. She is a Muslim. She was born to Afghan refugees. And now she is the first Afghan American elected to Congress and is favored to stay there after the midterms.

Wahab has joined a whole clutch of congresscritters who served in Afghanistan. With any luck at all, they will all talk to each other and learn something that will help this country make better choices in that part of the world. The power of change, beneath all the vain noises.

IT’S QUITE POSSIBLE THAT MASCARA has been the loudest thing on your face for years. And now, there’s a new trend that’s here to turn down the volume. ‘Ghost lashes’ refer to an effort to add a little impact to your fringe without the need for coat after coat of makeup. The idea: Curl, add a single whisper of brown mascara, and let your natural lashes take the lead.

It’s a decidedly different take—especially for someone like me. I wore lash extensions for more than a decade and mastered the at-home versions during the pandemic and beyond. But now,, I’m coming around to skipping mascara (though I’ll keep my tightlining, thank you very much). It’s beauty’s back-to-basics era, lash edition. Fewer products, less fuss, more of what you already have.

My lash drawer can finally, exhilaratingly, let out a deep exhale. “We’ve become so used to seeing a full lash that taking it away suddenly feels...interesting,” says celebrity makeup artist Benjamin Puckey. It tracks with what he’s seeing in the chair: Clients are asking for less mascara and no longer requesting falsies. The payoff reaches well past what’s sprouting from your lids. Leaving lashes bare allows the rest of your face finally gets a turn. “You notice the skin, the shape of the eyes or the lip color differently,” Puckey says.

Ahead, how to pull it off at home and what to know before you break up with black mascara.

Picture the no-makeup makeup look, but for your eyes. Puckey describes ghost lashes as lashes left bare or wearing so little mascara you barely register it. Curling is fine and a light coat of brown is fine. The thickness, the black pigment and, of course, the falsies are out. Makeup artist Tobi Henney keeps her definition refreshingly short: Ghost lashes are when “you don’t use mascara on the lashes and they are left natural.” She has the red carpet receipts, too. “I did this look in Cannes on Barbara Palvin,” she says. Her styling rule: The rest of your face “has to have stand out elements,” such as a flush of blush or a statement lip, “to carry the look.”

The trend grew out of the no-makeup movement, which started when Pamela Anderson gave up most makeup back in 2019. By 2025, it had trickled down to runways: Valentino’s spring/summer 2025 show and Gucci Cruise 2026 in May 2025 featured bare-lash looks. Then, more young Gen Z TikTok creators started posting clean makeup looks with no mascara, framing it as a recession-proof beauty move. The look spread further as Addison Rae, Kylie Jenner and Hailey Bieber posted bare-lash selfies and became a mainstream summer 2026 trend when Zendaya wore it to The Odyssey premiere.

We love it because it effectively ends the lash arms race. Skip the heavy stuff and your skin, your eye shape and your lip color get to shine. “It allows a little more of the person to come through,” Puckey says. It’s you, only a little more awake.

Another big part of this is the attempt to get your natural lashes to grown on their own, without the visual help of mascara. These products typically take several weeks to months to show results, but many can be extremely effective at boosting the length and volume of your natural lashes.

Plenty. A fair warning: Ghost lashes aren’t one-size-fits-all. Puckey says contrast matters. On a low-contrast face, where skin, hair, brows and lashes are close in color, black mascara can become the loudest thing you see, while brown feels more balanced. If you have naturally dark lashes against lighter skin, you may already have plenty of definition. Fair or sparse lashes can make the no-mascara look less appealing if you’re used to a stronger frame around your eyes.

Age may play a part, too. Puckey finds the fully bare version is often easier to wear under 40, though he wouldn’t call it a rule. As lashes get finer or less dense, a little mascara can make a real difference to how defined your eyes look. It’s also important to mind your technique. Puckey says the most common curler mistakes are squeezing too hard, curling over dried mascara and using a worn or damaged pad. Never pull the curler through your lashes while it’s clamped shut.

Then there’s the serum question. Henney is a fan: “I love the Sweed Eyelash Growth Serum, it works wonders,” she says. Her timeline for short or sparse lashes: “You need a few weeks or months to see results.” Puckey takes a different view. You don’t need a serum to try this look, and he wouldn’t start an expensive routine just to make a trend work. He leaves ingredient picks and timelines to a dermatologist or eye doctor. On safety, Henney says to “always check if you have contact lenses,” and Puckey says to ask an eye doctor, especially if you have sensitive eyes or wear contacts. If a product irritates your eyes, stop using it. If your lashes have suddenly gone sparse or patchy, Puckey says to have a dermatologist take a look.

Set your expectations before you start. Ghost lashes read softer and less defined than a full, mascara-heavy lash. A curler can make short lashes more visible, but it can’t create density that isn’t there. If you go the serum route, Henney’s advice is brief: “Be consistent.”

Here’s your homework. Finish your makeup, curl your lashes, then pause before you reach for the mascara. “You might like what you see,” Puckey says. And if you miss the definition, add a little brown. Going fully bare is optional. “There’s no need to follow the idea so literally that you stop feeling like yourself,” Puckey says.

#1157. How Prada Brought Its Hand-Drawn Holiday Campaign to Life

Animator John Pomeroy created every frame, from snow lynxes to snowballs.

Miuccia Prada and Raf Simons have a way of finding a new idea in something we already know. At their latest Milan Fashion Week show, they did so with the skirt, pulled apart and reimagined through shifts in fabric and proportion. For Prada’s 2026 holiday campaign, the human hand takes the lead in a series of films and images where hand-drawn illustrations turn winter scenes into a fairy tale.

The brand’s holiday story has a distinctly old-school hand behind it. The house tapped animator John Pomeroy to set its stars—actors Sadie Sink, Maya Hawke, Chen Haoyu, Luther Ford, and Kelvin Harrison Jr.—inside a world of hand-drawn winter fantasy. Every frame was drawn by hand, a painstaking approach that is something of a lost art in the age of CGI. Pomeroy knows the form well: His credits include the films Thumbelina, The Land Before Time, and Klaus.

The artist began the process by crafting characters from the “inside out,” as he put it. That meant imagining the characters’ wants and whims, like the Snow Lynx eyeing a mini Avoir handbag or a group of mice captivated by a pair of black-and-white dotted shoes. Lighting played a pivotal role, nowhere better exemplified than by the glowing embers of a fireplace cozily illuminating the scene where Sink appears.

Each movement was rendered by hand, including the launching of a snowball into Harrison Jr.’s nylon backpack. From there, the creatures were placed into live-action scenes, where photographer Glen Luchford’s lighting and perspective echo the hand-drawn scenes and complement the brand’s winter collection full of technical outerwear, candy-colored stripes, and occasion-ready knitwear.

The campaign celebrates the art of illustration and reminds us that even pure fantasy is made by humans.

“Animation speaks into stories like live action can’t,” Pomeroy says. “It’s a whole different universe. There’s a very distinct and purposeful reason to do hand-drawn animation, and that’s to see that there’s value in the human touch. And that hand-drawn and handcrafted work is still alive in the world today.”

#4467. UK ministers had plenty of time for AI datacenter bosses, but none for environmental groups

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Investigation finds businesses dominated govt meetings while communities were left out of decisions about AI Growth Zones

The British government has been busy courting AI datacenter companies while apparently finding little time to speak to the people who will have to live next door to their enormous server farms.

That’s according to a report published Tuesday by campaign group Spotlight on Corruption, which found that 84 percent of the stakeholders ministers met with to discuss datacenters between July 2024 and June 2026 were businesses or private-sector trade associations. Environmental groups, by contrast, didn't get a single meeting with ministers or senior officials.

The findings raise questions about who gets a say in Britain's AI Growth Zones, which the government hopes will attract billions of pounds in investment by making it easier and cheaper to build the infrastructure needed to power its AI ambitions.

According to the report, titled Zoned Out, ministers met representatives from 137 different businesses during the two-year period, while permanent secretaries and directors general held no meetings with community groups about datacenters.

Local residents didn't fare much better. Ministers met just two community groups, both after decisions had already been made to designate their areas as AI Growth Zones. In one case, the meeting took place on the very day the designation was announced.

The government also failed to conduct a national public consultation on its AI Growth Zone strategy, despite the potential consequences for electricity supplies, water consumption, and local communities. Spotlight found no independent consultations with residents in the selected areas, although some developers conducted their own.

Even local councils appear to have struggled to get a hearing. Of the ten local authorities and devolved governments affected by the five announced zones, half reported no communication with ministers or senior DSIT officials beyond their applications. Two had exchanged brief emails, while another secured a 15-minute meeting with a junior minister.

The government has been more accommodating to the companies building these facilities. AI Growth Zones offer developers faster planning decisions and better access to electricity infrastructure, with the aim of rapidly expanding Britain's computing capacity.

As we reported in March, the government has also been consulting on proposals that could allow strategically important datacenters to jump the electricity grid connection queue, potentially ahead of housing developments already waiting for power.

So while datacenter developers could soon find themselves jumping the grid connection queue, the people living near these facilities have had little say in the government's plans.

Spotlight also takes aim at the government's AI Energy Council, which includes representatives from Microsoft, Google, and Amazon Web Services but no environmental NGOs. Its Sustainability Working Group, established to examine the environmental impact of AI, managed just one meeting between April 2025 and July 2026.

Concerns also remain about who is influencing policy from outside government. Spotlight identified 38 appointments of former ministers and senior civil servants by AI companies between July 2024 and September 2026, including nine people who previously worked at DSIT.

Spotlight reckons Whitehall's transparency rules could make the picture even murkier. While ministers have to declare their meetings, unpaid advisers don't face the same requirements, even when they're helping shape government AI policy. So there's no way of knowing exactly who has been bending whose ear.

The campaign group wants the government to hit pause on new AI Growth Zones and datacenters until it tightens the rules on public consultation and forces developers to be more upfront about how much electricity and water their facilities will guzzle, and the emissions they'll produce.

The campaign group argues that Britain is falling behind countries including Germany, Canada, and Ireland, which have conducted national consultations on their datacenter strategies.

For a government determined to turn Britain into an AI powerhouse, there's clearly no shortage of companies willing to help build the infrastructure. But finding room at the table for the people expected to live alongside it seems like another matter. ®

And Stanford's payroll data has developers aged 22 to 25 sitting nearly 20% below their late-2022 peak, which is the one that actually keeps me up.

A couple I'd stop sharing, though. The "63% of devs spend more time debugging AI code" figure is everywhere, and I couldn't trace it to any actual study. And if you're still quoting "Stack Overflow is down 25%", that's ancient. In July 2026 the site got roughly 1,400 new questions. Its best month, back in 2014, had over 200,000.

Funnily enough, Stack Overflow's own 2026 Developer Survey, out this month, sums up the mood well. Most developers now use AI at work, nearly half trust its output only when they can easily verify it, and just 6.6% would trust it with an important decision.

Add it up and you get more code than ever, less understanding of it, and a lot of the time we "saved" quietly going into cleanup.

I've switched AI tools more this year than I switched IDEs in the five before it. I suspect you have too. Something better ships every few weeks, so being loyal to any of them feels a bit pointless.

What actually changed my results was boring: I started giving agents smaller jobs.

The models did get noticeably better towards the end of 2025. But the bigger jump for me came from not asking one agent to do everything. I break the work up now. Plan first, then architecture, then the build, then debugging, each as its own step.

Think about the tickets you've picked up over the years. "Add rate limiting to the login endpoint, five attempts a minute, return a 429" is a ticket you can finish. "Make the app more secure" is how you lose a week. Agents are no different, except they won't come back and ask what you meant. They'll just guess.

So I write the plan, I actually read it (this is the step people skip), and only then does an agent go near the code.

One more thing that took me a while to accept: these tools aren't deterministic. Paste the exact same prompt ten minutes later and you can get a different answer. Keeping up with your tools used to be a nice-to-have. Now it's part of the job. What worked last month might not work this month, and the only way to know is to keep learning how your agents behave, the same way you'd learn how a new teammate thinks.

I've seen this one play out a few times now, in slightly different forms.

Someone has an idea for a small web app and asks an AI to build it. An hour later there's a working draft with a very proper relational database. Strict tables, foreign keys, everything neatly joined.

Then the idea shifts, like ideas do. The data turns out to be messy and keeps changing shape, and what they really needed was something looser, a JSON column that can take whatever comes in.

The AI doesn't notice. It never stops to say "hang on, this schema doesn't fit any more." It just keeps building on top of it, wedging every new feature around the original design. Each change gets a bit slower and a bit more fragile, until pulling the latest version feels like Jenga with someone else's tower.

What fixed it wasn't a cleverer prompt. Someone had to make the call to throw the database out and start again with two plain tables, one logging every change over time and one holding the current state. Once the AI was told that was the new shape, the code got simpler almost straight away.

That's my main gripe with vibe coding. These tools are great at building on whatever's in front of them. They're terrible at telling you that what's in front of them is wrong. Somebody still has to own that.

It gets harder on older codebases. Greenfield is easy: you describe what you want and get 90% of it fast. The last 10% is where the system starts to age, and every real codebase carries years of crust, half-finished refactors and decisions nobody remembers making. Agents still struggle to properly understand that history before they start changing things. We used to handle this with documentation, design reviews and gates before code moved to the next stage. Those habits still matter, probably more for agents than they ever did for people.

Google's 2025 DORA report surveyed nearly 5,000 people in tech, and the line from it that stuck with me is that AI works like an amplifier. It doesn't fix a team. It turns up the volume on whatever the team already is.

If your systems are loosely coupled and your feedback loops are fast, you see real gains. If everything's tangled together and releases take weeks, you get very little. DORA also found that AI adoption now lines up with faster delivery and, at the same time, more instability in production.

GitClear's data points the same way. They analysed 211 million changed lines of code between 2020 and 2024, and 2024 was the first year copy-pasted lines outnumbered moved lines. Moved lines are basically their proxy for refactoring. In other words, more duplication and less tidying up.

You've probably seen this in your own repo. The AI writes a fourth validateEmail instead of using one of the three you already have. They all work, which is exactly why nobody deletes them, until the day you need to change the rule and have to find all four.

If your team already has shaky architecture, thin tests and rushed reviews, AI isn't going to save you. You'll just make the same mistakes quicker.

I'm genuinely glad PMs and domain experts can now knock together their own rough version of an idea. Instead of writing a spec and hoping engineering reads it the way they meant it, they can click around, hit the awkward bits themselves, and show up knowing what they want. That alone saves weeks.

The risk is an old habit of ours: the first prototype quietly becomes the production system. AI makes that so much more tempting, because now the prototype shows up in an afternoon and looks done.

It isn't done, though. Treat it as a sketch, then refactor it properly or rebuild it with what you learned.

Here's a workflow I'd recommend. Treat the messy prototype as the way you figure out the story, not the thing you ship. Once you know what you're actually building, use AI to help turn that into a proper spec. Then throw the prototype away and build again from the spec alone. You lose the leftover code, dead ends and orphaned files from a hundred iterations, and you keep everything you learned.

I'd also make one experienced person explicitly responsible for the final "ship it". Someone who asks the dull questions. If this breaks, what else breaks with it? Does it touch login, payments, customer data? Right now that person is often the only real check between AI output and production, so it should be someone's actual job, not a thing that happens if there's time.

That role gets more important once teams start running several agents side by side, one planning, one building, one debugging. Job titles get blurry, and the engineer ends up as the one steering the whole thing and owning the call at the end.

Teams will get smaller too. Work that once needed ten people might need two or three: someone shaping the idea, someone steering the agents, and someone owning production.

And for anything where failure really isn't acceptable, I'd borrow from safety-critical engineering, the world of aircraft software, where every change goes through a strict, audited process no matter who or what wrote it. The point isn't just more testing. It's checking that what the AI wrote actually does what you meant.

This is the bit I find hardest to write about, because I remember being junior and how much I learned from small, boring tickets.

A Harvard study of 62 million US workers found that at firms adopting AI, junior headcount dropped about 9% within six quarters while senior headcount barely moved. The US government data is interesting too. Jobs classed as "computer programmer", basically writing code to someone else's spec, fell 27.5% to their lowest since 1980. "Software developer" jobs, the people deciding what to build and how, hardly changed.

So I don't think companies have stopped wanting new engineers. They've lost the work they used to hand them. Fixing small bugs and building simple screens was how juniors learned, and that's now the exact work an agent finishes in seconds.

If I were starting out today, I'd put my energy into understanding systems. How the pieces fit. Why something decided in week one turns into a headache in month six. Syntax is cheap now; you can get a draft iOS app or a logo out of an AI in an hour. Knowing whether that app should exist, and what it'll break, is where the value has moved.

I also think CS courses could do with going back to fundamentals rather than teaching one language five times over. Pilots learn aerodynamics, not just which buttons to press, and that's why they cope when something happens that isn't in the manual.

You can already see this playing out on LinkedIn. The people posting real progress with AI tend to be the ones with solid computer science fundamentals, who start from the problem and the outcome and are happy to try different approaches to get there. The people whose whole identity is one language or one framework often sound like they've been left on the bench.

For juniors already in a job, a good middle ground is sandboxed prototyping. Give them real problems to explore with AI, away from production, and review what they learn. It channels the enthusiasm somewhere useful and teaches them how to brief an agent properly, which is fast becoming the core skill.

There are some encouraging signs. IBM says it's tripling US entry-level hiring in 2026, software developers included, after rewriting junior roles around things like customer work and checking AI's output. Not every company will do that, but it shows the junior role can be redesigned rather than deleted.

When an agent hands me a change, it helps to work out what kind of change it is before I start reading. Roughly, there are six:

New code: fresh files or functions, nothing existing touched. Usually fine.

Big rewrites spread across lots of files.

Tidy-ups like renames and formatting. Harmless on their own, but great at hiding the change that matters.

Silent behaviour changes, which are the scary ones. An API starts returning something slightly different, the frontend breaks, and nothing fails at build time.

If you don't fence off what an agent is allowed to touch, a single task can wander through all six. And the last one usually gets reported by a customer, not a test.

Agents love a rewrite. Ask for a small fix and you can easily get 2,000 lines across 15 files back. It looks impressive in standup, and honestly I think the tools lean into that.

The thing is, a model is optimising for something that looks right. A decent engineer is optimising for the smallest change that works. Those aren't the same goal.

I went in assuming big AI changes get reverted more often. The data I found mostly says no. Greptile looked at hundreds of thousands of PRs: some agents were reverted less than humans and some more, but large agent PRs weren't reverted any more often than large human ones. A Google study of 3.52 million code changes found AI changes were reverted slightly less often too. Where the cost shows up is review. That same Google study found AI changes picked up 1.92 times as many blocking review comments. And Faros's 2026 report, covering 22,000 developers, found median review time up more than 400% and 31% more PRs merged with no review at all.

So my worry isn't that giant AI PRs fail loudly. It's that they don't. They get approved, or skip review entirely, because nobody can properly review a diff that size at 4 p.m. on a Thursday, and everyone quietly hopes the tests caught it.

I treat an agent a bit like a very talented new hire on their fourth espresso. Quick, keen, sometimes reckless, and definitely not someone you leave alone with production.

The first rule is to keep diffs to roughly 50 lines. If something comes back much bigger, I don't try to review it. I throw it away, tighten the brief and run it again, which is nearly always faster than untangling it. Fifty lines is something I can read and actually stand behind. Anything much bigger is something I can only click "approve" on.

Pick the number that suits your team, then make it a hard check in CI. If a PR goes over the limit, it bounces back with a request for a clearer spec. A huge diff is usually a sign the brief was vague, not that the work was big.

The second is that secrets are off limits. Environment variables, API keys, tokens, passwords: none of it goes near an agent unless I'm watching. Paste a key into a prompt once and you might find it hardcoded in a dozen files by the end of the session. So secrets go into a vault or behind an environment variable reference before any AI work starts. CodeRabbit's own recommendations say much the same: keep credential handling in one place, block hardcoded passwords and run security scans on every PR.

A few smaller habits that have saved me more than once:

Get the agent to write tests before the feature

Turn on duplicate-code checks in your linter, so that fourth validateEmail gets flagged

Read the plan, not just the diff, because most bad code starts as a bad plan

Cheap code tends to get expensive later. I think of a lot of it as fast-fashion software. Looks great when it ships, starts coming apart a few months in.

And the effects reach well beyond our own repos. Tailwind is more popular than it's ever been, yet in January its founder wrote on GitHub that docs traffic was down about 40% since early 2023, and later said revenue was down around 80%. People ask an AI instead of opening the docs, so they never see the paid products that fund the project. Three of the four engineers were let go. Stack Overflow, where plenty of us learned to code, gets a tiny fraction of the questions it used to. The places AI learned from are drying up because of AI, and I don't think anyone has a good answer yet for what the next models will learn from.

There's a physical bill too. The IEA expects data centres to use around 945 TWh of electricity by 2030, more than double today and slightly more than Japan uses in a year, with AI as the biggest driver. Plus all the water for cooling.

And the prices we've been paying were never the real prices. Venture money has been covering a lot of every token we burn, and that's already unwinding. GitHub Copilot stopped counting requests and started billing by tokens in June, and Gartner now reckons AI coding costs will pass the average developer's salary by 2028.

How big could the correction be? Nobody knows, but we've seen how fast sentiment can turn. In January 2025, when DeepSeek released a cheap and capable model, Nvidia lost about $593 billion in market value in a single day, a record for any company on Wall Street. Meanwhile spending keeps climbing. Zylo's 2026 index found enterprise spend on AI-native apps more than doubled in a year, and its September update put the jump at 334%. When the dot-com bubble burst in 2000, the tech industry was a fraction of today's size. A shock now would hit something far bigger and far more dependent on AI.

The hopeful side of that DeepSeek moment is that efficiency can improve fast. Models that do more with less are the best news on the cost side, and we badly need them, because the energy and hardware to run everything people are promising simply isn't there yet.

I don't think AI is going away. The US and China are spending far too much on it for that, and m y guess is basic AI compute ends up treated a bit like roads or the electricity grid. But the all-you-can-eat phase is ending, and the teams burning tokens on vague prompts and giant rewrites are going to notice first.

One prediction for 2027: we'll start seeing programming languages designed mainly for AI agents rather than people. It's already happening in a small way. In May, Vercel Labs open-sourced Zero, an experimental language built for coding agents, with a compiler that talks to agents through structured, machine-readable commands. Early days, and humans still designed it, but you can see where it's heading.

And honestly, despite all of the above, I'm having more fun building than I have in years. I can start an idea on a Saturday morning and have a working prototype live by the evening. Not long ago that took a small team and most of a quarter.

So is AI helping developers or hurting them? Both, and which one you get depends mostly on habits. The people thinking in systems, keeping changes small and putting a human on the final call are doing great. The teams shipping first drafts and approving diffs nobody read are building up a bill they haven't seen yet.

Which brings me back to that 2 a.m. alert. The fix was never "use less AI". It's making sure that when something breaks at 2 a.m., at least one person on the team knows how it works.

#443. Roller coaster safety is a matter of science

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every print subscription comes with full digital access

Extreme ride guidelines don't all reflect the latest findings

Current industry guidelines for roller coaster design don’t account for all the forces riders face on extreme coasters from previous decades. Hershey Park’s Fahrenheit, pictured here, briefly held the world record for steepness when it opened in 2008.

The first roller coaster in the United States to loop upside down tucked riders by twos into bench seats, secured them with a single seat belt and subjected them to an estimated 7.5 times the force of gravity (some say it was closer to 12 g). The Flip Flap Railway opened in New York City’s Sea Lion Park in 1895, where the roller coaster reportedly strained backs, wrenched necks and dazzled spectators until the park closed in 1902.

Now, roller coaster safety is in the news again. In September, three lawsuits were filed against Six Flags and ride manufacturer S&S Global, alleging that riders sustained “catastrophic” brain injuries on the roller coaster X2 at California’s Magic Mountain theme park. These are just the most recent cases related to the ride, filed after a CNN investigation found dozens of injuries on X2 leading to hospitalization, disability and death. On September 29, Six Flags announced X2 would shut down permanently even though it said the ride “consistently passed a multitude of safety tests.”

We summarize the week's scientific breakthroughs every Thursday.

Weeks later, the company said it would temporarily close another nine coasters across the United States and Canada after a man fell from his seat on the roller coaster Goliath and died at Six Flags Over Georgia. Six Flags announced Oct. 24 that it would add seat belts to Goliath and other coasters, and a spokesperson told Science News that the company is “committed to rigorous ride inspection, maintenance and safety procedures.” But the incidents and closures are prompting new questions about coaster design and safety — particularly when they are operating correctly — and whether guidelines overlook crucial factors.

Roller coasters are not regulated at the federal level in the United States; each state has its own approach to their management. But an international technical group comprising over 1,000 industry professionals, the ASTM Committee F24 on Amusement Rides and Devices, publishes recommendations that are considered industry standards.

The first ASTM standards for roller coaster design were released in 1982. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission had been stripped of authority to regulate fixed-site amusement rides the year before. In the wake of this newfound freedom, it was a “roller coaster arms race,” says Brian Avery, an event and tourism management researcher at the University of Florida in Gainesville who studies risk and amusement ride safety protocols. The period was characterized by attempts to “build it taller, faster, longer.”

When X2, originally called X, opened in 2002, it was the world’s first fourth-dimension coaster. It catapulted people 60 meters high, traveling 122 kilometers per hour while its seats rotated independently on their own tracks. In the 24 years since, only 20 or so other fourth-dimension coasters have been built, and several have closed.

As roller coasters grew more extreme, injuries increased and were more publicized, sparking calls for further research and regulation.

Generally, roller coasters are quite safe. Out of 1.7 billion annual amusement park rides across North America, just 652 roller coaster injuries were reported in 2024 by parks choosing to share data. Studies suggest that riding a roller coaster is very unlikely to cause a brain injury, although most research on roller coaster brain impacts is about a decade or more old.

In 2002, a study used reported g forces from three roller coasters in a mathematical model to determine that their acceleration levels were below those predicted to cause head injuries. A 2017 study found brain effects on three roller coasters that were low on average, but “comparable with mild sports impacts,” like those sustained by heading a soccer ball, at their peak.

“The limiting of g forces alone might be an insufficient design criterion for roller coaster rides,” wrote the authors of the 2017 study.

In 2020, David Eager, a biomechanical engineer at the University of Technology Sydney, coauthored a paper on under-acknowledged forces affecting roller coaster safety, including jerk (the rate at which acceleration changes), snap (the rate at which jerk changes) and vibration.

“Roller coaster passengers need time to sense acceleration changes and adjust their muscle tension or suffer conditions such as whiplash,” Eager wrote.

The ASTM standards are exacting in some ways. Research conducted during the space race led to a 6g recommended limit on roller coaster acceleration exposure. However, ASTM doesn’t make recommendations about other types of motion on a roller coaster that might impact the neck, head and brain. It disregards forces lasting less than 200 milliseconds, no matter how intense.

But each tiny imperfection and variation in the circumference of the track — especially plentiful in coasters built before modern manufacturing techniques — causes a jolt, says Jurnan Schilder, a mechanical engineer and researcher who teaches roller coaster design at the University of Twente in Amsterdam. He studies ways to create smoother roller coaster rides.

Schilder’s research suggests that at each track imperfection, “theoretically, you experience infinite jerk.” On a fourth-dimension coaster, he says, two sets of tracks mean double the variations. “Not only is the train sometimes shaking, but also the tilting mechanism is shaking.”

Research beyond rides also suggests intense and repeated rattling, jostling and vibration could meaningfully impact the brain. The U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration recommends limiting the duration and intensity of whole-body vibration. In April, the Virginia Tech Helmet Lab, which studies the science of brain injuries, published an article suggesting some concussions experienced by baseball players may result from just milliseconds of vibration.

North Carolina high school student Kayleigh Bonner’s quest to understand her father’s death exposed similar risks. After her father, a retired U.S. Coast Guard surfman who captained boats through rough waves for 21 years, experienced symptoms of a brain injury and died by suicide, Kayleigh surveyed 77 other elite surfmen for a senior capstone project. She found that 89 percent of those she spoke to had some symptoms of brain injury likely caused by the intense, rapidly changing forces traveling through their bodies as their boats were tossed by waves. Her quest to understand her father’s death was written about in July in the New York Times, and soon after acknowledged by the Coast Guard.

Of course, professional baseball players and Coast Guard surfmen have more significant and prolonged exposure to these forces than occasional roller coasters riders. But as researchers discover more about how different kinds of motion impact the brain, safety standards seem to lag behind. Minimizing jerk and snap is “an important consideration,” Eager says.

The mounting roller coaster-related headlines have renewed scrutiny of both the existing coaster safety standards and the lack of federal regulation. U.S. Rep. André Carson, a Democrat from Indiana, has called to pass a bill he first introduced in 2025 that would create federal oversight of amusement rides. Since 1999, Democratic U.S. Sen. Ed Markey of Massachusetts has repeatedly introduced legislation to regulate fixed rides, but the bills have consistently stalled.

The amusement industry has long opposed federal oversight. In 2010, Avery coauthored a paper asserting that “the industry needs to find a collective voice and establish consistent self‐regulating policies in order to curb the incidents rates and avoid federal regulation.”

But Schilder says: “It makes you wonder: Are the current safety standards sufficient?”

For modern rides, Schilder says he believes they are. Modern manufacturing techniques mean roller coasters designed and built today are much smoother than predecessors. ASTM standards that filter out brief but intense forces, like vibration and jerk, are likely fine for rides that don’t vibrate and jerk very much. But at many parks, the ambitious and intense rides born of the roller coaster arms race are still in place.

Riders are “flirting, if you would, with danger when they experience rides and devices. They know that they’re more likely than not going to be safe,” Avery says. The point is to be “right on the cusp.”

#600. After 150 Years, Scientists May Have Solved the Mystery of Westminster Abbey’s Roman Coffin

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Radiocarbon dating and traces of a vanished wooden coffin suggest that a Roman sarcophagus at Westminster Abbey became the final resting place of a medieval abbot.

A Roman stone coffin at Westminster Abbey bears the name of a wealthy Briton who lived nearly 1,700 years ago. Yet the skeleton found inside appears to belong to someone who died centuries later. After more than 150 years of uncertainty, new archaeological evidence has narrowed the mystery to two medieval monks who once led the monastery.

The limestone sarcophagus carries an inscription commemorating Valerius Amandinus, a wealthy Briton who lived in the city around AD 300 to 400. Its lid, however, features a large Latin cross dating to approximately the 10th or 11th century. The difference in dates suggests that the Roman coffin was reused for a much later burial, leaving the identity of its occupant unresolved.

A new analysis by Professor Warwick Rodwell, Consultant Archaeologist at Westminster Abbey, concludes that the remains most likely belonged to a high-ranking medieval monk. His findings, published in the peer-reviewed Journal of the British Archaeological Association, identify two possible occupants.

The leading candidate is Aelfwig, who died around 1020 after serving as Abbot of Westminster Abbey, which was then a Benedictine monastery. Another possibility is his successor, Wulnoth, who died in 1049. Both men are believed to have been buried at the Abbey.

“We have two viable contenders for the occupant of the sarcophagus,” says Rodwell.

The coffin’s complicated history has made identifying its occupant particularly difficult. Workmen first accidentally uncovered it in 1820 while working in the area of King Henry III’s original sacristy on the north side of Westminster Abbey. During that encounter, the stone lid was broken into six pieces. The damage was repaired when the sarcophagus was officially rediscovered in 1869.

The burial site has since undergone several investigations. In 2009, Channel 4’s Time Team examined the archaeology and structure of the medieval sacristy. More recently, Pre-Construct Archaeology (PCA), one of the UK’s largest commercial archaeological and heritage practices, carried out an excavation completed in 2025. That work was needed to enable construction of the King Charles III Sacristy, which is scheduled to open in 2027.

One of the most revealing developments, however, came in 2016, when the Roman sarcophagus was sent to a conservation studio. Although the skeleton had long been believed to be lost, PCA researchers lifted the lid and discovered that an almost-complete set of human remains was still inside.

The researchers also noticed a rectangular outline marked by white residue on the interior of the stone coffin. The residue came from lime, a substance traditionally used in burials, and its shape suggested that a wooden coffin had once rested within the sarcophagus.

“Although no physical traces of that feature remained, it can only have been a timber coffin, which has completely decayed over the course of the seven centuries that the sarcophagus was buried in the sacristy courtyard,” writes Rodwell.

This evidence helps explain how the Roman burial container was reused. The medieval occupant appears to have been placed inside a wooden coffin, which was then enclosed within the older stone sarcophagus. Its lid was re-carved with a prominent Latin cross, adapting the Roman monument for a Christian burial.

Rodwell describes the surviving outline of the wooden coffin as a “remarkable new discovery.” He also believes the deceased was “honored” with a “fine sarcophagus,” suggesting that the burial was intended for someone of considerable religious standing, potentially an abbot.

Further evidence came from a detailed examination of the human remains. James Young Langthorne, a human bone specialist with PCA, determined that the deceased was a man between 35 and 49 years old who stood approximately 5 feet 9 inches (175 centimeters) tall.

Those findings differ considerably from an assessment made in the 1800s, which described the skeleton as belonging to a “robust young man” who was no more than 30 years old and stood about 5 feet 7 inches (170 centimeters) tall.

A sample of bone was also sent to the Scottish Universities Environmental Research Centre for radiocarbon dating. The results placed the remains between AD 987 and 1052, a period that includes the deaths of both Aelfwig and Wulnoth.

“This confirms that the reuse of the sarcophagus, and the associated remodeling of its cover, occurred in the late Saxon period,” says Rodwell.

The new age estimate and radiocarbon results support the possibility that either abbot was buried in the sarcophagus. Neither man’s birth date is known, making it difficult to distinguish between them using the skeletal evidence alone.

“We do not know the date of birth of either. But either would fit equally well if we assume the Abbot was in his early twenties when the appointment was made and close to fifty when he died.”

Aelfwig may nevertheless have a slight advantage as a candidate because of his earlier connection to the monastery.

“The fact Aelfwig was already a monk of Westminster might favor him.”

Although the evidence cannot yet establish which abbot occupied the coffin, the combination of radiocarbon dating, skeletal analysis, and archaeological discoveries has substantially narrowed the possibilities. The sarcophagus, originally created in Roman Britain and later adapted for a medieval Christian burial, is now on public display in the Queen’s Diamond Jubilee Galleries at Westminster Abbey.

#462. Common drugs may help dangerous viruses spread through the body

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Medications are designed to treat diseases and relieve symptoms, but some can also produce unintended effects that put patients at risk. While side effects are common with many treatments, researchers have identified a particularly concerning possibility involving drugs used to control inflammation.

Scientists at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU) have discovered that a widely used class of anti-inflammatory medications may make it easier for certain viruses to infect cells and spread throughout the body.

"We have found a surprising and potentially serious side effect of a class of anti-inflammatory drugs. They can help viruses spread more easily in the body," says Denis Kainov, a professor at the Department of Clinical and Molecular Medicine at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU).

Common Anti-Inflammatory Drugs May Help Viruses Spread

The medications at the center of the findings belong to a class called Janus kinase inhibitors, or JAKs. These drugs are widely prescribed to control inflammation, but their effects on the immune system may also leave the body more vulnerable to viral infections.

"Among the viruses that more easily affect the body with the help of these drugs are Rift Valley fever virus, influenza A, adenovirus and SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19," Kainov says.

These viruses can cause significant illness, making any treatment that potentially increases their ability to spread a serious concern.

Why Arthritis Drugs Can Weaken Antiviral Defenses

In a new study published in NAR Molecular Medicine, Kainov and his colleagues investigated how JAK inhibitors influence the body's ability to defend itself against viruses.

"JAK inhibitors are often prescribed for autoimmune diseases and inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis," says Erlend Ravlo, a PhD research fellow at NTNU and first author of the article.

Although these medications can be highly effective at reducing inflammation, their benefits come with an important trade-off. By suppressing certain immune responses, they can also interfere with the mechanisms that normally protect the body from viral infections.

"In particular, the drugs suppress an important immune signalling pathway that helps protect healthy cells from viral attacks," Ravlo said.

This pathway plays a central role in coordinating the body's response when viruses attempt to infect healthy tissue.

Scientists Discover How JAK Inhibitors Disrupt Immune Protection

The research team found that JAK inhibitors, including the medication baricitinib, can reduce the activity of genes involved in fighting viral infections.

"These genes play a crucial role in the body's response to viral infections," said Aleksandr Ianevski, an NTNU researcher and coauthor of the new paper.

When JAK inhibitors interfere with this immune signaling system, they weaken an important layer of antiviral protection. Without the usual defensive response, viruses may find it easier to establish an infection and spread between cells.

To investigate these effects, the scientists combined advanced virology methods with gene expression analysis and organoid technology. Their experiments included cells from the lungs, eyes and brain, along with miniature organ models grown in the laboratory.

These approaches allowed the team to examine how the drugs affect antiviral defenses across different types of human tissue.

"Although JAK inhibitors are effective in the treatment of inflammation, this shows that they can pose a hidden risk for patients who have latent or active viral infections," says Ravlo.

What the Findings Mean for Patients and Future Treatments

The results highlight the importance of carefully considering the risks associated with JAK inhibitors, particularly when patients may already have a viral infection or when outbreaks are occurring.

Researchers suggest that healthcare professionals should exercise caution when prescribing these medications during periods of increased viral transmission.

However, the same immune-suppressing properties that create potential risks for patients could also offer advantages in certain research settings. Under controlled laboratory conditions, these effects may help scientists develop vaccines or evaluate potential antiviral medications.

The researchers hope their findings will encourage greater awareness of how JAK inhibitors influence the immune system and support further investigations into their safest and most effective uses.

"We recommend further studies to better understand how the drugs can be used optimally, especially during virus outbreaks or pandemics," says Ianevski.
