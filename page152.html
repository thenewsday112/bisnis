The genesis of Foster's "Lost on Paradise" is something that’s been percolating in his fertile mind for many years.

I’ve never been one of those moms who turns school lunches into elaborate, three-dimensional recreations of The Very Hungry Caterpillar. I have enormous admiration for parents who do this, both for their creativity and their apparent mastery of time management, but that’s not me. Still, I appreciate a little whimsy in the kitchen, particularly when it’s not an elaborate production.

I make my kids eggs nearly every day, and while they usually prefer them frambled, I’m always interested in changing things up. So when I spotted an Instagram reel of beautiful little eggs in silicone flower molds, I was intrigued. They’d be lovely for Mother’s Day brunch or a baby shower, I thought, but why stop at flowers? With Halloween approaching, I decided to try the technique with pumpkin molds instead.

My first attempt was an unmitigated disaster. My second produced some of the cutest little pumpkin eggs I’ve ever seen. Fortunately, the difference came down to one very simple step.

The idea is simple: Instead of cracking an egg directly into simmering water, you crack it into a silicone mold, which holds its shape as the egg cooks. The result resembles a poached egg, only with a much more festive silhouette.

For Halloween, I used pumpkin-shaped molds, complete with little stems. But the possibilities extend well beyond October. Flowers would be charming for a spring brunch, hearts would be perfect for Valentine’s Day, and stars would fit if July has you feeling patriotic. You could even make them simply because it’s Tuesday, and breakfast could use a little personality.

There’s just one detail you absolutely cannot overlook: The molds need to be greased thoroughly.

I forgot this step during my first attempt, and realized my mistake while the eggs were cooking. I held out hope that the silicone might be forgiving. It wasn’t. What should have been a collection of cheerful little pumpkins emerged looking like they’d survived a particularly gruesome Halloween horror movie. The eggs were edible, but their resemblance to pumpkins was largely theoretical.

For round two, I gave the molds a generous coating of cooking spray, and the difference was remarkable. The eggs released beautifully, with their pumpkin shapes intact.

You’ll need eggs, cooking spray, food-grade silicone molds that are safe for cooking, and a skillet or shallow saucepan with a lid. Choose molds that are large enough to hold an egg without overflowing, but not so large that they won’t fit into your pan.

Start by coating the inside of each mold thoroughly with cooking spray, making sure to reach the corners and any decorative details. Crack an egg into each mold, then arrange the molds in a skillet with enough gently simmering water to come partway up the sides without spilling over the edges. Cover the pan and allow the eggs to cook gently.

Check the eggs as they cook, since deeper molds may need additional time. The whites should be completely set before you remove them.

Carefully lift the molds from the water, let them cool briefly, then gently loosen the edges and release the eggs onto a plate. Serve them on toast, alongside breakfast potatoes or however your family usually enjoys eggs.

I loved how well the pumpkins turned out, particularly considering that the whole process required little more effort than making our usual breakfast. And while I still have no plans to turn my kids’ school lunches into miniature works of art, I can certainly get behind a pumpkin-shaped egg.

#2558. Body Armor Ban Struck Down by N.Y. Federal Court

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From today's decision by Judge John Sinatra (W.D.N.Y.) in Heeter v. James:

New York enacted a first-of-its-kind law prohibiting law-abiding residents-who are not engaged or employed in an eligible profession-from purchasing body armor for self-defense. New York enacted this legislation "to further strengthen gun laws." New York's body armor legislation, however, violates the Second Amendment….

In D.C. v. Heller (2008), the Court made it clear what the term "Arms" meant when the Second Amendment was ratified. In explaining that "[t]he 18th-century meaning" of the term "Arms" is "no different from the meaning today[,]" the Court quoted multiple dictionaries from the Seventeenth Century. Specifically, "[t]he 1773 edition of Samuel Johnson's dictionary defined 'arms' as '[w]eapons of offence, or armour of defence.' 1 Dictionary of the English Language 106 (4th ed.) (reprinted 1978) (hereinafter Johnson). Timothy Cunningham's important 1771 legal dictionary defined 'arms' as 'any thing that a man wears for his defence, or takes into his hands, or useth in wrath to cast at or strike another.' 1 A New and Complete Law Dictionary; see also N. Webster, American Dictionary of the English Language (1828) (reprinted 1989).'' As a result, this Court is bound by Heller's textual analysis, which demonstrates that the word "arms" included armor at the Nation's founding (as it does today)….

The State submits that, during the Founding Era, armor "had become rare or even entirely obsolete in everyday American English" and "the protective leather gear of th[e] pre-Kevlar era was notoriously ineffective against bullets, meaning civilians did not typically wear such body protection either." This argument runs headlong into the definitions adopted by Heller, nevertheless indicating that the term "arms"—as it was defined in the Founding Era—included defensive body armor that (according to the State) was supposedly rarely utilized in 1791.

Body armor may be used more today than Defendants' understanding of its use in 1791. But it strains credulity to say that an arm that was rarely used during the Founding Era—yet still protected by the Second Amendment per Heller—is no longer protected despite being much more common among Americans today.

Moreover, this conclusion is consistent with Heller's clear statement that the Second Amendment "extends, prima facie, to all instruments that constitute bearable arms, even those that were not in existence at the time of the founding." While body armor may not have been in common use at the founding—although the commonly understood definition of "arms" included body armor—neither were other arms commonly used for self-defense, such as resolvers, semiautomatic pistols, and stun guns. See Caetano v. Massachusetts (2016) (Alito, J., concurring). The defensive body armor available today is "no more exempt from the Second Amendment's protections, simply because [it was] unknown to the First Congress, than electronic communications are exempt from the First Amendment, or electronic imaging devices are exempt from the Fourth Amendment." …

Even if the definitions put forth by the Supreme Court in Heller are dicta, this Court has an "obligation to accord great deference to Supreme Court dicta," absent a change in the legal landscape. Defendants have not suggested—nor can they—that a change in the legal landscape exists that would require this Court to ignore how Heller defined the term "arms." …

Because Plaintiffs have demonstrated that body armor is an "arm" protected by the Second Amendment, the burden shifts to the State to justify its regulation by demonstrating that it is consistent with the Nation's historical tradition of arms regulation. The State fails to do so….

The State argues that there exists a "centuries-long historical tradition" demonstrating that, "when body armor has been in use, it has been subject to government regulation." Yet, the State is unable to identify a single regulation on body armor post-ratification…. The State contends that its body armor ban is consistent with "English monarchs and parliaments" who "regularly issued laws forbidding or restricting the wearing of armor …." Not only is this analogy irrelevant to the regulation of arms in the United States, but it also ignores the historical reason the right to keep and bear arms is "understood to be an individual right protecting against public and private violence." Accordingly, the Court interprets the English history put forth by the State in light of these principles and articulates that it is not "sufficiently probative" to defend New York's body armor ban….

The State points to the Statute of Northampton for support for its position that there is a long-standing tradition of restricting who can purchase body armor. The law prohibited anyone to "go, nor ride armed by night, nor by day, in fairs markets, nor in the presence of the justices or other ministers, nor in no part elsewhere, upon pain to forfeit their armor to the King and their bodies to prison, at the king's pleasure." Notwithstanding the ink that continues to be spilled over this law, the Supreme Court has stated that "the Statute of Northampton—at least as it was understood during the Middle Ages—has little bearing on the Second Amendment adopted in 1791." N.Y. State Rifle & Pistol Ass'n, Inc. v. Bruen (2022)….

The State's analogy to the Statute of Northampton is an attempt to justify its body armor ban by speculating that individuals only wear body armor to terrify the public. Yet, this ignores the reality put forth by Plaintiffs, that the public is more likely to reach for body armor to protect themselves from terrifying circumstances. In Rahimi, the Supreme Court concluded that "[a]n individual found by a court to pose a credible threat to the physical safety of another may be temporarily disarmed consistent with the Second Amendment." Here, however, the State is suggesting that a merely broad-based suspicion over the population at large is sufficient to justify a universal ban on the sale and purchase of a specific type of arm. And to the extent that the State analogizes this to prohibiting criminals from purchasing body armor, other statutes already criminalize the use of body armor while committing violent crimes. The State's categorical prohibition of a specific type of arm to law-abiding citizens at large, is untethered from any historical tradition-including the Statute of Northampton….

#3634. I Didn’t Run for a Month Before Boston—and Still Crossed the Marathon Finish Line. How Cross-Training Kept Me on Track for Race Day.

With a doctor’s clearance, you can safely cross-train your way to your next starting line.

When I received my acceptance into my first-ever Boston Marathon, I hoped for every element of my training to go perfectly—speedwork, 20-milers, strength training, and everything in between. And, for a while, it all did. I hit my mileage goals, tackled my long runs, and incorporated quality workouts into my training, all with the hope of making it to the Boylston Street finish line feeling strong.

Then, I decided to push through a tough 18-mile long run during a busy work trip about six weeks out from Marathon Monday. I knew that I’d have no time to properly recover, refuel, and rest after the long run—but I completed the run anyway. That’s when my training plan started to go off the rails.

Nagging hip tendinitis popped up, and, in the days following, I couldn’t run without pain. I found myself sitting in the orthopedist’s office looking for answers. Not showing up for Boston was completely out of the question, and if the doctor said I could safely make it to the start line, my stubborn self would be there. I wanted to experience everything that makes the Boston Marathon so iconic—Heartbreak Hill, the Wellesley Scream Tunnel, the turn by the firehouse onto Commonwealth Avenue, the “right on Hereford, left on Boylston”—and wanted to take advantage of that BQ I trained hard to snag in the first place.

The doc gave me the all clear to continue training, but with one hitch: I needed to cross-train until the hip pain subsided and my tendinitis healed. So, for the final month of my Boston block, I traded my runs for hours spent on the elliptical. I missed the peacefulness of my morning runs on the local trail, and especially my marathon-pace, adrenaline-filled long runs, but each time I got on the elliptical, I knew that I could still keep some semblance of fitness by staying consistent. In the last 10 days before the race, I finally added running back into my routine.

Now, was my first run from Hopkinton to downtown Boston perfect? Far from it—of the four marathons I’ve completed, my finish time in Boston (my second-ever marathon) is the slowest performance I’ve had. But was it one of my favorite, most memorable races? Absolutely. I left Boston floored by the giant, screaming crowds of spectators that made even my toughest miles more enjoyable, and felt grateful that I had my dad cheering me on at numerous spots along the course. For the first time in my running career, I completely threw performance goals out the window in favor of soaking up the moment—and I crossed the finish line more than satisfied with my first Boston experience.

Any injury or setback that interrupts your race training is both frustrating and confusing. However, if you’re cleared by a medical expert, cross-training activities like swimming, cycling, or using the elliptical could help you sustain enough fitness to make it to your goal finish line.

“It is absolutely possible to maintain a significant amount of cardiovascular fitness through cross-training, but I think what’s important is that we separate being aerobically fit from being prepared to run whatever that event is,” says Amanda Katz, a New York City-based certified run coach, strength coach, and Equinox instructor.

Figuring out whether you should cross-train for your goal race depends on a variety of factors—and will likely require a stamp of approval from your medical provider.

Cross-training is better for staying on track toward your race than not exercising at all, but it still isn’t the same as completing your quality run workouts, long runs, and easy miles. Only running can truly prepare you for the demands of running, whether you’re training for a 5K or a marathon. Your bones, muscles, tendons, joints, and ligaments all need to be able to handle the physical impact that comes with running in order to make it to a finish line fully prepared.

But, like running, cross-training provides a physiological stimulus that can challenge your cardiovascular system and aerobic fitness. When you swim, cycle, or hop on the elliptical, among other cross-training activities, you work your cardio without the impact that running places on your muscles, joints, tendons, and bones. That makes cross-training great for runners overcoming an injury, since you’re taking out the mechanical load of running. It’s also a suitable training mode for runners with a past injury history and masters athletes looking to boost their recovery and maintain longevity.

Kylie Morgan, ACSM-certified clinical exercise physiologist and UESCA-certified run coach, suggests thinking of your body like a car, with your aerobic fitness as your engine. Cross-training can help keep the engine running, but you still need the chassis to tolerate the load and impact running puts on your body. “You can keep your heart and lungs working, but that doesn’t fully reproduce the repeated impact loading we get for our muscles, tendons, bones,” Morgan says.

Just like you would in an obstacle-free race training block, you’ll want to pair any cross-training regimen with strength training or injury rehab exercises. Switching out running for cross-training activities doesn’t make strength work optional, Morgan emphasizes, and it’s an important part of any runner’s training program for becoming race-ready. Even if you have an injury that makes some strength work challenging, your doctor or physical therapist will likely prescribe you with exercises you can successfully complete to treat your injury and build strength.

Create a cross-training plan that fits your needs, and it can lead to race day results. Look at Parker Valby. When the 2024 Paris Olympian competed for the University of Florida, she became notorious for her successful cross-training schedule, often only running three days a week and spending the rest of her time on the Arc Trainer elliptical. Replacing the majority of her easy runs with cross-training certainly worked out well for the current New Balance pro; Valby won six NCAA titles across cross-country, indoor, and outdoor track as a Gator.

But you also don’t want to slap cross-training onto your training plan like a Band-Aid. Ask yourself what the purpose for your cross-training is, and whether pushing forward to race day will still bring you a sense of satisfaction and achievement. Even with a solid cross-training and strength plan in place, err on the side of caution when it comes to setting realistic expectations for race day.

“Someone may be able to cross-train for several weeks and maintain a pretty impressive aerobic engine, and get to that start line and even finish line well,” Katz says. “But if the reason they aren’t running is an injury, I would really ask why are we trying so hard to make this particular race happen?” Sometimes, the smarter decision for your health and longevity in the sport may be deferring your race.

If you notice a niggle or painful flare-up that doesn’t feel normal, don’t panic. Before you begin self-diagnosing your injury with the help of Google, or convincing yourself that your race dreams are over, get a professional assessment as soon as possible for both peace of mind and jump-starting training plan adjustments as needed, Morgan says.

Once you get medical attention, start to game plan. If you’re diagnosed with an injury or are temporarily restricted from running, yet receive the all-clear to continue other activities, you can go ahead with determining the cross-training exercises best for you.

But your injury timeline also matters. I made my decision to cross-train for Boston largely based on the hip tendinitis recovery timeline provided by my doctor. The doc told me to manage and monitor my symptoms for the next month, which left around two weeks or so to return to running before race day. Keeping that in mind, I knew that if I stuck with a cross-training and strength regimen and noticed my symptoms subsiding, I’d still have time to test out running and make sure I didn’t have any lingering hip pain. If you find yourself faced with an extended period of time away from running spanning multiple months, it may be best to reevaluate your goals and consider deferring your race.

Of all the modes of cross-training—cycling, swimming, elliptical work, rowing, and more—there’s no perfect activity that’s best suited for runners. Morgan suggests choosing the activity you enjoy the most and can see yourself sticking with throughout your cross-training period. “The best cross-training option isn’t necessarily what looks like running, like in terms of biomechanics, but the one that [the runner] can safely use to create this training stimulus that we want, but also the one that they like,” Morgan says.

I settled on the elliptical before Boston because I enjoyed moving my body in a motion that is very similar to running. But for you, aqua jogging at your local pool or settling into the saddle of your gym’s stationary bike may not only fit the physical requirements of your recovery, but your personality, too. You should also complement your cross-training sessions with strength training, and regularly complete any prescribed exercises to help your injury heal.

Because cross-training omits the impact of running, no cross-training session will translate one-to-one. However, you should try to align your cross-training as closely as possible to the runs on your original training plan. Morgan recommends the rate of perceived effort (RPE) or recreating the goal of a run, such as intervals to get the most out of your cross-training. “Different exercise modes produce different physiological responses, muscular demands, and perceived effort, and heart rate responses can differ between running, cycling, deep-water running,” Morgan says. “So I wouldn’t look at the duration of the workout. I’d look at the purpose of the workout.”

The structure of your cross-training plan depends on the race distance, too. A marathon runner will need several hours of movement, while a 5K runner may want to put more effort into shorter, high-intensity sessions, Katz says.

Before my hip tendinitis flare-up, I ran from 55 to 65 miles a week. When developing my formula for cross-training success, I replicated workouts on the elliptical and focused on RPE, along with heart rate. My long runs became two-plus hours on the elliptical. My tempo runs became tempo elliptical workouts. I even tried to recreate speedwork, adding in short, intense efforts at higher resistance levels. I also aimed to keep my training volume high and I think that helped me maintain some fitness.

Finally cleared to run? Avoid trying to fit missed running into those last days before your race. “The workouts in a training plan are intended to create adaptations over several weeks. You can’t compress that into the final week or two before a race,” Morgan says. “That’s always going to end in bad news.”

Once you get that green flag, evaluate your mentality. Will you be okay shooting for a B or C goal rather than your top performance ambition? Are you willing to shift your priorities to focus on enjoying the race rather than shooting for a big PR? “If we know that they are in a place where they can start and they can safely finish, adjusting those expectations, incorporating a run/walk protocol potentially, and making sure that joy and completion are at the forefront versus a time on the clock are all things that we can do,” Katz says.

Relegated to cross-training for just a couple of weeks ahead of your race, or return to running with months ahead of your race? You may be able to stick with your original race goals. Find yourself solely cross-training for an extended period ahead of race day, and you’ll likely want to adjust your expectations, and evaluate whether making it to the start line is the smartest option for you. You may want to use a run/walk approach, adjust your pace, or cover up your watch to make for a more successful race experience, Katz says.

In fact, forgetting your high expectations or adjusting your goals could be the key to having a blast on race day despite the not-so-perfect training block. I wasn’t afraid to throw my 3:15 finish time target out the window, and instead focused on process goals, like staying in control of my pace in those first downhill miles. That turned out to be a lifesaver, as I turned all my attention that I would’ve given to my mile splits to embracing the unmatched Boston Marathon atmosphere.

Your checklist for cross-training for a race:

✅ Rely on your support system—doctor, physical therapist, run coach, family, friends, etc.—to decide whether cross-training toward race day will not only be safe but also still give you satisfaction

✅ Find the cross-training activities you like the most

✅ Use perceived effort and workout purpose to guide your cross-training

✅ Pair your cross-training with strength training and prescribed rehab exercises for supporting your injury recovery

✅ Embrace your race day experience—and don’t let your imperfect training block hamper your spirits

As resorts start snowmaking for the season, SKI chatted with experts to clear up the misconceptions about the process and examine the role it’s come to play in mountain operations.

Some skiers may wonder why they were encouraged not to water their lawns this year, while nearby resorts can pump snow onto the mountainsides. The answer is both simple and complicated, but we’ll keep it simple.

Water rights laws vary by state and get complex, but on a basic level, users like ski resorts generally have the right to use a certain amount of water in a certain way. Critically, snowmaking is a “non-consumptive” use, meaning the water either flows back into the watershed or evaporates.

“It all eventually ends up back in the environment,” explained Jackson Hole Slope Maintenance Director Austin Irby. “Instead of using it, getting heavy metals in it—some industries are polluting it—ours is good, clean water when it goes back.”

Courtney LaBrie, Director of Sustainability for the National Ski Areas Association, added that snowmaking can make runoff patterns more predictable, and even be mobilized as a wildfire suppression tool.

When it comes to resorts’ ability to access that water, she noted “impacts depend heavily on local conditions, such as water rights seniority, on-site storage, water sources, and snowmaking efficiency.”

For now, at least at the resorts we spoke to, all have some right and source that allows them to keep making snow. And, as they face the threat of climate change, many are making these systems more efficient and finding new ways to keep the slopes open.

Arapahoe Basin is typically one of the first ski areas to turn on the snow guns, as temperatures hit a crucial low point at the high-elevation mountain as early as October. Interestingly, Snowmaking Manager Blair James noted, snowmaking only occurs on a handful of trails.

Even so, “It’s a really critical toehold for the season,” said VP of Mountain Operations Louis Skowyra. How much ski areas utilize and rely on snowmaking varies, but the operation can be crucial even for those that cover a small piece of their footprint.

Jackson Hole only makes snow on around five percent of its terrain, Irby said, but that small number includes lower-elevation trails that allow skiers egress off the mountain, especially in the spring.

“Snowmaking acts as a resilience tool for ski areas to get seasons started on time, supplement snow in dry years and end seasons when scheduled,” LaBrie said.

Last season, it was essential to keeping some resorts open. That comes at a cost, one resorts have to accept as the price of doing business.

“It’s a significant expense, but many times it’s a needed expense,” said Red River Sales and Marketing Manager Reed Weimer. The family-owned New Mexico ski area covers around 85 percent of its terrain with snowmaking, and Weimer said last season it made snow for about a month longer than usual.

However, resorts know tough years will happen, and most are constantly upgrading their plans and systems accordingly.

“We definitely plan for the worst case scenario,” said Whitefish Public Relations Manager Chad Sokol.

As much as staff at these resorts prepare to weather the storms, or lack thereof, climate remains a constant variable. Industry experts know it’s changing, and a 2023 NSAA study found it’s likely, if global emissions continue at a high rate, that ski areas could face issues like water source vulnerability and higher temperatures in the coming decades.

#2235. Burkina Faso Restores Death Penalty

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Burkina Faso soldiers patrol aboard a pickup truck on the road from Dori to the Goudebo refugee camp, on February 3, 2020. © 2020 OLYMPIA DE MAISMONT/AFP via Getty Images

Burkina Faso’s unelected parliament has unanimously adopted a new penal code that restores the death penalty for high treason, terrorism, acts of espionage, parricide and importation of expired or prohibited products. The country had abolished the death penalty for ordinary crimes in 2018 while its last known judicial execution took place in 1988.

Reinstating capital punishment sends a chilling message to critics of the military junta and risks becoming another tool to suppress dissent. Since the September 2022 military coup, Burkina Faso’s junta has steadily dismantled civic and political rights. Authorities have banned media outlets, suspended unions and civil society organizations, abolished multiparty politics, and subjected critics to arbitrary detention, enforced disappearances, unlawful conscription, and torture.

Justice Minister Edasso Rodrigue Bayala defended the decision to reinstate the death penalty as a necessary measure to combat terrorism. He said that armed groups exploited the previous law’s leniency by promising new recruits they would face no more than life imprisonment if convicted of terrorist acts.

Human Rights Watch opposes all capital punishment because of its inherent cruelty. The unfairness of death sentences in Burkina Faso would be exacerbated by the country’s ill-equipped justice system. All terrorism-related cases are handled by the Specialized Judicial Unit Against Terrorism-Related Crimes, established in 2017. Human Rights Watch documented in April the special court’s slow investigations and serious procedural failures that undermine defendants’ fair trial rights.

A former terrorism court judge once explained to me that judges sometimes kept suspects in detention despite insufficient evidence, fearing the military might summarily execute them if released. He described the dilemma as “better to violate the right to liberty in order to preserve the right to life.” With the death penalty’s return, even that grim calculation may no longer hold.

Reversing course on the death penalty runs counter to a broader international movement toward abolition. The United Nations General Assembly and the African Commission on Human and Peoples’ Rights have urged states to establish moratoriums on executions, narrow the scope of capital punishment, and progressively reduce the number of offenses punishable by death with a view to its eventual abolition. 

Restoring capital punishment will not resolve Burkina Faso’s security crisis. In a system already plagued by serious due process failures, the death penalty merely heightens the risk of irreversible injustice.

Renderings show two rectangular pavilion structures that sit on a thin concrete plane, elevated off the ground to meet “resiliency guidelines”, likely in reference to Miami-Dade County’s stated floodwater risks due to sea-level rise. Courtyards will pass underneath the structure.

At the centre of the project, a backlit oculus will illuminate the passage under the structure at night.
