#753. Super El Niño could kill 870,000 people around the world, warn climate scientists

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

China, India and Nigeria are are among the countries expected to be hardest hit, according to researchers at the University of Chicago

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Super El Niño could lead to nearly 900,000 extra deaths worldwide over just 10 months, climate scientists have warned in an alarming new prediction.

The El Niño Heat Mortality Tracker, produced by the University of Chicago’s Climate Impact Lab, forecasts the human impact of the weather phenomenon. El Niño occurs when the average sea surface temperatures in the central and tropical Pacific Ocean rise above the long-term average by 2C or more.

Researchers estimate the climate pattern has already lead to 484,500 excess heat-related deaths between June and September this year, with around 372,900 more forecast for the next six months.

The mortality tracker map gives a breakdown of which countries are most badly affected, with Nigeria expected to see the highest death toll. Experts predict the African nation will see more than 56,000 additional deaths across the next six months, according to Climate Impact Lab.

It forecasts a further 55,000 heat-related deaths in India, while Southeast Asia is also under threat with around 29,000 deaths predicted in Indonesia.

China, Nigeria and India alone are predicted to account for more than one third of deaths related to El Niño, according to the figures.

“Our research puts new forecasting capabilities to work to answer one of the most urgent questions we face: where is extreme heat likely to cost lives?” Michael Greenstone, co-founder of the Climate Impact Lab said.

“Being able to anticipate the deadly effects of extreme heat is a major step forward. It means we can identify where people face the greatest risks and give decision-makers a chance to act before lives are lost. The Tracker provides this information for the Super El Niño we’re living through today.”

The Climate Impact Lab’s projected total additional heat deaths has risen dramatically from a September 2026 report, following the extreme heats experienced by many countries this summer.

“The two months turned out to be much warmer than forecasted”, it said, adding that “August was the warmest August and September the second warmest September on record”.

A regular El Niño event happens irregularly, every two to seven years, while a super El Niño occurs roughly every 10 to 15 years.

The super El Niño has already been impacting weather worldwide. It typically brings wetter and stormier conditions to the US, parts of South America, and Europe during autumn and winter.

It can cause long dry spells in Australia, Indonesia and South Asia, sometimes leading to severe droughts. In Indonesia, the haze from El Niño-fuelled wildfire season has caused a spike in respiratory illnesses at home and a dramatic decline in air quality in Singapore and Malaysia.

Temperatures can also increase globally due to heat being released from the ocean into the atmosphere.

Many regions experienced a record-breaking summer this year, with Earth suffering its hottest August on record, according to the World Meteorological Organization (WMO).

However, experts says this was as a result of climate change and global warming, rather than El Niño which typically produces its strongest impacts from late autumn into winter.

Earlier this year, a new study suggested that El Niños are getting stronger because of human-caused climate change.

El Niños are more than 36 per cent stronger than they were before the industrial age began in the mid 1800s, with a 16 per cent jump in just the last 40 years showing that the increase is accelerating, according to a study in Thursday's journal Science.

“That’s important because strong El Niños have much stronger impacts on climate hazards and extremes worldwide,” said study lead author Julia Cole, a paleoclimate scientist at the University of Michigan.

“When we set out to answer the question of why this might be happening, we set our data up against a record of global temperature, and we found that the increase in El Niño’s strength closely parallels the rise in global temperature that is due to human causes such as the burning of fossil fuels.”

The projections also come after a new report in the Lancet Countdown on Health and Climate Change said extreme heat contributed to a record 710,000 fatalities in 2023, with researchers attributing over 70 per cent directly to man-made warming.

#763. United States and Japan forge missile production alliance to counter China's power

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Japan’s defense minister said Tokyo will maintain close surveillance of Chinese naval operations while continuing to accelerate its own military buildup

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

The United States and Japan have agreed to establish a production facility in Japan to jointly manufacture advanced medium-range air-to-air missiles, according to the Japanese defense chief.

The decision marks the latest step by the two allies to bolster security capabilities amid heightened concerns over China's growing military assertiveness across Asia.

Japanese Defense Minister Shinjiro Koizumi announced the agreement during a news conference Wednesday evening in Cebu, a province in the central Philippines.

His remarks followed a bilateral meeting with U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth on the sidelines of an annual gathering of Southeast Asian defense chiefs and their major Asian and Western counterparts.

During a session on Thursday, Koizumi highlighted increased Chinese naval presence near southwestern Japan, noting that three Chinese destroyers alongside a coast guard vessel recently passed through the Osumi Strait south of Kyushu into the Pacific Ocean.

He stated that it was the first time his ministry had recorded such a fleet configuration.

Koizumi pointed to the transit as "an example illustrating the challenging security environment in the region," adding that Tokyo will maintain close surveillance of Chinese naval operations while continuing to accelerate its own military buildup in southwestern Japan.

The annual meetings aim to prevent major conflict and deepen regional cooperation, though the U.S., China, and their respective allies frequently exchange criticisms during the proceedings.

The joint manufacturing effort, titled "Operation Supercharge," will establish a final assembly and inspection plant in Japan for the Advanced Medium-Range Air-to-Air Missile, or AMRAAM.

Koizumi emphasized that these missiles are essential for F-35 stealth fighters, which are operated by both the U.S. and Japanese militaries.

Recounting his meeting with Hegseth, Koizumi said he "shared a sense of crisis … regarding the increasingly severe regional situation and we agreed to strengthen the deterrence and response capabilities of the Japan-U.S. alliance with greater speed and intensity than ever before."

He added: "We reaffirmed the importance of a free and open international order based on the rule of law, which rejects any attempts to unilaterally alter the status quo through the use of force, coercion or other means."

In a separate meeting with ASEAN defense ministers led by Philippine Defense Secretary Gilberto Teodoro Jr., Hegseth offered renewed assurances regarding Washington's commitment to regional stability.

Without calling out Beijing by name, Hegseth outlined the U.S. approach.

"The approach we’re taking, we describe as strong, quiet and clear," Hegseth told the defense chiefs, adding it is "Strong enough to deter coercion and aggression, quiet enough to avoid needless escalation or public theatrics to let our actions speak for themselves."

He emphasized that the position is "clear enough that our friends, our partners and potential adversaries understand precisely where the United States stands and that we will defend our interests and the interests of our allies and partners."

Hegseth reassured the Philippines, Washington’s oldest treaty partner in Asia, that the Trump administration will maintain robust deterrence against Beijing’s actions in the South China Sea, a critical trade waterway claimed almost entirely by China.

Australian Defense Minister Richard Marles pledged that Australian forces, alongside allies like the U.S., would continue upholding international law despite Chinese actions in disputed waters.

Referring to an incident last year where a Chinese J-16 jet released flares near an Australian P-8 Poseidon surveillance plane, Marles said: "That doesn’t deter us from doing what we need to do and that’s essentially asserting the rules-based order here in the end of the Pacific."

He added: "Freedom of navigation on the high seas is profoundly important for Australia."

Territorial claims in the South China Sea remain contested among the Philippines, Vietnam, Malaysia, Brunei, and Taiwan, with frequent standoffs involving Chinese and Philippine coast guard and naval vessels.

Washington maintains that it is bound by treaty to defend the Philippines if Filipino forces, ships, or aircraft come under armed attack. Separately, Tokyo and Beijing remain locked in a maritime dispute over Japanese-controlled islands in the East China Sea.

#773. Inside Trump’s killing of USAID and where we stand now: ‘A farce to stroke Elon Musk’s ego’

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

Nicholas Enrich tried to warn that shutting down America’s primary aid agency would lead to preventable deaths – he tells Liz Cookman that the destruction of USAID has seen the US ‘walk away from our role as a leader in global health’

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

For 42 days, Nicholas Enrich tried to save the life-saving work of the US government’s global health agency from the Trump administration as they set about dismantling it.

Having worked at the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) since 2013, Enrich was appointed head of the Global Health Bureau shortly after President Donald Trump was inaugurated in January 2025.

He had been tasked with restarting vital international health programmes after Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced a waiver to an aid funding freeze. A week before, and hours after taking office, Trump had ordered a 90-day freeze on foreign assistance from the US, the world’s largest aid donor.

Enrich spent the next few weeks “banging my head against a wall trying to get that done and being thwarted at every turn by DOGE, the political appointees and by the restrictions and disruption put in place by the administration,” he told The Independent.

DOGE, the Department of Government Efficiency, was an initiative led by billionaire Elon Musk – CEO of Tesla and Space X – that spearheaded efforts to slash federal spending, including by cutting USAID’s workforce.

“By late February, it was clear that I was not going to be able to implement that waiver and restart programmes, and that it was actually kind of a farce,” he says from his home in the US.

“Meanwhile, Elon Musk, the secretary of state [Marco Rubio], and others were publicly saying that our programme had restarted,” he adds.

Enrich began documenting the obstacles in internal memos, detailing how the administration’s shifting guidance, blocked payment systems and mass contract cancellations had made it impossible to restart life-saving programmes.

In a memo dated February 28 and circulated to colleagues on March 2, he warned that the failure to deliver aid would lead to preventable deaths. About 20 minutes after sending it, he informed staff that he had been placed on administrative leave.

His contract was later terminated when, in July, USAID officially closed, with the State Department taking over its remaining programmes. The agency, set up by President John F Kennedy during the Cold War, had been the backbone of US development assistance for more than six decades.

Enrich believes its closure saw the country “walk away from our role as a leader in global health”.

From the fastest and widest-spreading Ebola outbreak ever, first reported in the Democratic Republic of Congo in May, to tuberculosis, malaria and malnutrition, “the kinds of support USAID used to provide is now the exact areas where we are seeing a breakdown,” he says. “This is mostly a really, really bad story.”

“During the first year after the dismantling of USAID, we saw destruction, suffering, and death on a massive scale. All of which is preventable. And for what?” said Enrich. Estimates have suggested that USAID cuts could have caused between 500,000 and 700,000 global deaths in the first year. Rubio and the State Department have disputed that the aid cuts have directly killed anyone.

Now, a little short of two years after the initial halt to USAID operations, the devastating global consequences are becoming ever clearer, including widespread severe hunger, increasing maternal complications, shortages of lifesaving medicine and spikes in child marriage.

The speed and lack of transition left those on the ground with little time to find alternative funding – the agency had spent billions of dollars a year on disease prevention programmes, food aid, clean water, education, agriculture and emergency relief.

Despite the insistence of Musk and the US government that no one has died as a result of the agency’s dissolution, our reporting has shown otherwise.

The Independent has documented newborn deaths in sub-Saharan Africa, deaths of pregnant women who could no longer access hospitals in Afghanistan, and heavily underresourced humanitarian responses.

Funding for HIV responses in low- and middle-income countries fell by $2.1 billion (£1.6bn) last year, putting decades of progress to combat HIV at risk. The reductions are likely to lead to a resurgence of the AIDS epidemic, United Nations agencies have warned.

In Uganda and Zimbabwe people are dying of HIV because of a lack of access medication, Chief International Correspondent Bel Trew reported for Independent TV. In the Central African Republic, and beyond, hard-won successes in efforts to stop women and babies dying in childbirth have faced a serious setback due to cuts to aid.

The Ebola epidemic, the second deadliest ever, in the DR Congo, Uganda, Kenya and Nigeria has killed almost 5,000 people so far. Health workers have warned repeatedly that the response effort had been hampered by aid cuts.

The true measurable impact is hard to calculate as the eventual dissolution of USAID was compounded by cuts to international aid by the UK and elsewhere. Experts say the repercussions will also take time to manifest, with thousands of front line workers laid off – some of whom collected mortality data.

Enrich has documented his experience at the agency in his book, Into the Wood Chipper: A Whistleblower's Account of How the Trump Administration Shredded USAID. He believes that the dissolution of the agency was not about saving money – “there was no money saved” – or because the agency was wasteful or inefficient. “It was not even about realigning priorities of foreign aid, or [Trump’s] ‘America First’ agenda,” he says.

“It was just destroyed because there were a bunch of people whose job it was to stroke the ego of Elon Musk, who got upset when he wasn't able to do whatever he wanted inside of a federal agency … Then they spread a bunch of lies about what was actually happening there,” he adds.

The Independent has contacted the State Department for further comment.

However, Enrich believes that If USAID had built more sustainable systems over the last 65 years, vulnerable countries would have been made more resilient and we would not have seen the “destruction” that he says he have thanks to aid cuts.

“The idea of transitioning to a place where countries don't need to rely on American aid should, frankly, be the goal of foreign assistance,” Enrich says.

He hopes that the US can now use the opportunity created “from the wreckage that we're seeing now” to build a system in the future that does not come at the cost of dependency. He also has faith in the American people, who are “embarrassed about the way we have broken promises on the global stage and moved away from demonstrating American generosity on kind of a mass scale”.

“Americans are angry at what has been destroyed and what has been lost,” Enrich says, adding that he predicts the US will now decide to reinvest in global health development.

“It's in our national interest because it makes the world a safer and healthier place for people to live,” he says.

#791. Overweight offshore workers could lose job under new rules, industry body warns

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Please refresh the page or navigate to another page on the site to be automatically logged inPlease refresh your browser to be logged in

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Hundreds of offshore workers could be at risk of losing their jobs when a new weight limit comes into effect next week, an industry body has warned.

Trade body Offshore Energies UK (OEUK) is bringing in guidelines from November which will require all those working offshore to weigh less than 124kg (19.5st).

OEUK said this is required on safety grounds, as it is the most a person can weigh when they are being winched from a platform into a helicopter during an emergency evacuation.

Since announcing the policy in 2025, the body said it has helped operators identify and implement measures to slim down their offshore workforces.

OEUK health and safety leader Graham Skinner said this included the “wide uptake” of GLP-1 weight-loss jabs, with some employers installing additional fridges to store the medication.

However he said weight-loss jabs are just one of the measures workers are using to trim the pounds, and they are not being treated as a “blanket solution” to the problem.

The number of workers over the 124kg limit has fallen from about 2,200 in early 2025 to 840 earlier this year, with the OEUK saying it is “delighted” so many have achieved it.

However an estimated 241-300 workers are projected to still be above the weight limit when the policy comes into effect, meaning they could face losing their jobs.

Mr Skinner said it is possible some of these people no longer work offshore in the UK, or that they will be moved to onshore roles by their employers.

“We are hoping that the vast majority of workers will have found a way through this,” he said on Thursday.

“But the reality is also that if somebody has chosen not to lose weight (or) doesn’t particularly want to comply with the policy for any reason, then there will be a route out of employment as well.

“Ultimately, it’s a safety policy, and it is related to capability.

“If somebody cannot mobilise for their work because they’re over the weight limit, then they will not be able to work.

“In the worst cases, that would mean somebody will lose their job.”

In the past, the industry made changes to safety equipment to compensate for the growing waistlines of offshore workers.

This included making lifeboats larger and more robust, and ensuring larger workers were seated next to the largest windows when travelling on helicopters.

However Mr Skinner said this was a case of only “addressing certain symptoms” of the problem, and the industry had needed to find a solution.

He explained that while the 124kg limit is based on helicopter winches, capping the weight of workers will benefit “all aspects” of life-saving equipment on offshore platforms.

He also said it could reduce the risk of medical evacuations for health-related reasons – including things like heart attacks and strokes – which in 2025 accounted for 78% of all medivacs.

He said those with the most weight to lose have had the “greatest concerns” about the policy since its announcement last year.

“It’s fair to say that everyone’s gone through that psychological process, that change curve of feeling that initial disbelief, anger, and all those emotions that go on when you’re faced with change,” he said.

“That really landed with those that are greatest affected. Those that have had to face a challenge of losing a significant amount of weight have obviously had the greatest concerns.

“However, the vast majority of workers who are already under 124kg and really unaffected by this are very pleased with the introduction of the weight limit.”

The policy has met with some pushback, with the RMT union saying it is concerned it could provide employers with a “convenient mechanism” to sack workers without proper redundancy procedures or financial compensation.

RMT general secretary Eddie Dempsey said: “Nobody disputes that safety must be the overriding priority when working offshore.

“However, safety policies must be proportionate, evidence-based and developed through meaningful consultation with the workers they affect.

“RMT is calling on OEUK to withdraw the policy and engage in meaningful consultation with trade unions to establish a fair, proportionate and evidence-based approach to offshore safety.”

Bolsonaro made the claim in a letter he posted on social media after arriving in the United States on Oct 28, having left Brazil following his defeat to leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva by more than four million votes in the Oct 25 runoff.

Bolsonaro has cited the fact that a Lula-appointed Supreme Court justice extended voting by two hours due to Lula campaign allegations of voter suppression in left-leaning northeastern states, though Bolsonaro’s campaign had also sought a voting extension due to rain in conservative southern states.

Centre-right party Uniao Brasil, which backed Bolsonaro in the second round, said it was essential that institutions “remain greater than any individual.” The election was conducted with integrity and its results must be respected, it added.

The centrist Brazilian Democratic Movement (MDB) party, which also supported Bolsonaro in the runoff, struck a similar tone.

“The MDB reaffirms its total confidence in the Brazilian electoral system and, therefore, in the legitimacy of the result of the election last Sunday,” it said.

In his letter, Bolsonaro pledged to lead the opposition from abroad and “assume the risks” that local politicians could not, adding he would support a potential bid by Senator Rogerio Marinho for Senate president in 2027.

Marinho, who served as Bolsonaro’s campaign manager, told reporters that Bolsonaro remains “a leader” and “the most prominent figure on the right” in Brazil, given the support he received from nearly 60 million voters.

But Marinho also rejected allegations of electoral fraud.

“The Liberal Party cannot make that claim, nor can I make that claim, because there is no evidence to support it,” he said.

President Donald Trump weighed in on the firing of the Pentagon's top military planner, calling him a man with “big problems” after a report suggested Army Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia was removed over allegations of leaking classified information.

The Air Force OSI investigated Sylvia's alleged conduct.

Speaking to reporters on the tarmac of Andrews Air Force Base en route to South Carolina for a campaign rally, Trump appeared to refer to Sylvia with the pronoun "She."

“She’s got problems. She’s gonna have big problems. We’re very much looking for leakers,” the president said when asked about the firing.

Sylvia was removed Wednesday as head of strategy, plans and policy on the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the critical post known in military circles as the "J5."

The Atlantic first reported the dismissal.

A defense official, speaking on condition of anonymity to discuss sensitive personnel matters, said no immediate replacement has been named for Sylvia.

The same official said no one explained the sudden firing.

The departure decapitates the Pentagon's strategic planning hub at a perilous moment.

Typically, the J5 director and staff generate and coordinate major military plans directly with both the chairman of the Joint Chiefs and the president.

Sylvia is the second general to be ousted from the prestigious J5 role under Hegseth's watch.

His predecessor, Lt. Gen. Joseph McGee, was forced into retirement last October.

Following the dismissal, a spokesperson for the Joint Staff said, “The Chairman, the Joint Staff, and the Joint Force extend our deepest gratitude to Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia and his family for their 32 years of distinguished service to our nation,” and added that they “wish him and his family the best.”

Before joining the Joint Staff, Sylvia commanded the storied 101st Airborne Division at Fort Campbell, Kentucky, until May 2025.

He previously served as the vice director for the J5.

His official Pentagon biography has already been taken offline.

Sylvia is the latest casualty in what critics describe as an unprecedented purge of American military leadership.

He joins nearly two dozen generals, admirals, and top civilian defense chiefs, including the secretaries of the Army and Navy, who have been fired, forced to retire, or sidelined under Hegseth with little to no public explanation.

Hegseth recently ordered the military to slash its roughly 800 flag and general officer positions by 20% by January 2027.

Telling troops at Quantico, he said the cuts represent "accountability and long overdue."

Lawmakers have pushed back fiercely, with Sen. Mark Kelly warning that Hegseth is “purging a generation of great leaders of the military, and it’s going to take a generation to recover from it.”

Sylvia's sudden removal leaves the Pentagon without its chief strategist just as the Trump administration prepares for a potential dramatic escalation in the Middle East.

#801. Pentagon suspects hackers caused mysterious freezer malfunctions at military bases

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Military investigators are in touch with their French counterparts about a cyberattack that crippled supermarkets there

The Pentagon believes a spate of freezer malfunctions that spoiled food at military bases across the United States in August may have been the work of hackers, according to a new report.

The malfunctions affected at least six commissaries in late August, according to a Reuters review. Commissaries are stores on military bases where service members buy their food.

In at least one of the cases, the cooling units' temperatures were cranked up overnight, leading to mounds of wilted or melted food in the morning.

“Trust us when we say it was 'spoiled,'" Fort Huachuca in Arizona said in a Facebook post on Aug. 26 after freezers "went into defrost mode where it heated up all the food.”

Military investigators were in touch with their French counterparts about a similar cyberattack on commercial freezers that disrupted supermarkets there earlier this month.

The US Department of the Army Criminal Investigation Division said its Cyber Field Office was involved in the investigation and that it was working with French law enforcement to better understand "the refrigeration outages in Europe."

When asked about the investigation by Reuters, the Pentagon said that a unit of Cyber Command – the military's cyber force – was "working in close coordination with interagency and law enforcement partners."

The Pentagon seemingly has not reached a conclusion about what happened or who might be responsible. The spate of rogue freezer behavior and the possibility of cybersabotage were first reported on August 28 by a blog called Signal and Silence.

If the military base malfunctions are confirmed to be a cyberattack, it would be yet another illustration of how hackers can target vulnerable critical infrastructure, including cold storage.

In France, a freezer cyberattack overrode temperature controls on commercial cooling units in more than 350 establishments. Supermarkets' frozen food aisles were cleared of spoiled merchandise, leading to dramatic scenes.

The French hack involved cranking the temperature inside the units up rather than merely knocking them offline, similar to what happened in Fort Huachuca in Arizona.

Some French refrigeration units had the temperature turned up so high that they caught fire.

The French attack's total costs could add up to tens of millions of euros nationwide, one supermarket owner estimated earlier this month. The French cyberattack appeared to have been easy to pull off, experts previously told Reuters.

The Danish firm Danfoss, whose controllers ran many of the stricken units, said that many of the devices were obsolete or were running outdated firmware, which could have made it easy for hackers to break in.

Reuters could not establish whether Danfoss or any other brand of freezer controller were in use at the affected US bases and Danfoss did not return its messages seeking comment.

Refrigeration plays a critical role in the distribution of fresh food. The sabotage of cold storage facilities via the internet has long been seen as a nightmare scenario, alongside the loss of electricity or water plants.

Earlier this year, the Global Food Safety Initiative, urged food safety professionals and IT leaders to work together to ensure "robust cybersecurity" for refrigeration technology, stating that "a hacked cooling system is just as dangerous as a broken one."

Hackers have sabotaged fridges and freezers in the United States and elsewhere. In 2022, hackers either targeted or broke into supermarkets in Russia and Israel that were using obsolete versions of its cold storage controllers, Danfoss previously said.

In December 2025, federal prosecutors in Los Angeles accused a Ukrainian woman of being part of a hacker group that knocked out refrigeration at a meatpacking facility in 2024, ruining more than 2,000 pounds of meat.

Bill Moore, chief executive of industrial cybersecurity firm Xona Systems, said that controllers that were initially conceived as isolated interfaces to be serviced by an in-person technician were increasingly being connected to the internet, which was the main problem.

Concerns about online exposure were not unique to cooling systems but they could have particularly serious consequences there, he said.

"It can definitely disrupt the supply chain for food," he said.

#86. US and Japan agree to establish a Japanese facility for joint missile production

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

From left, Philippines Defense Secretary Gilberto Teodoro, Australia Defense Minister Richard Marles, Japan Defense Minister Shinjiro Koizumi and U.S.Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth prepare to leave after posing for pictures before holding a Quadrilateral Meeting on the sidelines of the 20th Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) Defence Ministers' Meeting (ADMM) and 13th ADMM-Plus Meetings in Cebu, Philippines, Wednesday, Oct. 28, 2026. Rolex Dela Pena/Pool EPA via AP hide caption

CEBU, Philippines — The United States and Japan have agreed to establish a Japanese facility for the joint production of advanced medium-range air-to-air missiles, the Japanese defense chief said.

It is the latest move by the allies, which have repeatedly expressed alarm over China's growing assertiveness in Asia.

Japanese Defense Minister Shinjiro Koizumi made the announcement in a news conference Wednesday night in the central Philippine province of Cebu, where he met U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth on the sidelines of an annual meeting of Southeast Asian defense chiefs and their major Asian and Western counterparts.

Koizumi raised concern Thursday about China's increasing naval activity in southwestern Japan. He said that three Chinese destroyers and a coast guard ship together crossed the Osumi Strait south of the southern main island of Kyushu and headed east to the Pacific Ocean this week. It was the first such fleet configuration his ministry has spotted, he said.

Koizumi said he cited the incident as "an example illustrating the challenging security environment in the region" in his remark at a session Thursday. His ministry will keep watching the Chinese activity with high level of interest, he said. Japan has been accelerating military buildup in southwestern Japan to deter China.

The annual meetings aim to avert a major regional conflict and strengthen defense cooperation. On the sidelines of the meetings, however, the U.S. and China and their allies have often traded criticisms.

The missiles that will be produced as part of a joint effort called "Operation Supercharge" are essential for the F-35 fighter jets, Koizumi said. The two sides agreed to build a final assembly and inspection plant for the Advanced Medium-Range Air-to-Air Missile, or AMRAAM, in Japan, he said. The U.S.-made F-35s are one of the world's most advanced aircraft and are also used by the Japanese military.

During his brief meeting with Hegseth in Cebu, Koizumi said he "shared a sense of crisis … regarding the increasingly severe regional situation and we agreed to strengthen the deterrence and response capabilities of the Japan-U.S. alliance with greater speed and intensity than ever before."

"We reaffirmed the importance of a free and open international order based on the rule of law, which rejects any attempts to unilaterally alter the status quo through the use of force, coercion or other means," Koizumi said.

Hegseth renewed the Trump administration's repeated assurances of commitment to help deter aggression in the region in a separate meeting on Wednesday with ASEAN defense ministers, who were led by the host, Philippine Defense Secretary Gilberto Teodoro Jr. Like Koizumi, Hegseth did not mention China by name in expressing his security concerns in the region.

"The approach we're taking, we describe as strong, quiet and clear," Hegseth told the ASEAN defense chiefs. "Strong enough to deter coercion and aggression, quiet enough to avoid needless escalation or public theatrics to let our actions speak for themselves."

His message is "clear enough that our friends, our partners and potential adversaries understand precisely where the United States stands and that we will defend our interests and the interests of our allies and partners," Hegseth said.

Hegseth has assured the Philippines, the oldest treaty ally of Washington in Asia, that the Trump administration will build a robust deterrence against China's increasingly aggressive actions in the disputed South China Sea. Beijing claims the busy waterway, a key global trade route, virtually in its entirety.

Australian Defense Minister Richard Marles said his country's forces, along with allies like the U.S., will press on with efforts to defend the rule of law despite China's increasing assertiveness, including in the South China Sea. Australia protested last year after a Chinese J-16 fighter aircraft released flares and passed within 30 meters (100 feet) of an Australian P-8 Poseidon surveillance jet over the disputed waters.

"That doesn't deter us from doing what we need to do and that's essentially asserting the rules-based order here in the end of the Pacific," Marles told The Associated Press in Cebu on Wednesday. "Freedom of navigation on the high seas is profoundly important for Australia."

The Philippines, Vietnam, Malaysia and Brunei, all ASEAN member states, along with Taiwan, also have overlapping claims in the South China Sea. Confrontations have particularly escalated between Chinese and Philippine coast guard and navy vessels in disputed shoals and outlying waters.

Washington has warned that it's obligated to defend the Philippines if Filipino forces, ships and aircraft come under an armed attack, including in the South China Sea.

Beijing and Tokyo have a separate dispute over tiny Japanese-controlled islands in the East China Sea, with both sides often accusing the other of infringing on their maritime territory.
