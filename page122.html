#1335. These 9 Dress Trends Are About to Be Everywhere This Fall

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

The season’s best styles embrace rich textures and moody blooms

The biggest fall 2026 trends for dresses are breathing new life into the wardrobe staple. The silhouettes that carried shoppers through warm summer days are getting cool-weather updates in richer fabrics, moodier colors, and more substantial proportions that feel just as right with boots, tights, and a jacket as they once did with sandals.

Texture helps make the transition. Leather and velvet bring weight and dimension to dresses this season, while autumnal shades like royal and eggplant purple offer an alternative to summer’s brighter palette. Prints are getting appropriately moody too, appearing in subdued color combinations that feel more suited for a crisp fall afternoon than a springtime garden party. Meanwhile, longer hemlines and sleeves provide some much-needed coverage as temperatures drop without sacrificing the ease of a one-and-done outfit.

That doesn’t mean fall’s dress trends are all serious business, no play. With holiday parties and winter wedding season on the horizon, designers are embracing plenty of drama. Metallic finishes will convince you to get a head start on festive dressing, while gothic lace brings a darker romance to the season. Ruffles, too, are showing up in designs that are more sophisticated than sweet, adding movement and interest to otherwise straightforward silhouettes.

Minimalists have plenty to choose from as well. The ’90s supermodel slipdress continues its comeback, offering a sleek option that works just as well layered under a sweater or blazer for the office as it does on its own after hours. One-shoulder dresses take an equally pared-back approach, using an asymmetric neckline rather than embellishment to make an impact.

Ahead, nine of the best 2026 dress trends to wear throughout fall and winter.

Few fabrics can make a dress feel as luxurious as velvet. This season, designers are using it on every style, from minimalist column dresses for every day to fluidly draped silhouettes made for a night out. The plush fabric does most of the work, meaning you can keep accessories relatively simple.

Royal purple is reclaiming its color-wheel throne. The saturated shade has an inherently regal quality, but this season’s dresses prove it doesn’t have to feel overly precious or formal. Look for streamlined silhouettes that give the historically opulent color a more modern attitude and pair with fall neutrals like black, brown, gray, and camel.

The leather dress has come a long way from its tough-girl reputation. Designers are treating the material with a softer hand this season, resulting in styles ranging from polished midi dresses to feminine fit-and-flare shapes. The contrast between a traditionally edgy material and a ladylike silhouette makes the trend feel fresh. Pair yours with knee-high boots for daytime, then swap in a sharp heel when the sun goes down.

Metallic dresses are getting an early start on party season, with silver, gold, bronze, and high-shine finishes turning the simplest of silhouettes into a holiday event. The key is letting the fabric be the focal point rather than piling on equally flashy extras. Finish with minimalist heels and understated jewelry. Or go embellishment-free, treating the dress as if it were jewelry.

Ruffles are turning up the volume this season, adding movement and drama to fall dresses. Rather than reading overly sweet, the best versions feel sculptural, cascading down skirts, adorning necklines, or appearing in unexpected asymmetrical placements. They’re particularly good for wedding-guest dressing when you want something special without resorting to sequins or sparkle.

Lace is heading to the dark side. Black lace channels a romantic, slightly Gothic mood for fall, whether as a sheer overlay or strategically placed panels. Depending on a dress’s silhouette, the effect can be Victorian, witchy, or sexy. Finish with a pointed shoe and minimal jewelry to keep the look more modern, less period dressing.

Florals aren’t disappearing along with summer—they’re getting moodier. Instead of cheerful ditsy prints, fall’s blooms arrive against black and other muted backgrounds. The result has all the femininity of a floral dress without feeling unseasonably spring-like. Ground one with boots and a coat to push the print even further into autumn territory.

The one-shoulder dress is proof that you don’t need elaborate embellishment to make an entrance. An asymmetric neckline creates visual interest while maintaining the clean lines minimalists gravitate toward. The silhouette works for cocktail parties and weddings, where it lands somewhere between understated and statement-making. Keep the neckline bare and opt for earrings or a cuff instead.

The ’90s slipdress is once again ready for its close-up. Think less lingerie dressing, more Carolyn Bessette Kennedy and Kate Moss off-duty: clean lines, fluid fabrics, and an unfussy attitude. Its simplicity makes it a styling chameleon; wear it alone with barely there heels at night or add a sweater and boots for a more relaxed take on the timeless trend.

#1344. Can We Really Know Anyone—or Anything? That’s the Question at the Heart of <i><em>You Can See Everything</em></i>

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The three-hour documentary about Elizabeth Holmes is a meditation on the nature of truth

We all wear tinted glasses. We cannot separate what we see from what we already know. Documentarians, journalists, and photographers strive to capture an objective portrait of their subject, but, for better or worse, they can’t entirely shed their personal perspective. It’s part of being human.

This curiosity is what drives writer and filmmaker Nathan Fielder, whose work orbits the how and why of human behavior and often involves pushing people to their limits. He sometimes toes the line between cringe and shock, but it’s all in service of understanding them better. On Nathan for You, a comedy series that originally ran on Comedy Central, he feigned business expertise to convince small businesses to adopt outlandish marketing strategies to increase their sales. On HBO’s The Rehearsal, Fielder staged elaborate, theatrical rehearsals to help people overcome personal challenges; later, he turned those same unconventional resources to help improve commercial pilot communication in an effort to avoid plane crashes (no, really). His docu-reality series stage intricate ruses that allow him—and us—to observe the uncanny behavior of regular people. Fielder brings a puckish quality to his projects, where he stages human experiments to observe people, often eliciting humor while also revealing new possibilities. These projects made him the perfect person to take on Elizabeth Holmes, the founder of Theranos, who lied to investors—and the world—about a revolutionary blood-testing device that turned out to be nothing more than smoke and mirrors. She is the protagonist of his latest project, You Can See Everything.

In the documentary, codirected by Lance Oppenheim (of Primetime), Fielder narrows in on Holmes and her husband, San Diego hotel heir Billy Evans, a little over a month before she surrenders for her 11-year prison sentence. The same unblinking, crystal-blue eyes that made Holmes the face of Silicon Valley feature heavily in You Can See Everything. This time, instead of appearing in cover shoots in her signature sleek black turtleneck and holding a small blood capsule, she sits opposite Fielder in a seaside rental in Del Mar, California. The couple has invited the filmmaker into their home 24/7 to show the world the real woman Billy contends has been smeared by the media. Given the chance to observe her up close, Fielder strives to portray this uncanny person objectively, only to find a story full of red herrings. This riveting documentary’s most surprising discovery is the sympathy Fielder manages to conjure for someone the world previously believed was empirically guilty. It leaves you asking, can we really know anyone?

Existing in a reality where Nathan Fielder—the character and the person—studies Elizabeth Holmes feels like an impossible stroke of fate. His trickster interrogation tactics function as a foil to Holmes’s house of mirrors. In January 2022, a federal jury convicted Holmes on four counts of corporate fraud. That’s fact. But Fielder finds that Holmes and her husband live in a reality they designed to fit their narrative better. Holmes not only believes she never defrauded anyone, she also asserts she’s incapable of lying. She backs this puzzling assertion with semantic games, telling Fielder that the investor misunderstood her when she said the machine “can” function in certain ways. For some reason, you want to believe her, especially after you realize she seemingly can’t understand the Abbott and Costello joke “Who’s on first?”

Fielder endeavors to fully understand Holmes. Ironically, that’s why Evans and Holmes invited him and Oppenheim into their home: to better portray Holmes’s public image and, they believe, give a more objective look into their lives. Fielder doesn’t spare Holmes from his skepticism, asking incisive questions about her complicity in the fraud and conspiracy, but he also gives her room to be seen beyond it. Some evidence suggests she was the victim of misunderstandings (albeit unlikely), as well as the scapegoat for a host of evil men, including her ex-boyfriend, Theranos cofounder Sunny Balwani, and her husband.

Billy, though initially the arbiter of transparency, actively obscures what we can see and then, later, when Holmes goes to jail, what we know about her. He repeatedly steamrolls Holmes throughout the movie, from snatching a bowl to whisk eggs for her to stopping interviews in their tracks when he feels Fielder is becoming overtly challenging or asking too many questions. His motivations soon begin to feel dubious, and a clear parallel exists between his allegation of media manipulation regarding Holmes’s sentencing and his own revisionist manipulation of her story. But every detail in You Can See Everything creates a maze of blind alleys, further obscuring what we understand about Holmes and her situation. Lacking conclusions, we remain privy to our own subjectivities—grasping at the conclusions we reach ourselves.

After Holmes reports to prison, her absence leaves space for meditation. Fielder then introduces an unexpected fourth character, Amanda Seyfried.

Her presence adds a dimensionality rarely seen in documentaries. Seyfried approaches Holmes’s personality from a new perspective, letting her subjectivity guide her as she weighs empathy and wariness. Reprising her role from The Dropout in Rehearsal-esque reenactments, she tries to parse whether Holmes’s conversations with Fielder are genuine or an elaborate ruse. The smallest details frequently lead Seyfried and Fielder into long discussions about intent, but the strangeness emerges in trying to pin down her knotty personality. She even challenges Fielder on whether he holds bias, acting as the documentary’s barometer of truth. But as they examine these actions, all these contradictions feel increasingly human. Are we not all at the mercy of our ego? Do we not all experience the world so radically singularly, subject to our own penchants? Can we ever be fully known by others, whether it’s loved ones or people who have spent time with us at our most vulnerable? Is there such a thing as an unbiased truth? In light of this, Holmes warrants our compassion; despite her guilt, she remains a captive of her own subjective reality, just like Fielder and all of us watching.

#2748. What Does the PCT Sound Like? Meet the Thru-Hiker Who Recorded His Journey.

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

On any hike, it’s natural to pause, close your eyes, and listen to the breeze through the trees, the lap of water against a lakeshore, the call of a bird. During his 2023 thru-hike of the Pacific Crest Trail, Tom Kelly stopped to listen, too—perhaps more often than most.

While some hikers document their journeys in journals, blogs, or photographs, Kelly, a 41-year-old Angeleno, captured the sounds around him using a couple of microphones and handheld recorders. Two years of editing later, those recordings now comprise a free, publicly accessible sound map representing 200 points along the PCT, offering listeners a chance to experience the trail through its soundscapes.

In the very first audio file titled “la posta creek cliff swallows,” you can hear the birds chitter as cars drive by. In another titled “insects at dusk,” chirping bugs pierce the night. Frogs, crickets, bees, quails, rushing water, and rustling grass make up some other sound moments in the files.

Each entry comes with Kelly’s field notes, including the recording gear he used, his observations, and even what he experienced in that place. For a nearly six-minute clip of intense wind, blowing sand, and fellow hikers walking by on the edge of the San Gorgonio Pass Wind Farm in California’s Coachella Valley, which he reached in May 2023, he wrote:

“finally got to the windfarm and recording a pct landmark. sitting in the colorado desert having just descended from the snowy san jacintos. it is hot, windy, exposed, and i’m getting absolutely sandblasted. hikers passing by as i record the ominous drones of the palm springs windfarm.”

And another titled “great-horned owl” captures the call of the elusive bird. Recorded in October 2023 in Northern California’s Plumas National Forest, Kelly wrote:

“maybe my favorite recording of the trek. i ended the recording just before the male showed up due to core temp concerns. an incredibly tender dialog ensued between the two of them. i’ll never forget just how excited the female was when the male showed up. heartbreakingly beautiful. a nat geo moment.”

Kelly’s attentiveness to sound began in childhood in Wisconsin; he recalls sitting in a sandbox in his parents’ backyard, listening to mourning doves one late afternoon. Music was an early fascination, too. Around the same age, he experimented with the weird sounds of a toy keytar. He went on to study music composition at Columbia College Chicago and continued making music. On a trip to Istanbul, he brought an audio recorder along for the first time, using it to document his travels.

“I was like, this could be cool because I can immerse myself in these spaces in a way that you can’t with photography or taking videos on your phone,” he says. “You can close your eyes, put on a soundscape, and kind of be transported back there.”

After graduating the music composition program in 2018, Kelly was ready for some extended time outside, away from a studio and desk. He completed his first three- to four-day backpacking trip on Minnesota’s Superior Hiking Trail. Then a 2019 visit to Yosemite unlocked a dream to hike the PCT, and he obtained a permit for 2020. The pandemic derailed his plans.

Instead, he explored Idaho’s Sawtooth Mountains and thru-hiked the Superior Hiking Trail over three weeks, carrying an audio recorder on both trips. After a year spent working mostly indoors in 2022, he reached a breaking point, quit his job, and set out to hike the PCT in 2023.

Kelly didn’t know what shape his recordings would take in the end. His only intentions were to document the PCT’s most natural soundscapes, free of human noise—a priority for soundscape conservationists who see natural silence as an increasingly scarce resource. But even in remote areas, planes, cars, and other people were often audible, making pristine recordings difficult to capture.

“I quickly realized that even though you’re on the Pacific Crest Trail in these very remote places, there’s a lot of human presence within earshot everywhere,” Kelly says. “There are so many airports along the West Coast that it becomes a logistical nightmare to try and really significantly alter how you’re traveling in order to record natural soundscapes.”

Finding quiet was only part of the challenge. Stopping to record and then getting started again would disrupt the rhythm of the long-distance hike. Any longer than 30 minutes and Kelly’s body would go into recovery mode. Some days, he’d hike through interesting sound moments if he wanted to make up miles. Other times he’d stop to record only to come away with nothing.

The equipment he carried also evolved along the way. Two breaks from the trail allowed him to reevaluate his setup. He started out with a Sony a10 handheld recorder and small LOM mikroUsi microphones. His final load out was a high-quality microphone with a makeshift windshield and Zoom F3 field recorder, which he carried in a Cotopaxi 1.5L hip pack.

In the end, the 200 recordings included in Kelly’s map—pared down from 350 recordings, totaling about 50 hours—is a combination of pristine nature soundscapes and clips with obvious human presence, such as the wind farm outside Tehachapi, California.

The resulting body of work balances soundscape conservation with a broader portrait of the trail, offering every kind of listener something beyond an idealized version of wilderness.

“I just want to create a nice experience, and there’s something for everybody in there,” he says.

For Kelly, the project was a way to take himself out of the picture—literally, but also as a break from a culture that often makes experiences about documenting the self. The months spent recording nature became both “political resistance” and “personal therapy.”

The deeper he got into the hike, he says, the more he synced up with nature’s glacial pace rather than the pace of society. Things got quieter, more spacious, and a bit more minimal as he progressed. Without the usual demands on his attention, he began to notice subtle sounds that might otherwise have faded into the background, like the staccato of a creek or the flutter of leaves.

Through this curated sound map, he hopes to share the feeling of being on the trail with people who want to experience it, are living it now, or long to connect back to it.

#1367. <b><strong>Have a Tough Time with Rejection? It Could Be Because of This Common Condition</strong></b>

📅 Oct 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Whether it’s getting ghosted after a date or realizing you weren’t invited to a friend’s party, rejection stings. But for some people, it hits way harder: sudden anger, tears, shame, or all-consuming emotional pain that feels impossible to get over.

Those people aren’t just “too sensitive” or “overly dramatic.” There’s a real term for what they’re experiencing: Rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD). While RSD isn’t an official clinical diagnosis, that doesn’t make it any less real for people who live it, according to research in PLOS One.

RSD is often linked to attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), but just about anyone can be affected by it. Ahead, psychology experts explain the science behind RSD, how to spot it, and how to cope with rejection.

Meet the experts: Sasha Hamdani, MD, is a psychiatrist, ADHD clinical specialist, and author of Too Sensitive: Rejection, Resilience, and the Science of Feeling Deeply. Janice Castro, PhD, is a California-based psychologist and founder of J.C. Psychological Services.

“RSD describes an intense emotional response to real or perceived rejection, criticism, or disappointment,” says Sasha Hamdani, MD, a psychiatrist, ADHD clinical specialist, and author of Too Sensitive: Rejection, Resilience, and the Science of Feeling Deeply. The reaction is often much bigger than the event that caused it. “A lot of people can recognize that their reaction is ‘disproportionate’ but still struggle to control its intensity,” Dr. Hamdani says.

When you have RSD, even a mild interaction like a friend not responding to a text can trigger overwhelming feelings of shame, sadness, anger, or inadequacy, Dr. Hamdani says. Some people may feel hurt if their friend didn’t respond to a text, while others may not even think about it. A person with RSD might feel all-consuming emotional pain or distress that seems unbearable as they wait for a reply.

RSD isn’t recognized in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), the official manual psychologists use to diagnose people with mental health conditions. That just means that it’s not an official diagnosis—but experts still find it important to recognize and understand. Janice Castro, PhD, a California-based psychologist and founder of J.C. Psychological Services, has seen RSD in her own work with clients—especially those with ADHD—and says that more therapists are starting to use the term. “Just because science hasn’t caught up yet doesn’t mean the experience isn’t real,” she says.

The RSD conversation is important because it gives many people a name for an experience they previously didn’t have words for, Castro says. The more therapists and the general public talk about RSD, the closer we get to having more tools and strategies people can use to navigate their experience. “For those of us who have loved ones that experience RSD, we can also start having open conversations about how to support them—and maybe have a little more empathy rather than judge or criticize.”

You can thank evolution. “If you think back to the Stone Age, for example, people were just trying to survive in community, and the idea of not having social connection or social bonds was a threat to survival,” says Castro. Nowadays, your body can react to rejection on a visceral level, as if you were in danger, and the surge of stress hormones can send you into fight-or-flight mode.

From a neuroscience perspective, rejection can overwhelm the brain. The amygdala (which helps detect potential threats) activates a stress response, while the prefrontal cortex (responsible for emotional regulation) may have difficulty putting the situation into context, Dr. Hamdani explains. “Essentially, the emotional response can happen faster than the brain can logically navigate,” she says.

Still, it’s unclear what exactly causes RSD. But if you deal with anxiety, depression, or naturally engage in a lot of self-criticism, you may be more likely to experience it, says Castro. Genetics, environmental context, relationships, and family history are important considerations, too, she says.

Anecdotally, RSD is often associated with neurodivergence—which can include ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, dyslexia, and dyspraxia, to name a few—but more research is needed to understand how common it is.

RSD is most often talked about in relation to ADHD. People with ADHD can feel things intensely and quickly, and react impulsively, says Castro. If they feel rejected or excluded while already struggling to regulate their emotions, they may react in a way that’s disproportionate to what happened, she explains. This is one reason RSD is so often linked to ADHD. However, RSD isn’t exclusive to ADHD, Dr. Hamdani says. “People with anxiety, trauma histories, or other mental health conditions can also experience similar patterns.”

One example is social anxiety disorder. “There’s often an intense fear that you’re going to be rejected—or that something you say or do is going to be extremely embarrassing,” Castro says. You might avoid certain people or settings to evade potential discomfort. RSD is also common for people with borderline personality disorder (BPD) since it can involve deep fear of abandonment and criticism, Castro says.

You also might be more sensitive to rejection if you have obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), especially if you tend to ruminate on social situations. It can be an exhausting cycle: “You’re constantly thinking about a conversation, what you said, how it landed, then criticizing yourself and seeking reassurance,” Castro says.

Plus, people with a history of trauma can be extremely aware of social cues and, as a result, rejection. They might constantly wonder if others approve of them or fear upsetting others, Castro says. If they’ve had a traumatic experience, their nervous system is likely heightened, making them more sensitive to negative things happening again, she explains.

Read on for symptoms of RSD, per Dr. Hamdani. Remember: Anyone can experience a combination of any of the below emotions after being rejected (it stings, no matter what!). But, if you’re feeling these consistently and intensely, that could be RSD.

RSD looks different for everyone, and coping skills will depend on the individual. That said, here are a few recommendations for navigating RSD, whether you have ADHD or not, per experts:

Dr. Hamdani recommends creating space between the rejection (or feeling of rejection) and your response. “This might mean taking a few slow breaths, physically stepping away from the situation, or asking yourself, ‘What do I actually know happened, and what am I assuming happened?’” she says.

To help with this, Castro recommends 4-7-8 breathing. “Breathe in through your nose for four seconds, hold your breath for seven seconds, then release through your mouth for eight seconds,” she says. Pretend you’re exhaling through a straw so the air comes out slowly. “It's not going to reduce the pain, and it's not going to make the rejection go away—but it will reduce the intensity of your emotion so that you can pause before reacting,” Castro says.

Self-compassion, the practice of being supportive toward yourself when you’re experiencing challenges, is an important part of coping with RSD. This is especially true if you have ADHD and were diagnosed in adulthood, since you’ve likely “faced ongoing judgment, criticism, and reprimanding for making mistakes [due to ADHD]” over the years, Castro says. “That’s decades of hearing messages that you aren’t good enough, which becomes internalized negative self-talk and self-imposed pressure,” she explains.

If you’re going through RSD, Castro recommends trying a self-compassion exercise from Kristin Neff, PhD: Ask yourself, How would you respond to a friend in this situation? “As humans, we are our own worst critics, yet the way we talk to our friends is often with kindness,” Castro says. “Why not try treating yourself like a good friend and see what happens?”

Whether you have ADHD or not, taking care of yourself is crucial—that way if and when RSD hits, you at least have a good foundation for well-being, which will help you cope more effectively. Dr. Castro recommends prioritizing sleep (around seven to nine hours, ideally), balanced nutrition, and hydration to help regulate your mood.

Stress and anxiety can make RSD even worse, per Cleveland Clinic, so it’s important to manage them effectively. If you have ADHD, setting reminders for yourself can be an important part of stress management. “People with ADHD put in a lot of work every day to keep up with structure and routines that most neurotypical-functioning adults perceive as ‘easy,’ or that neurotypical folks ‘just do,’” Castro says. Little reminders can help you feel less overwhelmed even if you don’t have ADHD, so don’t be afraid to put those alarm settings to good use.

Both cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) can be helpful for RSD, Dr. Hamdani says. Working with a licensed therapist can help you “recognize rejection-related thought patterns, challenge your assumptions, and develop healthier emotional responses,” she says.

If RSD is causing you to avoid social outings or work meetings, or is otherwise affecting daily life, it’s time to talk with a professional, Castro says. Role-playing is one of her favorite tools since it lets clients practice how they want to respond in future scenarios.

If you struggle with ADHD or any of the mental health conditions mentioned earlier—or if it feels like RSD is taking over your life—consider asking your doctor or psychiatrist if medication would be a good option. For people with ADHD, various ADHD medications can help with broader emotional regulation, but no medication is specifically FDA-approved for RSD and the research is limited, Dr. Hamdani says.

Rejection is never fun, and it’s normal to feel hurt. But if you’ve been told you’re “too sensitive,” know that your emotions are valid and there’s support out there. And to anyone who says you need to tone down your feelings, Dr. Hamdani would like a word: “The goal isn’t to become less sensitive, but to feel more in control.”

If you’re relatively new to the world of woodworking and DIY projects, it’s a good rule of thumb to opt for simplicity when you’re outfitting your shop with power tools. Too many features can overwhelm new users, and you risk losing sight of the basics, which are important to master.

One area, however, where something is actually too basic—and risks complicating work for newer users—is the single-bevel miter saw. Single-bevel models may sound like less complicated pieces of machinery, but in operation, they ask users to actually do more. That’s why we recommend the dual-bevel Bosch GCM12SD as the best miter saw for most people.

When you use a miter saw, you’re busy concentrating on measurements, blade placement, support of your workpiece, and keeping your hands safely clear of that descending, spinning blade. Add an angled joint, and now you’re trying to visualize how the two pieces you’re cutting will fit together. That’s a lot before you make a cut that you may already be second-guessing.

A miter saw makes different kinds of angled cuts. Swiveling the table left or right changes the miter angle. Tilting the blade sideways changes the bevel angle. Perform the two together and you’re making what’s known as a “compound cut.”

A single-bevel saw generally tilts in one direction only, usually left. Furthermore, the motor sits to the right of the blade, which leaves a clearer view of the cut line for the typical right-handed user.

A dual-bevel saw, on the other hand, tilts left and right, giving you more flexibility to position the workpiece for a comfortable stance and a visible cut line from both sides—another reason I prefer the option to tilt both ways. That overall versatility allows you to make opposing bevel cuts without having to flip or otherwise reorient the board to compensate for the saw’s limitation. That might sound like a minor convenience—until you’re the one flipping the board around.

To put things in terms of spatial awareness, imagine working with a long piece of trim in a garage that also contains a workbench, a leaf blower, and a set of storage shelves. It’s great to have that opposite bevel, if only to avoid maneuvering around all of that typical workspace chaos.

With a single-bevel saw, you reposition the material, get it all supported again, and check that everything is facing the right way before you cut it. All of that movement and revisualizing the opposite cut can be both challenging and frustrating—a perfect environment for simple yet costly mistakes.

And during actual renovation work, the saw isn’t always conveniently set up beside whatever you’re building. Sometimes you’re measuring trim in one room and walking down flights and outside to make your cuts, all the while keeping track in your mind of how it fits, which way the angle should run, and which side of your mark to cut. Now imagine having to flip that board around because the blade won’t tilt in the direction you need.

Even with shorter pieces, changing orientation gives you another opportunity to lose track of what you’re cutting. A dual-bevel saw can save you from having to rethink everything you’re doing just because you need the blade to lean the other way.

Furthermore, dual-bevel alone doesn’t make a saw any more accurate, give it a better blade, or increase its cutting capacity. Those features still deserve consideration when buying any miter saw.
