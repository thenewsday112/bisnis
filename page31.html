The U.S. is facing a housing crisis — as in, there isn’t enough affordable housing available for the number of people who need it.

The housing shortage is driving up costs of buying. Low supply, teamed with ongoing inflation eating into people’s budgets and mortgage rates over 7%, means there doesn’t seem to be much relief in sight.

When would-be home buyers can't afford to purchase a home, they may remain in the rental market longer, adding to demand for rental housing. In markets where rental supply can't keep pace, that can put further upward pressure on rents.

The problem isn't simply that the U.S. needs more housing. More to the point, it needs more housing that people operating at different income levels can actually afford. In other words, we need more “affordable housing.”

In the dictionary sense of the phrase, “Affordable housing” means that occupants pay no more than 30% of their gross income for housing and utilities costs. It means the other 70% of people’s income can go toward other costs of living. Those who spend over 30% of their income on housing are considered to be housing cost burdened.

Roughly 70% of families with extremely low income use more than half of their income on rent, according to the National Low Income Housing Coalition, which also says there is currently a deficit of 6.8 million affordable housing units available.

So how can policy — and politicians — solve the challenges of affordable housing?

Candidates in the 2026 election (mostly Democrats) frequently make “affordable housing” a refrain in their housing proposals, but what they mean by the term varies widely from race to race.

Plans to make housing more affordable typically address different parts of the housing crisis: Some focus on expanding homebuilding, cutting permitting red tape, subsidizing rents or helping first-time home buyers afford their purchase. The range of candidates’ approaches demonstrates how differently they’re defining the problem. Here are some examples.

Who says it: Amy Klobuchar, Democratic candidate for governor of Minnesota.

What she’s promising to do: Put Minnesota on a path to create 100,000 new homes. She says she would support factory-built housing and make it easier for construction projects to move forward by standardizing the permitting process. She would also create a low-interest revolving loan fund for developers in rural areas, ban price-fixing for rents and make the state housing tax credit permanent.

Who says it: Claire Valdez, Democratic candidate for U.S. House in New York’s 7th Congressional District.

What she’s promising to do: Create a federal housing developer in order to finance and build millions of permanently affordable homes. She says she would also repeal restrictions on new public housing construction and tie federal housing funds to local zoning changes that would allow more development of multifamily homes. She also proposes national rent control, expanded housing vouchers, a federal good-cause eviction law and stronger enforcement against landlords who neglect their properties.

Who says it: Mike Rogers, Republican candidate for U.S. Senate in Michigan.

What he’s promising to do: “Builders” is the name of Rogers’ housing plan. He says he would expand tax-advantaged savings for first-time homebuyers, allow renters to build credit through on-time rent payments, and let borrowers defer student loan payments while saving for a down payment. He also pledges to cut regulations and permitting barriers, create tax-free residential housing zones to incentivize new construction and expand skilled-trades training to increase the construction workforce.

Who says it: Abdul El-Sayed, Democratic candidate for U.S. Senate in Michigan.

What he’s promising to do: Make major federal investment in new housing, streamlinee building regulations and expand Low-Income Housing Tax Credit allocations to boost the affordable housing supply in Michigan. He says he would also ban large corporations from buying homes for speculation, ban price-fixing for rents, charge short-term rental platforms like Airbnb dislocation fees, and establish a federal renters’ “bill of rights” to protect tenants.

Who says it: Chris Pappas, Democratic candidate for U.S. Senate in New Hampshire.

What he’s promising to do: Cut red tape and speed up permitting to boost homebuilding growth, expand the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit to expand affordable housing and provide tax credits to help first-time home buyers with down payments. Pappas also says he supports increasing loan limits for manufactured housing and property improvements. He has co-led legislation to create a federal grant program that offsets state and local impact fees on new housing developments.

Who says it: Ken Paxton, Republican candidate for U.S. Senate in Texas.

What he’s promising to do: Provide a $50,000 tax deduction for first-time homebuyers and a separate $50,000 tax deduction for homeowners moving to a new primary residence. He has also proposed restricting corporations from buying single-family homes.

The AI boom has led to a gargantuan surge in energy demand across the country. Critics say this upswell in connections is enacting a stress test on America’s aging grid infrastructure. Worries abound that, in the short term, the energy crunch could lead to surging electrical bills and, in the long term, may eventually lead to outages or other technical problems.

At TechCrunch Disrupt earlier this month, industry insiders tempered much of this criticism with optimism about how the flood of new energy connections could actually be opening the door for grid modernization. Apoorv Bhargava, founder and CEO of WeaveGrid, Tim Heidel, CEO of Veir, and Kristin Eberhard, VP of policy at Rewiring America, said that though the grid faces new challenges, there are also plenty of new opportunities as well.

“It’s a very different electric grid, right?” said Bhargava. “We’re dealing with a ton of new variables, including renewable energy. We’re dealing with a ton of new load in the form of electric vehicles and data centers and heat pumps, and the ability to manage and orchestrate is fundamentally what utilities are now grappling with.”

While all of those new connections are driving an ever larger amount of stress to the grid, Bhargava’s company hopes to provide the kind of load orchestration and management that helps utilities maximize the potential of the current system. WeaveGrid’s software can help organizations efficiently deploy energy while also optimizing connections to reduce utility payers’ bills, the founder said.

Heidel, on the other hand, runs a company that is working to build out and expand the physical infrastructure of the grid, while also making it run much more efficiently. Veir sells superconductive power lines that promise to transfer energy at a much more efficient rate than traditional ones. Veir’s cables can carry power five times farther than traditional cables while also requiring 20 times less space, the company has said. Earlier this month the startup raised $110 million in a Series C funding round.

“The scale of the investment in AI infrastructure is driving a tremendous amount of innovation,” said Heidel. “There’s a ton of new technologies that are coming off the shelves and starting to get to scale that, pulled together, can actually really make sure that data centers are the best community neighbors and citizens they can possibly be.”

He added: “I think that we really need to look at ways that we can really adopt all of that as fast as possible to ease this next stage of growth.”

Eberhard, whose organization advocates for household electrification, also said that while the surge in data center energy demand has spurred a new urgency to the modernization cause, it has also created an environment where industries are ready to try new things.

“I feel like this era of AI and load growth is a very improv era,” Eberhard said. “There’s a lot of yes-and-ing going on. So yes, we need more transmission lines. Yes, we need more centralized power plants, and we also need to make sure that we’re taking advantage of all this other stuff that we had previously been leaving on the table when we didn’t have this urgency of the AI demand.”

Within hours of the iPhone Duo launch last Friday, people reported that the inner screen on some brand new foldable phones was already broken. The issue was similar across many photos shared on social media: some kind of issue or crack would appear in the middle of the screen above the hinge, with lines appearing on the right side of the display. On Monday, Apple acknowledged an “issue” and said it’s replacing damaged units, without going into much detail about how many broken phones have been reported so far.

Shahram Mokhtari, the lead teardown technician at iFixit, has actually taken apart the iPhone Duo and has a pretty good idea about the potential cause, telling The Verge that it’s due to an issue along the hinge, where a series of gears, springs, and joints work together to let the device close flat, while also trying to avoid creating a deeper crease by giving that screen space to bend more gently inside while it’s closed.

At the troublesome spot, it appears that the flexible inner screen is pushing up against a part of the hinge. That inner screen is already “extremely sensitive,” he says — if you took a pick or a sharp tool and poked at the edges of the screen, “you’re going to break that screen.” If you look at the pictures of damaged Duos on social media, there’s often a black spot where the damage is focused on the display, and that’s where the metal is hitting the screen, Mokhtari says.

You can see a visual representation of what Mokhtari is talking about in iFixit’s written and video Duo teardowns, which reveal where the hinge transitions, as the center hinge support makes space for a crossing flex cable, creating several edges that could ultimately be the culprit. The damage iFixit has been seeing is at a point where the center support bar might not always drop down far enough while the screen folds, possibly creating a stress “hot spot” between the flat bar and the bent screen inside a closed iPhone Duo.

At least one user with a broken Duo screen said they “felt something around the internal hinge” while showing it to a friend, kept opening it, and heard an “audible crack,” with the screen breaking. YouTuber CuriousHagi, who covered a Duo with dirt as part of a strenuous durability test, captured something similar on video, and the screen break appears in a similar spot. In a teardown video, CuriousHagi found that a part of the hinge that connects to the central bar had snapped.

Apple says the issue affects a “small number” of Duo units, and Mokhtari says that “In terms of numbers, we don’t know. Apple definitely has all the data here. And I suspect they know more than they’re telling us as well.” But he notes that because foldable screens are so delicate, a “certain number” of them will fail, as will a number of the hinges because they’re also “extremely complicated and delicate.” And while Apple isn’t recalling the Duos, “If three months from now, we’re still seeing the same kind of failure, then it’s a more widespread problem, and that might lead to a recall,” Mokhtari says.

Mokhtari points out that since this is the first generation of the iPhone Duo, it’s the first time Apple’s engineers are getting data on the hinge, and he says that “I guarantee you” Apple will redesign what’s at fault here for its next-generation foldable. He also notes that, generally speaking, “the phones do seem to be in people’s hands and people are using them, and they’re not running into any issues whatsoever.”
