#963. Nobelitis — How winning a Nobel Prize can give you delusions of grandeur

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

After being chosen for one of the world's most prestigious awards, some Nobel laureates lose touch with reality and suddenly start spouting unscientific nonsense. They're afflicted by what some have called "Nobelitis."

Linus Pauling won not one, but two Nobel Prizes — the first, for chemistry, in 1954 for his research into the nature of the chemical bond, and the Peace Prize in 1962 for his work in the anti-nuclear weapons movement.

The American chemist not only won two Nobel Prizes, but won them on his own, a rare feat for any scientist. Such an honor often turns laureates into global superstars overnight, with millions hanging on their every word.

That instant fame can be dangerous, said Nils Hansson, a professor in the Department of History, Theory and Ethics of Medicine at Heinrich Heine University in Düsseldorf. Especially, he said, when these world-renowned experts "start spreading crazy, unproven scientific theories" — ideas that, when disseminated by Nobel laureates, risk being taken seriously.

That's what happened with Pauling. Beginning in the mid 1960s he began claiming that vitamin C was a universal remedy, a cure for everything from cancer to cardiovascular conditions and degenerative diseases like Alzheimer's disease, multiple sclerosis or osteoarthritis.

Pauling developed an alternative treatment he called orthomolecular medicine, based on specific doses of vitamins and minerals for each patient. To this day, some 60 years later, evidence for his methods remains inconclusive.

Pauling's case is a classic example of "Nobelitis," or "Nobel disease" — not an official diagnosis, but an informal term used to describe how some laureates begin to suffer delusions of grandeur later in life. It was first coined by Paul Nurse, president of The Royal Society, the UK's national academy of sciences, and himself a winner of the Nobel Prize.

"Be skeptical of everything, including yourself and your own ideas," Nurse said in a 2019 interview with KU Leuven University in Belgium, when asked what made someone a good scientist. In cases of Nobelitis, that self-skepticism is the first to go.

That was also the case with virologist Luc Montagnier, who was jointly honored in 2008 for his discovery of the human immunodeficiency virus, HIV, with colleague Francoise Barre-Sinoussi in the early 1980s.

Montagnier would later go on to support homeopathic medicine, another alternative treatment of disease and claim that HIV transmission in Africa could be diminished through better hygiene and nutrition.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Montagnier said the SARS-CoV-2 virus had been created in a lab with parts of the HIV genetic code. "It's difficult for a practicing virologist to say that a Nobel laureate in virology is spreading nonsense," said German virologist Christian Drosten on NDR's Coronavirus Update podcast in May 2020. "But that is utter nonsense."

James Watson, who was never known for his modesty, also let fame go to his head. In 1962, he was awarded the Nobel Prize along with Maurice Wilkins and Francis Crick for their discovery of the structure of the DNA molecule. But in the early 2000s, Watson repeatedly spread racist theories that Black people were less intelligent than white people.

For medical historian Hansson, Watson is a prime example that the Nobel Prize is not automatically awarded to a person who should be considered as a role model.

"You may have made a fantastic discovery, but that doesn't make you a universal genius," he said.

But the great expectations of an admiring public may also contribute to the development of Nobelitis. Paul Nurse, who won the Nobel Prize in physiology or medicine in 2001, soon found himself getting daily requests for interviews on all sorts of topics.

"As if suddenly I was acknowledgeable about all workings of the world and of human beings," he told DW.

Nurse had this advice for researchers: "Be careful quite what you say. Talk about things that you do know something about that would be fine. And if you don't know so much or you're not sure about something, be really clear that you're not sure."

Hansson has also noticed that the media eagerly report whatever Nobel laureates say — even when prize winners stray far from their area of expertise.

Nobelitis sufferers are leaders in their fields and rightly honored for their work with the Nobel. But they're also regular individuals, not immune to megalomania and other behavioral problems — meaning that homeopathy, orthomolecular medicine and other pseudosciences don't automatically become credible just because they're backed by a Nobel Prize winner.

#957. Suspected pneumonic plague in Siberia: What we know about the disease

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

After a worker at a Russian plague research institute in Siberia died October 2, 2026, nearly 200 of her contacts were under medical observation. But authorities there denied pneumonic plague had caused the death.

UPDATE: Following initial reports raising concerns that a lab worker had died accidentally of pneumonic plague, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) said it was now also "closely monitoring the situation."

However, in a statement published October 6, 2026, the ECDC said that "based on the limited information currently available, there are no reports of secondary cases and no evidence of sustained human-to-human transmission that would cause further spread of the disease."

Following the death of a Russian laboratory worker, there is speculation about an outbreak of pneumonic plague, a highly contagious bacterial infection of the lungs. Russia denies there is a broader risk to the community.

Russian officials have said the laboratory worker's death was not caused by a plague pathogen. The 28-year-old died of "pneumonia of unknown origin," according to Russia's Interfax news agency, citing Rospotrebnadzor, Russia's disease-control agency. According to that agency, samples taken from the woman showed no microorganisms "related to her professional activities." This contradicts earlier information.

Local media had previously reported that the woman, who worked at an institute dedicated to fighting plague, had dropped a test tube.

She reportedly died on Friday, October 2, 2026, at the district hospital in Shelekhov, a town near Irkutsk in Siberia.

According to official Russian figures, authorities placed two hospitals and a children's clinic under quarantine. Local reports said staff at the institute were being quarantined at their workplace. The US news site Axios has reported that around 200 people who had been in contact with the woman had been placed under observation. There were no further reported infections.

Even so, distrust of how Russia handles information persists. On one of DW's Russian-language social media channels, a user wrote: "It wouldn't surprise me if this turns into a disaster now, both in terms of the spread and the quarantine measures."

All that is known for certain, at the time of writing and first publication (October 5, 2026), is that pneumonic plague is highly contagious.

Pneumonic plague is a form of plague that infects the lungs. Like every form of the disease, it is caused by Yersinia pestis, a bacterium usually found in small mammals, such as rodents and in the fleas that feed on them.

The World Health Organization describes the pneumonic form as the most virulent type of plague.

Its incubation period can be as short as 24 hours, and it is the only form that passes easily from person to person through respiratory particles released when a sick patient coughs. This is why, according to the WHO, it can trigger severe epidemics.

Symptoms appear quickly: high fever, headache and weakness, followed by a rapidly worsening pneumonia with shortness of breath, chest pain and a cough that often brings up blood.

A person can catch pneumonic plague by breathing in the bacteria directly, or by developing it when bubonic and/or septicemic plague go untreated and spread to the lungs.

Pneumonic plague is not a separate disease. It is one of three forms of plague: bubonic, septicemic and pneumonic.

What sets them apart is where the infection settles in the body, and that shapes how the disease spreads and how deadly it is.

Bubonic plague is the most common form and the one most people think of when they hear the word "plague." It usually follows a flea bite. The bacteria travel to the nearest lymph node, which swells into a painful lump known as a bubo. According to the WHO, it rarely spreads between people.

If bubonic plague goes untreated, the bacteria can move further through the body. If they spread into the bloodstream, the disease can become septicemic plague, which causes bleeding under the skin, tissue death in the fingers and toes, and organ failure. If the bacteria reach the lungs, the illness becomes pneumonic plague, the form that can pass from person to person through the air. Both septicemic and pneumonic plague can also develop directly, without a bubo appearing first.

Left untreated, bubonic plague kills between 30-60% of patients. The septicemic and pneumonic forms are always fatal without treatment.

Pneumonic plague is curable, but only if treatment starts quickly. The WHO warns that without treatment it can kill within 18 to 24 hours of the first symptoms.

Common antibiotics can cure the infection, and recovery rates are high when patients are treated within about a day of falling ill.

Speed is what decides the outcome. Doctors are advised not to wait for laboratory confirmation and to start antibiotics as soon as plague is suspected, according to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

The drugs themselves are not rare or specialized. They are standard, widely used antibiotics, and patients usually need a course of up to two weeks. To stop the infection spreading, patients are kept in isolation, and wearing masks helps protect those around them, the WHO says.

People close to a patient are treated as well, even if they have no symptoms. Both the WHO and the CDC recommend a seven-day course of preventive antibiotics for close contacts of anyone with pneumonic plague.

The WHO does not recommend a plague vaccine for the public. It reserves vaccination for high-risk groups, such as laboratory staff who work with the bacterium and health workers.

#943. Early-onset cancer rising worldwide: Causes, risk, prevention

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Across Latin America, early-onset cancer rates have risen, especially of bowel, breast and cervical cancers. But it is a worldwide trend.

The numbers are striking. Some cancer rates have doubled in the past 20 years. It's just not yet clear whether we're seeing a rise in actual cases or whether we're seeing more early-stage cases because of better awareness, diagnosis and treatment in hospitals.

Take, for example, colorectal (or bowel) cancer: In 2001, about one in every 26,000 people under 50 in England were diagnosed with it each year. By 2023, the rate of diagnoses was about one in 12,000. Twice what it was at the turn of the century.

That's according to the World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF), an international network of charities, which analyzed registry data from England, the United States and the Netherlands. It found the number of new cancer cases had risen for seven cancers in England alone.

So, rates have doubled, but the numbers overall are still small, said Amy Berrington de Gonzalez, a professor of clinical epidemiology at the Institute of Cancer Research in London.

"Cancer is still very uncommon among young people," Berrington de Gonzalez told DW. "Three times a small number is still a small number."

But she and other scientists still want to know why this is happening, because it is a worldwide trend. Latin America is a case in point.

A 2025 study analyzed registry data from 50 countries and territories up to 2017. It found early-onset colorectal cancer rising in 27 of those regions.

In Latin America, rates rose in Chile, Argentina, Ecuador, Costa Rica and Puerto Rico. Chile's rise — at almost 4% per year — was among the highest.

In Brazil, the evidence comes directly from hospitals. At the A.C. Camargo Cancer Center in Sao Paulo, the number of colorectal cancer patients under 50 grew by 7.6% per year between 2000 and 2023. That's a five-fold increase.

The number of patients over 50 grew even faster — at 8.1% — or about six-fold.

But since these figures came from a single hospital, they do not show whether cases are rising across Brazil. They may only reflect that this one hospital is treating more patients.

"We need better cancer registration systems," said Berrington de Gonzalez, "especially in countries like Brazil and Mexico, where obesity rates have also been increasing."

Marc Gunter, professor of cancer epidemiology at Imperial College London, said he expected Latin America to follow richer countries as diets there become more Westernized and lifestyles more sedentary. "I think it's already happening," he said.

In another study in 2025, involving 42 countries, Berrington de Gonzalez compared data from two age groups — 20 to 49 years and 50 years and older — between 2003 and 2017.

Six cancers — thyroid, breast, colorectal, kidney, uterine and leukemia — had risen among the younger groups in most countries. All but colorectal cancer were seen to rise in older adults, too.

In fact, colorectal cancer stood out. In about a third of the countries, it rose faster in younger adults than in older ones. "It is the one cancer that seems to be increasing in younger people whilst decreasing in older people," said Berrington de Gonzalez.

This has consequences for research. If cancers are rising in all age groups, the causes are likely to be widespread, so studies looking only at young people risk chasing complicated explanations with too little data to test them, she said.

A US study of pancreatic cancer in adults aged 15 to 39 years found the rise was almost entirely made up of early-stage tumors.

The authors said this suggested that instead of the number of cancer cases going up, it was the number of early diagnoses that had gone up. And that can be explained by better awareness in the community, with people getting checked more often.

But the data for colorectal cancer looks different: Some US studies show it rising in advanced stages of the disease as well, which suggests an increase in the number of cases, not just the number of diagnoses.

Obesity is an established cause of at least 13 cancers, according to the WHO's International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).

According to a 2021 review of existing studies, young adults with obesity were almost twice as likely to develop colorectal cancer before the age of 50.

Obesity has also spread fastest among the young. The WHO reports that obesity rates have doubled among adults since the 1990s and quadrupled among adolescents.

Some also say ultra-processed foods and microplastics cause cancer. But Gunter said the evidence was inconclusive.

"There's a lot of hype," Gunter said, adding that more research was needed. Berrington de Gonzalez agreed: "Diets have changed, but we don't yet know the role they might play."

Gunter said individuals should not be blamed for living in societies built around convenience foods and desk jobs, and that lasting change needed public health policy.

But he also said that young people could lower their cancer risk by maintaining a healthy weight, moving more, eating more fiber and cutting back on processed meat.

"Going for a walk, being more physically active, eating more fruits and vegetables," he said. "Those things are maybe not so trendy, but they are really important."

For a 30-year-old reading this, Berrington de Gonzalez's message was one of reassurance: Cancer at a young age remains relatively rare.

#916. How green is Europe's urban public transport? A data-driven reality check

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Greener buses and cleaner fleets are reshaping public transport across Europe. But are cities moving fast enough to meet their zero-emission goals?

An electric plug icon on their windows makes them easy to spot in cities like Brussels and Madrid. Quietly navigating the streets, electric buses have become an increasingly common sight on Europe's streets in recent years.

Michela Le Pira, assistant professor of transportation at Italy's University of Catania, says removing tailpipe emissions — the gases and particles generated when a combustion vehicle is running — might be "the easiest strategy to improve public transport."

Most Europeans live in urban settings, and road transport harms air quality. In 2023, almost 300,000 deaths across the EU were caused by air pollution levels exceeding the World Health Organization's (WHO) recommended thresholds.

Of all fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentrations in European urban areas, about 14% come from road transport, including public, private and freight transport. Additionally, towns and cities account for nearly a quarter of the European Union's transport greenhouse gas emissions (GHG).

DW and the European Data Journalism Network evaluated more than a decade's worth of data from more than 100 reports and sources on public transit and the emissions it causes, and identified 148 sustainability commitments by local authorities and transport operators in 33 cities across Europe. The analysis focuses on motorized mass transit, leaving out bikes and pedestrians as well as taxis, shared vehicles and e-scooters.

The EU wants to become the first climate-neutral continent by 2050. A key part of that plan is cutting transport-related GHG emissions by 90% from 1990 levels.

As DW's analysis shows, Copenhagen is the only EU capital that has almost electrified its entire bus fleet — its vehicles do not have combustion engines and therefore do not produce tailpipe emissions. In cities like Rome, Lisbon and Nicosia, more than 90% of buses still run on diesel, and metros and trams account for less than 10% of their public transport fleet.

Although cars still dominate European streets, "electric buses are one way to reduce local air pollution," said Martin Baierl, data analyst with Transport & Environment, an advocacy non-profit for clean transport.

EU funds enabled many cities to increase their electric and alternative fuel bus fleets from 2021 on.

Electric buses comprise 20% of the total fleet in EU capitals and selected cities. Their number increased by 55% between 2021 and 2025 — equivalent to 1,234 more such vehicles on the road. The number of diesel-only buses has been declining since then, although they still outnumber those running on alternative fuel.

In addition to decarbonizing the bus fleet and helping to reduce air pollution, embracing electric models can help to lower noise and public transport operating costs. All the more so if the city produces its own electricity, explains Baierl. However, the outlay for e-buses is higher than for diesel models. In 20 out of the 31 cities with data to analyze, over 50% of the bus fleet is made up of traditional vehicles.

Brussels, for example, was an early adopter of moving away from traditional diesel-powered buses, switching to hybrids instead. Now the Belgian capital is in the process of electrifying its fleet, but at a slower pace. "It's not easy to know what the right technology will be in the end," Baierl said.

Another example is hydrogen-powered buses, which also produce zero emissions but are more costly to operate than their electric counterparts.

Transportation is the only major sector where greenhouse gas emissions remain above 1990 levels, despite the EU's ambition of a 90% reduction by 2050.

On average, public transport — including buses, trams and metros — is used for more than a quarter of trips in the 10 largest EU capitals, according to recent data from the non-profit group CDP (formerly the Carbon Disclosure Project). In Stockholm, the share is 49%, while in the smaller Slovenian capital of Ljubljana, it's 11%. DW analysis of transport sustainability pledges reveals that only four cities have explicitly committed to achieving zero emissions in buses or all public transport.

Yannis Papadimitriou lives 85 kilometers (about 53 miles) away from Athens, the EU capital with the highest air pollution mortality risk. He travels to work by commuter rail, a means of transport he'd like to see promoted more.

"For me it is a very conscious choice to use electric public transport, especially trains, which are the safest, most environmentally friendly and quickest way of transport."

Though trains, trams and metros run on electricity, there are more buses within city boundaries, and in many urban areas the majority still run on diesel.

In Northern Europe, 15 kilometers away from Copenhagen, Poul Kattler also used to commute to work. He is now retired and takes the bus more frequently with his grandchildren. Despite Copenhagen having an almost completely fossil-fuel-free public transport system (only one bus runs on biomethane), the metro expansion, which is responding to the city's growth, comes with its own set of concerns.

"The whole construction phase [of the new metro line] is extremely environmentally demanding," Kattler said. If the construction emissions are considered, "the CO2 balance would be very poor for the metro now."

But in the long run, the benefits of a new metro line in reducing CO2 emissions outweigh its construction-related emissions, Le Pira said.

Combustion or exhaust emissions are not the only way transport pollutes the air and the environment. Indirect emissions, like those coming from electricity generation or fuel production, add to transport's CO2 footprint. Brakes and wheels release non-exhaust particle matter when wagons or vehicles slow down.

Since 1992, buses and any vehicle running on fossil fuels in the EU have been subject to stricter emissions standards. Fine particle emissions from buses and heavy-duty vehicles had dropped by 92% in 2022 according to the European Environment Agency. Yet overall road transport non-exhaust emissions from tire wear have increased 58% in two decades, as cars have become heavier.

With a few exceptions, EU member states are not close to cutting greenhouse gas transport emissions by 90%, as envisioned by the EU's 2050 target.

Zero-emission public transport is one step toward decarbonizing transport in cities and meeting GHG reduction goals. The next challenge is to get people out of their cars.

"If we talk about sustainable mobility in general and not only clean public transport, the main problem is the modal shift from private cars to public transport," Le Pira said.

On average, private automobiles account for more than a third of transport in the largest EU capitals, according to CDP data.

Cheaper ticket prices for groups, like students, and disincentives for car use, such as low-emission zones, are two complementary ways of attracting more passengers while reducing private transport.

In her research work, Le Pira investigates how public transport can be more efficient and appealing for city dwellers, for example, by combining transport modes where people step off their bikes and onto a train. This kind of solution could be particularly relevant for small cities "where cars are still the main transport mode."

According to Baierl, places with good public transport draw people in. "Cities where not everybody has to use a car are just more livable," he said.

#826. Brazil's elections matter for the planet. This is why

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Brazil is heading back to the polls, but the impact could be felt worldwide. With the Amazon vital to the health of the planet, the election could help shape its future.

Few national elections carry as much environmental weight as Brazil's presidential race. And this one is being watched from far beyond the country's boundaries.

Home to the largest section of the Amazon rainforest, Brazil is guardian of a carbon sink of vital importance, making the country's conservation policies globally relevant.

Environmentalists have already voiced alarm at Flávio Bolsonaro — son of right-wing former president Jair Bolsonaro — coming out 2% ahead of incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in the first round of elections.

"It's going to be a second coming of Bolsonaro, therefore a more radical Bolsonaro," said Claudio Angelo, international policy coordinator at Climate Observatory, a network of Brazilian NGOs.

"At the risk of sounding alarmist, I would say Lula's re-election is the only thing standing between us and the end of the Amazon."

The Amazon rainforest stores huge amounts of the carbon dioxide (CO2) released into the atmosphere from the burning of fossil fuels. As a result, it helps regulate the Earth's climate.

For decades, the tropical forest has acted as one of nature's most powerful defenses against climate change. But it has also suffered from widespread deforestation.

During Jair Bolsonaro's presidency, from 2019 until 2023, environmental agencies were weakened and enforcement efforts curtailed, meaning tropical forest was cleared at unprecedented rates.

"The environment agenda was disregarded and the level of control the government had on pollution overall, but specifically on deforestation, was reversed," said Claudio Forner, head of climate policy at Berlin-based think tank Climate Analytics.

Over Bolsonaro's four-year term, an estimated 45,586km2 of the Amazon was destroyed, an area bigger than the size of Denmark. And the forest is not as resilient as it once was, with scientists warning that parts of it are now emitting more carbon than they absorb.

Lula came to office with a message of protection for the Amazon. Besides calling for an end to deforestation by 2030, he increased monitoring of the forest and strengthened Brazil's environmental enforcement agency IBAMA.

Deforestation has plummeted under his leadership, with 2026 set to be its lowest level in a decade. That represents a 41% drop overall compared to Jair Bolsonaro's tenure.

Experts have already criticized Flávio Bolsonaro's record on forest protection. As a senator in 2019, he authored draft legislation that could have stark impacts for the Amazon. While landowners are currently required to preserve 80% of their property for the growth of native plants, the bill would allow them to use their holdings as they please.

"The signal from the president will say 'now you can deforest' or 'it's harder to deforest,'" said Ana Yang, director of the Environment and Society Center at London-based think tank Chatham House.

"The moment that you decrease enforcement capability, deforestation grows," she told DW.

Bolsonaro has also pledged to end illegal deforestation by 2029, a year before Lula. But Claudio Angelo is critical of that message.

"He will end illegal deforestation not by enforcing the law, but by making it legal," he said.

More generally, environmental experts fear Bolsonaro might dismantle many of the protections strengthened under the current president.

"Unfortunately, climate change has become a partisan issue, so rather than offering a solution from their perspective, the right pictures climate change as an invented problem...that regulation goes against economic growth or it goes against competitiveness, that it's the aim of the left to control the means of production," Forner said.

In an interview with Brazilian media in August, Bolsonaro said he regarded extreme weather events to be cyclical and did not believe they were solely linked to human influence.

A Bolsonaro win is likely to see Brazil move away from its position as a climate leader, a role the country embraced last year as it hosted the COP30 climate conference in the Amazonian city of Belem.

But even a Lula victory would not represent a straightforward climate success story, say experts. While the current administration has overseen significant reductions in deforestation, it has also backed plans to expand oil production.

Angelo said the decision to allow exploratory drilling was a "betrayal" of Lula's mandate but added that the president's overall record on climate had been "quite impressive".

"Lula is the world's worst climate leader, except for all the others," he told DW.

The current president will face a runoff election with Bolsonaro on October 25. But while experts say the outcome could determine the fate of the Amazon and the wider efforts to curb global warming, for many Brazilians climate is not high on their list of priorities.

"Ultimately what people care about is access to education, sanitation and health," said Yang. A road built through the Amazon to access those services, while criticized from outside Brazil for destroying parts of the rainforest, would be desirable to voters, she added.

"They don't have time to care. Not because they don't want to. They care about their own survival," she said.

#234. Evacuation from Oleshky: How are preparations going?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A lawyer with the Save Ukraine Foundation, Myroslava Kharchenko, told DW about what has been happening in Russian-occupied Oleshky since a temporary ceasefire brokered by Melania Trump took effect.

Since a ceasefire took effect in the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky in Ukraine's Kherson region on October 27, preparations have been underway to evacuate residents trapped there.

Following Russia's occupation of the town in 2022 and the flooding caused by the destruction of the nearby Kakhovka dam in 2023, residents were cut off from the outside world. All access routes to Oleshky, which lies on the Dnipro River, had been mined. Former Oleshky residents who managed to escape and talked to DW described hunger, no access to medicine, and no electricity or water. About 2,000 people are still living in the town.

According to the Save Ukraine Foundation, it has rescued at least 300 civilians from Oleshky over the past six months. DW spoke with lawyer Myroslava Kharchenko, who works for the organization, about preparations for the upcoming evacuation.

DW: A ceasefire is already in effect in Oleshky. Do you know whether any evacuation efforts have begun?

Myroslava Kharchenko: No, we do not have that information yet. What we do know is that the ceasefire came into effect on October 27 at 3 a.m. Kyiv time, which is 4 a.m. in Russia. Mine-clearing operations on the road began at around 8 a.m. What is happening inside Oleshky itself, however, is entirely within the military's remit. Everyone involved in this operation has been asked to maintain strict confidentiality. Any careless remark or action could jeopardize the entire evacuation effort.

What I can see is that information is being actively collected through closed internal local chat groups. Registration lists and application forms are being shared, and people are filling them out themselves. Organizers are contacting those who previously requested evacuation to verify details such as their date of birth and current place of residence. They are also asking people to provide contact information for neighbors or relatives who may be nearby or in the same building, because communications are extremely unreliable.

The organizers are also collecting information about the health conditions of those planning to leave. Among them are people who are ill, have limited mobility, or are recovering from recent injuries, including blast and mine-related wounds. We therefore expect those organizing the evacuation to ensure that medical assistance is available for those who need it.

How exactly is the evacuation expected to take place? Will people be evacuated through Russian-controlled territory or via the Dnipro River? Have any routes already been agreed upon?

As far as we know, a river route would be extremely dangerous. We have not heard any confirmation that such an option has been approved. It therefore appears likely that the evacuation route will pass through the temporarily occupied Ukrainian territory and possibly through Russia before people are able to reach Ukraine. For security reasons, however, none of these details are being made public.

What do you know about the number of people expected to be evacuated?

We are actually very pleased that the evacuation is not expected to take place in a single day. That is important because we hope that those who manage to leave safely during the first stage will serve as an example for others.

People still trapped in Oleshky are extremely afraid. There have already been numerous provocations, and this is not the first attempt to establish such a route or humanitarian corridor.

As a result, there is a great deal of suspicion. Those concerns are based largely on uncertainty, and some people may choose not to leave during the initial phase of the evacuation. Overall, however, we estimate that around 500 people may eventually be evacuated.

How would you assess the role of Melania Trump in making this evacuation possible?

We are immensely grateful to Melania Trump for drawing attention to the humanitarian catastrophe currently unfolding in Oleshky. What is taking place there is in fact genocide committed by the Russian Federation.

We are thankful for her efforts and hope they will help make this evacuation possible. We will be even more grateful once most people are safely removed from the combat zone and arrive in Ukrainian government-controlled territory.

When could the physical evacuation of civilians begin?

I can't say how long it will take. What seems clear, however, is that time will be needed to clear mines from the roads leading into the city. We also understand that many streets inside Oleshky are mined, so additional work will be required to make routes within the city safe.

There is a hospital from which people need to be evacuated. Nearby is a morgue where bodies also need to be recovered.

I do not know whether that will be included as part of this humanitarian corridor, but it is undoubtedly important. The bodies need to be identified and the causes of death established. That process will also take time, regardless of how quickly the military is able to work.

It is difficult to make any predictions because, as you can see, everything is being conducted under strict secrecy. Statements from both the Russian and Ukrainian sides have been extremely cautious, and that is clearly justified under the circumstances.

#57. Berlin's Elif Eralp under fire over Der Spiegel interview

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Left candidate Elif Eralp has sparked controversy after Der Speigel reported she was advised against mentioning a pro-Palestinian activist for fear of reprisals against her family.

Testosterone has shown “insufficient” evidence to support social media claims that it can improve certain symptoms linked to the menopause, according to a new study.

Social media has been rife with claims that testosterone can improve brain fog and memory symptoms in menopausal women.

However, experts say that while anecdotal information exists around the effects of testosterone, there is currently not sufficient evidence to make any definitive claims.

According to University College London (UCL), prescriptions for testosterone have “increased across NHS and private clinics in the UK”, which are largely “reflecting growing interest in its role during menopause”.

The team reviewed five studies which looked at testosterone can improve learning and memory in post menopausal women.

Some 244 women with an average age of 57 involved in the review were given either a placebo dummy drug or testosterone at varying levels.

The studies took place over four to 26 weeks. The research team did not find statistically significant improvements for cognitive function among participants taking testosterone.

“The findings should be interpreted as insufficient to establish an effect, rather than as evidence of no effect,” the authors wrote in the journal Reproduction And Fertility.

Professor Aimee Spector, senior author of the study and director of the UCL Menopause Mind Lab, said: “Social media is flooded with claims that testosterone improves memory and brain fog, and we hear of many women taking testosterone specifically to manage these symptoms during the menopause.

“While there may be some anecdotal evidence for this in perimenopausal women, we cannot claim testosterone improves cognition during menopause without further evidence.

“More research is needed to understand any links between testosterone and brain health.”

Corresponding author Dr Alexandra Faulkner, from UCL’s division of psychology and language sciences, added: “Our review found that the available evidence is limited and insufficient to determine the effect of testosterone therapy on cognition in women during the menopause transition and post menopause.

“This reflects a lack of sufficient evidence rather than evidence of no effect.”

Around 13 million women are estimated to be peri or menopausal in the UK. Menopause happens when a woman’s periods change and eventually stop due to lower hormone levels.

It is thought that testosterone levels do not acutely change during menopause, but instead begin to decline from a woman’s mid-20s to early 30s, the UCL team said.

The NHS website states that testosterone gel or cream can help to improve low libido for some women in the menopause.

The hormone can be obtained on prescription from a menopause specialist, the website adds.

The research team called for more work to examine the impact of testosterone on cognition during the menopause.

Experts at Cardiff University are currently studying whether adding testosterone to standard hormone replacement therapy (HRT) can reduce menopausal symptoms beyond its effect on libido.

The trial, which will involve 400 women and is due to conclude in 2029, aims to publish findings on whether testosterone can impact cognition, exercise, motivation and energy levels among menopausal women.

Earlier this year it emerged that women are posing as men to buy testosterone from online pharmacies to get hold of prescriptions.

Menopause specialist Dr Michelle Olver told the BBC that she had seen women with up to 12 times the safe level of the hormone after online purchases.

#72. What killed 500-plus penguins? How scientists solved the mystery

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A small group of Adélie penguins return to their colony. A mega-colony of these penguins was hit by a big die-off in early 2024. Ralph Vanstreels hide caption

Sailing toward the Danger Islands, Dr. Ralph Vanstreels had a good idea of what he might find: A lot of dead Adélie penguins.

It was March 2024 and a virus had been sweeping through the area, leaving deceased birds in its wake.

Vanstreels got the crime right but the culprit wrong. There were dead penguins but a virus was not to blame.

As his vessel pulled up to a landing ramp on one of the islands — located just off the Antarctic Peninsula — Vanstreels and his fellow researchers immediately noticed bumps in the snow.

"Was each bump a rock or was it a carcass? We had to do quite a bit of intensive searching [through the snow]," says Vanstreels, who is based in Argentina and is a wildlife veterinarian with the University of California, Davis.

The team ultimately found more than 537 penguin carcasses on just the ramp, which he estimates was about the size of an airport runway.

There were many more carcasses elsewhere on the Danger Islands, which are home to two mega colonies for penguins. The researchers estimate there were thousands of dead Adélie penguins and other species of birds. But they had to work quickly and couldn't do a complete count, Vanstreels says.

A view of the Adélie penguin colony at Beagle Island, one of the Danger Islands. A few penguin carcasses are scattered on the rocks. Ralph Vanstreels hide caption

"You cannot stay there very long because you might be locked down with an iceberg blocking your way out," he says.

As the team has pieced together this tragic penguin mystery, Vanstreels says, the case holds broader lessons for tracking the spread of diseases globally.

Now let's time jump to Australia a few months ago for another bird disease conundrum.

In June, H5N1 — high pathogenicity avian influenza also known as bird flu — arrived in mainland Australia. Up until that point, Australia had been the only continent to dodge the virus which has traversed the globe, leaving a trail of carcasses in its path.

Since late 2021, the virus has led to the death of nearly 300 million poultry birds. Either the virus killed them or they were culled as a preventive measure to stop the spread of the disease, according to the World Animal Health Information System. Further, bird flu is estimated to have killed millions of wild animals and marine mammals, although it's hard to get an exact figure.

Researchers were amazed that Australia could stay bird-flu-free for six years. And the virus didn't take the path that scientists expected.

"We have a lot of migration of birds between Australia and Southeast Asia, so for the first few years, we're like: That's where the virus is coming from," says Dr. Amandine Gamble, an assistant professor at Cornell University in the Department of Public and Ecosystem Health.

The way bird flu eventually got there was from the other direction. It came from Antarctica likely via migrating birds.

"This is a virus that's not assisted by airplanes," Michelle Wille, senior research fellow at the Center for Pathogen Genomics at the University of Melbourne, told NPR in 2025. "This is a virus that's traveling by mass mortality after mass mortality after mass mortality after mass mortality."

So when Vanstreels set sail to check on the penguins, he assumed bird flu would be the culprit.

Bird flu arrived in Antarctica in late 2023 and early 2024. Vanstreels and his colleagues rapidly arranged for a trip to try to figure out exactly what was happening.

"The expedition was initially fully set up to look for bird flu because that was the panic," he explains, adding that ongoing fear of a continuing global panzootic — or pandemic among animals — is justified.

In several layers of warm clothes covered by protective gear — double gloves, coveralls, glasses and masks — Vanstreels and the team started taking samples of the penguin corpses they found.

"We looked a little bit stiff when walking — like a penguin walking almost — because of all the gear," he says. "We had very strict biosafety standards."

They brought samples from the deceased penguins back to a mini-lab on their boat. By evening, they had early results. But not the results they expected.

"There is bird flu here, but the virus loads are low," he recalls. "That was a little bit puzzling."

A scientist conducts the necropsy of an Adélie penguin chick collected at Heroina Island, one of the Danger Islands where disease struck. Ralph Vanstreels hide caption

The carcasses were not very old, so Vanstreels would have expected to find far more bird flu virus if that had killed the birds. Perhaps something else was the main driver? "We were unsure what was happening," he recalls.

So what killed the 500-plus penguins on the landing ramp and the many others on the island?

Back on land several months after the voyage, in the Netherlands, Vanstreels' colleagues did more investigation. "Under the microscope, you can see that there's bacteria in the tissues," he says — not virus.

They discovered a different disease, a bacterial disease that causes avian cholera (which is distinct from human cholera).

Vanstreels and his team's findings were published in October in the journal the Proceedings of the Royal Society B.

So how worried are they about avian cholera?

The disease is not considered a major threat to humans since the strain that is specialized to birds is distinct from the strain specialized to mammals. "The risk for human infection is not really that high when you see an outbreak on birds," Vanstreels says. But, it does kill birds worldwide — especially in cold environments where the bacteria thrives.

While there's not enough research to know exactly how common avian cholera is, the disease has been on the radar of scientists for decades. However, it's not exactly top of mind. The last time researchers looked for it in the Antarctic Peninsula was 25 years ago.

"One could be misled to think that: Well, that was a unique thing that happened back in 2001, and nothing's happened ever since," says Vanstreels. Instead, it is possible that avian cholera die-offs could be occurring regularly, only scientists haven't been checking.

One of his big takeaways from this bird-flu-that-turned-out-to-be-avian-cholera event?

"Yes, there is a big thing with bird flu. But we still want to keep checking for the other diseases because they might be mixing up in the middle," Vanstreels says.

Like any good mystery, the case of the cholera-stricken penguins has got scientists wondering: Whodunit?

There's a theory that avian cholera might have been introduced to Antarctica decades ago from researchers who brought poultry from the U.S., Vanstreels says.

The Adelie penguin mega-colony at Heroina Island at the peak of the breeding season. The photo was taken in January 2025, a year after the outbreak. hide caption

Gamble, of Cornell University, adds that the strain of avian cholera found in penguins in Antarctica is "very close" to the strain found 5,000 miles away on Île Amsterdam in the Southern Indian Ocean.

"Antarctica is way more connected to the rest of the world than we think it is," she says.

It's hard to track diseases in Antarctica but, she says, it's turning out to be an important piece in the global pathogen puzzle.

The work of keeping tabs on what's happening has fallen to a number of different countries since Antarctica is not governed by any one government. It was designated as a continent of peace and science by the mid 20th-century Antarctic Treaty, which now has 58 parties — among them are the U.S., France, Japan and Russia. Many nations have research stations and team up on scientific undertakings, including tracking pathogens — be it bird flu or avian cholera — that travel great distances and don't respect borders.

"So this Avian cholera outbreak is just showing this is not a unique event," says Gamble. "We really need international collaboration."

A special tribunal in Bangladesh on Thursday (October 29, 2026) sentenced Awami League's Joint General Secretary Mahbubul Alam Hanif to death for the killing of six people during the July Uprising.

A three-member tribunal led by Justice Nozrul Islam Chowdhury and comprising Justices Md. Manjurul Bashid and Nur Mohammad Shahriar Kabir delivered the verdict.

"He will be hanged until death,” Mr. Chowdhury ruled after convicting him of killing six people in western Kushtia district during the 2024 violent student-led street protests.

Ms. Hasina and her party leaders have called the tribunal a “kangaroo court” while international rights groups like Amnesty International, U.S.-based Human Rights Watch and prominent jurists questioned the fairness of the trials.

The ouster of the Awami League government installed an interim regime with Muhammad Yunus as its head. The tribunal's chief prosecutor Md. Aminul Islam welcomed the verdict, describing it as timely.

"The tribunal delivered its verdict today after completing the trial in this crimes against humanity case. Today's verdict is very timely. Paying tribute to the July martyrs, we welcome the verdict," he said. The convicts were defended by state-appointed lawyers.

Researchers hope to better understand the drivers of major health challenges facing younger people, such as mental illness and rising cancer rates, through the recruitment of 100,000 people for Australia’s largest intergenerational study of wellbeing and ageing.

The independent and not-for-profit research institute the Sax Institute on Thursday launched the 18 and Up study, which will follow the health of those aged 18 to 45 throughout their lives.

Two decades ago, the institute started 45 and Up, now the largest population-based cohort study in the southern hemisphere, with more than 267,000 taking part ranging in age from 45 to older than 100.

Since then, participants have been repeatedly surveyed and their responses linked to other datasets, leading to significant discoveries about smoking, dementia prevention and differences in cardiovascular outcomes between men and women.

18 and Up’s scientific director, Adjunct Prof Justine Gatt, said there was a lack of long-term data for younger generations, with existing studies often only giving researchers information from a snapshot in time.

“Almost two in five young Australians aged 16 to 24 experienced a mental disorder in the past year, and that number has been rising,” Gatt, a neuroscientist and mental health researcher said. “Yet we still lack the long-term evidence needed to understand why.”

Suicide is the leading cause of death among Australians aged 15 to 44, and researchers have said there is an urgent need to understand and address driving factors to improve prevention efforts and mental health treatments.

Children and grandchildren of 45 and Up participants will be invited to participate, which Gatt said will help researchers understand how health is influenced by a complex mix of family history, lived experiences and social conditions, and how these factors interact across generations.

The chief executive of the Sax Institute, Dr Martin McNamara, said along with mental health, questions around cancer will also be an important part of the initial round of surveys.

Since 2000, there has been an increase in early-onset cancers – defined as those diagnosed beyond adolescence but before the age of 50 – in Australia and other parts of the world. This includes bowel, thyroid, breast and other cancers.

While scientists think increased screening, changing lifestyles and different exposures to risk factors in early life and young adulthood may all be factors, there have been calls for urgent research to better understand these and other influences.

Participants will be asked about health, lifestyle, social circumstances and background and, with consent, this information will be linked to other health records, such as cancer registries and Medicare.

“It’s important we follow people over time … so that we can figure out ways to fix and re-organise our health and care systems,” McNamara said.

“We’ll have a really good picture of their lives, the things that are important to them, their education, their employment, where they live.”

The study will evolve with participants to receive age-relevant questions, and emerging health conditions or pandemics will be factored in. It is being funded by a $4.5m National Health and Medical Research Council grant, with recruitment beginning in November.

A professor of epidemiology and public health at the Australia National University, Emily Banks, is a former scientific director at the Sax Institute who used 45 and Up study data to reveal in 2025 that smoking-related illnesses kill 66 Australians every day – more than previously thought.

Banks said large-scale cohort studies are powerful as they give researchers a window into the population and help detail what people are exposed to during their lives. She said the 45 and Up data has become an important collaborative resource.

It has been used by more than 800 researchers worldwide.

“We’ve had hundreds of researchers using the data. We also have around 10% of the population of New South Wales in that age [45+] group in the study,” she said.

“Some of the biggest advances in health have come from using this kind of information.”

Cultivated areas reached 43,000 hectares (around 1,06,200 acres), up from 10,000 hectares in 2025, while estimated opium production jumped fivefold from 300 tonnes to 1,500 tonnes, the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) said in a report.

Long the world’s leading producer of poppies, Afghanistan was overtaken by Myanmar after a 2022 Taliban government ban crushed the trade in the country. But rampant poverty has driven some farmers to return to the trade.

Afghanistan’s 2022 crackdown drove cultivated areas down from more than 2,00,000 hectares to 10,000 hectares in 2025.

Myanmar cultivated 53,000 hectares in 2025, with no 2026 figures available. The Taliban authorities claim to have destroyed more than 22,000 hectares.

Mr. Mohammad lives with his parents in Badakhshan which has become Afghanistan’s main opium poppy-growing province.

In May, two people were killed in clashes that erupted as Afghan security forces moved to destroy opium poppy fields in Badakhshan.

“Opium prices slightly decreased from US$ 570 per kilogramme in 2025 to US$ 500 per kilogramme in 2026, remaining well above pre-ban levels of less than $100 per kilogramme,” UNODC said in a press release.

#894. US hospitals and nursing homes are breeding grounds for deadly superbugs – CDC

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

More than four in five US deaths linked to drug-resistant infections involve pathogens commonly associated with hospitals and nursing homes, according to a new report from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

The agency’s Antimicrobial Resistance Threats Report released this week draws on data from 2023 and estimates that 19 types of drug-resistant bacteria and fungi cause 9.7 million infections and 50,800 deaths annually in the US. Seven bacterial threats commonly associated with hospitals and nursing homes accounted for more than 42,000 of those deaths, along with nearly $12 billion in healthcare costs.

Antimicrobial resistance occurs when germs develop the ability to withstand drugs designed to kill them, making infections difficult or impossible to treat. Elderly people, those with weakened immune systems, and patients undergoing invasive medical procedures are particularly vulnerable.

Among the pathogens classified as urgent threats is Candida auris, a potentially deadly fungus that can resist multiple medications and survive on surfaces in healthcare facilities. It primarily affects seriously ill patients and can cause dangerous bloodstream infections.

A separate CDC study published in July found that reported cases of Candida auris more than doubled from 2022-2024, rising from 2,882 to 6,197. Most cases for which the type of medical facility was identified were detected in hospitals, including those that provide long-term treatment for seriously ill patients.

The CDC noted that many healthcare-associated infections are preventable, but much of the progress in combating drug resistance was lost during the Covid-19 pandemic, when staffing shortages, overcrowding, and disruptions to infection-control practices contributed to a rise in infections.

The warning comes as US hospitals face mounting financial and staffing pressures. A Harvard-led study published earlier this month found that 432 hospitals closed nationwide from 2010-2025, resulting in a net loss of 216 facilities and more than 24,000 beds.

Nursing homes are also struggling with bed and staffing shortages. An investigation in September by KFF Health News found that declining capacity forced some patients to remain hospitalized longer because suitable care facilities were unavailable.

By Maxim Suchkov, Director of the MGIMO Institute for International Studies (IIS)

When Donald Trump returned triumphantly to the White House in January 2025, his supporters believed they could finally finish what he started during his first term and reverse the Democrats’ social engineering agenda, commonly known as ‘wokeism’, curb illegal immigration, accelerate technological development, and above all, ‘make America great again’.

But Trump’s determination to achieve everything at once, from restructuring the federal government and securing favorable trade deals to pursuing ambitions involving Greenland and Venezuela, has kept American politics in a state of turmoil, while his confrontational approach has also unsettled allies and opponents abroad.

The decision to launch joint military operations with Israel against Iran has proven particularly divisive as it fractured Trump’s conservative coalition, with some supporters accusing him of betraying his promise to end America’s ‘endless wars’, while others fear the political consequences for Republicans in the approaching midterm elections.

The Iran war has also hurt American consumers. Over the past year, gasoline prices have risen by 38% and diesel by 71%, while food prices have increased by 2.5%.

The blockade of the Strait of Hormuz isn’t solely responsible, but Democrats and sympathetic media outlets have made it central to their criticism, while they also point to an increase of $3.8 trillion in the national debt since Trump’s return, allegations of corruption within his administration, and questions about the competence of senior officials, particularly Secretary of War Pete Hegseth and Health Secretary Robert F. Republicans, including those who oppose the Iran war, are trying to redirect voters towards the administration’s achievements – despite the turbulence, the US economy has grown by 3.2% since the end of 2024, and investment is booming in certain sectors, particularly technology, while illegal crossings at the southern border have reportedly fallen by 95%.

Vice President J.D. Vance, who is increasingly discussed as Trump’s successor, has urged Americans to give Republicans another chance, or at least another two years, to complete what they have begun – but for now, that opportunity appears uncertain. Yet contrary to expectations, losing control of Congress wouldn’t necessarily be a disaster for Trump.

Even with Republican majorities in both chambers, he has preferred to govern through executive orders, issuing more than 290 since January 2025, and this is hardly unprecedented. Bill Clinton issued 364 over his eight years in office, including during periods when Republicans controlled Congress.

For presidents, demonstrating immediate effectiveness and scoring political points often matters more than ensuring continuity.

Democratic threats to impeach Trump and members of his administration may sound formidable, but removing officials from office requires a two-thirds majority in the Senate, a much higher hurdle, and even if Democrats capture both chambers, converting their numerical advantage into meaningful political influence will not be straightforward.

They could, however, obstruct legislation, block nominations to senior administration positions and the federal judiciary, and subject the White House to sustained congressional scrutiny.

Their minimum objective would be to turn Trump into a lame-duck president for his remaining two years, but this strategy carries risks of its own – in the 2028 presidential campaign, Republicans can argue that the Democrats prevented them from completing their program.

For Moscow, however, the consequences of the midterms could be considerably more serious.

The urgency could also reflect an attempt to secure lasting advantages for the administration before the political landscape changes.

The argument that there’s little time left, and that Moscow must therefore accept an agreement now or face worse conditions later, shouldn’t be allowed to overshadow Russia’s own interests.

Russia has an objective interest in normalizing relations with the US, but a settlement of the Ukraine conflict must be based on mutually beneficial terms, rather than unilateral concessions extracted under the pressure of America’s electoral calendar.

Ultimately, as Americans prepare to vote in the midterms, Russia should be concerned less with Trump’s political fortunes than with protecting its own interests.

#1052. Charity begins at home: Ireland must get its priorities straight

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

By John Gaffney, Irish-born editor and former retail businessman who has been living in Russia since 2018

The Irish political week has once again been marked by bitter exchanges, this time over the Triple Lock Agreement, our long-standing tradition of military neutrality, and whether a referendum should be held before any fundamental change is made to it.

Taoiseach (Ireland’s prime minister) Micheal Martin has accused the country’s largest opposition party Sinn Fein, which fiercely opposes the idea of dismantling Triple Lock, of not giving a “continental” about Irish neutrality. The word is his own, and it is worth reflecting on its meaning.

But I have a question of my own for Mr. Martin: does he give a continental about the health and well-being of the Irish people?

What does neutrality mean to a parent whose three-year-old child is suffering in agony while waiting nine hours in Galway Regional Hospital? What comfort does it offer families struggling to access essential medical care, or parents and students caught up in this week’s education strikes? And what of the many other problems that continue to weigh upon ordinary Irish households?

The latest figures, obtained from the HSE by Padraig Rice, Social Democrats TD and Chair of the Oireachtas Committee on Health, make the situation even more difficult to ignore. Between January and July of this year, more than 70,100 people in Ireland either left emergency departments before completing their treatment or did not wait to be seen. A further 31,821 patients waited longer than 24 hours to be admitted or discharged. The average waiting time in emergency departments was 9.3 hours.

These matters concern real people, their children, their dignity, and their daily lives. Before our leaders lecture us about international responsibilities, should they not first explain why such basic needs remain unmet at home?

Ireland is not a wealthy country merely because figures on a government spreadsheet say so. A country’s true wealth lies in the quality of its health service, the education of its children, the reliability of its transport, and the infrastructure that allows people to live and work with confidence. By that measure, we still have much to do.

Yet Irish taxpayers are repeatedly asked to support ever greater expenditure connected with the conflict in Ukraine. At what point do we pause and ask whether our priorities are in the right order?

Mr. Martin, charity begins at home. Have you forgotten the recession of 2008 and the painful years that followed? Irish families know what it means to endure financial insecurity, lost opportunities, and the consequences of decisions made far above their heads. We should remember, too, the countries that stood by Ireland during difficult times, and consider carefully where our obligations lie today.

This week, the European Union’s foreign policy chief, Kaja Kallas, has again been pressing for further support for Ukraine while criticizing US President Donald Trump. Ordinary European citizens are expected to accept the bill.

But surely there comes a moment when the people who pay must be entitled to ask questions.

I believe European citizens should stand up for themselves, examine the priorities of their leaders, and refuse to treat every pronouncement from Brussels as unquestionable wisdom. Supporting a country in distress is one thing; allowing an open-ended political commitment to overshadow pressing needs at home is quite another.

Imagine what could be achieved if public money were spent with greater care and accountability. Imagine health services where children did not have to wait hours in pain, schools where disputes did not repeatedly interrupt education, and transport and infrastructure that served communities as they deserve to be served. These are not some extravagant dreams – just the reasonable expectations of a modern society.

Meanwhile, EU leaders continue to speak as though the destruction of Russia were a realistic or desirable political objective. I believe this approach is dangerously misguided. Russia is a major power, and European security requires clear thinking, diplomacy, and an honest assessment of the risks of prolonged confrontation. Declaring ever more money and resources necessary does not, by itself, bring peace any closer.

Nor does constant hostility offer a convincing vision for Europe’s future. António Costa, Kaja Kallas, Ursula von der Leyen, and other senior figures in the European hierarchy should be judged by the consequences of their decisions, not by the strength of their rhetoric. The EU was supposed to offer cooperation, stability, and prosperity. It should not become an institution in which confrontation abroad takes precedence over the needs of its own citizens.

And what of Ukraine itself? If European leaders genuinely care about the Ukrainian people, surely their first concern should be the preservation of human life and the search for a sustainable peace. Endless conflict brings suffering, displacement, destruction, and the loss of generations who might otherwise have built a future for themselves. The human cost deserves at least as much attention as the political ambitions of those directing the response.

I do not believe that the future of Europe should be built upon the assumption that Russia must be destroyed. I believe it should be built upon realism, diplomacy, accountability, and the recognition that war always extracts its price from ordinary people first.

Ireland has its own history of hardship, resilience, and recovery. We know what it means to struggle, to rebuild, and to depend upon the solidarity of others. That history should make us thoughtful about the suffering of people elsewhere, but it should also remind us of our responsibility to our own citizens.

Before asking Irish taxpayers to shoulder yet another burden, our government should explain what it is doing to ensure that a sick child receives timely treatment, that pupils receive the education they need, and that public services function as they should.

Mr. Martin, the question is not whether Ireland should care about the wider world. Of course it should. The question is whether caring about the wider world has become an excuse for neglecting the people who elected you.

Charity begins at home. So does responsibility. And so, ultimately, should political accountability.

It is time for Ireland, and for the EU, to put ordinary people back at the center of public life. Our taxes should serve the public good, our leaders should be answerable for their choices, and diplomacy should seek to preserve lives rather than prolong their destruction.

The people of Europe deserve nothing less.
