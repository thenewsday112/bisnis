Astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope may have discovered a supernova erupting just 1.1 billion years after the Big Bang.

Astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have found what may be one of the most distant supernovas ever observed. And in a stroke of cosmic luck, the team thinks they're seeing what the explosion looked like just days after it ignited.

The supernova, named AT 2026abck, dates to around 1.15 billion years after the Big Bang, when the universe was less than one-tenth of its current age and bursting with massive stars flickering into life. The massive exploding star violently released its light over 12.5 billion years ago, according to the researchers — so long ago that the distance between us has effectively ballooned sixfold due to the expansion of the universe.

The discovery was described in a paper that was uploaded to the preprint server arXiv Sept. 29 and has not yet been peer reviewed. According to the study authors, this ancient speck of light is stupendously bright.

"At its peak, the supernova is about 8 billion times the brightness of the sun … almost as bright as the whole Milky Way Galaxy," study co-author Xiaohui Fan, an observational cosmologist and professor of astronomy at the University of Arizona, told Live Science via email. "Over the course of the explosion, it radiates the energy that the sun will radiate over about 2 billion years."

The newly discovered supernova shines brilliantly at over 30,000 degrees Fahrenheit (16,000 degrees Celsius). Its incandescent shell of ejected material has billowed to a radius of approximately 46 astronomical units ‪—‬ about 46 times the distance between Earth and the sun, or about as far as Pluto swings at its farthest point from the sun.

The supernova's host galaxy, designated ID101411, holds about 6.3 billion solar masses and is "forming new stars more than ten times faster than our Milky Way," first author Mingyang Zhuang, an observational astrophysicist at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, told Live Science via email.

As with other early galaxies, ID101411 is deficient in metals, meaning any elements heavier than hydrogen and helium, because not enough time had passed to create those heavier elements in the forges of massive stars and their apocalyptic explosions.

The study authors estimate that they observed the supernova as it would have looked just four to 10 days after it exploded. Although this is relatively common, spotting one at such an incredible distance "is extremely rare," Zhuang emphasized. "You have to observe the exact same tiny patch of sky repeatedly with [JWST], and the explosion has to happen at the right moment. We were lucky."

There's another layer of luck that made the discovery even more remarkable: It was observed without the aid of a cosmological quirk called gravitational lensing — a phenomenon predicted by Einstein in which the gravity of a massive object in space bends and magnifies the light emitted by objects behind it.

"Most supernovas this far away were found with help from a massive foreground galaxy cluster acting as a natural magnifying glass," Zhuang explained. "It's the most distant one we've actually seen appear."

This supernova was discovered as part of the NEXUS Treasury program, in which JWST intermittently scours patches of the sky for ancient explosions. It had previously observed the explosion's host galaxy over the past few years and finally witnessed the supernova on Aug. 7 of this year.

Intriguingly, the star had not yet exploded in JWST's previous viewing, on May 28, 2026, meaning that 71 days passed on our Earthly clocks, even though only about 12 days passed in the supernova's own time frame.

This highlights a mind-bending cosmological effect: The universe's expansion doesn't just stretch space; it slows down time from our perspective.

"Because the universe has expanded since this light was emitted, everything about this explosion appears to us in slow motion, about six times slower than it actually happened," Zhuang said. "Changes that took one week at the supernova take about six weeks to unfold from our point of view."

Mysteries still remain about the nature of this ancient explosion that shone so brilliantly we can observe it across nearly the entire universe.

Rather than a conventional core collapse supernova ‪—‬ in which a star between approximately eight and 50 times the mass of the sun exhaust its nuclear fuel and suffers a rapid core collapse and subsequent explosion ‪—‬ the recently discovered supernova may be one of the universe's rarest, most powerful explosions, the study authors said. Known as a superluminous supernova, this type of explosion releases tens or hundreds of times more energy than conventional supernovas. Whether the explosion continues to heat up or starts to cool could reveal exactly what is happening in its environment and what caused it, the team said.

"Our interpretation is still preliminary," Zhuang concluded. "Follow-up observations will tell us more about exactly what kind of explosion this was."

A federal jury in the District of Maryland convicted a laboratory owner yesterday for taking advantage of nursing homes that needed COVID-19 tests during the pandemic and fraudulently billing Medicare more than $18 million for other expensive respiratory tests that nursing home residents did not need, physicians did not order, and, in many cases, were never performed.

“Mr. Britton-Harr’s decision to exploit elderly nursing‑home residents and inexperienced interns for personal gain during a global pandemic is unconscionable. This verdict reinforces our message: there is nowhere to hide for those who commit fraud. We will find you and prosecute you to the fullest extent of the law,” said U.S. Attorney Kelly O. Hayes for the District of Maryland. “The U.S. Attorney’s Office, along with our law-enforcement partners, remains committed to rooting out fraud and holding accountable those who exploit our vulnerable victims and who steal our taxpayer money.”

“This verdict sends a clear message: the FBI will aggressively pursue anyone who steals from American taxpayers,” said FBI Baltimore Special Agent in Charge Jimmy Paul. “Patrick Britton-Harr will now face the consequences of his greed and brazen crimes. In addition to this healthcare fraud scheme, Britton-Harr was previously convicted for investment fraud in which the FBI uncovered a separate scheme where he defrauded private citizens. The FBI and our law enforcement partners will not rest until fraudsters like Mr. Britton Harr are brought to justice.”

According to court documents and evidence presented at trial, Patrick Britton-Harr, 42, of Annapolis, offered COVID-19 tests to nursing homes across the country in 2020 when these facilities were desperate for COVID-19 testing services. After specimens were collected from nursing home residents and other laboratories performed COVID-19 testing, Britton-Harr billed Medicare for separate, high-priced respiratory pathogen panel (RPP) tests purportedly conducted in a laboratory he owned in Arizona. Britton-Harr falsely claimed to Medicare that treating physicians ordered these extensive RPP tests, which screened for more than 30 respiratory pathogens. In reality, physicians never ordered the tests for their nursing home patients, and several physicians testified at trial they had never ordered an RPP test for any patient during the entirety of their medical careers.

Through his company, Provista Health, Britton-Harr fraudulently billed Medicare $18.7 million for RPP testing purportedly performed in just six months, of which Medicare paid more than $7.2 million. Evidence at trial demonstrated how Britton-Harr exploited inexperienced employees, including a family member and high school and college aged interns, by directing them to bill Medicare for an RPP test every time a COVID-19 test was performed. Britton-Harr further directed the billing of RPP tests supposedly performed at his Arizona laboratory after he had already closed the laboratory, barred staff from working in the laboratory, and no longer had the equipment necessary to run RPP tests. Additional evidence at trial proved that Britton-Harr billed Medicare for RPP tests that were never performed, including billing for tests for nursing home residents who were already deceased.

Britton-Harr used the proceeds of his fraud scheme to fund a lavish lifestyle, purchasing, among other things, a $112,500 Porsche 911, and making $25,000 monthly mortgage payments on his Annapolis waterfront mansion.

The jury convicted Britton-Harr of five counts of health care fraud and one count of transactional money laundering. Britton-Harr faces a maximum penalty of 10 years in prison for each count. A sentencing hearing will be scheduled at a later date. A federal district court judge will determine any sentence after considering the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines and other statutory factors.

Trial Attorney Chris Wenger of the Fraud Division’s Health Care Fraud Section and Assistant U.S. Attorney Ari Evans for the District of Maryland are prosecuting the case.

On April 7, the Department of Justice announced the creation of the Fraud Division. The Fraud Division is laser-focused on investigating and prosecuting those who commit fraud against the American people. The Department’s work to combat fraud supports President Trump’s Task Force to Eliminate Fraud, a whole-of-government effort chaired by Vice President J.D. Vance to eliminate fraud, waste, and abuse within Federal benefit programs.

The Department of Justice’s Health Care Fraud Strike Force Program, currently comprised of nine strike forces operating in federal districts across the country, has charged more than 6,200 defendants who collectively billed federal health care programs and private insurers more than $45 billion since 2007. In addition, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, working in conjunction with the Office of the Inspector General for the Department of Health and Human Services, are taking steps to hold providers accountable for their involvement in health care fraud schemes. More information can be found at www.justice.gov/criminal-fraud/health-care-fraud-unit.

#1373. Judge Orders Kanye West to Pay Nearly $200,000 in Tadao Ando Mansion Fiasco

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The saga surrounding West’s $60 million beach house drags on, with a court forcing him to give his ex-contractor $321,000.

On a beachfront stretch of Malibu, Pritzker Prize–winning Japanese architect Tadao Ando crafted what would turn out to be one of his most controversial projects: The Sachs House. Completed in 2013, the modernist Southern California residence stands on massive caissons and features a concrete shell typical of Ando’s Brutalist-inflected architecture, with a network of ocean-facing terraces linked via outdoor staircases.

In 2021, fresh off his divorce from Kim Kardashian, Kanye West (now legally named Ye) paid more than $57 million for the 4,000-square-foot house and began taking it down to the studs. He said he was “reimagining” the three-story structure but ultimately abandoned the project mid-demolition and sold it, thus leaving it uninhabitable for a new owner. Now, the property is once again making headlines, as renovations have stalled due to non-payment, and it’s facing foreclosure. West himself also suffered a string of legal defeats, with a judge ordering him to pay more than $321,000 in total, as of October 2026.

To untangle the saga, ELLE Decor goes back the beginning.

The story begins with financier Richard Sachs, a Bear Stearns alum and art-world fixture who once dated Ashley Olsen. In 2007 he enlisted Ando, along with Marmol Radziner’s Ron Radziner as executive architect, to design a house for him on an oceanfront parcel in Malibu. Construction kicked off in 2009 and wrapped up in 2013, requiring some 1,200 tons of poured concrete and 200 tons of steel. With its boxy silhouette, the minimalist four-bedroom house featured stainless-steel surfaces and floor-to-ceiling windows that framed views of the Pacific. Sachs listed the property in April 2020 for $75 million, per Zillow.

At the time, broker Branden Williams of Hilton & Hyland told The Real Deal that, “We were planning on listing the property in February… Then Covid hit a month later.” They opted to put the home on the market “before there was a rush of properties” for sale following pandemic restrictions.

West enters the picture in September 2021, seven months after Kardashian filed for divorce. West ended up paying $57.3 million for the Sachs House, nearly 24 percent off the asking price. He intended to “reimagine” the estate, per The New Yorker, calling Radziner back to the property in order to knock out the cabinetry and replace internal staircases with ramps. The architect demurred, leaving West and girlfriend Bianca Censori (who herself has a master’s degree in architecture) to find an alternative plan.

Instead, the same month that West bought the house, the couple enlisted contractor Tony Saxon to dismantle the interiors, apparently without a permit, for $20,000 per week. For a month and a half, Saxon (who slept on a mattress on the floor inside the barren home) led a team that used sledgehammers to shatter glass panes, marble walls, and concrete chimneys. West told him he wanted “no kitchen, bathrooms, A.C., windows, light fixtures, or heating.”

Even though Saxon delivered on gutting the house, a chasm developed in the relationship as West began turning his attention to the architectural enhancements and additions, including ramps that were not ADA accessible. On November 5, 2021, Saxon walked away from the project for good over an argument with West “about money, electricity, and Saxon’s apparent reluctance to take out the ocean-facing windows in the living room,” per The New Yorker.

Saxon filed suit against West in September 2023 for $1.7 million, alleging hazardous working conditions and failure to pay wages. He claimed he worked 16-hour days as a project manager, full-time security guard, and on-site caretaker, while maintaining that West ignored his complaints about the unsafe environment. He also claimed to have suffered a back injury as a result of working on the home.

In late 2023, West called Radziner once again to see if he would help to repair the structure, at the time lacking windows and fully exposed to the harsh coastal elements. Radziner gave him an estimate north of $10 million, per The New Yorker, and West never responded. Instead, he put the whole thing on the market for $53 million with Selling Sunset broker Jason Oppenheim. They slashed the price down 26 percent, to $39 million, the following April.

An entity called Belwood Investments, founded by Steven “Bo” Belmont, acquired the crumbling, vacant property for $21 million in September 2024. (That’s roughly 60 percent cheaper than West’s original asking price.) According to the Wall Street Journal, Belmont’s plan was to spend $8.5 million on restoring the estate with the goal of flipping it for upwards of $50 million. Radziner also joined the project.

The business model for Belwood relied on fractional investors that paid as little as $1,000 each for a piece of the renovation, with their money (plus a share of expected profits) being returned to them once the house sold. In total, Belmont collected approximately $7.5 million from nearly 400 people. He also secured an $18.5 million acquisition loan from a group linked to Mike Nijjar, a California landlord who later faced accusations from California Attorney General Rob Bonta of exploiting tenants.

Radziner stopped work due to non-payment in March 2025, and Belmont put the house on the market for $39 million, per Zillow. By the summer, a deal looked like it might materialize with Montana-based developer Andrew Mazzella for roughly $30 million. The sale, however, ended up falling through, and investors began worrying about the safety of their funding.

Shortly after, Belwood employee David Contreras sued the firm and contended it was running “nothing more than a Ponzi scheme.” The group defaulted on its acquisition loan in November 2025 and now faces foreclosure if Belmont cannot secure financing. He is alleged to have missed more than $800,000 in payments to his lender.

While Belmont’s financial situation deteriorated, Saxon and West headed to trial in Los Angeles over their own dispute earlier this year. After Saxon’s initial $1.7 million civil suit, West counter-filed with his own litigation, stating that Saxon publicized “an unlawful mechanic’s lien” on the Sachs House. In March, a jury ordered West—who fell asleep on the witness stand, according to jurors—to pay Saxon $140,000 for lost wages and medical bills.

As the home’s current owner, Belmont is now racing to secure funding to avoid foreclosure. “I am working tirelessly to find a win in this situation,” he told the Wall Street Journal in March. The outlet reports that a March 19 auction is set to take place in Pomona, California, if he fails to do so. As of press time, the house is listed on Zillow as “pre-foreclosure.”

With little fanfare, the structure traded hands on July 7 for $22.1 million, per Zillow. Its most recent asking price had been $12 million, down from the $39 million Belmont initially wanted.

On top of the $140,000 in damages from the March trial, Judge Brock Hammond ordered West to pay more than $181,000 to Saxon in October, per Rolling Stone. The new figure covers trial-related costs: $147,000 in attorneys’ fees, and $34,433.93 for Saxon’s legal team at West Coast Trial Lawyers. The firm originally sought a higher sum, while Hammond had ordered West to pay a lower fee. However, Saxon’s lawyer, Ron Zambrano, requested the judge revisit the ruling, which he did.

“I respect Judge Hammond’s openness to consider my argument why his initial assessment was perhaps missing a critical point. Though I believe the value of what we brought to get Mr. Saxon his verdict is above where Judge Hammond ultimately landed,” Zambrano told Rolling Stone.

The new figure brings the total West owes Saxon to more than $321,000. Saxon originally sought $1.7 million in damages.

#2853. Three Climbers Establish Giant Himalayan Route in Enviable On-the-Clock “Training”

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Dream jobs come in all shapes and sizes. Some might consider my job at Climbing dreamy. Others might hope to hangboard their way into professional climbing one day. My former colleague Duane Raleigh once made a very serious list of climber “dream jobs” that included roofers, fast food cooks, and snow shovelers.

I am here to tell you that none of these jobs are the best climber jobs. The actual best job for a climber, without any shadow of a doubt, is to be a member of the French military’s 27th Mountain Infantry Brigade. Why?

Allow me to describe the group’s latest work trip.

Last Wednesday, October 14, Sergeant Konrad Piontek and Corporals Amaury Fouillade and Clovis Paulin stood on the 6,654-meter summit of Peak 41 in Nepal. On the 1,400-meter North Face, they had just opened a stunning new route linking long sections of vertical ice with burly rock roofs and pitches of deep snow. They called the line Bon Matin (ED+ WI5+ M6 A1 90° snow), which translates to “Good Morning.” (Not sure what “ED+” means? Check out this article about international alpine grades.)

The three first ascensionists were part of a team of six from the 27th Mountain Infantry Brigade, better known as the Groupe Militaire de Haute Montagne, or GMHM. They had arrived in Nepal in mid-September along with a military doctor. After a period of acclimatization, the group split into two rope teams according to their skill level and how well each had adjusted to the altitude.

The Piontek-Fouillade-Paulin team eyed an attractive, unclimbed smear running plumb down Peak 41’s north side, to the left of a Korean team’s route from 2016. They began climbing on October 11 and summited after “four days of particularly committed climbing,” according to a statement shared with Climbing.

Over the 1,400 meters of the face, the team had to deal with numerous steep and poorly protected pitches, including sections of vertical snow. “We weren’t expecting pitches as steep as that, especially in snow,” said Corporal Paulin in the statement. But they also climbed what can only be described as truly classic mixed terrain: solid rock and squeaky ice, hanging above tons of exposure.

After summiting, the team descended the peak’s southeastern aspect via 15 rappels and significant downclimbing, turning their outing into one massive loop back to basecamp. “Reaching the summit was a real physical relief,” said Corporal Fouillade. “The mental release came later.”

Officially, France’s 10-person GMHM is tasked with “mastering extreme terrestrial environments, particularly vertical terrain and high altitude,” said a spokesperson to Climbing. This fluency in alpine terrain aims to contribute to the development of the entire French army’s mountain capabilities.

“The whole point of the GMHM is to practice high level alpinism,” said the group’s spokesperson. “What is learned regarding mountaineering or cold weather environments is passed on to the French mountain units, but the GMHM has otherwise no militaristic purposes.”

Similar mountain-military units exist across the globe, but—tragically—nothing so geared toward climbing excellence exists in North America. (If you happen to have a French passport and the pre-requisites, I’d be refreshing this recruitment page regularly.)

As far as we know, there is no fixed contract length for GMHM members; some alpinists have been part of the dream team for years and years.

In addition to one high-altitude expedition each year, the group travels to Greenland to teach the Groupement de Commandos de Montagne (literally: “mountain commandos”) about how to thrive in cold weather. They also take minor trips to develop rock and ice climbing skills, whether that’s locally in the French Alps or abroad to Yosemite. In plain terms, that means that 10 climbers get to occasionally don camo and earn a decent government salary to climb full time, around the world.

Did I mention the GMHM is the ultimate dream job?

Three Java Champions share how they use GitHub Copilot to solve problems, accelerate development, and keep humans responsible for the results.

AI coding tools can generate code quickly, but speed alone does not solve the hardest software engineering problems. Developers still need to provide context, validate results, protect maintainability, and remain accountable for what ships.

To learn how experienced developers strike that balance, we spoke with three Java Champions, all with over a decade of experience in using Java. They shared concrete examples of using GitHub Copilot in production and open source, along with the practices they use to turn AI-generated output into reliable software.

How has GitHub Copilot helped you recently?

I’ve been using it to analyze, design, and implement various features in both Hypersistence Utils and Hypersistence Optimizer.

For example, I’ve been wanting for a long time to develop a web application that provides Optimizer event history so we can see how performance improvement efforts are materializing in application performance. Before AI, I kept postponing this task because it would have taken me weeks to develop. But using Copilot is like having a new team member, so I used it to create an app that will ship in the next version of Hypersistence Optimizer. Instead of weeks, this task took just two days to implement.

What’s your typical usage? Are you using GitHub Copilot as an IDE plugin or the GitHub Copilot app?

I use GitHub Copilot in IntelliJ IDEA because it’s convenient to run while reviewing source code or during a debug session.

Can you share some tips and best practices?

The biggest thing I would recommend is: stop one-shot prompting.

For anything non-trivial, I want to understand the problem before execution starts. Quite often I explicitly ask the agent to interview me first. The purpose of that interview is to uncover assumptions and expectations that are still implicit.

A good agent can execute the wrong idea incredibly quickly. That does not make the detour cheaper.

I experienced this myself while working on my Spring Boot auto-configuration optimiser. Initially I wanted to support both Spring Boot 3.5 and 4.x. There were architectural differences, so we implemented both approaches. Later I realised I no longer needed the 3.5 path and removing it was painful.

The lesson was simple: detours are expensive. Get the requirements right before you accelerate.

Once the intent is clear, I want a plan. When I review that plan, four things matter especially to me: are assumptions visible, are verification gates defined, does the approach fit the architecture, and does it avoid unnecessary work?

The second principle is to define task-specific verification gates.

“Done” should not mean that the model says it is done.

For a refactoring, the gates might include passing tests, proving that all intended files were handled, updating documentation and leaving no TODOs behind. For the slide migration, the gates included screenshots, visual diffs, animations, PDF output and content completeness.

The gates depend on the task. The principle does not: the agent should be able to provide evidence that the work satisfies the definition of done.

I recently came across a skill called unlazy that follows a similar idea: create explicit gates, work through them deliberately, and prove that they have been completed rather than taking shortcuts.

The third principle is to turn your lessons into reusable skills.

I have started writing and maintaining my own Agent Skills. When I learn something from an execution—perhaps the agent misunderstood a convention, a useful check was missing, or I found a better way to approach a task—I update the skill.

The next time I perform that task, the lesson is already there.

One example is a skill I call maintainer-audit, inspired by work in the open agent skills community. I use it across my own repositories. It checks and, where appropriate, fixes the things I expect a maintained project to have: repository documentation, CI pipelines, Dependabot configuration, CodeQL, consistent cleanup and other project standards.

The goal is not to generate another audit report for me to read. It brings the repository back toward the baseline I want automatically. That makes my projects more consistent and easier to maintain.

I think of these skills as living engineering knowledge rather than static prompts.

The fourth principle is to put deterministic engineering tools around the probabilistic model.

I have my own quality harness that wraps tools such as Checkstyle, PMD and Spring Modulith architecture checks and produces a consolidated report that the agent can consume. I can run that from hooks and let the agent iterate:

change → execute checks → read findings → fix → execute again.

It continues until there are no findings left.

That does not mean I stop looking at the software. I still run the application, interact with it and decide whether it behaves and looks the way I expect. Sometimes I deliberately implement feature by feature because the feedback loop is much tighter.

I learned that asking for everything at once because “the AI can do it” is usually a bad idea. Large, generic specifications create large, generic implementations—and cleaning them up afterwards can be harder than building smaller increments correctly.

More broadly, I think we developers need to talk much more about how we use these tools. Share the experiments that worked. Share the ones that didn’t. Borrow ideas from each other and adapt them to your own context instead of looking for one universal AI development process.

That is also why I like the idea of AI as a sparring partner. On a Friday night, when I want to experiment with something and no human pair is available, I suddenly have somebody—or something—I can challenge an idea with, review an approach with, or use as a very sophisticated rubber duck.

But I still take those ideas back to real people. Human feedback, product understanding and real-world context remain essential.

How do you think AI is changing our profession?

I don’t think software engineering students should primarily aspire to become code-producing machines. They need to become strong problem solvers. They need to understand users and stakeholders, discover the real problem behind a requirement, challenge assumptions, understand products and markets, make architectural trade-offs, think critically, work in a structured way and transfer useful solutions from one context into another.

The model is extraordinarily good at generating possibilities and executing things. But as a human, I usually know much more of the actual context—including the things nobody ever documented.

So I want to remain in charge of shaping the solution and let the agent take over more of the execution.

In that sense, I think AI is putting the engineering back into software engineering.

It’s a perfect companion to evaluate pull requests and investigate bug reports.

One remarkable story is about the Pi4J library that lets you build Java applications to interact with electronic components on the Raspberry Pi. In February 2026, we released V4 which has a new plugin using the Foreign Function & Memory API. We tested it thoroughly before releasing, but still, shortly after the release someone reported that using 5 input buttons doesn’t work as expected. Pushing one of the first four configured buttons triggers a code execution as expected, while nothing happens with the fifth button.

This is the kind of report you don’t want to get. Nothing was visibly broken. No exception, no warning in the logs, the fifth button was simply silent. And it got worse: with the older gpiod plugin, the exact same hardware and the exact same application, worked fine. So the problem was in our new FFM code, which sits on top of the Linux kernel’s GPIO character device and talks to it through native calls. That is not the part of the codebase where you expect to find a quick answer.

So I assigned the issue to the Copilot coding agent and gave it two file paths as a starting point, the provider class and FFMDigitalInput. About half an hour later there was a pull request, with an explanation I really did not expect.

Each input runs an event watcher that blocks in a native poll() call, waiting for the kernel to report a change on its GPIO line. Those watchers ran in virtual threads. And a virtual thread that blocks inside a native call cannot be unmounted from its carrier thread. It stays pinned there for the whole duration of the call. A Raspberry Pi 4 has four cores, so the virtual thread scheduler has four carrier threads. Register four inputs, and all four carriers are parked in poll() forever. The fifth watcher is created, but is never scheduled. That is exactly the bug that was reported. Not “events are flaky”, but “the fifth one does nothing.”

I thought the goal of virtual threads was exactly to step away from the platform thread limit? So how is this a problem that four virtual threads block the four threads on a four-core processor like the Raspberry Pi?

Check the pull request for the full conversation. Copilot was able to link the use of the Raspberry Pi which has four CPU cores with the reported problem. It was able to propose a simple fix for the problem, plus some extra improvements it found during its investigation.

When most people plan their kitchen layout, they often consider proximity to key appliances and storage; however, Seth Ballard of Ballard + Menusa Architecture thinks in terms of “butts.” Really. “Everyone with a small kitchen wants more space because everyone ends up in the kitchen,” he says. “People get in each other's way, can't function comfortably, and become stressed, not to mention tripping over each other or bumping butts.”

Cheekiness aside, having more butts in kitchens marks a significant shift in how these culinary spaces are used today. Grace Rosenstein of Atelier Roan says kitchens were once a hidden space for work—and, in the Downton Abbey days, staff—but are now “truly the heart of the home.”

“It has become both a functional hub and a major focal point of the home,” she explains. “Today, it’s where families naturally gather: Not just for meals, but for daily connection, conversation, and time together.”

More space means people can gather and join in on the meal prep.

“Food is a group sport, whether sharing a glass of wine with a friend or just enjoying a glass of wine while your friend cooks,” says Laura Jenkins, an Atlanta-based designer and House Beautiful Next Wave alum. “If there is truly only one good prep zone, a kitchen really can only accommodate one person at a time.”

When space allows, Ballard tries to design spaces so the entire household can move freely within the kitchen at once.

“A successful kitchen needs to let multiple people cook, prep, unload the dishwasher, grab a snack, or sit nearby without constantly getting in each other's way,” he says.

Here, designers share a few things to consider:

Thoughtful placement of appliances is paramount, but Ballard wants households to retire the traditional work triangle. “It doesn’t really account for how families use kitchens today,” he says.

Instead, he recommends creating intentional, well-defined zones. “Prep and cooking zones should be close together, while cleanup areas should be near where dishes are used,” Ballard recommends. “We also use beverage refrigerators, properly located prep sinks, ovens positioned away from the cooktop, and secondary cleanup areas.”

While it’s important to have designated spots to prep, cook, and clean, Jenkins encourages you to think outside the box. If you have room to spare, add a coffee station or wet bar so guests can pour a stiff one without distracting the crowd.

But remember: People are seldom stagnant in a kitchen, so it’s important to choreograph the entire cooking process. “Be mindful of creating zones that cross each other's paths when cooking,” she says. “A back-to-back prep zone placed in the path to the range is probably going to create a bottleneck.”

Rosenstein agrees and also encourages you to think about how people “gather, move, and spend time together” beyond meals. “Give meaningful thought to how you actually live,” she suggests.

Many of her clients want to be able to see “the game” from their kitchens, so Rosenstein often creates cased openings into nearby TV rooms, adds speakers, or positions bar stools so people can gather without interrupting the flow.

“It might be clichéd, but an island is a perfect way to create a multi-person kitchen,” Jenkins says. Nowadays, however, there’s no one way to incorporate this culinary staple. Jenkins used a baker’s cart as a space-efficient island, while Rosenstein says her clients “feel strongly” about incorporating a distinct-yet-casual dining space.

“A separate breakfast table or sitting area gives the kitchen another layer,” she says. “It creates a place to linger over coffee, have a casual meal, or sit with family and friends without everything happening at the island.”

If you don’t have room for an expansive island, Ballard says a U- or L-shaped layout works just as well.

For Ballard, an open-concept floor plan can be a small space dweller’s secret weapon. “Opening up a typical small-house kitchen to the dining area or family room can make the space feel bigger,” he explains. “More importantly, it allows guests and helpers to work in the dining room without getting in the cook's way.”

Or, if you’re living in a rental apartment, add a beverage refrigerator just outside of your kitchen. “It separates people coming and going for sodas or beers from the person cooking, so they aren't constantly bumping into the chef,” he shares.

The pursuit of adding more butts to a small kitchen means something has to give and, for Rosenstein, it’s cookware that no longer serves you. “It becomes much more about quality over quantity,” she shares. “You don’t need ten pans; you need the right pans.”

Rosenstein says paring down your kitchenware can help you reclaim meaningful square footage for circulation, prep space, and, ultimately, more people in the kitchen.

#3048. Seven Alpine-Style Climbs Happening Now in Nepal

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Several small teams are attempting difficult climbs on Nepal’s 6,000 and 7,000m peaks. Unfortunately, they rarely report on their plans or progress until they return, and often only if they succeed.

This is what we know about the ongoing expeditions:

Three young French climbers, members of the Military High Mountain Group from Chamonix, celebrated their organization’s 50th anniversary with a new route on Peak 41. This 6,654m mountain is located in the Khumbu region and is often called the “true Mera Peak.”

Konrad Piontek, Amaury Fouillade, and Clovis Paulin took advantage of a good weather window and achieved the first ascent of the peak’s northeast face. They climbed in an alpine-style single push over four days. The result is a beautiful 1,400m line with difficulties up to ED+ 5+ M6 A1 90°. They descended via the southeast face. The team named their line Bon Matin (good morning).

Nivel Meroi and Romano Benet are acclimatized and ready to attempt a new route on Jugal II, but the Italian couple will be on their own. Tine Cuder of Slovenia, the third team member, has left due to illness.

Meanwhile, in the Kangchenjunga region, a large Russian team has also finished acclimatizing. The team trekked in the area and crossed a number of high-altitude passes, the latest of them at the top of an icefall at 5,750m. They reported lots of snow above 5,000m but are ready to attempt a new route on the formidable Jannu (7,710m) from its southeast side.

The team comprises Ivan Artemov, Ivan Volkov, Artem Zhdanov, Konstantin Neumoin, Roman Shilin, and Vasily Yagodin.

Hidesuke Taneishi and Tetsu Hashimoto of Japan traveled to Nepal in September, aiming to achieve the first ascent of Gangapurna West (7,455m), also known as Asapurna or Peak Lachenal. They have provided no news since, but they probably had to retreat when an intense snowstorm hit the Annapurna region three weeks ago.

Location of Gangapurna West on a Google Earth screenshot. Notes by Lindsay Griffin for the American Alpine Journal.

Meanwhile, a second team has reached the base of the peak with the same objective. Valery Shamalo, Natalia Zanina, Maksim Trufanov, and Ruslan Kirichenko arrived at the beginning of October and saw no one else there. They reported that recent precipitation had left the trail in a poor state; the climbers had to cross landslides and swollen rivers to reach base camp.

The team has completed two acclimatization climbs, looking for a passage through the icefall at the base of the mountain and then a series of couloirs. They reached 5,900m and, on a second trip, 6,600m. Deep snow covered the peak, which slowed progress. Finally, they launched a summit bid last week.

From 6,600m, they spent a day on technical ice sections of 70–80º. In poor visibility, they endured spindrift and were showered by small ice chunks.

“In the afternoon, the weather deteriorated sharply,” Natalia Zanina said. “Heavy snowfall began, and we started being struck by large, continuous avalanches. At 6,800m (340m from the summit), we decided to turn back.”

The journalists I spoke with paint a picture of an emerging craft, rather than a settled method. Here’s some of what I found.

On one hand, you have the traditional investigative journalists digging through large datasets from major leaks, who approach the material with the classic reporting toolkit, corroboration, interviews, contextualizing the data…On the other hand, there’s this newer, more technical niche where people work with constantly emerging, often smaller-scale data exposures, like scraping dark web dumps, pastebins, or unsecured cloud buckets, and turning those into fast-paced stories about breaches, vulnerabilities, and digital privacy. It’s less about deep-dive investigation and more about monitoring and exposing ongoing data exposures in real time.

Hackers themselves are becoming increasingly strategic about their relationships with the press. One reporter following a specific cyber beat emphasized this:

“I’d say that [hackers] of all kinds love to flex and get credit. A lot of them don’t, but many absolutely do. I’m not sure if the desire to flex has increased, but you’ve definitely seen the maturity of some hackers in how they approach the media. It’s not just about posting things to Twitter like it might have been before.

That maturity means developing awareness of how a story will be framed and disseminated and calculating which journalist to approach. Hackers now may select their outlets of choice, matching a leak to the reporter who they think is capable of understanding the story and carrying it further.

There are different kinds of hackers, wearing different and/or multiple hats. Interviewees drew distinctions between financially motivated cybercriminals, whose leaks are often equivalent to extortion by other means, and state-affiliated actors, who may also pose as independent hacktivists to launder politically motivated leaks into credible coverage.

At the same time, some hackers or hacker collectives — like Phineas Fisher, known for his activist remit against surveillance companies — may act for political and public interest–oriented reasons, reaching out to journalists to expose wrongdoing, unethical issues, or crimes. Notoriously, though, hacking is not a field in which hats are worn straightforwardly: Identities, definitions and agendas, including mere trolling, may blur into one another.

Hacked material needs to be handled with a great deal of skepticism. When working with hacked sources, one journalist said, it’s a matter of “treating every dataset as if it has chlamydia.” But the verification techniques the journalists said they use — such as confirming that an email address exists in a company’s records, cross-referencing with known facts, or examining a file for signs of tampering — are fairly conventional. This type of reporting requires more disclosures, especially when a source’s identity can’t be established. One journalist working in this area said:

I think you should be very transparent about that in your reporting. You should say something like, “We don’t know who the source is; it could be the GRU [Russian Federation] or whoever, but we have confirmed that this data is real and this is what it shows.”

Declared openly, the uncertainty may become a meaningful part of the story itself.

Source protection is further complicated when it comes to hackers. Some interviewees extend the full protection they would give any source to hackers, refusing to assist law enforcement even if the source is a self-declared criminal. Others prefer to know as little as possible about their sources, framing the choice as harm reduction for both parties. As one journalist in my sample put it:

The less I know about these things, the harder it’s going to be for me to burn my source or to publish something that inadvertently turns out to be harmful to my source.

Reporters working exclusively with material already circulating online in cybercrime forums or other repositories on the web treat source protection as beside the point, because the origins of the information cannot be traced and because the material is in effect “out there.”

Beneath all of this sits the finding with the widest implications: Ethical decisions in this domain resist strict codification. No general rule can be written in advance, the reporters I spoke with suggested, and judgment in the individual case is the only workable guide. Is publishing hacked material justified? It depends on the specifics of the case at hand, one reporter told me.

I kind of think it all depends on what you’re exposing. Maybe it’s generally wrong to break into people’s computers and steal data, but if you’re exposing serious crimes, then I think it can be justified.

Interviewees described practical wisdom under pressure: the ability to read a specific situation and act appropriately n it, without having the opportunity to refer to an established code or checklist.

Newsrooms, meanwhile, may need to develop verification standards suited to information of illicit origin, transparency norms that treat uncertainty as something to disclose rather than paper over, and a shared language for the ethical judgments that journalists currently make case by case on their own.

Experts argue that the mysterious Voynich manuscript was incorrectly bound — a discovery that "moves the ball" in finally deciphering the 15th-century document.

The mysterious and undeciphered Voynich manuscript has caused consternation among code-breakers, linguists and scholars of medieval literature for more than a century. But now experts think they've worked out why it's so hard to crack: It was bound incorrectly in the 15th century, meaning the pages are out of order. The new linguistic analysis of the document has moved researchers closer to figuring out what the text was all about.

The Voynich manuscript is a medieval codex made up of leaves of parchment carbon-dated to the early 15th century. Its 240 pages are covered with about 38,000 words of a unique script called "Voynichese" along with illustrations of people, plants and astrological symbols. Also known as the Beinecke Cipher manuscript, the document was initially owned by a Holy Roman emperor and was purchased by Wilfrid Voynich in 1912. Since 1969, the manuscript has been in the collection of Yale University's rare book library. Experts have unsuccessfully tried to decipher the manuscript for more than a century.

"We don't know if it's a code," Lisa Fagin Davis, study co-author and executive director of the Medieval Academy of America, told Live Science. "But there are some odd characteristics of the Voynich manuscript in terms of its physical structure."

When doing research on the number of scribes who copied the document, Davis realized from the handwriting that the manuscript's pages alternate in ways that don't make sense. In addition, she said, "there are several examples where the image goes off the page in the middle and comes out on the other side, even though there are a bunch of pages in between."

In a study published Monday (Oct. 19) in the journal Digital Medievalist, Davis and co-author Colin Layfield, an information systems researcher at the University of Malta, suggested that the inconsistent structure of the enigmatic manuscript is the result of someone mis-binding a manuscript they were unable to read.

An illustration of how the Voynich manuscript may have been bound incorrectly.

Medieval manuscripts were made up of rectangular sheets of parchment (animal skin), typically folded in half to produce what experts call a bifolium. Around five bifolia would typically be nested together, creating what experts call a quire. Then, a stack of quires would be bound together along the spine to create a book. However, Davis and Layfield suggested that the Voynich manuscript was intended to be a stack of singulions — a series of loose bifolia never meant to be bound together as a quire or a book.

This is not the first time a researcher has suggested the Voynich manuscript has pages out of order. But Davis and Layfield analyzed the graphic properties of the untranslated document to see if their hypothesis made sense.

"We're viewing it as a text and assuming it has a message," Layfield told Live Science, which means it should be possible to statistically compare blocks of text to see if they're similar or disconnected. Layfield used a technique called latent semantic analysis (LSA), which is a natural language processing technique invented in the late 1980s to find hidden patterns in texts.

LSA is particularly useful for small sets of data, Layfield explained. "Modern AI techniques need ridiculous amounts of data to train on, which we just don't have. We've got just 35,000 or so words. That's it." The researchers' LSA analysis of the Voynich manuscript revealed that some pages had low similarity, suggesting they may not have gone together, while others had high similarity, bolstering the idea that they were supposed to be read together.

By looking at the Voynich manuscript as a collection of singulions, Davis and Layfield solved some of the nagging issues with the document.

"When you start to think about it in the context of the other strange features of the manuscript, all of those problems are resolved," Davis said. "It solves the problem of each scribe writing one sheet. It solves the problem of the images crossing the gutter."

This means that any previous work on the Voynich manuscript that assumes it was ordered correctly now needs to be reconsidered, she said, but it also means there is new progress on the book.

"This work helps us think about whether there's actual meaning underneath it all," Davis said. "It doesn't get us there," she said about a definitive translation, "but it moves the ball because you can't make sense out of a book where all the pages are mixed up."

Davis is optimistic that someone will crack the Voynich mystery, in part because a similar modern puzzle known as Cain's Jawbone has been successfully deciphered by a handful of people. This devilishly difficult linguistic puzzle from the 1930s consists of 100 pages of a murder mystery arranged in the wrong order. Due to the unique nature of the text and its vague, stylized language, even AI has failed to solve it.

"We have some ideas for other work we want to do," Davis said. For example, there's a large water stain in the upper margin of some of the pages of the Voynich manuscript. "The stain should be symmetrical from one page to the next but it's not. That also supports the idea that the manuscript is bound out of order."

Davis and Layfield are thinking about ways to compare the stain shapes across the pages, as that may help them reorder the incorrectly organized pages.

Canned beans, tomatoes, and soup move through plenty of kitchens at a steady clip, and the empties usually get a quick rinse before they go out with the recycling. At that point, old tin cans still have plenty of life left. They’re stiff, hold soil and water without leaking, and cost nothing after being discarded.

Cans make for one of the easiest garden recycling projects, and the basic projects need nothing fancier than a hammer, a nail, and a can opener. One can might hold a pepper seedling in March, then lose its bottom at transplant time and slip right back over that same plant as a cutworm collar. That’s a lot of mileage out of something headed for the curb.

I start my stash in fall. Soup and chili season brings in a steady supply, and a box of clean cans in the shed will be ready when seed starting rolls around in late winter. Some can go to work right away, too – as lanterns on chilly evenings or as a winter home for hollow stems cut back during fall cleanup.

Clean cans first. Hot, soapy water and a bottle brush get the food out, and a soak loosens the label and glue. The edges are the real hazard. If you’ve got a smooth-edge can opener, like this Oxo Good Grips one on Amazon, use it, since it cuts through the side seam and leaves a dull lip. Regular openers leave a sharp ring. Pinch any jagged bits flat with pliers or take a file to them, and wear gloves for this part.

Won’t they rust? They will, eventually. On most cans, the “tin” is only a thin coating over steel, and rain and wet soil wear through it over time. That takes a while, and a can with a few orange spots still holds soil and water just fine for seasonal jobs. I paint the outside of any can that’ll sit out for a long stretch, and punch drainage holes so water doesn’t sit in the bottom. A can too far gone can usually still go in with the metal recycling.

Without further ado, in this latest installment of our Dirt Cheap Gardening series, here are my eight favorite ways to upcycle tin cans.

Thin plastic cell packs can split or tip over, but a tin can is heavier and less likely to get knocked around. You can use cans to start seeds or root cuttings. Turn one upside down on a scrap board and tap a nail through the bottom in several spots to make drainage holes – a basic 16-ounce claw hammer, like this Irwin one from Amazon, is plenty for the job.

Bare hands make a mess of granular fertilizer, bone meal, and compost, and a clean can scoops them out without bending or cracking the way a plastic cup might. Make it do double duty. Thyme, chives, and oregano stay compact enough to grow in a can on a sunny windowsill or balcony rail. Get the drainage holes in first, then paint the outside. Skip painting the inside, and let the paint cure before you plant. This time of year, aim for a south- or west-facing window. In summer, a can on a hot patio can dry out quickly.

Punched-tin lanterns throw speckled light across a patio table, and they’re a nice touch now that it gets dark earlier. Freeze a can full of water first. The ice keeps the walls from denting while you tap a nail through them in dots, swirls or stars. Once it thaws, dry it and paint it if you like, then drop in a battery-operated LED tea light, which you can get from Walmart in a pack of 12. An LED carries far less fire risk than a candle if wind tips a lantern into dry leaves. Thread wire through two holes near the rim to hang it.

Many native bees are solitary and nest alone in narrow tunnels rather than hives. A tall can filled with hollow stems gives them just that. Fall cleanup supplies the material. Bamboo, reeds, and the hollow or pithy stems of sunflowers, sumac, or raspberries all work in mixed widths. The metal stands up to rain better than paper labels, and you’re giving a useful second life to something that might otherwise go in the recycling. Be careful handling the edges, which can be sharp.

Parker argues that Khan’s emphasis on community arts, rather than the professional creative potential of ethnic minorities, unintentionally perpetuated a “patrician, paternalistic, quasi-colonial view” that lingers today. Though representation is better than it was, between 2010 and 2020 less than 3% of funding for regularly funded (National Portfolio) organisations went to Black, Asian or minority-led groups: “funding decisions are still largely made by individuals unfamiliar with the communities they are assessing”.

The same can be said of disabled people, though Jo Verrent, the director of the disabled artists commissioning organisation Unlimited, points out that 25% of the British population has a disability of some kind: “the arts sector has systematically excluded disabled artists” and she is “exhausted”.

While Nigel Higgins, the chairman of Barclays, makes a coherent economic case of funding the arts, “leaving the poetry to others”, the essays conclude with ACE’s chief executive Darren Henley, in post since 2015, giving another account of his tours around the country. His title is “Excellence. For Everybody. Everywhere.” ACE’s new mantra. He uses the term regularly, but nowhere does he dare define what excellence is.
