#1630. Chanel's New High Jewelry Collection Takes Its Cues From Coco's Paris Apartment

📅 Oct 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

How volumes on a bookshelf, botanicals in a Riviera villa, and the mosaics of a French abbey find themselves in a necklace.

“There are things we live among,” the Pulitzer Prize–winning American poet George Oppen wrote, “and to see them is to know ourselves.”

Oppen, a man who organized for workers’ rights and was investigated by the House Un-American Activities Committee, might be a surprising reference in a story about high jewelry, but stay with us. For there are things Gabrielle “Coco” Chanel lived among, and to see them is to know her jewelry. The first Chanel High Jewelry collection, presented in Paris in 1932 and the only one Coco designed herself, featured a series of diamond comet and star pieces. Those designs were direct translations of the comets she witnessed streaking across the Aubazine night sky and of the stars carved into the mosaics of the abbey she was raised in.

Almost a century later those motifs remain, joined in Chanel’s 2026 High Jewelry collection by the lions of her birth sign (the beasts adorned multiple furnishings in her Rue Cambon apartment) and by the camellias inspired by Alexandre Dumas’s 1848 novel La Dame aux Camélias, one of the many volumes that lined her bookshelves.

If there were any doubt of the impact meaningful objects have on the design of Chanel jewelry, the name of the latest collection makes the case irrefutable: Signes & Symboles, an 85-piece array that includes turquoise lion rings, diamond and onyx star cuffs, and a pink sapphire camellia. Necklaces featuring a new lozenge shape called Talisman often combine all three. The Talisman Graphique necklace bears amulets pavé set with yellow sapphires and engraved stars punctuated by a camellia flower set with a 3.57-carat -emerald-cut rare diamond. The Talisman de Symboles necklace is on a tubular chain with hanging plaques bearing a camellia, a star, a sun, and a lion in pink gold and pink sapphires.

But there’s more here, notably a presence of color not often found in Chanel High Jewelry. The use of stones like onyx, carnelian, turquoise, chrysoprase, and spessartite garnets is a marked departure from past patterns dominated by white diamonds. And since we’re in the Chanel universe, where everything is a symbol—where, as Coco herself put it, “every jewel represents an idea”—what could we make of these stones of color and mystery? The answer, it turned out, was coming from inside the house.

Signes & Symboles was presented to press and clients earlier this year at La Pausa, the 10-acre Mediterranean estate in Roquebrune-Cap-Martin that Coco Chanel purchased in 1928 and was bought back a decade ago by the company, which recently commissioned a restoration by ELLE Decor A-List architect Peter Marino.

The villa, which now hosts select company events as well as artist residencies, is surrounded by gardens that include jasmine-laced stone walls, fields of lavender and iris, and 250 ancient olive trees. Gazing up at the villa’s shutters, painted a custom blue to match the original color, and then looking at a necklace of emerald beads fastened with a turquoise clasp, makes the signs and symbols of inspiration perfectly clear.

#3551. These Are the Three Accessories You Need for a New Bike—And the One You Don’t

📅 Oct 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The right accessories can make your first rides safer, smoother, and more fun.

Gear-obsessed editors choose every product we review. We may earn commission if you buy from a link. How we test gear.

During my time working in bike shops, I have sold hundreds, if not over a thousand, bikes, and significantly more accessories. I’ve seen some people buy every accessory under the sun while they get their new bike, and others opt out of any accessories other than a bottle cage. A bottle cage is a good start, but there are a few other accessories to make sure you have the best possible first ride.

New bike day is a big day. The price of a new bike from a name brand manufacturer can be a big expense, so it’s understandable to hesitate when having to buy more. However, I’ve seen too many bikes become a clothes rack in a basement because someone didn’t have the experience they were hoping for on those first crucial rides. Getting the right gear from day one can change those initial impressions from negative to positive, but be aware you don’t need to buy all the things at once.

Here we’ll talk about the three accessories I recommend on day one, the one accessory I’ve steered a lot of riders away from when getting a new bike, and a few honorable mentions to consider as you ride more. Whether you’re a seasoned rider or just looking for tips on your first bike purchase, this story is for you.

Assuming you’re buying a bike that comes with pedals, helmets have always been the first piece of gear I suggest to riders buying a new bike. While helmets are not foolproof, they are a significant line of defense in keeping you safe as a rider. I’ve personally broken five helmets over the years, one of those times the doctor told me it likely saved my life. Keep in mind you don’t need to spend hundreds of dollars on a great helmet. When considering a new helmet, Virginia Tech Helmet Rating is the go-to resource on helmet safety testing.

Tara Seplavy, deputy editor at Bicycling, recently published a story on some of the Bicycling team’s favorite picks from the top-rated Virginia Tech helmets. One of the highest value helmets from the top ten on the Virginia Tech list is the P2R A20 Mips. This helmet comes in at only $60. “With its excellent safety rating, great adjustability, and budget-friendly price, the A20 Mips outperforms many helmets that cost three, four, or even five times its price,” says Seplavy.

If you’re buying your fifth bike and think you don’t need a new helmet, consider this—most of the major helmet manufacturers recommend replacing a helmet every three to five years. This is due to things like sun exposure, normal use, and even repetitive small impacts over time. For riders looking for a higher level of performance out of a budget helmet, then consider the aero helmet from P2R. It’s priced at $100 and as our resident tester Tom Messina said, “it has no business being this good.”

I know, I know—a flat kit consists of multiple accessories. Typically my basic flat kit for people getting a new bike consists of: some type of inflation, tire levers, a multitool, the correct tube for the new bike, and something to carry it in. Some brands sell a flat pack that consists of all the above. If your new bike has internal storage and you plan on putting your flat kit in there, just be sure to make sure it all fits before your first ride.

Even for riders that have multiple bikes I suggest a new flat kit. Bikes have different requirements for tube sizes and valve types and lengths for different rims. There’s nothing worse than getting a flat and taking everything apart just to realize you have the wrong size tube or your valve isn’t long enough (let alone if your spare tube has a Schrader valve, but you need a presta).

If you have multiple bikes keeping a flat kit on each one also makes sense because when you’re taking a bike out you don’t have to switch around the flat kit. This means you’re less likely to forget something, and it takes away a hurdle from you getting out the door.

There are plenty of options to go with when considering inflation. I typically carry one to two CO2 cartridges and a CO2 inflator in a flat pack or frame storage because they take up such little room and can (sometimes) reset a bead on a tubeless tire. I also carry a frame-mounted hand pump on longer rides, because I’ve been stranded after running out of CO2 in the past. Mini electric inflators have been gaining steam in the past few years, and the convenience of them is hard to pass up.

A lot of bikes these days have more than one place to put a bottle cage, so if your new bike allows I suggest going with two bottle cages. Current full-suspension mountain bikes typically only fit one (I’m not a fan of a bottle on the down tube of a mountain bike), so that’s an exception to the suggestion.

I typically suggest recommending bottle cages to people getting a new bike over something like a hydration pack. The most impactful reason is because when you use a hydration pack it puts more weight on your sit bones when you ride. Water is one of the heaviest things you’ll carry on the bike, but you need it as you ride. Since you have to bring it, put that weight on the bike instead of having the weight sit on your sit bones.

There are two honorable mentions worth talking about—one has to do with safety and the other has to do with comfort. The first is a taillight. I didn’t mention it above because it’s less of a necessity if you’re strictly trail riding off road.

One of the things I would always hear about in the shop was, “but I’m not riding at night so I don’t need a tail light.” Daytime running lights have that name for a reason—you run them day or night. These lights help increase visibility of cyclists as we share the road with vehicles. Drivers can more easily spot riders thanks to varying strobe patterns of the light and some even have radar and cameras built in like the Garmin Varia series of lights. The radar in the Varia detects cars coming from behind and gives you a heads up on your phone, watch, or head unit. I couldn’t imagine riding on the road without a radar taillight at this point.

The second honorable mention is a pair of cycling shorts—specifically bike shorts that have a built-in chamois pad. These shorts help improve comfort like few other things when riding a bike. It took me years to convince my wife to try them, but now she won’t ride without a pair. I’ve tried a lot of bike shorts and I’ve learned that the bib style stay in place better throughout a ride compared to the standard shorts.

Another essential piece of advice when picking the right chamois is you get what you pay for. I’ve tried a $20 Amazon pair and I’m currently testing the Dottore Clima Bib Shorts from Q36.5. The Q36.5 bibs are by far the most comfortable bike shorts I have ever tried whether I’m doing shorter lunch rides or doing 100 mile gravel days. The flip side is they are also by far the most expensive pair of bike shorts I’ve ever tried, but there is a significant level of comfort no matter how many hours I’m in the saddle.

At this point it is safe to say that many of us have a smartphone. There are always exceptions, but throughout my time working in a shop I’ve come across a small handful of folks still using a flip phone. In addition to smartphones becoming an ingrained part of society, smartwatches are seen more and more on people’s wrists. If you have either of those things you don’t need a bike computer—especially to get into riding.

Bike computers are nice to have for training purposes and navigating a new area or route. But most of those things can be done on a phone or smart watch these days. I have used watches from Apple, COROS, and Garmin for tracking rides, navigation, and as a guide for training on a ride with success. If you’re a new rider and have a smartphone I typically suggest downloading Strava because it’s easy to track rides, the navigation tool is fairly user-friendly, and it’s easy to see your progress the more you ride.

Don’t get me wrong—I love my bike computer. It pools data from my heart-rate sensor, CORE body temperature sensor, cadence and power from my power meters, and even my sweat data through my Nix sensor. I can see every metric I can think of and most head units allow you to customize the layout on the screen. But the reality is that a worthwhile head unit is typically a few hundred dollars. Conversely the phone mount that I typically suggest costs less than $30 and is fairly universal. Bike computers make sense for some riders, but don’t start here if you’re prioritizing certain accessories over others for your new bike.

#3293. Weekend Warm-Up: Sending Ivindo Falls

📅 Oct 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Gabon Uncharted: Sending Ivindo Falls follows a crew of young kayakers, as they attempt to paddle 150km down the Ivindo River’s many rapids, gorges, and waterfalls.

The four kayakers are what the film’s narrator calls the “top of the crop.” After taking a moment to admire this charming malaphor (I think our narrator has combined “cream of the crop” and “top of the class”) we meet the Send team. Leader Adrian Mattern of Germany spent years planning their trip to Gabon, bringing with him the talkative Dane Jackson, patient Kalob Grady, and Bren Orton. Orton signed on with the shiny-faced, inevitably short-lived optimism of a youngster in 1914 headed for the front, sure of being home by Christmas.

“Apparently,” the narrator notes, “no one told him what he was getting into on this trip.”

Once on the ground, they load up their supplies and set out on a long stretch of flat water. Struggling along behind, we meet the film crew, an interesting behind-the-scenes look at the process of filming a professional, well-funded adventure documentary. Filmmaker Olaf Obsommer, photographer David Sodomka, and narrator David Arnaud do everything the Send team does, backward and in heels. Or, no — I’m thinking of Ginger Rogers.

Obsommer was part of the first team to scout the river by kayak in 2007. They portaged past most of the rapids for safety. Nowadays, better kayaks and the ability to scout ahead by drone will, hopefully, let them complete what he started.

At the first of four whitewater sections, Kongou, they settle in to take the rocky labyrinth piece by piece. If they take the wrong branch, they’ll be heading over a 25m waterfall.

Adrian is entranced by Kongou Falls, and only when the team delicately sit him down and explain that he would very likely drown, which would be awkward, does he agree to move on. Photo: Screenshot

They work their way down the river looking for rapids that will not kill them immediately. On the way, they get advice from Juste Parfait Zangonga, a local expert, on how to not be killed by elephants, gorillas, crocodiles, vipers, or hippos.

The next rapids, Mingouli, are less maze, more rollercoaster. The Send team can finally start living up to their name. Between first descents, they paddle past a family of elephants and get devoured by insects.

First descent down a tricky section of Mingouli. Photo: Screenshot

Kouata Mango, section three, is where Mattern finally gets to send a massive waterfall. The team agrees that this one is less likely to kill him badly, and over the side he goes.

Tsengue Lelendi, the final set of rapids, rounds out the trip. The laconic Kaleb takes a look at one particularly rough section and tells our narrator that the rapid is unrunnable. The rest of the team spends a long time debating it, anyway. Lo and behold, he’s right. After scouting with drones, mapping every angle, and discussing the route at length, they agree to skip a section of the rapid. The chain will still have just a few missing links left.

“A part of this river will remain untouched,” Arnaud concludes, “as it should, probably.”

Place a rack in middle of oven; preheat to 325°. Lightly coat a 9x9" pan with nonstick vegetable oil spray. Line pan with parchment paper, leaving overhang on two sides. Toast 2 cups blanched hazelnuts on a rimmed baking sheet, tossing halfway through, until golden brown and fragrant, 15–18 minutes. Let sit until cool enough to handle, then coarsely chop; set aside.

Cook 1 cup (2 sticks) unsalted butter in a small saucepan over medium heat, stirring often, until butter foams, then browns (do not burn), 7–9 minutes. Remove from heat.

Whisk 2 large eggs, room temperature, 1 large egg yolk, room temperature, 1¼ cups (250 g) granulated sugar, ¼ cup (packed; 50 g) light brown sugar, 2 tsp. vanilla bean paste or vanilla extract, 1½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¾ tsp. Morton kosher salt, and ¾ tsp. instant espresso powder (if using) in the bowl of a stand mixer fitted with the whisk attachment on medium-high speed until mixture is pale and doubled in volume, 6–9 minutes (mixture will form an unbroken ribbon when whisk is lifted from bowl).

Reduce speed to low and with motor running, gradually pour in brown butter in a thin, continuous stream; beat until incorporated, about 2 minutes. Remove bowl from mixer and gently fold in 1½ cups (187 g) all-purpose flour with a rubber spatula, scraping down sides and bottom of bowl, until no large, dry streaks of flour remain. Scrape batter into prepared pan; smooth surface. Top with reserved hazelnuts and press in gently to adhere (surface will be smothered with nuts).

Bake blondie until edges are golden brown and center is just set, 35–45 minutes. Transfer pan to a wire rack and let blondie cool in pan. Using parchment paper to help, lift blondie out of pan and transfer to rack.

Meanwhile, combine 3 large egg whites and 1 cup (200 g) granulated sugar in the clean bowl of stand mixer and set over a saucepan of just simmering water (make sure bottom of bowl does not touch water). Heat, whisking constantly, until sugar is dissolved and an instant-read thermometer registers 170°.

Fit bowl onto mixer fitted with clean whisk attachment. Beat egg white mixture until thick and glossy and mixture is barely lukewarm, 8–10 minutes. Add 1 tsp. vanilla bean paste or vanilla extract and ½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¼ tsp. Morton kosher salt; fold in with a rubber spatula just to combine. Taste meringue and season with more kosher salt if needed

Peel away parchment paper from blondie and transfer to a heatproof platter. Using an offset spatula, spread meringue evenly over blondie, covering from edge to edge. Using a kitchen torch, toast meringue until deep golden brown. Sprinkle with flaky sea salt. Using a long knife and wiping off with a damp kitchen towel between cuts, slice blondie into 9 squares.

Do Ahead: Blondie can be baked 24 hours ahead. Store, loosely covered, at room temperature.

#4076. Scientists discover 5 hidden brain patterns behind depression

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Two people diagnosed with depression can experience vastly different symptoms. One might struggle with persistent anxiety and repetitive negative thoughts, while another may experience trauma related symptoms or substance abuse. But do these differences also appear in the way their brains function?

Researchers at the University of Helsinki investigated this question and uncovered a striking finding: people with the same depression diagnosis can have dramatically different patterns of brain activity. In some cases, communication between brain regions was unusually strong, while in others, it was weaker than normal.

Depression is a major global health concern. According to the World Health Organization (WHO, 2025), approximately 332 million (5.2%) adults worldwide are affected. In Finland, depression is the leading individual cause of both extended sick leave and disability pensions.

Brain Study Reveals Five Distinct Depression Profiles

To investigate the biological differences associated with depression, researchers examined brain activity in 263 people diagnosed with major depressive disorder and 75 healthy participants who served as a comparison group.

The analysis revealed five distinct groups based on differences in functional connectivity, a measure of how closely activity in different brain regions is coordinated. When two regions show synchronized activity, it suggests they are working together as part of a larger brain network. However, stronger connectivity does not necessarily mean the brain is functioning better.

"What was particularly interesting was the contrasting patterns of brain activity found under the umbrella of the same depression diagnoses. In some individuals, the functional connectivity between brain regions was stronger than usual, while in others it was weaker," says Director Satu Palva of the Neuroscience Center, University of Helsinki.

These differences were also associated with particular symptoms. Participants in one group showed especially strong connections between brain regions, along with more pronounced substance abuse problems. Another group displayed weaker connections and a greater prominence of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms.

The contrasting results could help explain why earlier studies examining the brains of people with depression have sometimes reached conflicting conclusions. If researchers study different combinations of patients, they may observe very different patterns, even though everyone involved has received the same diagnosis.

The findings add to growing evidence that depression may not have a single, consistent biological signature. Instead, the condition could involve several distinct patterns of brain function.

The Five Brain Patterns Linked to Depression

The researchers classified participants with depression into five groups based on how strongly different brain areas communicated and the frequencies at which their activity was coordinated.

Each group had a characteristic pattern of brain connectivity and associated symptoms:

Group 1: Strong connectivity and severe symptoms

Participants generally showed relatively strong connections between brain regions. This profile was associated with more severe symptoms across several areas, including depression, anxiety, rumination, and reduced ability to function in daily life. Rumination refers to repeatedly dwelling on distressing thoughts, worries, or negative experiences.

Group 2: Weaker connectivity and milder depression

This group was characterized by relatively weak communication between brain regions. Compared with the other groups, participants generally experienced milder symptoms.

Group 3: Widespread weak connectivity and trauma symptoms

People in this group showed reduced connectivity across substantial portions of the brain. Symptoms associated with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) were particularly prominent.

Group 4: Mixed connectivity and multiple challenges

This profile combined unusually strong connections in some brain regions with weaker connections in others. Participants tended to experience more severe depression, greater substance abuse problems, and lower overall well-being.

Group 5: The strongest connectivity and substance abuse symptoms

Participants in the fifth group had the strongest connections between brain regions of any group identified in the study. Substance abuse was a particularly prominent issue, while trauma related symptoms were less pronounced than in the other groups.

Importantly, all five groups also showed differences from the healthy participants. These distinctions extended beyond whether brain connections were strong or weak. The groups also differed in which brain regions were involved and the frequencies at which their activity was synchronized.

Together, the patterns suggest that people diagnosed with depression may experience substantially different changes in how their brain networks operate.

Measuring Brain Activity With Millisecond Precision

To capture these differences, the scientists used magnetoencephalography (MEG), a brain imaging technique that detects extremely faint magnetic fields produced by electrical activity in the brain.

Unlike methods that track slower changes associated with brain activity, MEG can follow electrical signals as they unfold over extremely short periods. This makes it particularly useful for investigating the timing and coordination of communication between brain regions.

Using MEG, the researchers recorded brain activity in all 263 participants with depression and the 75 healthy control subjects.

"MEG enabled us to monitor electrical brain activity with millisecond precision, helping us get closer to what actually happens in the brain at any given moment. Previously, depression phenotypes have been studied using methods with slower responses," Palva notes.

This high level of temporal precision allowed the team to examine rapid changes in brain activity that might be difficult to distinguish using slower measurement techniques.

By studying these fast signals, the researchers were able to identify differences not only in the strength of connectivity but also in the frequencies at which brain regions appeared to work together.

Could Brain Activity Lead to More Personalized Depression Treatments?

The discovery raises an important possibility for the future of mental health care: treatments might eventually be selected partly according to a person's individual pattern of brain activity.

Today, finding an effective depression treatment can involve trying different medications or therapies and evaluating how well each works. Because patients can respond differently to the same treatment, identifying the most suitable approach is not always straightforward.

Brain activity measurements could eventually provide additional information to help guide these decisions. However, the researchers emphasize that their findings are not yet ready for clinical use.

"We're not yet at the point where brain measurements can be used to choose the right treatment for patients, but the study does show one possible route," Palva says.

A major long-term goal is to better understand how particular symptoms relate to the way brain networks function. Connecting these biological patterns with patients' experiences could help researchers develop more precise ways to identify and characterize depression.

Eventually, such knowledge might allow doctors to determine which therapies are most likely to help individual patients, potentially reducing the amount of trial and error involved in treatment.

For now, the five brain profiles offer a different way to understand depression. Rather than assuming that one diagnosis corresponds to one underlying pattern of brain activity, the findings suggest that depression can involve markedly different biological changes, sometimes even in opposite directions.

From injectable semaglutide and tirzepatide to a pill GLP-1, drug companies have been innovating nonstop in recent years. At the same time, supplements claiming to offer similar effects have been popping up, as well as robust telehealth and gray market routes for getting the drugs.

Basically, the GLP-1 industry is booming—to varying degrees of success and safety. The latest GLP-1 innovation that you may have seen making headlines is retatrutide. Just like semaglutide and tirzepatide, this medication is being developed by drug companies as the newest iteration in the GLP-1 space, and it’s purportedly the strongest one yet.

But is that super strength a good thing? And is it as safe as its peers? Here’s everything you need to know about retatrutide, according to doctors.

Meet the experts: Holly Lofton, MD, is director of the medical weight management program at NYU Langone Health. Jennifer Manne-Goehler, MD, is an assistant professor of medicine at Harvard Medical School and a technical consultant on GLP-1s to the World Health Organization.

First, a quick refresher on GLP-1s: These medications mimic a hormone (called glucagon-like peptide-1, or GLP-1, for short) that is responsible for a bunch of things that have to do with regulating blood sugar and fullness, like triggering insulin secretion and signaling satiety to the brain.

Semaglutide (the generic name for meds like Ozempic and Wegovy) mimics just the GLP-1 hormone, while tirzepatide (the generic name for Mounjaro and Zepbound) targets an additional one called glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP). It’s thought that combining the two offers a more comprehensive approach to managing diabetes and obesity, but in terms of weight loss their effects seem to be about the same, with tirzepatide having a slight edge.

Retatrutide adds a third hormone receptor agonist, glucagon, for an even more powerful approach to diabetes and obesity care. “We call this the triple G agonist,” says Holly Lofton, MD, director of the medical weight management program at NYU Langone Health and a principal investigator in a retatrutide clinical trial at the hospital. “The additional benefit of glucagon agonism, beyond tirzepatide’s GLP-1/GIP agonism, is greater delay in gastric emptying and improved fat-cell metabolism.” Basically, it’s a stronger, faster-acting drug than its predecessors.

Well, that’s the case when it comes to weight loss, says Jennifer Manne-Goehler, MD, an assistant professor of medicine at Harvard Medical School and a technical consultant on GLP-1s to the World Health Organization. Despite being a triple G agonist, “reta,” as it’s called for short, seems to have the same effect on lowering glucose levels in people with type 2 diabetes as other GLP-1 medications. Where it really shines is weight loss, making it a potentially game-changing drug for those with clinical obesity, she says.

Both Dr. Lofton and Dr. Manne-Goehler say that retatrutide appears to be similarly safe as other GLP-1 medicines thus far. “In the leading clinical trials to date, gastrointestinal symptoms were the most common form of adverse events, as they are for other GLP-1 therapies,” says Dr. Manne-Goehler.

However, retatrutide is still in the clinical testing phase and hasn’t yet been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). (It’s pretty far along in that process, though. Eli Lilly, the maker of retatrutide, is nearing the end of phase 3 of testing. Phase 3 testing, which compares retatrutide to existing medications like semaglutide and tirzepatide, is the final step before sending it to the FDA for regulatory approval.)

Unfortunately, in the meantime, versions of the drug have been showing up on the gray market, just as they did for semaglutide and tirzepatide. Both experts stress that this is definitely not the way to go if you think you may want to try retatrutide—for a few reasons.

First, you may not be getting the real thing, so it may not even work. “I have had reports of patients taking reta from gray market vendors who have had no weight loss,” says Dr. Lofton. “This is due to the fact that these compounds often don’t contain any active ingredient, but may also contain [additives] that could be harmful.”

Which brings us to the second, more important problem: The drug may be outright unsafe. You never know what might be in a medication you get on the gray market because it’s unregulated. There’s no quality control, no cleanliness and safety assurances, or even any consistent recipe to make the drug—and it’s much harder to get accountability if something goes wrong.

Unfortunately, two recent deaths are linked with gray market versions of retatrutide. The first was reported to the FDA in July, when a woman went into cardiac arrest. The second was reported in September, after a 49-year-old experienced severe dehydration, malnutrition, and fainting and suffered a cardiac arrest. These deaths have many more questions than they do answers, but go to show how important it is wait to get FDA-approved versions of the drugs you take.

A lot of the discussion around GLP-1 medications in recent years has highlighted the dangers of rapid weight loss. Changes in bone density, loss of muscle mass, higher risk of gallstones, pancreatitis—these concerns and more can make the prospect of starting a weight loss drug a little nerve-racking. But both our experts agree that retatrutide doesn’t currently appear to come with any additional major risks compared to the semaglutide and tirzepatide options already on the market.

“I think the most important point is that, so far, we haven't seen a completely unexpected safety signal with retatrutide,” says Dr. Lofton. The potential negative side effects have looked pretty similar to the existing weight loss drugs, particularly when it comes to those gastrointestinal issues, she explains. That said, there are a few things she says she is paying particularly close attention to with the retatrutide clinical trials she’s currently leading.

The weight loss achieved with retatrutide is comparable to bariatric surgeries, per Dr. Lofton, which means patients can lose about 28 percent of their body weight over the course of two years, compared to 10 percent with GLP-1s. “Because the degree of weight loss is so impressive, we must keep in mind that this drug is not for cosmetic weight loss but for those with clinical obesity, which is a chronic medical condition,” she says.

One major thing providers should keep their eyes on is what exactly is behind the decreasing number on the scale. “I think we have to pay attention not just to the percentage of weight lost, but to what that weight consists of,” Dr. Lofton says. The risk for muscle loss or decreases in bone density exists with any rapid weight loss, but potentially especially so when it happens as quickly as it does for folks on retatrutide, she says.

Relatedly, doctors should pay close attention to gallbladder health. “Rapid weight loss itself increases gallstone risk, so I don't think we should automatically attribute those events to reta use, but with the degree of weight loss we're seeing, the gallbladder deserves particular attention,” Dr. Lofton says.

Phase 2 studies showed some risk for an increase in heart rate while on retatrutide, Dr. Lofton says, which is not the case for other GLP-1s. The effects were minor and peaked at around 24 weeks after starting the drug, but then subsided on their own. That said, any patients with cardiovascular disease or arrhythmia risk will want to pay close attention to any changes in heart rate or opt for other GLP-1 medications, Dr. Lofton says.

Unlike with other GLP-1s, dysesthesia, or altered skin sensation, has been popping up as a side effect across early trials, Dr. Lofton says. Some patients in phase 3 trials reported mild to moderate dysesthesia that typically resolved itself during treatment, but “that's something that I would want to understand better with longer follow-up,” she says.

Overall, for folks with clinical obesity, retatrutide is emerging as an option that will lead to more weight loss, and faster. That speed and magnitude come with some extra questions, but as the trial and approval process continues on, researchers are feeling hopeful about this newest option to treat obesity.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."
