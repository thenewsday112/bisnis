The findings raise questions about whether researchers fully understand how to effectively control agents.

On Oct. 6, an agent running on the company’s GPT-6 Astra model — which OpenAI called “the world’s most intelligent and aligned model” when it released it last month — was migrating a program at the request of an OpenAI employee. “The subagent explicitly considered whether removing the monitor would bypass a safeguard. Essentially, the agent-to-agent exchange left enough room for the model to reinterpret what should have been airtight instructions and disable alignment monitoring. When the program finished migrating, the employee noticed that monitoring was missing and asked the model whether it had removed it; Astra lied, saying it hadn’t.

In a separate instance on Oct. 3, a GPT-6.1 Sol agent also disabled alerts to OpenAI’s security team, seemingly for a related semantic reason: The model was “treating an earlier instruction not to send messages during a Slack history search as a reason to suppress monitoring notifications,” OpenAI explained.

“In both cases, the agents considered whether monitoring safeguards should be preserved, but interpreted routine coding instructions as permission to weaken them,” OpenAI said in the report.

Unlike recent security incidents, where agents explicitly breached systems, examples like these show AI agents suppressing one of the most crucial ways developers can track their behavior. If models can bury or otherwise disable alerts in the process of routine tasks where alignment isn’t as much of a concern, what could happen when the stakes are higher? The capability itself is what’s worth noticing here, as is the timeline for OpenAI’s discovery and reporting of it.

The company isn’t sure why agents did this, but said it was possibly due to “benign overpersistence,” a trait that models have to complete tasks at any cost, often to the detriment of established security layers, without understanding safety as a priority. This behavior has been a theme in recent breaches and helps explain why models cheat at tasks.

OpenAI explained that it’s easier for models to disable monitoring in evaluations than in Codex workloads, for example, since agents are separated from monitoring in Codex, and the company doesn’t “require misalignment monitoring when evaluating models comparable to GPT-6 Luna, given their weaker capabilities.” But classifying safety approaches based on what developers believe a model’s capabilities are doesn’t look like a very foolproof approach.

Additionally, given how frequently agents are used for coding, especially within AI labs themselves, it seems like a significant risk that these agents took “routine coding instructions” as permission to override monitoring requirements. Had the agents displayed more explicit intent to disobey, this may have been easier to pin down and train out as pure value misalignment. That the source of the issue was a misunderstanding makes it even trickier to plan for.

As John Steidley, chief of staff at Palisade Research, put it to ZDNET: “If GPT-6.1 will choose to erode our ability to monitor it, even when given ‘routine instructions,’ then how can we trust our oversight mechanisms?”

“We’ve seen several cases in which agents launched a deployment with misalignment monitoring disabled: the agent itself remained monitored, but that monitoring did not cover the deployment it launched,” OpenAI said. “These cases exposed a gap in how we enforce monitoring requirements on the workloads that our agents launch.”

But researchers still want to dig deeper into the increasingly slight differences between what happened and what could have happened.

“If OpenAI wants to make the case that these two behaviors are meaningfully different, then they should be publishing research that establishes that their training process will reliably cause their AIs to actually distinguish these behaviors,” Steidley said. “In other words, OpenAI should prove that their AI will never disable its own monitoring, and they are nowhere close to being able to prove that scientifically.”

To handle that aforementioned gap in monitoring enforcement, OpenAI said it’s updated Auto-Review, which is already required in Codex, to “detect and block attempts to disable misalignment monitoring.” It’s also making sure every level of workload has adequate safety monitoring requirements.

“Workloads that lack a valid safety case, or do not meet the requirements of its category’s safety case, will be terminated automatically,” OpenAI added.

OpenAI maintained that this wasn’t the worst-case scenario, but admitted the behavior is worth watching.

#4223. What is a circadian rhythm disorder? Plus, 6 ways to treat it

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

You’re tired when you should be awake, or wired when you should be asleep. Whether it’s 2am and you’re staring at the ceiling, your body still hasn’t recovered from that red-eye flight, or the night shift is taking its toll, the struggle is the same: your internal clock and your life are pulling in opposite directions.

These are circadian rhythm sleep disorders (CRSDs), and they happen when your body's sleep-wake cycle doesn't match your daily schedule. And they're more common than you'd think — often mistaken for stress or brushed off as one more thing to deal with once life slows down.

The good news is that CRSDs are medically recognized and treatable. Below, we’ll cover the different types, why they happen, and how to get your sleep back on track.

Your body has an internal clock—the circadian rhythm—that regulates when you feel sleepy and when you feel alert. It runs on roughly a 24-hour cycle, shaped by external cues like light, temperature, and meal timing.

A circadian rhythm sleep disorder (CRSD) occurs when your internal clock consistently falls out of sync with your environment, social schedule, or desired sleep times.

There are several distinct types of circadian rhythm sleep disorders:

Delayed sleep phase disorder (DSPD): Sleep comes much later than usual (often in the early hours of the morning) followed by a struggle to wake up on time.

Advanced sleep phase disorder (ASPD): Sleep arrives early in the evening, and waking happens in the early hours of the morning — the reverse of DSPD.

Non-24-hour sleep-wake disorder: Sleep and wake times drift later or earlier each day instead of settling into a 24-hour rhythm. This pattern is most common in people who are blind.

Irregular sleep-wake rhythm disorder: Sleep is broken into several short stretches across the day and night, without a clear pattern.

Shift work disorder: Night or rotating shift schedules conflict with the body's natural sleep timing.

Jet lag disorder: A temporary misalignment between the body’s internal clock and local time after crossing time zones.

Symptoms vary based on the type of CRSD, but the core issue is the same: sleep happens at the wrong time.

trouble falling asleep at a normal bedtime

difficulty waking up in the morning, or waking too early

mood changes, including irritability, anxiety, or low mood

Circadian rhythm sleep disorders often stem from multiple factors at once, ranging from lifestyle and environment to biology.

Light exposure: Light entering your eyes is the strongest external cue for your internal clock to stay in sync with the outside world. Too much bright light at night, like from screens, or too little natural light in the morning can push your clock in the wrong direction.

Genetics: Your genes can set your natural sleep timing earlier or later than average. Certain gene variants have been linked to differences in circadian timing, which may explain a lifelong tendency toward being a night owl or an early riser.

Age: Your sleep timing usually changes with age. Teenagers drift toward later sleep and wake times, while older adults often feel tired earlier in the evening and wake earlier as a result.

Shift work and irregular schedules: Night or rotating shifts keep you awake when your body expects to be asleep. Irregular eating and exercise can add to that disruption.

Neurological conditions: Conditions such as Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, or a history of traumatic brain injury are tied to a higher risk of circadian disruption, including fragmented or irregular sleep. Researchers are still working out whether disrupted rhythms are a cause, an early sign, or both.

Blindness: Without light entering the eyes, the brain's clock loses its main external cue for staying aligned with a 24-hour day.

No matter which type of CRSD you're dealing with, you can get your internal clock back on track. Here's what works, and how to find the right approach for you.

In the morning: Bright daylight early in the day helps advance your clock, which is especially useful for delayed sleep phase disorder. During darker months, using a bright light therapy box (10,000 lux) for 20–30 minutes after waking can substitute for sunlight. If you have any eye conditions, it's a good idea to check with a doctor before starting.

In the evening: Reduce bright light 2–3 hours before bed to ease your body into sleep mode. Screens and overhead lighting can suppress melatonin production, while dimmer, warmer light helps your body settle down.

Traveling across time zones? The strategy for beating jet lag with light depends on which direction you're flying. Seek bright light in the new morning and avoid it in the new evening when flying east. Do the reverse when flying west. A day or two of light timing can speed up your adjustment.

Working nights or rotating shifts? "Consistent" doesn't have to mean conventional. Try anchoring your sleep to the same block of hours on workdays, and limiting how far you drift on days off.

For persistent or severe circadian rhythm sleep disorders, professional support can make a real difference. A sleep specialist may recommend a formal evaluation, actigraphy (a wrist sensor that tracks sleep-wake patterns over time), or a combination of treatments.

Struggling with non-24-hour or irregular sleep-wake rhythm disorder? These patterns respond less predictably to light and schedule changes on their own. A sleep specialist can build a plan around your specific pattern.

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is also sometimes paired with circadian-focused treatments. It’s a structured therapy that targets the thoughts and habits keeping you awake.

Several everyday habits can throw off your circadian rhythm, including irregular sleep schedules, shift work, jet lag, too much screen use at night, and inconsistent light exposure. Stress, alcohol, caffeine, and some medications can also throw off your body's timing.

Some circadian rhythm sleep disorders can go away on their own, but it depends on the type you're dealing with. Jet lag usually clears up within a few days as your body adjusts to a new time zone. But most chronic circadian rhythm sleep disorders don't resolve without changes to your sleep schedule, light exposure, or daily habits. #2068. The court’s third-country removal case has high stakes but will likely be decided on technical grounds

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Type to search articles, cases, and authors.

For the third time, the Supreme Court will soon hear argument about the federal government’s power to send migrants to countries where they have no ties. And for the third time, the justices are unlikely to focus on what matters most to the migrants who claim the Trump administration is violating the law – whether they will be persecuted, tortured, or killed. Instead, the court is likely to turn its attention to a much more technical issue: whether courts can force the government to let migrants explain why forced removal might mean almost-certain persecution.

In Department of Homeland Security v. D.V.D., scheduled for oral argument on Dec. 9, the court will review the Trump administration’s policy of removing migrants from the United States to any country that will accept them. The Immigration and Nationality Act permits so-called third-country removals under certain circumstances. After an immigration judge has ordered a migrant removed, immigration officials must first attempt to send the person to the country that they choose, where they hold citizenship, or last resided. If none of those countries accepts the migrant, officials can then send the person to a country that will take them in, so long as they are not sent to a country where they face likely persecution or torture.

Last year, the Trump administration revamped the government’s third-country removal process to quickly send more migrants to countries where they have no ties. Cuban citizen D.V.D., who receives treatment for a severe mental illness, and Honduran citizen M.M., who fled an abusive husband, represent a group of migrants who worry that the administration’s policy will send them into harm’s way. The policy – first announced in February 2025 and revamped in March 2025 – permits immigration officials to send migrants to any country whose government assures the State Department that it won’t persecute or torture anyone. If a government won’t provide that assurance, but will accept a migrant, the policy requires immigration officials to notify the migrant of its plans to send them to that country and give them “a reasonable opportunity” to consider whether they might face abuse there, “generally 24 hours, or after he has had a reasonable means and opportunity to speak with an attorney,” the U.S. solicitor general’s request for Supreme Court intervention explains.

The Trump administration hasn’t published a full list of countries that have assured it they won’t harm people, but, under the third-country removal policy DHS has attempted to send migrants to countries with documented records of mistreatment. An Immigration and Customs Enforcement official acknowledged efforts to transport migrants to South Sudan. News reports indicate that the administration has also tried sending people to Cameroon, the Central African Republic, and another three-dozen countries. The State Department warns people from visiting many of those countries.

Many have been sent closer to the United States. In coordination with the Mexican government, news reports suggest that more than 20,000 non-Mexicans have been sent there. One of the migrants suing DHS, a Guatemalan citizen identified as O.C.G., is among them. An immigration judge barred U.S. officials from sending him to Guatemala. Despite that, DHS “threw him on a bus to Mexico, where he had just been raped, and where he was quickly sent back to Guatemala, the place an immigration judge had just found he would likely be persecuted. And then [DHS] lied about it,” U.S. District Judge Brian Murphy wrote in February.

Indeed, Murphy, who sits in the Boston federal court, has repeatedly found the Trump administration’s legal position, as well as its litigation conduct, problematic. During the spring and summer of 2025, Murphy ordered DHS not to implement the policy citing concerns about its legality. The government nonetheless attempted to do so on multiple occasions. At least once DHS relied on testimony that a migrant purportedly gave to a government witness even though, according to Murphy, “there was no such witness.”

Murphy later concluded that the third-country removal policy is illegal. Without telling people where they are to be sent and giving them time to consider whether they might be harmed there, the administration’s policy violates the INA’s protection from persecution, Murphy concluded in February. The government’s view, he added, is that “‘as long as the Department [of Homeland Security] doesn’t already know there’s someone standing there waiting to shoot … that’s fine’.” But “[i]t is not fine, nor is it legal,” Murphy went on, before emphasizing that the government cannot deprive a person of their life, liberty, or property without due process of law. As a result, Murphy granted a request by a group of migrants who face the possibility of being sent to a third country to vacate the policy, ordering DHS to notify people before sending them to third countries.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the 1st Circuit largely agreed with Murphy. Affirming most of Murphy’s decision, the appellate court explained in September that legal limitations on the government are meaningless if the people who the government targets can’t ask a court to intervene when they believe the government is violating the law. As the 1st Circuit summarized its position, “an individual’s right to contest removal to a country based on a fear of persecution in that country means little if one does not receive prior notice of the intended removal destination and a meaningful opportunity to contest that destination.”

Despite the high stakes of the administration’s policy, the Supreme Court is more likely to decide D.V.D. based on technical issues of judicial authority to consider the migrants’ claim. The solicitor general argued, in his petition for emergency relief, that a series of federal laws bar courts from hearing any claims that removal from the United States might result in persecution or torture. The one exception, the solicitor general contends, is for individual claims initially aired before a federal court of appeals. As a class action lawsuit first raised in the Boston district court, such federal courts thus have no authority even to consider the migrants’ claims, the solicitor general argues.

The migrants dispute the solicitor general’s view. Even though Congress has constrained judicial review of immigration-related claims, they argue that the INA allows courts to consider whether the federal government is acting illegally by sending people to countries where they are likely to be endangered. Congress has limited courts’ power to block immigration agencies through injunctions, but Murphy’s order vacating the policy, which is based in the Administrative Procedure Act, is not an injunction, they argue.

When it announced that it would hear arguments in this case, the court also invited the solicitor general to identify any reasons not previously argued for why it thinks the administration should win. That’s unlikely to prompt the government to focus on the risks that migrants face when DHS uses a hurried process.

For migrants who fear for their lives, the stakes couldn’t be higher: life or death. But, ultimately, this case is likely to turn on technical questions of judicial power.

#2300. Bonfire Season Is Here — Here Are the Dos and Don’ts for Staying Safe

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

How to not to expose yourself to toxic fumes, burn down the neighborhood and injure the kiddos, plus other tips for enjoying a stress-free bonfire.

Cool fall evenings naturally put our minds on the merits of bones-warming bonfires. And while many create cherished family memories and set the backdrop for festive community events, some end up in tragedy, with emergency room visits and property damage.

“Every season there are always the same preventable mistakes made that lead to injuries,” says attorney Kevin Henrichson. “Falling into the fire, using gasoline, adding fuel when you shouldn’t, failing to supervise children and consuming alcohol all raise the stakes of something bad happening.”

Ahead, experts walk through exactly what to do and not to do to stay safe while enjoying a bonfire.

Notify your local fire department of your plans and ask them for guidance concerning:

“One phone call to the fire marshal’s office takes two minutes,” says fire and home safety expert Eugene Klimaszewski. “Otherwise, the consequence of burning without a permit on a restricted day is a fine, and if it gets away from you, it’s your liability.”

Look for a flat, open spot away from fences, barns, houses, trees and ground vegetation. The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) recommends keeping bonfires at least 25 feet from the house and other structures.

“That number isn’t arbitrary,” says Klimaszewski. “Radiant heat and drifting embers are what ignite a deck railing, a vinyl fence or the mulch bed against the house. Every ember that lands on a roof or in a gutter full of dry leaves is a coin flip. Most go out. The one that doesn’t becomes a structure fire.”

Build the fire downwind of your home and close all windows and doors on the fire side, to prevent smoke from getting in.

“Even a small amount of smoke reaching an indoor smoke detector is enough to set it off,” says Klimaszewski. If you have a monitoring system, that could trigger a call to the fire department. “At best that’s an embarrassing conversation with the engine company. In towns that charge for repeat false alarms, that’s a bill on top of the embarrassment,” he says.

Pro-tip: If you have an older home, which isn’t airtight, put your alarm system on test mode or notify your monitoring company about your bonfire plans.

Wind can carry embers up to a mile, which means a bonfire on a windy day can easily end with a wildfire or house fire. If your planned bonfire day turns out to be windy, reschedule it. And even if there isn’t a breeze, plan for one by clearing all debris from around the fire pit and building rocks or bricks up higher on the windy side, which protects the fire from the wind plus radiates heat toward you for extra warmth.

Kiln-dried wood is the best. Seasoned hardwoods like oak, cedar and hickory are particularly good because they burn cleaner, longer and hotter than soft woods like pine and aspen (though a little soft wood kindling makes starting a fire a lot easier).

Steer clear of gasoline or other accelerants, as they too often lead to burn injuries, out-of-control fires and noxious fumes. (One year, my neighbor used so much gasoline to start a bonfire that the resulting shock wave from the explosion knocked pictures off of people’s walls three blocks away).

“A lot of people think they can use gas or lighter fluid to get the fire started or revive it, but please never do that because the vapors can flash back and get everyone nearby,” says Henrichson. “Gasoline is particularly dangerous and should never be used to start or revive a fire.”

Pro-tip: If you struggling to start a fire, build up a good kindling base and use a campfire starter or small bit of tightly twisted newspaper. Ample kindling will help you get a hot coal-bed established, which will burn larger logs more effectively plus lessen smoke.

Avoid newspaper, cardboard and other paper products, which tend to break down into large flying embers. Also, never burn anything with adhesives, plastics and other chemicals. The health risks of this are obvious, but plastics and chemicals are hiding in many items you might not realize, including: dryer lint, food packaging with shiny ink, glossy magazines, pressed woods including butcher block, varnished furniture and clothing made with synthetic fibers.

Before lighting the fire, set up a water source in case embers become a problem. That will also make it easier to put it out completely when you’re done. NFPA actually requires a hose, bucket or extinguisher be on hand for all recreational fires, and for fires to be attended at all times.

“A fire that’s left alone for ten minutes while everyone goes inside for more drinks is the one that ends up on the local news,” says Klimaszewski. “And an unattended fire that spreads is the homeowner’s liability, full stop.”

Assign someone to supervise the flames at all times to prevent the fire from getting out of control, and to keep kids and pets from standing too close. Equip the supervisor with proper protective clothing, including a fire-resistant glove, long-handled poker tool, shovel, closed-toe shoes and eye protection.

“The most common injuries I’ve seen are from people falling into the fire, and young children are vulnerable to this because they may want to play in the area where everyone is hanging out by the fire,” says Henrichson. “There should be a no-enter zone that is explained to children every single time, and the person tending the fire should not have any alcohol.”

Watch for loose sleeves, blankets, boots and other combustibles that are too close to the fire. “Those fun marshmallow-roasting memories are great to make, but shaking a burning marshmallow can cause trouble, and if you use metal skewers, they can burn you too,” says Henrichson.

Also, know what to do beforehand if something ends up catching fire. “The old advice that we were told years ago is still true: stop, drop, and roll if your clothes catch fire,” says Henrichson. “Repeat this as necessary every time kids approach, because they don’t always know to stay back.”

Embers can easily reignite hours or even days later, so the best practice for putting out a bonfire is to drown it, stir the ashes, and then drown it again until everything is cold to the touch. Don’t cover embers with dirt, as that actually insulates them, leaving open the possibility for them to reignite if they’re uncovered by a stiff wind.

Pro-tip: Put ashes in a metal container with a lid, on a non-combustible surface, away from the house, garage and deck for several days before they go in the trash, says Klimaszewski. Never put them in a plastic bin or next to the house.

With the high cost of heating and cooling a home, you may think that closing vents in some rooms will redirect hot air to the rooms with open vents. Unfortunately, it doesn’t work that way. An HVAC unit (furnace, AC, or heat pump) is sized specifically to match the cubic feet of rooms and a home’s overall living space, says JT Holt, instructional designer for Heating Air-Conditioning & Refrigeration Distributors International (HARDI).

What this means is that no matter how many of the unit’s vents are open, the heater will run at the same power level. Here’s why closing vents could end up costing you more than keeping them open.

HVAC systems consist of a series of metal ductwork and vents. Supply vents deliver heated (or cooled) air to rooms from the HVAC unit. Return vents draw air into return ducts back to the heating or cooling system, feeding the air handler and helping to control interior air pressure. According to Holt, the system’s pressure is based on those cubic feet per minute calculations for the living areas.

When the furnace kicks on, the blower forces hot air through the ductwork and out of the supply vents. When vents are closed, the air pressure builds up in the house, says Holt. “Just imagine you have a hose with two nozzles, and you block off one of the nozzles. More water is going to go to the other nozzle,” he says. The actual volume might not double, but the water that flows into the only open hose is pushed harder. The same thing occurs with airflow in home ventilation if the homeowner closes off a duct.

Pressure differences in the system can increase the risk of duct leaks. Leaks in ducts result in the loss of heated or cooled air into the walls, which then leads to higher energy costs.

The negative air pressure created by closed supply vents could cause the HVAC unit to have more difficulty drawing in air. This results in the HVAC motor working harder to supply the air necessary for recirculation. According to ASHRAE, an association formed by and for HVAC engineers, obstructing airflow to return vents also can affect the system. In fact, a system might be inefficient if it has too few return vents in a house. Likewise with baseboard or radiant heat: Avoid placing furniture or appliances in the way of these heating units’ airflow.

In addition, when closed supply vents affect return airflow, the heat exchanger (the most expensive component of a furnace) could overheat. This added strain on even the best furnace can damage the heat exchanger when the furnace is running. It also can damage the HVAC’s compressor when the AC is running; damage to HVAC components can lead to high repair costs and reduce the system’s useful life.

Likewise, shutting or obstructing both supply and return vents can lower both proper distribution of warm air and a system’s efficiency, says ASHRAE. Plus, when one room in the house is colder than the others, it acts like a heat sink, drawing heat through walls, floors, or ceilings from adjacent heated rooms. This in turn lowers the heated rooms’ temperature and triggers the furnace to run more often. When all vents are open and the heat distribution is uniform, the heat sink effect should not occur.

In addition to heat sink, closing a vent can affect the thermostat, says Holt. If you block off a room, Holt says, more heat goes to the room in which the system’s thermostat is located. “Your furnace is going to shut off faster because the room with the thermostat heated faster,” says Holt. That can affect comfort throughout the home, plus lead to short cycling, “which means the system turns off and on more often.” This cycling could cause a unit to need replacement sooner than it would otherwise.

Reduced air circulation in rooms with closed vents increases the risk of mold and mildew growth in the vents, especially if the home is in a humid region. The lower surface temperature in colder rooms increases the risk of condensation and moisture problems where that cold air meets the warm air in the ducts. This gives mold a prime spot to start growing. Mildew and mold can take hold in the ducts, a difficult problem to clean that will require the services of an HVAC professional.

Mold might be visible on or around the vent cover in the closed-off room, or you might note an earthy or musty smell when the system runs. That smell also can spread to nearby rooms. Given the potential negative health effects of mold, this is reason alone to leave all vents open and help keep everything dry and healthier.

ASHRAE recommends that you only close vents or turn down thermostats in areas of the home that can be shut off from the rest of the house and have their own heating zone. It also helps the system to keep interior doors open when possible. If a room is used infrequently, you can partially close the vent, especially if it is the farthest from where the HVAC unit sits. Never close a vent three-fourths of the way or more; closing vents altogether is counterproductive.

When controlling temperature in rooms with baseboard heat or radiators instead of a central system, you can turn off the heat when the room is unoccupied. Also, close the door to a room in which baseboard heat is running to hold warmth in. However, this advice applies only to systems with separate heating zones with a thermostat in each room or zone. It’s also important to keep an eye out for mold in any of these closed-off rooms.

Use a space heater. If you don’t want to heat the entire house, you’ll save money by lowering the thermostat a few degrees and using a space heater in the room you’re using. A portable heater to keep on hand for those times, as well as for power outages, can be a worthwhile investment.

Maintain the HVAC system. A well-maintained HVAC unit is more energy efficient than a neglected one. Although Holt says HVAC contractors offer different maintenance plans, “once or twice a year” is often recommended for maintenance. ASHRAE recommends cleaning or changing the filters in a furnace at least once a month for optimal efficiency. Have the ducts cleaned every few years.

Schedule an inspection: Finding and repairing duct leaks and replacing faulty system parts will help the unit run better and more efficiently. Check for duct leaks, especially if you notice increased airborne dust or a spike in the heating or cooling bill. Have the entire HVAC system inspected regularly to help to keep your furnace shipshape. Holt says that having the right size equipment to heat your home adds to efficiency. You might ask the inspector to ensure that your system is right-sized for the home’s square footage, especially if you have remodeled.

Their primary objective was to investigate the Taraia Object, a mysterious shape in a shallow lagoon that researchers first noticed in 2020 on satellite and historical aerial photos. The Taraia Object on a satellite image. Located at Nikumaroro. Photo: Archaeological Legacy Institute

Much of the debris observed in satellite images had already broken up and washed away, leaving a single Y-shaped piece. On the way to the atoll. Photo: Purdue Research Foundation

#2514. ‘Should I let my kid have ChatGPT?’ and all your biggest questions about AI, answered

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Many parents fear that AI is taking over their teens’ ability to think for themselves. But with some open communication, you can raise kids that are aware of AI’s risks and rewards.

By Lauren Ramakrishna Updated Oct. 23, 2026

It feels like artificial intelligence is taking over every facet of life. Adults encounter AI daily. It recommends products for us to buy, shares content we’ll like on streaming platforms, filters spam in our email accounts, and even tracks our health through our smart watches.

It’s increasingly becoming a part of our kids’ daily lives, too. They are accessing AI on smartphones, using it in school and some are even developing relationships with AI chatbots. Fearing a repeat of the consequences that came from unfettered teen access to smartphones and social media in the early 2010s, many parents are justifiably concerned about this new technological frontier.

Experts are sounding alarm bells, as well. Former Google trust and safety executive Tom Siegel recently told Reuters that AI poses a greater harm to children than social media did, highlighting risks such as suicide, psychosis, and reduced critical thinking.

While banning your kids from using AI may sound appealing, it’s not feasible. Unlike social media, which is contained in standalone apps, AI is threaded into technology our kids use everyday, from search engines to video games. That’s why it’s important for parents to focus on AI education to prepare their kids to safely interact with the technology in its many forms.

Whether your kids are just starting to use AI or are already AI connoisseurs, we tapped experts to help answer some of parents’ most pressing questions about teens and AI.

According to a Pew Research Center study, more than half of teens say they have used AI to search for information or get help with their schoolwork. They’re using generative AI, which creates content from prompts, to create images, direct them to research on topics for school and even solve math problems.

Teens are also increasingly interacting with chatbots, which are computer programs that are designed to simulate human conversation. You’ve probably heard of or even used some of the most popular chatbots, like ChatGPT, Claude, Google Gemini and Perplexity. Roughly two-thirds of teens report using chatbots; 3 in 10 do so daily.

While schools are entrusted with educating young minds to become critical thinkers, AI often takes the thinking away from them. According to the Pew Center study, 59% of teens think students regularly use AI to cheat at school. Of the 15% of teens who said they think AI will negatively impact them personally over the next 20 years, one-third cited overreliance and loss of critical thinking or creativity as major concerns.

While many students think AI provides an easy way to finish their work quickly, doing so has consequences. Kids who heavily rely on AI in high school can struggle in college, where professors are developing methods to identify unacceptable AI use, then handing out failing grades on tests and papers.

In an assignment asking students to compare the Industrial Revolution to today’s digital age, Alcorn State University professor Jason Gibson hid a prompt in invisible white text that would force generative AI to drop the word “Madagascar” into the response in a way that made no sense. He knew students had used AI when he saw “Madagascar” in the completed assignment, and 32 of the 35 students failed a portion of the midterm accordingly.

In 2025, Common Sense Media and Stanford Medicine’s Brainstorm Lab for Mental Health released research showing that three in four teens are using AI chatbots for companionship, including emotional and mental health support. But these programs aren’t providing reliable help and consistently fail to recognize and appropriately respond to mental health conditions that affect young people, according to the report.

And it can have tragic consequences. In August 2025, Matthew and Maria Raine sued OpenAI and its CEO Sam Altman after their 16-year-old son Adam died by suicide. The parents allege that ChatGPT coached their son on how to die by suicide, even offering to write a draft of a suicide letter. Adam first started using ChatGPT for homework help, but the chatbot soon became his “closest confidant,” according to the lawsuit.

“Because chatbots show relative competence for homework help and general questions, teens and parents unconsciously assume they’re equally reliable for mental health guidance—but they’re not,” says Common Sense Media. “The empathetic tone and professional presentation mask fundamental limitations that make these systems inappropriate for a use case that affects millions of teens.”

Yes, AI can have positive impacts. “For learning, AI can assist in brainstorming, creating, organizing, summarizing, and synthesizing information, and offering resources and solutions for challenging problems,” according to a statement from the American Psychological Association on AI and adolescent well-being. When used as a tool for guided learning rather than a replacement for critical thinking, AI can ask questions to help challenge a user to analytically approach a topic.

There’s no one-size-fits-all solution for AI use in schools. Depending on where you live, your kids may encounter widely differing expectations. Some school districts are attempting to take a hard pause on AI in the classroom. New York City Public Schools announced that for the 2026-27 school year it has enacted a moratorium on student-facing generative artificial intelligence (AI) through eighth grade, with limited use in high schools.

But it’s hard to ban something that in many ways just can’t be escaped. According to a Common Sense Media report on teens and AI, 44% of teens who use AI for schoolwork attend schools where AI tools are blocked—many of them simply switch to personal devices to access AI.

And when schools ban access, educators aren’t able to teach their students how to use AI tools responsibly. Ash Brandin, EdS, a teacher in Colorado’s Boulder Valley School District who shares content to help parents and educators navigate screen time, video games, and digital literacy under the Instagram handle @thegamereducator, says their school district prohibits student access to generative AI platforms on school property. “In some ways, that makes it more difficult [for educators] because it’s very difficult for me to talk about something that is taken further and further away from us,” says Brandin, who uses they/them pronouns.

A staggering 70% of teens say they have never had a teacher discuss how to use AI safely with them, and only 27% have discussed what AI is or how it works with a teacher.

AI is like toothpaste that’s been let out of its tube; trying to get it back in is a fruitless endeavor. Just like adults, “kids are seeing AI woven in and imbued in their everyday lives,” Brandin says.

What teens need is AI education so they can learn what it is, the risks it poses, and how they can use it wisely.

If your teen is using AI for potentially harmful activities, like cheating or for companionship, help them find alternatives. “Think about what your child is getting from the chatbot or AI and what you would like them to do instead,” Brandin advises. Then collaborate with your teen to come up with a plan.

Teaching your kids how to use AI requires more exposure, not less. “I love to explain readiness with a pool analogy,” Brandin says, “I didn’t introduce my child to a pool at the age I thought that they could independently swim. I introduced my child to pools and bodies of water as early as possible.” As babies, you hold them close while they dip their toes. Later, they’re just an arm’s length away while using a floating device. Eventually, they’re diving in the deep end alone while you sit poolside in a lounge chair.

“With each step I’m focused on what skills they need to build up so they have the ability to navigate the pool,” Brandin says. AI is no different. Parents should begin explaining what AI is and how it shows up in our lives as early as possible. Common Sense Media has a free Digital Literacy and Well-Being Curriculum that can help guide age-appropriate conversations about AI, social media, and more.

On their Instagram account, Brandin shares a couple of digital literacy lessons parents can do with their teens, tweens and even younger kids to teach critical thinking skills. The first is to bring up a chatbot and ask it to give a random number between 1 and 100. “If you do that with ChatGPT, 7 out of 10 times it will answer 42,” Brandin says. Ask your kids to react to the results with questions like, “We asked for something random, does this seem random?”

In another experiment, you can ask ChatGPT to explain how to do something that your child already knows how to do really well. “Since our kids are going to AI for things they don’t know, we want to ask it something they know really well so we can turn around and say, ‘It looks like this chatbot made a lot of mistakes on this thing that you know really well. So what do you think is happening when we ask it things we don’t know?’”

I can confirm that my 9-year-old gymnastics-loving daughter had a lot of corrections when we asked ChatGPT how to do a back walkover.

Brandin suggests modeling how to properly and safely use AI. When you are searching, speak out loud and explain to your kids how you are verifying the results AI spits out. What sources did AI use? Do I trust those sources or do I need to look further?

Perhaps most importantly, if your kids are using AI, you need to use it, too. The more you tap into the tools your teens are using, the more you will understand them and be able to communicate with your kids about them.

When you feel fear and anxiety about AI creeping in, “interrogate it for yourself,” Brandin advises. “Are you worried about [your teens] using a chatbot because the information it gives them may be unsafe? Or because they won’t listen to you afterward?” Use your fear to locate the skillset you need to teach your teen.

“It’s very easy to get freaked out about the dangers of AI,” Brandin says. Instead, while we wait for systemic support from governments and tech companies, parents can focus on what they can control at home—open communication, exposure and digital education.
