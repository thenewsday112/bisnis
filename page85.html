#1515. What Not Dating Is Really Like, According to Relationship Experts

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Spoiler: You can want sex and love while not actively looking for all of it.

Maybe you've deleted the dating apps. Maybe you've stopped entertaining the guy who only remembers you exist after 10 p.m. Or maybe the thought of sitting across from another stranger, explaining what you do for work and how many siblings you have sounds about as appealing as a root canal.

Whatever the reason, you've decided you're not dating right now. And apparently, it seems like everyone you tell this to has some type of question. Are you heartbroken? Have you given up on love? Aren't you lonely? And, perhaps most importantly to the nosiest people in your life, Does this mean you're not having sex?

Take one of my girlfriends, for example. After moving to New York City in 2022, she spent much of her first few years wrapped up in a relationship. Since their breakup two years ago, she hasn't really been dating. Not because she's heartbroken or has given up on love, but because she's been busy building a life of her own. She's made lasting friendships, explored new neighborhoods, picked up hobbies, and even gotten a dog. Not to distract herself from being single, but because these are things she genuinely wants to do.

She's open to love. She even checks the apps occasionally. But unless her dog's leash gets tangled with that of a particularly attractive stranger at the park, dating simply isn't a priority.

And that's the thing about not dating: It doesn't necessarily mean you're lonely, healing, closed off to romance, or even celibate. Sometimes folks simply aren't interested in dating, full stop.

So, I asked relationship experts to debunk five of the biggest misconceptions about not dating, including where celibacy fits into the picture.

There's a difference between not wanting a relationship and not wanting to date. You can absolutely want to fall in love, get married, and live out your happily-ever-after fantasy while also wanting nothing to do with dating apps, awkward first dates, or another situationship that goes nowhere.

"There's often a misconception that everyone who isn't dating doesn't want a relationship," says Alyssa Digges, a mental health counselor at Insight Therapy NYC. "Some don't, but some deeply crave a relationship, they've just burned out on modern dating."

And fair enough. Wanting a partner doesn't automatically mean you have the energy to go looking for one, especially when dating starts to feel more like an obligation than something you're genuinely excited about.

For some people, stepping away is temporary. "I actually see the opposite when people take a break from dating," says Dr. Sabrina Romanoff, PsyD, a clinical psychologist and relationship expert at Hily, of the assumption of giving up on love. "This usually stems from their intentional decision to reinvest their energy, become the best version of themselves, and eventually return to their dating life in a better position."

But here's the thing: Not everyone who's stopped dating is planning their big return to the apps. Not dating doesn't have to be a phase you eventually graduate from, and it certainly doesn't have to be a strategy for finding love.

Whether you're taking a break, leaving the door open for something unexpected, or happily keeping it closed, your decision doesn't require an expiration date.

Sure, being single can get lonely sometimes. So can being in a relationship. Having a significant other isn't some magical guarantee that you'll never feel alone again.

"Romantic relationships are only one way people can find connection," says Romanoff. "There are many other ways people can find connection and fulfillment, including with friends, family, colleagues, and by making new connections."

She also points out that plenty of people in long-term relationships experience loneliness and isolation. In other words, being coupled up and feeling connected aren't necessarily the same thing.

For someone who's not dating, connection might come from spending more time with friends, strengthening family relationships, or investing in a community. And without the time and energy that dating can demand, there may be more room for those relationships to flourish.

"I won't pretend that being single can't feel lonely sometimes, but many of the single people I work with have full, rich lives filled with love and support," says Digges.

Being single doesn't automatically mean being lonely, just as being in a relationship doesn't automatically mean being fulfilled. And while romantic intimacy is its own kind of connection, it isn't the only one worth having. A Saturday night spent with your friends–or solo–isn't inherently less meaningful than one spent with a partner.

There's a strange cultural tendency to treat being single as a problem that needs solving. If you're not dating, people assume you either can't find someone, are too picky, or have some emotional baggage keeping you from putting yourself out there.

"Being single or partnered carries significant weight in our culture as if it assigns your value or worth," says Romanoff. She explains that the longstanding idea that marriage is a woman's greatest accomplishment can be difficult to shake, even today.

And when being partnered is treated as the default, choosing not to date can seem like a decision that requires an explanation. Suddenly, everyone from your mother to your married friend who hasn't been single since college has a theory about why you're still on your own.

But being single doesn't necessarily mean you're struggling to find someone willing to date you.

"It's not that no one wants them, but rather they haven't found someone they truly want," says Digges of many of the single people she works with.

She also pushes back against the idea that being selective means your standards are too high. While superficial preferences, like a specific height or appearance, might be worth reconsidering, she argues that values-based standards are another matter entirely.

For Digges, qualities like kindness, compassion, and intelligence aren't things someone should have to compromise on just to find a partner. Having those standards might narrow your dating pool, but it can also help you avoid relationships that wouldn't have been right for you.

And for people who aren't interested in dating at all, there's no need to defend their standards, attractiveness, or relationship potential in the first place. Being single isn't evidence of a personal deficiency.

The phrase "taking a break from dating" often comes with the assumption that something terrible must have happened first. A breakup, a betrayal, a particularly brutal situationship—surely there's an ex somewhere responsible for this decision. And sometimes there is! Taking time to heal after a difficult relationship can be a perfectly valid reason to step away from dating.

But not every person who's stopped dating is nursing a broken heart, recovering from a toxic ex, or embarking on a post-break-up journey of self-discovery.

"Not dating is often a choice, not a waiting room," says licensed mental health counselor Anna Green, co-founder and chief clinical officer at Thrive Mental Health. "Plenty of adults step back from dating to rebuild friendships, focus on work, or just stop performing for apps."

My friend's experience is a perfect example. After spending so much of her early New York life in a relationship, she's finally getting to experience the city on her own terms. And while the breakup may have been what initially made that possible, it isn't what's keeping her single. She's not spending her weekends distracting herself from her ex. She's spending them doing things she actually enjoys, with people she loves.

There’s a difference between healing from a relationship and remaining single after realizing how much you enjoy life without one.

There's also a tendency to frame every dating break as an opportunity to become the best version of yourself before eventually finding a partner. And while self-reflection can be beneficial, your time outside the dating pool doesn't have to be productive, transformative, or preparation for anything.

Aimee Evnin-Bingham, LMFT, an AASECT-certified sex and relationship therapist, says that when her clients become frustrated with dating, she encourages them to revisit why they want to date in the first place. If the answer is simply that they feel they should be dating, that's worth examining.

How often are we pursuing a relationship because we genuinely want one, versus because we've been conditioned to believe we should always be looking?

That said, there's a distinction between choosing not to date because you genuinely don't want to and avoiding connection because you're afraid of it.

"The one thing I'd watch for: if not dating is really about fear, if you want connection but keep pulling away from people, that is worth talking through with someone," Green says.

The difference isn't necessarily what your dating life looks like from the outside, but how you feel about it. If you're content with your decision, great. If you're unhappy with it or feel stuck between wanting intimacy and avoiding it, that may be worth exploring. Either way, not dating doesn't automatically mean there's something you need to fix.

Now for the question everyone seems particularly interested in: If you're not dating, does that mean you're also not having sex? Not necessarily!

Despite often being lumped together, not dating and celibacy are two different things. Not dating generally means you're not actively pursuing romantic relationships, while celibacy typically refers to intentionally abstaining from sexual activity. And neither automatically requires the other.

"Not dating and celibacy are not the same thing," says Green. "Some people who aren't dating are celibate, some aren't, and some couples go through celibate stretches together. What matters is whether the choice feels like yours."

My friend, for example, hasn't actively dated in two years, but that doesn't mean she's spent those years abstaining from sex. She's had the occasional casual encounter, without feeling the need to turn it into something more. Her decision not to pursue a relationship hasn't required her to put her sex life on hold.

And she's hardly alone in that distinction. Someone who's taking a break from dating might still have casual sex, maintain a friends-with-benefits arrangement, or enjoy sexual pleasure on their own. Others might decide they don't want to engage in partnered sexual activity at all.

Evnin-Bingham emphasizes that stepping away from dating doesn't mean stepping away from pleasure. "This could be a great time to learn more about your body and what turns you on like erotic stories, listening to something sexy or practicing new ways to have solo sex," she says.

She also notes that someone who isn't dating might choose to have casual sex in a way that feels safe, playful, and aligned with their own desires. Of course, not everyone wants casual sex, and choosing not to have it doesn't automatically mean they're sexually frustrated or missing out.

Celibacy itself can mean different things to different people, too. Some define it as abstaining from partnered sex, while others include other forms of sexual activity. And people choose it for all sorts of reasons, including personal values, religious beliefs, emotional well-being, or simply not wanting sex at a particular point in their lives.

Importantly, celibacy isn't exclusive to single people. Someone in a committed relationship can also choose to abstain from sex, either individually or as part of a mutual decision with their partner.

It's also worth acknowledging that not dating can sometimes mean missing physical intimacy, even when you're perfectly happy with your decision.

"For many people, the loss of sex alone doesn't hit as hard as the loss of physical intimacy that comes with dating or being in a relationship," Digges notes. As a single woman myself, I can attest: Sometimes I find myself yearning for a good cuddle sesh more than sex.

But missing sex or physical affection doesn't mean you have to start dating again, just as wanting sex doesn't mean you have to pursue a relationship.

Your romantic choices and your sexual choices can be connected, but they don't have to be. You can be single and sexually active, partnered and celibate, or somewhere in between. None of those combinations requires a justification.

The answer is different for everyone. For some, it's deleting the apps and enjoying a few months without romantic distractions. For others, it's an indefinite decision to prioritize friendships, personal goals, or simply the freedom to do whatever they want without factoring a potential partner into the equation.

It might involve sex. It might involve celibacy. It might be a temporary break or a long-term lifestyle choice. And yes, it might occasionally involve feeling lonely, missing physical intimacy, or wondering whether you should give dating another shot. None of those experiences makes the decision any less valid.

As Green puts it, what matters most is whether the choice feels like yours.

Because not dating doesn't mean your romantic life has failed, your sex life is over, or you're sitting around waiting for someone to come along. Sometimes, you're just not dating. And that's the whole explanation.

Retailers will need to decide which customers justify a higher acquisition cost. A large first order could be one indicator, but repeat purchases and profitability might need a different customer list defined.

One of the art world’s most famous couples once lived and worked in a gated townhouse a block from Washington Square Park. Jackson Pollock and Lee Krasner didn’t own the MacDougal Alley home—a brick-faced carriage house built in the 1870s—but rather stayed there in the winter of 1950 due to the generosity of its owner, a fellow celebrated artist and their friend Alfonso Ossorio.

Now, Ossorio’s 1,500-square-foot residence is on the market for the first time since 1988, per The New York Post. The asking price for the two-bedroom, two-bath home is nearly $8 million.

The timing comes at a moment of renewed interest in the late couple, who are the subject of a new exhibition at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, “Krasner and Pollock: Past Continuous,” on view through January 31. The retrospective includes 120 works on loan from more than 80 institutions around the world. It is the first show to put Krasner’s and Pollock’s complete bodies of work in dialogue with one another. The exhibit contains many pieces made during the couple’s time in their house in Long Island’s the Springs, where they lived in a modest farmhouse.

Additionally, just weeks ago, ELLE Decor exclusively revealed that Pollock’s onetime artist loft and home in the West Village hit the market for just under $2 million.

Over on MacDougal Alley, both Pollock and Krasner used the 25-foot-wide residence as a studio, per the Post. The three-story structure features hardwood floors, beamed ceilings, and a wood-burning fireplace.

The second-floor kitchen (there’s also one on the ground level) sports a massive skylight that functions more like an angled wall, with a grid of glass panes allowing sunshine to flood the space. A lofted bedroom lined with bookshelves overlooks the space.

Amenities include a south-facing balcony on the second floor, a garden planted with mature trees, and a deeded parking space in front of the home. There’s even the potential of turning the currently bare rooftop into a landscaped sanctuary.

#3836. Palazzo Sogni Brings a Dreamlike New Chapter to Florence

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In Florence, the past is never far from the present, but designing within a centuries-old palazzo requires more than an appreciation for history. At Palazzo Sogni, a new 30-room hotel steps from the Duomo, designers Elena and Giulia Sella faced the challenge of preserving Renaissance architecture and historic frescoes while introducing their own interior identity. Their approach brings together Florentine craftsmanship, contemporary furniture, and a playful sense of discovery, with the idea of “dreaming” connecting spaces throughout the property.

The building dates to the 16th century, when it formed part of Palazzo Testa, constructed by the Martelli family. Among its most significant features are a monumental nymphaeum with a stone fountain traditionally attributed to sculptor Baccio Bandinelli and historic frescoes spanning several centuries, including works by Luigi Catani and Antonio Marini. The conservation process involved removing layers of paint to reveal decorations that had been hidden for years. Rather than treating these historic elements as decorative backdrops, the designers used them to inform the new interiors.

“From the beginning, it was essential to preserve the character and soul of the palazzo,” the Sella sisters explain. Original ceiling frescoes were carefully restored, while marble flooring was recreated to echo the building’s original checkerboard pattern. Contemporary additions, including amber-toned mirrors, custom furniture, and richly textured fabrics, were developed in response to the existing architecture.

That relationship between old and new is particularly evident in the hotel’s 30 rooms and suites, where no two interiors follow the same formula. Historic frescoes informed individual color combinations and material selections, while the proportions of each room determined furniture arrangements and the inclusion of pieces such as chaise longue or dedicated seating areas. Lighting was equally considered, with integrated architectural illumination drawing attention to ceiling paintings and dedicated fixtures highlighting wall frescoes.

The color palette takes its cues directly from the historic decorative schemes. Powder blue, which the designers associate with the hotel’s dreamlike concept, became its signature shade, appearing throughout the reception, corridors, and boutique. Mint green, blush pink, and ochre provide complementary tones, often paired with velvets, silks, and warm metallic accents.

“We focused on materials that create richness through texture rather than excess,” the designers explain. Some velvets have a subtly iridescent finish that introduces depth and movement, while original lime-based wall finishes were preserved wherever possible. The combination allows contemporary furnishings to hold their own against the elaborate historic architecture without competing for attention.

Rather than sourcing existing furniture collections, Elena and Giulia designed every piece specifically for Palazzo Sogni, working with specialized artisans and workshops in Florence and the surrounding region. #2803. Does Contrast Therapy Actually Help You Recover Faster? I Tested It for One Month.

The first 30 seconds are always the worst. My entire body is telling me to get out. My breathing speeds up, my shoulders creep toward my ears, and suddenly all I can think about is how unnecessary it seems to be immersed in very cold water.

Cold plunges are everywhere—alongside saunas at gyms, spas, hotels, and resorts built around moving between hot and cold. Spend enough time on social media, and you’ll see fitness influencers touting contrast hydrotherapy. It can seem like a few minutes in icy water followed by a few minutes in a hot tub is all that stands between a tough long run and better recovery.

As a longtime runner, I’m interested in recovery, but skeptical of wellness trends that promise to make it faster or better. I’ve tried plenty of things that made me feel better without necessarily making me run better.

So I decided to test contrast therapy more consistently.

I kept my running relatively normal rather than designing my training around the experiment. Since I wasn’t training for a race, I averaged about 35 miles a week.

I used contrast therapy primarily after harder efforts—long runs, speed workouts, and runs that left my legs particularly fatigued. Two to three times a week, I did three rounds of ten to 15 minutes of heat followed by two minutes in cold water, keeping the protocol consistent throughout the month.

I tracked how sore and fatigued my legs felt immediately afterward, the following morning, and on my next run. On comparable runs, I also watched my pace and heart rate. I wasn’t expecting those numbers to prove whether contrast therapy worked, but they gave me something to compare with how recovered I felt.

The research on contrast therapy is less definitive than its popularity might suggest. Evidence suggests contrast therapy can reduce muscle soreness and perceived fatigue after strenuous exercise, but whether feeling better translates to improved performance the next day is less clear.

“The benefit is real, but most of it shows up in how recovered a runner feels,” says Raj Desai, MD, a board-certified physical medicine and rehabilitation and pain medicine physician at DISC Sports and Spine Center based in California.

That distinction became central to my experiment. Feeling great afterward didn’t necessarily mean my body had recovered faster. But if I was less sore, slept better, and felt more ready to run, I wanted to know whether that counted for something—even if it never showed up on my watch.

The biggest difference after contrast therapy? I consistently felt less sore and more relaxed—sometimes almost euphoric—after moving between heat and cold. I felt calmer for the rest of the day, often into the next, and slept better, particularly on nights when I did a session. Those benefits were enough that I started looking forward to the ritual itself.

What I didn’t see was the same payoff in my running. My pace and heart rate didn’t noticeably change, nor did I consistently feel more energized on my next run. On a few occasions, I actually felt more tired the following day. In the end, contrast therapy didn’t make my legs feel faster or consistently fresher—it simply made me feel better between workouts.

Whether contrast therapy makes sense may depend largely on when—and why—you use it. Feeling less sore before another race is different from maximizing long-term training adaptations.

“Cold makes the most sense to me when recovery right now is more important than adaptation later,” says Tom Ingegno, DACM, an integrative health practitioner at Charm City Integrative Health. That might mean back-to-back events, heavy training blocks, or racing in significant heat—not automatically plunging after every workout.

That’s partly because cold comes with a potential tradeoff. Regular cold-water immersion immediately after resistance training may blunt some muscle and strength gains, although the evidence for endurance adaptations is less clear. “Feeling recovered and actually being adapted are two different things,” says Adam Nessim, MD, an interventional spine and pain medicine doctor.

Neither side of contrast therapy needs to be extreme. Orthopedic surgeon Nolan Horner, MD, notes that 50 to 59°F is a well-studied range for cold-water immersion, with no clear evidence that painfully colder water works better. Excessive cold can cause hypothermia and cardiovascular strain, while too much heat can lead to dehydration and hyperthermia. He recommends that runners who are pregnant or have heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension, or arrhythmias consult their physician before deliberate hot or cold exposure.

After a month, I wouldn’t put contrast therapy ahead of sleep, smart training, hydration, or healthy eating. And I certainly wouldn’t buy a cold plunge because the internet told me I needed one.

But I would do it again. It didn’t make me faster or consistently more energized on my next run, but I felt less sore, more relaxed, and slept better on the nights I did it. For me, the biggest benefit wasn’t performance; it was how I felt between runs.

I also enjoyed the ritual. Moving from heat into cold forced me to stop. I couldn’t answer emails from inside a plunge or think about my pace. For a few minutes, I was simply paying attention to my breathing. Contrast therapy didn’t erase a hard workout, but it gave me a genuine mental reset.

Unlike a communal spa or bathhouse, the entire experience is private, so you can move between heat and cold at your own pace.

My favorite part was heating up in the sauna, then stepping outside and climbing into the tub I’d filled with cold water. The contrast between the hot sauna, cold water, and quiet wooded setting made this one of the most memorable sessions I tried. Google’s new “Only index content meant for Search” section follows the guide’s advice on spotting spammy accounts and reads:

A neurocognitive study from the University of Exeter reveals that non-invasive brain stimulation can temporarily eliminate a perceptual signature linked to the sexual objectification of women. Using transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) on 76 participants, researchers normalized the classic “inversion effect”, a visual processing marker showing that sexualized female bodies are typically processed mechanistically like objects rather than configural human beings. The electrical stimulation improved recognition of upright female bodies, erasing the perceptual disparity between how male and female images are visually encoded.

Sexual objectification, reducing an individual to a physical body or collection of body parts to be observed or utilized rather than perceived as a complete person, represents a pervasive societal issue. Amplified across advertising, film, and social media feeds, objectification is clinically linked to tolerance of sexual harassment, systemic gender discrimination, and heightened vulnerability to body shame, anxiety disorders, and depression in women.

Cognitive psychologists have long documented that objectification alters visual perception at an automatic, pre-conscious level. When humans look at people, the brain deploys “configural” or holistic processing: we recognize whole bodies and faces far better when upright than when inverted upside down. For inanimate objects like tables, chairs, or cars, this “inversion effect” is largely absent because objects are analyzed as disjointed, piecemeal parts.

Crucially, previous laboratory testing has shown that for both male and female observers, the upright recognition advantage shrinks significantly when viewing sexualized photographs of women, but remains robust when viewing sexualized men, providing empirical evidence that sexualized female bodies are visually encoded more like inanimate tools or objects.

Now, a study led by Professor Ciro Civile at the University of Exeter, published in the journal Social Neuroscience, demonstrates that this automatic perceptual distortion can be temporarily overridden using mild electrical current applied to the scalp.

The randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled investigation enrolled 76 university students (54 female and 22 male). Participants completed an image recognition memory task requiring them to memorize photographs of male and female models in underwear posed in sexualized postures, displayed in both upright and inverted orientations. Participants were subsequently asked to identify the previously viewed models among novel distractor images.

During the task, half of the cohort received 10 minutes of transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS)—a non-invasive neuromodulatory technique passing a weak, sub-sensory electrical current between two scalp electrodes positioned over the left side of the forehead and above the right eyebrow. The remaining participants received a 30-second placebo (sham) stimulation, producing an identical brief scalp-tingling sensation without sustained neuromodulation.

In the placebo cohort, participants replicated the classic cognitive bias: upright images of sexualized men were recognized significantly faster and more accurately than inverted ones, confirming normal configural human processing. For sexualized female models, however, this upright advantage shrank drastically, reflecting fragmented, object-like analysis.

In contrast, participants who received active tDCS displayed a marked perceptual shift:

“Sexual objectification means treating a person as a body to be looked at or used, rather than as a whole person. It’s dehumanising and most often experienced by women,” said Professor Civile. “Our findings are initial evidence that the way our visual system processes sexualised images of women can be changed, at least temporarily, by mildly stimulating the brain. This was a lab study – we didn’t measure attitudes or behaviour, and brain stimulation is no cure for sexism – but it gives us a new way to study how objectification works.”

Historically, social psychologists attempting to mitigate visual objectification modified external variables—such as blurring sexualized body segments, providing humanizing autobiographical backstories, or priming participants to feel lower social power.

“Until now, studies had reduced this bias by changing the images or the viewer’s mindset – for example, by pixelating sexual body parts, giving humanising information, or making people feel less powerful. We acted directly on the brain of the person doing the looking instead,” Professor Civile explained. “Both groups saw exactly the same pictures, so the bias seems to lie in how we process the images rather than in the images themselves, which opens new avenues for understanding how gender-based biases arise in perception.”

The experimental stimulation montage adapts a neurostimulation paradigm previously refined by Civile’s laboratory to attenuate other automated recognition biases, such as the “other-race effect” and own-age bias in facial recognition. However, while those prior facial recognition studies attenuated bias by reducing the processing advantage for in-group faces, the present study achieved parity by elevating the perceptual processing of sexualized women to match holistic human standards.

Because testing occurred during and immediately following stimulation, further studies are required to determine how long this perceptual reset persists and whether modulating these early visual pathways can alter real-world social attitudes, implicit biases, or interpersonal behaviors.

Disrupting objectification: transcranial Direct Current Stimulation (tDCS) enhances the inversion effect toward sexualized women

Sexual objectification is associated with viewing women in an object-like manner, often indexed perceptually by a reduced inversion effect for sexualized women relative to men. Prior research has shown that this index can be attenuated through social manipulations; here we investigated whether it can be modulated through neuromodulation.

Using a double-blind, between-subjects design, participants (N = 76) completed a recognition task involving upright and inverted sexualized targets while receiving either anodal transcranial Direct Current Stimulation (tDCS) (10-mins, 1.5 mA, anode at Fp3) or sham/control stimulation (30-secs, 1.5 mA). The sham group exhibited a significantly reduced inversion effect for sexualized women relative to men, a finding interpreted as an index of objectification.

Critically, this pattern differed between groups (Orientation × Target × tDCS interaction) and was not observed in the anodal tDCS group: the inversion effect for sexualized women was larger than under sham and did not differ significantly from that for men. This modulation was accompanied by improved recognition of upright sexualized women.

These findings provide initial evidence that objectification, as indexed by the inversion effect, can be modulated through neurostimulation. More broadly, the results suggest that the perceptual processes captured by this index are modifiable.
