#1005. Weakened US fuel-efficiency rules and promises of cheaper cars could see drivers pay higher running costs while also increasing carbon emissions.

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Weakened US fuel-efficiency rules and promises of cheaper cars could yet see drivers pay higher running costs while increasing planet-heating emissions.

As part of its "Freedom Means More Affordable Cars" initiative, the US Department of Transportation this week rolled back fuel efficiency rules under the guise of lowering vehicle prices.

The Trump administration's transport agency says US taxpayers will save $138 billion (€122 billion) over the next five years — new vehicles will be $1,300 cheaper on average — since automakers will no longer have to invest in more fuel-efficient fleets.

But this does not account for soaring gas prices as Middle East conflicts choke global supply.

"Any savings on the front end get eaten up and spat out by excess costs of fuel… Fuel economy standards have saved drivers hundreds of billions of dollars," said Democratic Senator Sheldon Whitehouse from Rhode Island in a social media post.

Under the revised Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) rules, vehicle fleets only require an average fuel efficiency of about 35 miles per gallon (14.7 kilometers per liter) by 2031, down around one-third from 50 miles per gallon. Implemented by then-President Joe Biden in 2024, the updated efficiency rules aimed to incentivize the production of low emission electric vehicles and hybrids. The US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) estimates the standards would have saved 70 billion gallons (265 billion litres) of fuel over 25 years.

Meanwhile, nonprofit advocacy group the Union of Concerned Scientists (UCS) says an earlier version of CAFE regulations has saved US consumers about $321 billion at the gas pump since 2010.

As the world's biggest petrol consumer, the US burns through 20 million barrels of oil per day, which is 20% of global consumption, notes Dan Becker, director of the Safe Climate Transport Campaign at the US-based nonprofit environmental group Center for Biological Diversity.

"Transportation is the biggest consumer of that oil, making strong auto standards the most effective way to cut pollution and oil use," Becker said in a statement.

The rules that have been diluted this week would have prevented more than 710 million metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions by 2050 — the equivalent of running 165 million gas-powered passenger vehicles for a year.

"With transportation representing nearly 28% of US greenhouse emissions, rolling back fuel economy standards is a dangerous setback for near-term climate goals," said Ben Scott, head of Energy Demand at UK-based climate think tank Carbon Tracker. "It risks keeping inefficient combustion engines on the road much longer than climate science demands," he told DW.

But following President Donald Trump's elimination of greenhouse gas emissions standards for light- and heavy-duty vehicles last year, Dave Cooke, senior vehicles analyst for the UCS Clean Transportation Program, said the rollbacks merely compound "a much more significant step backwards."

He said the trend sends "a specific signal to automakers," adding that "the United States is seeking to maximize oil usage to increase oil company profits."

This will be to the detriment of climate targets, with high-polluting oil contributing one-third of global fossil fuel emissions that continue to rise — along with temperatures.

The world just had the hottest August on record as severe heatwaves in Europe caused more than 16,000 excess deaths in Germany alone.

Sales of low emission EVs are down in the US in 2026, especially after Donald Trump scrapped the $7,500 tax credit for new electric vehicles.

American automakers also continue to push back against regulations that would incentivize a switch to EVs, arguing there is a lack of consumer demand.

"It's highly unlikely that the United States will suddenly become awash in electric options when neither the auto industry nor regulators seem interested in providing such choices for consumers," said Cooke.

But Ben Scott believes that while softening US fuel economy standards "will create temporary headwinds", it won't stall an inevitable transition to electric vehicles.

"With EVs delivering vastly lower running costs, electrification becomes a clear-cut economic choice," he said of cars running on "cheaper, domestically secure energy that will drive the transition forward, regardless of short-term politics."

As less efficient cars and trucks consume more oil that's more expensive and in shorter supply, "the fragility of oil-dependent transport" is set to be exposed, he said. Edited by: Tamsin Walker

#955. Nobel Prize in chemistry goes to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai won the Nobel Prize in chemistry for their groundbreaking discoveries related to the origin of chirality and life's building blocks.

The Nobel Prize in chemistry on Wednesday was awarded to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai "for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis."

Kagan, who is 95 years old and was born in France, is affiliated with France’s Université Paris-Sud, and Soai, who is 76 years old and was born in Japan, is based at the Tokyo University of Science.

The scientists share the Nobel Prize for answering longstanding questions in chemistry about life's origins.

Specifically, the duo solved an age-old mystery that has long puzzled chemists: some molecules, like amino acids, exist as two variants that are mirror images of one another, but just one of the variants out of the two is found in living organisms.

The two answered questions about why life chooses one variant over another.

The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences announced the winner in Stockholm.

In chemistry, life's building blocks are what scientists call homochiral, after the Greek words for "same" and "hand."

The Nobel Committee said the chemistry prize recognizes discoveries that have enabled chemists to drive chemical reactions that lead to homochirality.

"Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai have provided a solution to a chemical mystery that is over a century old: how homochirality can emerge spontaneously," Heiner Linke, chair of the Nobel Committee for Chemistry, said in a news release. "The chemical reactions they have developed are spectacular."

The duo's discoveries, the news release said, "have been decisive for chemists who design reactions for the manufacture of pharmaceuticals."

#34. 'Star Trek' star Patrick Stewart dies age 86

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The Shakespearean actor landed the role of a lifetime as "Star Trek" Captain Picard. He passed away at age 86.

For Star Trek fans, he was the legendary Captain Picard; the British actor and director Patrick Stewart, longtime member of the  Royal Shakespeare Company, died on Thursday at the age of 86 following a "brief and poignant journey" with Alzheimer's disease and Lewy body dementia, according to an official statement on his social media accounts.

In Los Angeles, California, far away from the small English village of Mirfield where he was born, Stewart achieved world fame. He was 47 years old when he landed the role of a lifetime in the legendary science fiction series Star Trek: Playing Captain Jean-Luc Picard in "Star Trek: The Next Generation," he stepped into the shoes of the legendary Captain James T. Kirk, played by William Shatner.

Stewart brought a calm, measured and distinctly British presence that quickly made Picard a fan favorite and a cult figure in the Star Trek universe. He returned to the role decades later in the spin-off series "Star Trek: Picard," which ended in 2023, proving the character's enduring appeal.

As Captain Jean-Luc Picard, he led the crew of the starship Enterprise, a spaceship with the sizeable mission: "To explore strange new worlds. To seek out new life and new civilizations" — and most importantly, "to boldly go where no man has gone before!"

Stewart's interest in acting was sparked in the early 1950s when a teacher asked him to recite a work by Shakespeare. His passion for Shakespeare stayed with him until the end.

His parents were far removed from the glamor of the theatrical world. His father was a highly respected officer in the British Army during World War II, but at home, he was an abusive husband, and young Patrick had to helplessly witness repeated violence against his mother. Stewart wrote about his experiences with domestic violence in The Guardian in 2009. The stage, he confessed there, seemed to him "a far safer place" than reality; he saw acting as a way to escape his home life.

Patrick began acting at the age of 12. At 16, he left school early and, thanks to a scholarship, studied acting in Bristol, England. Along the way, he worked as a journalist. In 1965, he became an official member of the Royal Shakespeare Company, and remained with the theather company from 1966 to 1982. Such was his passion for the great English playwright and lyricist that he even shared his sonnets on his Twitter account during the 2020 coronavirus pandemic.

Stewart dazzled the theater world with his own stage adaptation of Charles Dickens' 1843 tale "A Christmas Carol." He played more than 30 roles himself. After its 1987 premiere in Stewart's hometown of Mirfield, the one-man show became a critics' favorite and a long-running hit on Broadway in New York.

In the same year,  Stewart went from being a stage actor to an international TV star. Star Trek producer Robert Justman offered him the role of Captain Jean-Luc Picard, one of the main characters in a new edition of the popular TV series "Star Trek: The Next Generation."

The successful Shakespearean hesitated. Until then, he had never worked in Hollywood. But his agent encouraged him to accept the role, Stewart recalled in a 2020 interview with the German newspaper Die Welt. "You can accept the offer as a start for now. It won't be a big deal, you can't revive a series as iconic as Star Trek anyway."

But his agent was to be proven wrong: Stewart led the Starship Enterprise through its galactic adventures for seven seasons and four full-length feature films. The TV series had astronomical success on Earth in a very short time. At first, the producers were at odds as to whether Stewart's British accent could really fit the role of commander of a US starship in the 24th century, but it soon became impossible to imagine the series without the subtly diplomatic Captain Picard, who often quoted Shakespeare while at the helm. It seemed as if all the time he had spent as a Shakespearean actor had prepared him to command the Enterprise, Stewart once remarked with a wink. When the cult series ended in 1994, he had ruled out ever returning to the Enterprise's command center.

In 2010, the actor was knighted by the Queen of England. Stewart considered it the greatest honor of his career to stand alongside acting greats such as Sir Laurence Olivier and Sir Alec Guinness.

Beyond the screen, Stewart has long used his voice for social and human rights causes. In recent years, he has drawn attention to issues such as violence against women, age discrimination and the protection of children.

In a June 2020 interview, "Playboy" asked the New York resident whether he was considering retiring from acting. "Never! That would mean giving up my life," replied Stewart, who was approaching his 80th birthday at the time. He was filming the second of three seasons of "Star Trek: Picard." The series ends with a final episode in which the retired Starfleet admiral makes his last appearance. In the series, Picard retires to his family vineyard in France but is drawn back into space to confront new threats to the Federation.

This series also showcases Stewart's affection for his pit bull, Ginger. In "Star Trek: Picard," Picard also has a pit bull, a loyal four-legged companion named Number One — a deliberate reference to Stewart's real-life love of the breed. For his efforts to combat prejudice against pit bulls and to support their adoption, Stewart was honored by the US animal welfare organization ASPCA, among others.

In 2024, he published his memoir "Making it So." In it, he recounts his passion for theater and his love of Shakespeare.

Now, at the age of 86, the British actor has finally departed for another galaxy. Farewell, Patrick Stewart!

#70. Trump approval rating is historically low for a president heading into the midterms

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Republicans are facing a difficult political environment ahead of November’s midterm elections, driven in large part by President Donald Trump’s historically low approval ratings.

With the pivotal elections approaching, Trump’s approval rating dropped to 30 percent earlier this month, according to polling from the Pew Research Center. The slump marks the lowest point of his second term and represents the lowest level any sitting president has reached heading into the midterms since Harry Truman in 1946, as reported by TIME.

“This is historically low approval ratings for a president,” Peter Enns, a professor in the department of government at Cornell University and co-founder of the survey research company Verasight, told TIME.

Data indicates that much of the public discontent with the administration stems from economic performance. An Associated Press-NORC Center for Public Affairs Research poll released earlier this month found that only 26 percent of U.S. adults approved of how Trump was tackling the economy, and just 17 percent approved of his handling of the cost of living.

Foreign policy has generated similar pushback. The same AP-NORC poll showed that only 28 percent of respondents expressed support for Trump’s handling of the war in Iran.

“Part of the reason Trump’s approval ratings are so low is he’s pushing policies and pushing positions that are very unpopular, and so the conflict in Iran is a great example; the public understands that this was Trump’s decision, and they understand this connects to extremely high gas prices,” Enns told TIME. “It’s very unusual for a President to position himself on the side of so many unpopular positions.”

These voter priorities could prove decisive at the ballot box. Pew Research data shows that 84 percent of registered voters rank the economy as a “very important issue” heading into the midterms, while 57 percent say foreign policy is “very important” to their vote.

Midterm elections are routinely difficult for the party controlling the White House, as voters frequently express frustration with the sitting administration. This dynamic is a “well-known phenomenon” resulting from dissatisfaction among both opposition voters and a president's supporters who feel policy progress has lagged, Enns told TIME.

“That doesn’t mean everybody’s changing their vote,” Enns said. “It just means the backlash, the tides, are working against the incumbent president.”

Costas Panagopoulos, a professor of political science at Northeastern University, told the magazine that voters often sought “partisan balance in Washington,” particularly when one party held unified control over the executive and legislative branches.

“There’s simply nobody else to blame for government actions and current policies if voters are unhappy with those besides the Republican Party [right now],” Panagopoulos said. “It’s very easy for [voters] to point the finger to the Republicans if they’re not happy with what they’re seeing.”

Though historical precedents usually favor the opposition party, rare exceptions have occurred. Following the September 11 terrorist attacks, President George W. Bush maintained a 63 percent approval rating, allowing Republicans to gain seats in both the House and Senate during the 2002 midterms.

Current polling metrics favor Democrats in voter engagement. A recent PBS News/NPR/Marist poll showed that 91 percent of Democrats expressed enthusiasm about voting in the midterms, compared to 86 percent of Republicans. Democrats have also shown strength in competitive state races, including in Texas, where Democratic Senate candidate James Talarico holds a narrow lead in polls over Republican candidate Ken Paxton.

“I think we can expect Republicans to be facing an uphill battle at the polls,” Panagopoulos said. “We can expect that they will likely lose seats in the House and also the Senate. And the question is, will they lose enough seats to lose the majority in either or both those chambers?”

#303. Cornell begins no confidence vote over handling of alleged gang rape

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Faculty vote will continue until next Friday and could send message that school’s president has lost their confidence.

Cornell University has begun a no-confidence vote for the school’s leadership after an alleged gang rape prompted public outcry over sexual assault on college campuses and scrutiny over the university’s handling of the case.

Faculty members started the no-confidence vote on Thursday that is expected to continue until November 6. The initiative is non-binding, but could send a message that president Michael Kotlikoff has lost the confidence of the school’s faculty.

“Hundreds of students, staff, faculty and alumni approached the sponsors of this resolution to emphasize to us that the Jane Doe case is but a tip of the iceberg,” said Paul Ortiz, the lead faculty sponsor of the resolution.

A lawsuit filed last month alleges that a female student, identified as Jane Doe, was drugged and gang raped by seven male students at a fraternity house in 2024.

The case has raised questions about whether the university acted with sufficient urgency or downplayed the alleged abuse in order to protect its reputation. The university initially shut down the fraternity and suspended the male students, two of whom were later expelled.

Faculty considered on Wednesday whether to postpone a vote of no confidence, with some urging patience and others arguing that the vote was a necessary response to institutional reluctance to take the issue seriously.

The Associated Press news agency reported that the debate took place after a group of 20 female science and engineering faculty members called on Kotlikoff to resign in a letter to trustees.

“What our community needed was clear leadership; instead, it faced a leadership vacuum,” the letter stated.

Kotlikoff sought to address the fallout in a statement earlier this month in which he listed a number of preliminary responses.

“I am deeply sorry for the harm Jane Doe suffered in 2024, and appalled that this act of sexual violence occurred within our Cornell community,” he said.

Two of the men accused of taking part in the alleged rape of Jane Doe have asked for New York Attorney General Letitia James to be removed from her role overseeing the renewed criminal probe into the case over social media posts from the attorney general expressing support for the accuser prior to her appointment.

A judge on Thursday appeared sceptical of one of the challenges, which James’ office has said are “without merit”.

#655. ‘A clear alliance’: Nations accused of undermining science at UN nature talks

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Saudi Arabia, Russia, Argentina and Brazil are said to be frustrating progress at the UN biodiversity meeting in Yerevan, Armenia.

YEREVAN - Political tensions are mounting in the final days of the United Nations biodiversity talks with a handful of countries accused of trying to weaken the science that underpins global nature policy.

The growing feud over the role of science has alarmed veteran negotiators and follows recent efforts by fossil fuel-reliant nations to delay reports by the IPCC, the global authority on climate change.

“The same thing is happening here as what is taking place with the IPCC,” said a French government delegate at the COP17 summit in Yerevan, Armenia.

The delegate requested anonymity to discuss ongoing negotiations.

The delegate said four countries – Saudi Arabia, Russia, Argentina and Brazil – were frustrating progress.

The pushback is directed at two scientific assessments by the UN-backed IPBES biodiversity panel on the overlap between the nature and climate crises and the economic forces driving them.

Nearly 200 nations were due to agree how these landmark findings should guide efforts to protect nature but some countries “did not want to acknowledge them”, the French delegate added.

A negotiator from another developed nation told AFP these four countries were also leading this push.

“There is a clear alliance of Russia, Saudi, Argentina and, to some extent, Brazil, all working together to try to water down IPBES reports, not welcome them, or use them as bargaining chips in other negotiation items,” the negotiator said on condition of anonymity.

Much of the dispute boils down to a few paragraphs of text in a draft agreement and whether countries should “welcome” or “take note” of the IPBES findings.

The latter phrasing is considered far weaker in the language of UN negotiations, where every word is closely scrutinised.

The current draft was “a text without ambition”, said the French delegate.

“If we no longer have a scientific basis to work from, then everything we discuss here is merely a matter of opinion,” the delegate added.

Academics and civil society activists at COP17 have already issued a joint statement of concern at the state of negotiations.

When approached by AFP in Yerevan, IPBES chair David Obura declined to comment as the talks were still ongoing.

Although the summit is due to end on Oct 30, a number of outstanding matters – including around the science – remain unresolved.

The dispute echoes a growing pattern in consensus-based global negotiations, where oil-producing countries are accused of frustrating efforts to tackle fossil fuels, plastic trash and other environmental harm.

Climate science has long been a flashpoint and the IPCC is currently deadlocked, as countries including Saudi Arabia and India push to delay publication of its next set of benchmark reports on global warming.

In an unprecedented decision in 2025, a crucial summary of the Global Environment Outlook, a flagship UN scientific report, was torpedoed as nations rowed over fossil fuels.

“We’ve pretty much always seen IPCC reports being politicised...,” said the negotiator from the developed country.

“But in the biodiversity world they’ve been much less contentious until the last few years, and this COP in particular.”

“They’re never easy, no decision is. There’s always nitpicking over language – but it’s gotten far worse.”

Bethan Laughlin, a senior policy specialist for the Zoological Society of London who observed the COP17 negotiations firsthand, said the trend was worrying.

“The point really isn’t this particular text, although it is highly significant. The point is the precedent it sets,” she told AFP in Yerevan. AFP

#981. An EU country has reopened the Russia channel. What brought it back?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

By Ksenia Smertina, senior lecturer at the HSE Institute for Media, expert at the Russian International Affairs Council on Eastern and Central Europe

By Ksenia Smertina, senior lecturer at the HSE Institute for Media, expert at the Russian International Affairs Council on Eastern and Central Europe

On October 26, 2026, the Russian-Slovak Intergovernmental Commission resumed its work. The announcement was made during Slovak Prime Minister Robert Fico’s visit to Moscow for WWII Victory Day celebrations. The timing of the meeting is particularly favorable for the ruling coalition led by Fico’s SMER party. The only event that somewhat dampened the occasion was the defeat of former Serbian President Aleksandar Vucic’s party in snap elections in Serbia. Vucic’s foreign policy has often been likened to Fico’s approach in Slovakia. Below, we explore why these stories are fundamentally different and what is really happening in the context of Russia-Slovakia relations, as well as in Slovakia itself, ahead of the election on September 26, 2027.

Although the resumption of the intergovernmental commission was announced in May, the talks did not take place until six months later. On the Slovak side, they were led by a close ally of Fico and the current foreign affairs minister, Juraj Blanar. The lists of negotiators on both sides were not disclosed until the last moment. The date of the negotiations was also shrouded in secrecy and was not revealed until last week during an EU summit, which, according to reports, was marked by a “terrible” atmosphere and was “unworthy of a meeting of the European Council.”

Fico’s attendance was uncertain right up to the last minute, with Bratislava skillfully playing this card. Slovak diplomats maintained suspense, suggesting that Fico’s visit depended on the “substantiality of concessions,” keeping the EU on its toes regarding a possible geopolitical demarche. Ultimately, Fico stayed in Bratislava, crafting a perfect political alibi before Brussels while sending a full government delegation to Moscow to tackle pressing political and economic challenges. He skillfully employed this maneuver, illustrating that in today’s landscape, ‘glitch-art diplomacy’ – characterized by protocol breaches, unpredictability, and exploiting systemic errors – remains effective. Though not large in size, Slovakia is a critical node through which the East and West continue to exchange signals.

The timing of this event strikes a perfect balance for the ruling authorities, both in terms of domestic political dynamics and the wider European context. First, municipal elections held in Slovakia over the past weekend confirmed the strength of the current coalition. Fico and his allies demonstrated their dominance across regions and industrial hubs, leaving the pro-Western opposition confined to Bratislava. This electoral victory has secured a solid backing for the prime minister, giving him the freedom to pursue bold foreign policy initiatives.

The external context vividly illustrated to the Slovak audience the validity of the ruling coalition’s stance. The recent EU summit only deepened the rift among allies. The European Commission attempted to impose new climate quotas, but the countries of the Visegrad Group clearly expressed their dissent, with Czech Prime Minister Andrej Babis openly criticizing Ursula von der Leyen for her ‘green transition’ dogmas. This unfolded against the backdrop of a shocking report from Eurostat, revealing that most EU countries are facing budget deficits, with the highest figures recorded in Hungary (7.2%) and Romania (9.2%).

Amid this dismal European landscape, news from Slovakia underscores the looming threat of deindustrialization. German automotive giant Porsche recently announced its decision to shift production of its Cayenne model from Bratislava back to Leipzig, aiming to salvage its parent company’s facilities in Germany, which are struggling due to the energy crisis. The risk of German capital flight poses an existential threat to the national industry, considering the fact that businesses depend heavily on accessible energy resources.

In light of these developments, Fico’s rejection of Ukrainian leader Vladimir Zelensky’s proposal for a €1.5 billion compensation package in exchange for halting gas transit appears to be a rational choice. For the Slovak public, which is opposed to prolonging the conflict, there’s no clear way to survive without supplies of hydrocarbons from the East. By refusing the offer, Fico has made it clear that virtual ‘paper’ funds from Kiev won’t heat factories or convince foreign automotive companies to remain in the country.

The defeat of Vucic’s team in Serbia during the early parliamentary elections is unpleasant news for Bratislava. Many Western analysts predict a similar fate for Fico in the upcoming parliamentary elections in September 2027. However, a fundamental difference between Belgrade and Bratislava must be noted. The Serbian scenario demonstrated that, in turbulent times, success goes to those who can balance the loyalty of the silent majority with pressure from the crowds.

Fico is purposefully venturing into this precarious territory. Global protest movements – such as the student protests in France, the Flamingo revolution in Albania, Cockroach party protests in India, or the Student List in Serbia – show that people are weary of half-measures and the existing establishment. The paradox of Fico lies in his unique strategy. He manages to remain the foremost systemic critic of Brussels’ supranational doctrines while being a key player in European politics. Fico deftly harnesses protest potential before it can turn against him.

This position is driven by a need to protect the country’s real economy. Slovakia relies heavily on exports, which account for around 90% of GDP, with the automotive industry being the cornerstone of the economy. This sector employs over 230,000 people, meaning that nearly 1 in 10 Slovak families depends on it directly. This established industry cannot be sustained by illusions of a green transition or even by Slovakia’s own nuclear power, since its costs remain tightly linked to fluctuating gas prices.

This is why Fico demonstrates remarkable flexibility in foreign policy matters, except when it comes to energy supplies from the East. This sets him apart from Vucic, who tried to maintain a delicate balance and compromise with Brussels, ultimately trapping himself in a web of sanctions, losing legitimacy among his patriotic electorate, and facing a significant electoral defeat. Fico learns from the mistakes of others and avoids half-measures entirely. Amid the wave of protests and strikes sweeping across Europe, his overt refusal to follow the EU’s general approach of replacing Russian energy resonates with ordinary Slovaks. People see Fico as a leader who is ready to defend national industries rather than abstract EU directives.

On October 26, a Slovak delegation arrived in Moscow, illustrating Fico’s commitment to defending national interests. Behind the closed doors of the 22nd meeting of the intergovernmental commission, Foreign Minister Juraj Blanar and Russian Minister of Science and Higher Education Valery Falkov focused exclusively on practical and vital issues for Bratislava.

As anticipated, energy security and the nuclear sector took center stage. Blanar signed final protocols ensuring stable resource supplies for Slovak energy companies and factories. Additionally, both sides strengthened cooperation in pragmatic areas such as agriculture and ecology, while also addressing culturally sensitive topics relevant to conservative Slovak voters, such as humanitarian issues and military memorials.

“We reject the construction of a new Iron Curtain between the EU and Russia, and we consider it of utmost importance – even during this challenging period – to maintain open lines of communication,” Blanar said in Moscow.

In summary, the resumption of the work of the intergovernmental commission should not be viewed as a concession to Russia. Fico understands that his political survival, along with that of his party in the September 2027 election, hinges not on the approval of the European elites or the slogans of Bratislava’s liberal street protesters. His fate will be determined in the factories of automotive giants and in the kitchens of ordinary Slovak families, where heating bills and job security take precedence over the geopolitical ambitions of Brussels.

The recent talks in Moscow can be seen as an effort to establish a protective shield over industry before the European energy crisis completely devastates Eastern Europe’s automotive sector.

Unlike Serbia, where the government fell victim to its own half-measures, Bratislava is betting on clarity. By turning down ‘paper’ compensation from Kiev, Fico offers his electorate tangible results.
