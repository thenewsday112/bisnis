#727. A Huge and Powerful New Simulation Helps Explain the Universe's Reionization

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Modern astronomy features massive telescopes with segmented mirrors, huge digital cameras, sophisticated data pipelines, and suites of powerful instruments with different specific purposes. But that's the observational side. Making sense of the exquisitely-detailed data these telescopes deliver requires its own approach.

This is where simulations come in. We live in an era of increasingly detailed cosmological simulations, all run on the world's fastest supercomputers. Cosmologists use them to understand everything from star formation to the expansion of the Universe.

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope (NGRST) are the two most capable space telescopes in existence. They're especially good at illuminating the Universe's very early days with their infrared capabilities. While the NGRST is just getting going, the JWST has been busy for a few years and has already delivered some surprises. The NGRST was only launched two months ago, and its mission hasn't really started yet. But once it does, it may deliver some surprises of its own.

This is where a new simulation from the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian (CfA) comes in. Its name is Lumina, and it's one of the largest and most detailed simulations yet.

Lumina is presented in new research in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society titled "Introducing the lumina project: large-volume radiation-hydrodynamic simulations of the epochs of hydrogen and helium reionization." The lead author Oliver Zier, an Institute for Theory and Computation Fellow at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian.

"Understanding how galaxies and active galactic nuclei (AGNs) jointly drive the reionization of the intergalactic medium (IGM) across cosmic time remains a major challenge in cosmology," the authors write. "We present Lumina, a large-volume radiation-hydrodynamic simulation thatself-consistently follows the coupled evolution of the IGM, galaxies, and AGNs through H i, He i, and He ii reionization down to redshift z = 3."

Redshift z=3 corresponds to a lookback time of about 11.7 billion years, or about 2.1 billion years after the Big Bang. This is in the Cosmic Noon, which is when star formation peaked in the Universe, and when quasars were most active. It's also at the end of Helium II reionization, which is usually considered the last phase transition in the IGM, after neutral hydrogen had already reionized.

"Its galaxy population remains consistent with the original IllustrisTNG project, while the larger volume improves statistics for rare objects, large-scale environments, and cosmic variance, enabling forward modelling of observables linking H i and He ii topologies to the evolving galaxy and AGN populations," the researchers explain.

Exactly how the Universe's neutral hydrogen and helium reionized is still not fully understood. The contribution from the various sourcesâstars and supermassive black holes and their AGNâis unclear. The timeline on which it all played out is also something cosmologists are still puzzling over. Did it end all at once, in a smooth denouement? Or was it patchy? The JWST's observations are helping drive a new effort to understand it all.

âWith JWST, the early universe has become an exciting new frontier in astrophysics,â lead author Zier said in a press release. âTo understand what we are seeing with this telescope, we need to translate our theories into detailed predictions. After several years of work, with plenty of ups and downs, it is incredibly rewarding to see the simulation results and watch our predictions come to life in video.â

Lumina has a larger volume than another well-known simulation, Illustris TNG. TNG 300 was its largest run, with a box side of about 3.3 megaparsecs. But Lumina has a box side of 500 Mpc. (Simulator nerds are right to point out that these are co-moving parsecs.) So Lumina simulates an area about 1.5 billion light years on each side, which is roughly the same as astronomers can see with our most powerful telescopes.

Though hydrogen reionization has been studied extensively, since it's the most abundant element in the Universe, helium reionization hasn't been. Lumina captures helium reionization and feeds those results into the rest of the simulation. This sheds light on helium reionization's effect on the cosmos.

While hydrogen was reionized predominantly by lower-energy ultraviolet photons from star-forming dwarf galaxies, they weren't energetic enough to reionize helium, which made up about 25% of the IGM. It had to wait for a massive population of quasars and extremely luminous active galactic nuclei, and those populations peaked during Cosmic Noon.

But extending a simulation to cover helium reionization wasn't easy. "Modelling He ii reionization introduces significant computational challenges," the authors explain. "To reach z = 3, the simulation must evolve for almost an additional gigayear after the end of H i reionization, during which the matter distribution becomes increasingly clustered."

To accomplish that, Lumina runs on more than 15,000 graphics processing units on the Oak Ridge National Laboratory's Frontier supercomputer, the third fastest supercomputer in the world, after El Capitan and Line Shine. Frontier had the power to compute a massive volume of space at the required level of detail.

Lumina's first results on He II reionization are a good match with observations.

"He ii reionization begins at z (about equal to) 6 and is driven by the hard radiation field from AGNs. We find a midpoint at z (about equal to) 4 and near completion by z = 3, with a residual He ii volume filling fraction below 1 per cent, in agreement with current observational constraints," the authors write. " This demonstrates that Lumina can self-consistently follow the onset, topology, and completion of helium reionization alongside hydrogen reionization."

Simulations and observations together drive progress in cosmology, and it looks like theorists now have another powerful tool in their toolbox.

âWe feel incredibly fortunate to be building simulations like Lumina,â said co-author Aaron Smith, an assistant professor of physics at the University of Texas at Dallas. âFor the first time, we can follow along as both hydrogen and helium changed into their modern form across such an enormous volume of the universe, while still resolving the galaxies and other luminous objects driving that transformation.â

#735. Scientists Can Now Spot Hidden Sunspots on the Solar Far Side

Unlike the Moon, the Sun is constantly rotating from our perspective - but we still only see half of it at any given time. Since it takes 27 days to complete a full rotation, that leaves plenty of time for changes to happen to the side we canât see that could directly impact Earthâs own space weather a few weeks later. Now a team of physicists led by Bibhuti Kumar Jha of the Southwest Research Institute has published a paper in The Astrophysical Journal Supplement Series that uses probability to map out what might be happening on the far side of the Sun, even if we canât see it directly.

In the past, space weather forecasters have struggled with this critical cosmic blind spot. Coronal Mass Ejections, one of the most dangerous forms of space weather, can fry satellites or overload ground-based power grids. And the conditions for them could form on the side of the Sun facing away from us so that we only get warning of them once they turn the corner of the Sun itself.

Sure, we had computer simulations of what might be happening around the Sunâs back, but they were mainly just guesses. So the authors turned to a technique that, though it has been around for a few decades, is still an evolving field of research. Helioseismology is similar to traditional seismology on Earth, where geologists use seismic shockwaves from earthquakes to map the interior parts of Earth that we canât see from the outside.

Solar physicists took that same general concept and modified it for use on the Sun. It may sound impossible to âseeâ through a giant ball of burning plasma, but it's possible with high energy acoustic waves. The churning motions that power the convective zone of the Sun itself also create sound waves that both bounce inside the Sun itself and reflect off its surface. Crucially, if those sound waves happen to pass through an active region, the magnetic fields concentrated there will slightly shift their travel time.

While that doesnât sound like much to go on, instruments like the Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager aboard NASAâs Solar Dynamics Observatory and the ground-based Global Oscillation Network Group are able to measure these slight ripples on the near side of the Sun, and use some fancy math to figure out where the magnetic knots representing sunspots are gathering on the far side. The problem has been reliability.

To solve the noise problem, Jha and his co-authors turned to historical data from a mission called STEREO. This involved two NASA spacecraft that were placed around the far side of the Sun, but still within direct communication lines of Earth. The impossible is becoming possible. Last week, Italian climber Pietro Vidi announced that he had made the second ascent of the world’s hardest trad climb: Drifter’s Escape (5.15a) in Squamish, British Columbia.

The 65-foot route was first climbed by Connor Herson in July 2025. Afterward, Herson kept his send a secret for seven months until he was ready to release a documentary about it. The achievement later earned Herson a Golden Piton.

Drifter’s follows a series of parallel cracks through an otherwise blank headwall of white granite, with two V11 cruxes. The climb does include two bolts at the beginning, which makes it technically a mixed climb. But traditional gear protects the rest of the route, including the cruxes.

Local climber Ethan Salvo, who has projected the route with both Herson and Vidi, said the difficulty of Drifter’s primarily stems from “how punchy the whole upper half is.” Herson famously tackled the first V11 boulder problem by performing a “pogo.” As he bumped his right hand up,  he generated momentum by swinging out with his right leg—a sporty move typically seen in the gym, not on rock.

While the first ascent and first repeat of Drifter’s Escape mark a new era in hard trad climbing, both Herson and Vidi agreed that the discipline will continue to progress in terms of grades. “Trad is not even near the limit,” Vidi told Climbing.

“Trad climbing has the most immediate potential for progression,” Herson shared with Climbing shortly after announcing his ascent in February. Both climbers also feel aligned that the primary challenge in pushing the limits of trad further is finding more hard trad lines.

Vidi has been ticking hard lines for many years, but he gained particular notoriety in the spring of 2025, when he made the second free ascent of Lurking Fear (5.13c; 2000ft), one of the most popular aid lines on El Cap. Since Beth Rodden and Tommy Caldwell first freed the route in 2000, no climber had managed to free every pitch until Vidi ended the quarter-century dry spell.

#657. Scientists Reverse 60 Years of Aging in Human Cells, Turning 80-Year-Old Cells Into 20-Year-Olds

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Cells from an 80-year-old were reprogrammed to a molecular age below 20, and the slow-motion reset could help reveal how cellular rejuvenation works.

What if scientists could turn back the biological clock of an 80-year-old human cell, making it behave like one from someone younger than 20? Researchers in Germany have accomplished just that, reversing more than 60 years of molecular aging while transforming blood cell precursors into neural stem cells capable of producing neurons.

The discovery, made by scientists at the University Hospital of Bonn and the University of Bonn, could provide new insights into one of biology’s biggest mysteries: how aging can be reversed at the cellular level. Unlike previous methods that reset a cell’s molecular age relatively quickly, this approach works gradually, allowing researchers to watch the rejuvenation process unfold over more than 100 days.

Published in Aging Cell, the study offers scientists an unusual opportunity to investigate what drives cellular rejuvenation, what slows it down, and whether those mechanisms could eventually help address age-related diseases such as Alzheimer’s.

Most cells in the body carry essentially the same genetic instructions, yet develop into specialized types with different jobs. Their identity depends largely on which instructions they use. Under normal conditions, a blood cell cannot simply become a neuron.

Researchers can redirect a cell’s identity through reprogramming, using combinations of proteins called transcription factors to change gene activity and guide its development. This approach has already allowed scientists to grow nerve tissue from skin cells, which could eventually contribute to treatments for neurodegenerative diseases.

The Bonn team used reprogramming to turn red blood cell precursors into neural stem cells. In earlier experiments, neurons produced through this approach formed connections with existing neurons after transplantation into mouse brains.

“In our current study, however, we focused on a different phenomenon: namely, the observation that cells become significantly rejuvenated during reprogramming,” says Prof. Dr. Oliver Brüstle, director of the Institute of Reconstructive Neurobiology at the University Hospital of Bonn and a member of the Transdisciplinary Research Area (TRA) Life & Health at the University of Bonn.

To track rejuvenation, the researchers examined chemical modifications to DNA that change with age. These modifications influence how genetic information is used without rewriting the DNA sequence itself (epigenetics). Epigenetic clocks use patterns in these markers to estimate a cell’s molecular age.

After reprogramming, cells from an 80-year-old registered an age below 20, a reduction of more than 60 years after 50 days.

“We also know that this epigenetic rejuvenation ensures that the cells actually behave like young cells,” says Brüstle.

Scientists had previously observed rejuvenation during a two-step reprogramming process. A specialized cell is first returned to a pluripotent stem cell state, giving it the potential to become virtually any cell type. Researchers then direct it to become a neural stem cell, with a much narrower range of developmental possibilities.

Unlike the conventional two-step approach, which produces rapid rejuvenation, the Bonn team converted blood cell precursors directly into neural stem cells without passing through a pluripotent stage. The cells’ molecular clocks reset gradually, allowing researchers to track the process for more than 100 days.

That extended timeline gives scientists an opportunity to examine the process as it happens and test what speeds it up or holds it back.

“Rejuvenation that extends over such a long period of time is ideal as an experimental model: since the epigenetic clocks are slowly reset over several weeks, we can use this model to investigate which factors and active substances accelerate or slow down the rejuvenation process.”

For Brüstle, understanding those controls is particularly relevant to brain research: “After all, age is the most important risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s!”

#1247. 7 Designer-Approved Paint Tricks That Make Any Small Room Feel Bigger

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

There are plenty of tried-and-true tricks for making a small room seem bigger: mirrors that bounce light and allow reflections to create the feeling of space, reducing clutter, and carefully curating your decor. While these approaches certainly work, designers are making the case for turning to paint. In fact, there are some who believe it’s the most effective way to make a diminutive space seem larger.

“Paint is a great way to make a small room feel larger because it changes the visual perception of the space,” says Joe Waroquier, founder and principal designer of Joe Waroquier Home.

Amber Guyton, interior designer and founder of Blessed Little Bungalow, agrees, and says, “Painting a small space makes the room feel bigger because it gives the eye an illusion that the space has no bounds. This is one of my favorite things to do with powder rooms and bedrooms that need to feel like you're stepping into a different world that's cozy versus cramped.”

To harness paint tricks that designers always turn to for small rooms, read on.

If you don’t want to go all in with a color, painting an accent wall is a great way to integrate color while not overwhelming the space.

As Guyton says, “Painting an accent wall can give a space a pop of color while also visually elongating the room to one that feels larger.”

On the flip side, if you’re willing to go bold with color in a small space, color drenching, when done just right, can be a way to make a small space seem larger.

“My favorite, and likely the most effective, way to make a room look bigger is to select one color to apply to all of the walls, trim, doors, and ceiling in a space,” says interior designer Jewel Marlowe. “This color-drenching technique blurs the architectural lines, and the eye loses track of where walls stop and ceilings begin.”

She says that this trick works when using saturated colors (such as deep green, blue, or burgundy) or light colors.

“If you like the concept, but want a little more visual interest, try painting the walls in a wipeable matte finish and the trim and cabinetry in a high-gloss finish,” Marlowe adds.

In a small room, “pay attention to undertones, not just whether a color is light or dark,” Waroquier says. “There is this line of thinking that light, pale colors automatically make a room feel larger—but warm, muddy undertones of light-colored paints actually can counteract the 'light and bright' feeling of softer paint colors. Always test several large swatches in the room and check them throughout the day under different lighting conditions before committing to a shade.”

Painting the ceiling has become more and more of a design trend, and it’s one way to trick the eye into thinking a room is larger.

“Ceilings, also known as the fifth wall, tend to go overlooked, but considering it as a key part of the design process can change how small or big a space feels,” Waroquier remarks. “Continuing the wall color onto the ceiling softens the overall transition between vertical and horizontal surfaces. It's a long-standing design trick because it works.”

Guyton adds that you can consider painting the ceiling a shade darker or lighter than the walls for a monochromatic look.

Guyton calls this the “glass is half full” method of painting a room to make it feel larger—and it works if you’re nervous about committing to a color for an entire wall.

She advises going up only four to six feet with paint on a wall, which is considered chair-rail height, or you can add wainscoting.

“My living room ceilings were 18 feet high, and I didn't want to commit to that much paint or potential darkness in the room,” she shares, saying that painting halfway up gave it a “custom look” and “the right amount of a pop of color in the open space.”

Although it’s long been thought that dark paint colors can make a room feel smaller, many designers believe the opposite.

“Embrace darker, moodier colors when you can,” Waroquier says. “Just as people think light colors equal bigger spaces, many think that dark colors can close a room in or make it look smaller. But a deep, saturated shade can actually blur the edges and corners of a room, so you get this beautiful, cocooning effect that generally offers more visual depth and interest.”

Waroquier says that you should always let a small room’s natural light guide your paint choice.

“I always consider the natural light in a space before choosing a paint color,” he says. “In a darker room with little to no natural light, I prefer to avoid stark white because it can often read flat or even gray. A softer neutral or even a more saturated shade works better with existing light in a room.”

#4052. Why Adapting to Change Is Harder in Autism

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A neuroimaging and behavioral study published in The Journal of Neuroscience identifies why many autistic individuals struggle to adapt to shifting environmental demands. Researchers from Nottingham Trent University and The Hebrew University of Jerusalem discovered that individuals on the autism spectrum display significantly weaker neural responses to negative feedback and reduced sensitivity to internal decision confidence. This dual blunting of external correction and internal certainty impairs dynamic behavioral updating.

Source: Society for Neuroscience (SfN) / Nottingham Trent University / Hebrew University of Jerusalem

A core characteristic of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) involves difficulties in cognitive flexibility—the capacity to seamlessly modify behaviors, expectations, and strategies in response to dynamic, unpredictable changes in the environment. While rigidity and insistence on sameness have traditionally been evaluated through behavioral observations, the computational and neurobiological mechanisms governing how the autistic brain evaluates decision outcomes have remained unclear.

Now, a collaborative study led by Nathaniel Zuk, PhD, at Nottingham Trent University, and Jaroslav Slobodskoy-Plusnin, PhD, at The Hebrew University of Jerusalem, alongside researchers from University College London, sheds light on the cognitive architecture of adaptation.

Publishing in The Journal of Neuroscience (JNeurosci), the team demonstrated that reduced behavioral adaptability in autism stems from an underlying attenuation in how the brain processes both external performance feedback and internal confidence cues.

To explore how the brain integrates outcome evaluations, the research team recruited autistic and neurotypical adult participants to complete a perceptual pitch discrimination task. Across repeated trials, participants were required to detect subtle frequency shifts between auditory tones and immediately received visual feedback confirming whether their responses were correct or incorrect.

Simultaneous neurophysiological monitoring focused on event-related neural potentials specifically tied to outcome evaluation and performance monitoring.

The electrophysiological data revealed a significant group divergence: participants with autism exhibited significantly attenuated neural amplitudes in response to negative performance feedback compared to non-autistic peers. When errors occurred, the autistic brain generated a weaker neurophysiological error signal, suggesting that corrective environmental inputs are registered with less biological salience.

In everyday life, external feedback is interpreted alongside internal subjective states—specifically confidence. When a person feels absolute certainty about a decision but is proven wrong, the brain typically generates a substantial prediction error, driving rapid behavioral reassessment. Conversely, unexpected feedback on a perceived guess carries less informational weight.

The researchers tested whether these internal metacognitive signals modulate feedback differently in autistic individuals by systematically altering the difficulty of the pitch discrimination trials.

In neurotypical participants, task difficulty and subjective confidence profoundly shaped both feedback-evoked neural responses and subsequent performance. After succeeding on easy trials, neurotypical individuals displayed a pronounced perceptual bias, anchoring their subsequent choices to the pitch relationships of the preceding easy trial.

Autistic participants, however, showed minimal behavioral modification following easy trials. Their performance remained largely uninfluenced by shifting trial difficulty or subjective confidence levels, demonstrating that the dynamic interplay between internal certainty and external feedback is decoupled.

“According to the researchers, this work supports the idea that autism may reduce the sensitivity of individuals to both external (‘You are incorrect.’) and internal (‘I feel confident about my choice.’) feedback about decisions they make,” the study highlights.

Because adaptive decision-making requires continuously calibrating internal predictions against external outcomes, a blunted sensitivity to both signals creates substantial barriers in fast-changing social and sensory environments. If the brain does not register error signals or calibrate them against internal confidence, it cannot efficiently recalibrate future behavioral responses.

Moving forward, the researchers plan to investigate the localized cortical circuitry mediating these outcome dynamics, specifically targeting the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), a frontal brain structure critical for conflict monitoring, error-related negativity, and reinforcement learning.

Understanding how the anterior cingulate cortex evaluates outcome contingencies may provide targeted computational frameworks for supporting autistic individuals during complex educational, vocational, and social transitions.

Neurocognitive Characteristics of Perceptual Feedback Processing Differ in Autism

Autistic individuals often show reduced adaptation to changing environmental demands, but the neurocognitive mechanisms underlying this difference remain unclear. We asked whether it may be explained by reduced sensitivity to external feedback and changes in task difficulty.

Autistic and non-autistic adults of either sex, matched for cognitive ability, performed a pitch discrimination task in which their response was immediately followed by feedback.

Accuracy was similar between the two groups, yet autistic participants exhibited a markedly reduced feedback-related negativity (FRN), an event-related EEG potential differentiating explicit negative versus positive feedback. They also showed weaker modulation of feedback-evoked EEG responses by perceptual difficulty.

Lastly, they showed reduced trial-by-trial behavioral updating of perceptual bias following recent correct and easy trials. These effects were replicated in a follow-up experiment conducted 1–2 years later.

When combined they classified autistic and non-autistic participants with about 80% accuracy. Together, our findings indicate that multiple processes for evaluating perceptual decisions and adjusting subsequent behavior differ in autism and likely contribute to slow updating in changing contexts.

#4421. Billion-query-per-second lookup tables in C++

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Daniel Lemire is a software performance expert. He ranks among the top 2% of scientists globally (Stanford/Elsevier 2025) and is one of GitHub's top 1000 most followed developers.

A lot of software spends its time mapping strings to values. A web server must identify the HTTP method (GET, POST, …). It must recognize header names. A URL parser must recognize the protocol (http, https, ftp, …). A compiler must recognize keywords.

In C++, the default approach is to put the strings in a std::unordered_map.

It works. But notice something: the keys are known when you write the code. They never change. And yet we pay at runtime as if they could change at any time: we hash the whole string, we pick a bucket, we follow a pointer, we compare, maybe we walk a list.

A hash function maps keys to slots. When two keys land in the same slot, we have a collision, and we must deal with it. Collisions are what make hash tables complicated and slow.

If you know all of your keys ahead of time, you can construct a hash function designed for your keys, a function that never produces a collision. It is called a perfect hash function. The idea is old: the GNU gperf tool has been doing it since 1989. You give it a list of keywords, and it generates C code.

We follow the same design as gperf. The slot for a key is computed as follows.

We add the length of the key to a few small values taken from an association table (asso) indexed by the characters at a few selected positions. Usually, one or two positions are enough. The table size is a power of two, so the modulo is a single bitwise AND.

Consider the four HTTP methods GET, PUT, POST and DELETE. We use only the first character. Set asso['G'] = 0, asso['P'] = 1 and asso['D'] = 0. Then GET goes in slot 3 + 0 = 3, PUT goes in slot 3 + 1 = 4, POST goes in slot 4 + 1 = 5 and DELETE goes in slot 6 + 0 = 6. No collision.

To look up PUT, we compute the slot (4), and we compare PUT with the one key stored in slot 4. One hash, one comparison. If you look up PATCH, which is not in the set, you land in slot 5 + 1 = 6, you compare with DELETE, and you are done: not found. There is no probing, no chain to walk.

How do we find the association values? We sort the characters by how many keys they touch. We then take them one at a time and try 0, 1, 2, … until we find a value that does not cause a collision. We never revise a value once it is set. It is a greedy algorithm with bounded work. The bound matters because we do all of this at compile time, and C++ compilers limit how much work a constexpr function may do.

With our library, you write ordinary C++23. There is no external tool to run.

The map is a compile-time constant. The compiler searches for the perfect hash function when it compiles your code. At runtime, computing the hash can take as few as four instructions: load one byte of the key, load one byte from the association table, add the length, apply a mask.

Computing the hash is cheap. But we still need to check that the string we are looking up matches the key stored in the slot. And comparing strings can be expensive: the textbook approach is a loop over the bytes with a branch for each byte. You can easily end up with dozens of instructions and several branches, and the branches are hard to predict when the strings vary.

Instead, we treat the key as a vector. Our processors, whether ARM or x64, have 16-byte registers. So we load 16 bytes unconditionally, even if the key is only three bytes long. We then use a shuffle instruction (tbl on ARM NEON) to zero the bytes past the end of the key. The stored keys are zero-padded at compile time. A single vector comparison handles the whole key. We go from about forty instructions and eight branches to five instructions and no branch.

But wait: is it safe to read 16 bytes when you only have three? Reading past the end of a buffer is fine as long as you do not cross into another memory page, and pages are at least 4 kilobytes. So we check whether the address is within 16 bytes of the end of its page. If it is, we take a slower path. Otherwise, we do the wide load. We traded several unpredictable branches for a single branch that is almost always taken the same way.

I ran the benchmarks on an Apple M3 Max with AppleClang 15. We look up the keys in random order and we report the time per lookup. Let us first consider the 20 standard HTTP header names.

We need about 1.3 ns per lookup, or about 5 cycles. The classic gperf needs 7.7 ns. Abseil’s flat_hash_map, an excellent hash table, needs 10.9 ns. And std::unordered_map needs 16.6 ns. We are thirteen times faster than the standard library.

It is not one lucky key set. Here are the results over several sets of keys, from 6 URL protocols to the 100 tickers of the S&P 100.

We stay between 1.2 ns and 2 ns per lookup. We are 3 to 6 times faster than gperf, and 7 to 13 times faster than std::unordered_map. Notice how gperf struggles with the JavaScript reserved words: there we are 17 times faster.

Why is std::unordered_map so much slower? The C++ standard essentially requires a chained hash table. A lookup hashes the whole string by calling a function, follows a pointer to a heap-allocated node and walks a list. Our lookup does a few loads and has no function call. The processor can retire over 8.6 instructions per cycle with our code, which is about as good as it gets on this chip, and it almost never mispredicts a branch.

On an Apple M4, with the URL protocols, we exceed one billion lookups per second.

If your keys are known at compile time, you should not pay for collision handling at runtime. A perfect hash function gets you down to one comparison. With some care, a lookup costs about five cycles. The library is a single header you can drop in your project: github.com/ConstexprCore/perfect_hash.

Testing 32 participants exposed to concurrent dialogues, scientists found that conversations carrying high-arousal, unpleasant, and socially relevant emotional meaning significantly strengthened neural speech tracking. Furthermore, when dialogue jumped abruptly across topics rather than following a coherent narrative, the brain mobilized cognitive resources for longer periods to process emotionally charged speech.

Source: Society for Neuroscience (SfN) / University of Helsinki

Navigating a crowded room where multiple conversations overlap simultaneously, famously known in cognitive psychology and audiology as the “cocktail party problem”, represents one of the human auditory system’s most complex computational feats. To follow a chosen speaker amid the auditory din, the brain must selectively filter acoustic interference, parse phonemes, track speech rhythms, and extract conceptual meaning in real time.

While classical psychoacoustic research focused on physical properties like voice pitch, spatial location, and loudness, how semantic and emotional dimensions drive selective listening in naturalistic dialogue has remained less understood.

Now, an electrophysiological investigation led by Pyry Heikkinen and colleagues at the University of Helsinki reveals that the emotional charge of speech and the narrative coherence of conversation serve as primary neural tuning knobs.

Publishing in The Journal of Neuroscience (JNeurosci), the research team recorded neural activity across 32 adult participants tasked with attending to specific dialogues while competing voices played concurrently in the background.

The researchers compared brain responses as listeners followed two distinct conversational scenarios: one exchanging emotionally meaningful, socially relevant narratives featuring unpleasant, high-arousal themes, and another exchanging neutral, everyday statements.

Neural decoding models revealed that high-arousal, emotionally significant speech dramatically boosted neural entrainment—the fidelity with which cortical oscillations synchronize to the acoustic rhythm and amplitude envelope of spoken words.

“We could predict the rhythm of speech a participant was hearing more accurately from their brain activity alone when the conversation contained emotional meaning,” explained Heikkinen.

The findings indicate that emotional salience acts as an automatic sensory filter, signaling subcortical and cortical attention networks to allocate greater processing bandwidth to socially and biologically critical vocal cues.

Beyond measuring emotional intensity, the investigators manipulated the structure and flow of the dialogue. In one condition, statements followed a logical, cohesive narrative thread; in another, comments jumped abruptly and unpredictably between unrelated subjects.

When dialogues lacked narrative continuity, the brain was forced to mobilize substantially more neural resources over extended temporal windows to decipher the emotional messages. Without a predictable contextual scaffold, the auditory cortex and associated language hubs could not rely on top-down predictive coding to anticipate incoming words.

Conversely, when comments were sequentially linked, listeners leveraged established conversational context to anticipate upcoming phrases, streamlining neural processing and reducing cognitive overhead.

“These findings suggest that the brain handles emotional speech differently depending on how the conversation flows,” said Heikkinen. “Sudden topic changes may require more work to process, while connected comments help the brain use the previously established context to predict what comes next and thus process speech more efficiently.”

Detailed temporal analyses further pinpointed the sequential activation of specific cortical networks as they prioritized and decoded emotional speech over competing background chatter.

The authors note that this efficiency boost in coherent dialogue likely underpins successful real-world social interaction. When conversational flow allows the brain to rapidly predict a speaker’s words, listeners can allocate spare cognitive bandwidth to interpret subtle vocal inflections, gauge social intent, and formulate timely, empathetic responses.

By defining how affective meaning and conversational coherence interact during selective listening, the study provides fresh insights into social communicative deficits, offering a neural framework for why individuals with sensory processing differences, social anxiety, or hearing impairments find noisy, fragmented group settings particularly exhausting to navigate.

Emotional Content Enhances Attentional Tracking of Conversations through Context-Sensitive Modulation of Auditory–Language Networks

Engagement in conversation often requires continuous monitoring of relevant speech in a continuously evolving auditory environment. Emotional salience of semantic content may influence this process, but its role in attentional tracking of continuous speech remains unclear.

We address this question using an audiovisual “Cocktail-party” paradigm combined with source-localized magnetoencephalography and encoding–decoding analyses. Participants (18 female, 14 male) selectively attended to one dialogue while ignoring a concurrent distractor dialogue, with both emotional salience and semantic coherence of the conversations manipulated independently.

Decoding analyses revealed that negatively valenced, high-arousal, and socially relevant semantic content strengthened neural speech tracking, but only for attended dialogues. This effect depended on semantic structure, emerging earlier in semantically coherent than incoherent dialogues.

Encoding analyses further showed latency- and region-specific effects across temporal, parietal, and frontal cortical regions. Emotional salience and coherence jointly modulated mid-latency (100 – 250 ms) responses, whereas late-latency (270 – 450 ms) responses were driven primarily by emotional salience.

These findings demonstrate that emotional meaning enhances neural prioritization of attended speech and semantic coherence shapes the temporal dynamics of this enhancement.

Together, these results indicate context-sensitive emotional modulation of auditory–language processing during selective listening of continuous speech.

The term "cult classic" has been loosely bandied about in cinematic circles until its definition has diluted into nothingness, but never has it been more appropriately applied than to director Richard Kelly’s strange psychological sci-fi thriller, "Donnie Darko", which celebrates its milestone 25th birthday today.

Starring Jake Gyllenhaal, Drew Barrymore, Mary McDonnell, Maggie Gyllenhaal, Patrick Swayze, Jena Malone, and Noah Wyle, "Donnie Darko" was released on Oct. 26, 2001, into an unstable world still shocked by the catastrophic Sept. 11 terrorist attacks less than two months earlier.

The thrust of the narrative revolves around an alternative universe accidentally forming via a supernatural anomaly after a jet engine falls off a commercial airliner and crashes through the roof of a suburban Virginia home, landing in the bedroom of our troubled title character.

As the frightening phantom of Frank the Bunny warns, this parallel reality has a ticking clock that’ll result in its imminent collapse in just 28 days, 6 hours, 42 minutes, and 12 seconds.

From the haunting voice telling our heroic teen, "Wake up, Donnie," as the grandfather clock in the Darko residence ominously chimed midnight, to its exploration of the nature of mental illness, time travel paradoxes, fraudulent motivational speakers, teenage angst in the 1980s, and the logistics of interdimensional portals, "Donnie Darko" became an ideal parable for turbulent times.

It’s a raw meditation on death more than anything, and its labyrinth of quantum entanglements feeds our existential core.

DigitalOcean has announced the public preview of Learning Services and Agent Data Services, enabling developers to build, evaluate, and continuously improve AI agents within a unified, AI-native, cloud platform.

The new services build on DigitalOcean's Inference Engine and Managed Agents to help agents learn from real-world interactions, identify weaknesses, test improvements, and leverage company data, the company says:

We built Inference Engine so agents could think, Managed Agents so they could act, and now Agent Learning Services and Agent Data Services so they can improve, on one open cloud and on the customer’s own data.

Learning Services combines three capabilities: Insights, which records agent activity; Signals, which identifies failures and performance gaps; and Simulations, which tests potential improvements through realistic conversations simulated before deployment.

Although separate from Learning Services, the enhanced Inference Router plays a key role in DigitalOcean's approach to continuously improving agents. It optimizes model selection based on task requirements, balances cost and quality, and reruns the same evaluations on new model versions.

Agent Data Services provides a secure, managed data foundation for AI agents. It processes and indexes documents, video, audio, logs, and agent sessions, making this information searchable and accessible through a unified catalog with built-in governance and access controls. It also integrates with Learning Services to support continuous agent improvement:

Agent Learning Services are built on [Data Services]: Signals writes sessions into it, and Optimizer draws its training and test data from it only where the customer expressly opts in.

Commenting on the announcement on LinkedIn, LakeOps co-founder Jonathan Saring observed:

The write-back loop is the part I find most interesting. Once traces, tool calls and outcomes feed evaluation or learning, they stop being “just logs” and become production data.

On a similar note, senior engineer Deepanshu Udhwani argued:

The write back what happened part is underrated compared to the read side everyone talks about with agent data layers. Most teams build great retrieval and skip logging outcomes properly, so six months later nobody can tell which tool calls actually worked versus which ones just looked fine in the moment.

Summarizing DigitalOcean's launch clip, TinyFish co-founder Keith Zhai, emphasized how Signals and Simulations can turn a failed user session into a fix before hundreds of other users encounter the same issue:

Signals and Simulations pull that session into a fix before the four-hundredth person hits the same wall. [...] Green tool calls next to abandoned users is still where most agent stacks break. The bottleneck is whether anyone can read the session the user actually lived.
