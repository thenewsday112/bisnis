#3518. Manual's camera shop in New York is imbued with "philosophy of skate shop"

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Design-build studio Protean Practice and camera company Manual have collaborated on the brand’s first storefront and photo lab, outfitting a Chinatown shop with plywood seating, metallic finishes and baby-pink accents.

Manual founder Malcolm Dia told Dezeen that creating a downtown gathering spot for the next generation of photographers drove the design. It is a space where they can “pull up in the morning, grab a camera, go live your life, and then come back, get it developed and hang out”.

“We thought of it as making us home base for photographers,” said Dia. “[There is] a wall of product and ways to inspire yourself, ways to interact, and then the other half is an area to hang out and chill. It’s kind of like the philosophy of a skate shop.”

Founded in 2017, Manual produces a pocket-sized, translucent digital camera called Holo, disposable cameras and film. It initially operated out of pop-ups before opening its Chinatown store this past month.

The Manual Photo Lab is the brand’s first permanent brick-and-mortar shop and also operates as a film processing lab. It’s located along Allen Street in a former point-of-sale (POS) supply store, with a retail space towards the front and a pink curtain that conceals the shop’s operations at the back.

The facade is floor-to-ceiling glass, with a translucent polycarbonate upper bulkhead that exposes the 1800s brickwork from storefronts past, as well as the shop’s name displayed in straightforward black font.

Protean Practice co-founder Saji Abu-Gold told Dezeen that the choice of polycarbonate material on the facade of the building and the interior ceiling was partially informed by Holo’s coloured plastic shell, which exposes its underworkings, such as the camera’s battery.

The studio attempted to save elements of the original signage instead of a full replacement. It stripped the covering, but maintained the structural skeleton, and infilled it with the polycarbonate sheathing.

It also restored a fluorescent light that was behind the original bulkhead.

“[When] we started to take off the layers of signage and the giant neon sign that was above, we realized that the frame, while a bit uneven, had a really nice structural skeletal shape to it underneath all the layers of vinyl and signage that people had put,” said Abu-Gold.

“We finished installation at night, and it had this refraction effect that is similar to the flashlight coming through the plastic of the Holo camera, so it ended up working out really well.”

To encourage gathering, the team created a large, multi-level Baltic birch seating unit that takes up most of the left side of the store. Prints curated by talent agency Silvertooth Agency and a commissioned painting by artist Leon Xu were arranged around the unit for the shop’s opening.

Abu-Gold said the unit was partially informed by the work of American designers Ward Bennett and Joe D’Urso.

“At the front, we met the platform with the lip of the window edge, which is something [D’Urso] did often, and then [we] brought up the tiers further throughout the store,” said Abu-Gold.

The lower lip was created for visitors “to sit on the floor comfortably without it being an actual floor”, while the upper level “encourages people to stay awhile”.

Cold-rolled steel wall panels line the shop on either side. An oversized, backlit steel pegboard by designer and fabricator Ian Chapin was cut into one along the entrance, which holds product.

Towards the back of the store, a column of custom aluminium shelving displays zines and other merchandise, while plywood, pink and steel speakers by Miami studio Lupo hang in the corners.

A baby-pink curtain lines the back wall beyond a retail counter, and conceals Manual’s operations. Visitors can drop off film at the counter, and Manual couriers it to a family-owned shop and longtime partner in Flatbush for processing.

Dia said the shop is geared towards people picking up a camera or film for the first time, and that the store is not trying to “take any business away” from other long-standing photo labs in the area such as Sammy Photo Lab and Elize Digital – the latter recently went viral for its Passport pictures.

“A big reason why I wanted to make the stores was to make it for the kids who come down the street and pick up their digital camera or roll of film for the very first time, and we want to be that for them, the next generation,” he said.

Elsewhere in Chinatown, architecture studio Spaced Agency recently designed a street-level expansion to long-standing Chinese restaurant Wo Hop, while a much-contested jail is now under construction.

Protean Practice operates between Los Angeles, New York City and London and was founded in 2022 by apparel designer Saji Abu-Gold and photographer Steven Traylor. The studio works across residential and retail projects, with a focus on low-emission, local material sourcing and maintaining existing site conditions where possible.

#1012. The Most Consequential Ballot Measures for the 2026 Midterm Elections

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

A week before voters hit the polls, we break down everything you need to know

This November 3, voters will head to the ballots for the midterm elections. In addition to choosing the people who will represent them in office, they will also be making important decisions about policies that will shape everything from abortion rights to income taxes.

Nearly 150 statewide measures will be determined on the ballot during this year’s midterm elections. Key issues that have been dominating the news cycle—including voter ID registration, transgender rights, and affirmative-action laws—will be reflected in these initiatives, with the outcome affecting the lives of millions of people.

With just a week before Election Day, we’ve put together a guide to some of the ballot measures up for discussion in this year’s election. Keep scrolling to learn more.

As income inequality in the U.S. continues to worsen, some voters will get to decide whether or not to impose an income tax on their state’s richest residents.

In California, Proposition 40—colloquially known as the “billionaire tax”—would enact a one-time 5 percent emergency tax on any resident whose net worth exceeds $1 billion. Revenue from the tax would be used to fund state health care, education, and food-assistance programs. It is estimated that it would raise about $100 billion in five years, according to the health-care workers union that helped put the measure on the ballot.

Naturally, the measure is facing pushback from the state’s powerful and wealthy. California governor Gavin Newsom has publicly opposed Proposition 40, joining detractors who argue that the state relies on these billionaires and that such a tax would push them out. Opponents have even placed two rival measures on the same ballot: Proposition 41, which requires audits of new tax programs, meaning that Proposition 40 would automatically violate the provision since it wasn’t designed to follow the state’s annual spending limit; and Proposition 42, which blocks taxes based on the ownership of personal property, including financial accounts, investments, and art collections, which are the types of properties that Proposition 40 specifically targets. If either or both of those initiatives pass, then the passage of Proposition 40 will be invalidated. Among Propositions 41 and 42’s proponents is Google cofounder Sergey Brin, who has an estimated net worth of $242 billion and is one of the richest people in the world. He has reportedly spent $138 million on the campaigns for Propositions 41 and 42—or about 0.057 percent of his total net worth.“It’s very disappointing that they’d go to these lengths and spend all of this money when really they could do the moral and ethical thing of being part of our social contract,” Suzanne Jimenez, chief of staff for SEIU-UHW, told LA Public Press. “They could pay a very minimal tax that, quite frankly, folks like Sergey Brin are going to make up in mere months after they would have to pay this tax.”

Four years after the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade, voters are continuing to determine the fate of abortion rights on the ballot.

In Idaho, a vote “yes” on Proposition 1—also known as the Reproductive Freedom and Privacy Act Initiative—will establish the right to abortion across the state. Currently, Idaho prohibits abortions except in the case of medical emergencies that will save the life of the pregnant person or in cases of rape or incest.

Voters in Virginia and Nevada will decide on constitutional amendments for access to abortion and reproductive freedom. In Missouri, voters will decide on Amendment 3, which would repeal an amendment that Missourians previously passed in 2024 that enshrined the right to an abortion in the state constitution.

Six ballot measures in five states will shape the rights of transgender people.

Both Propositions 134 and 135 in Colorado affect the lives of the state’s transgender residents. Proposition 134 would require students in all public schools, from kindergarten to college, to play on athletic teams that align with their assigned sex at birth. Voters in Arizona, Washington, and Nebraska will also decide on initiatives that could potentially prohibit trans students from participating in school sports teams.

It’s unsurprising that some states are attempting to litigate the freedom of trans student athletes considering that, in June, the Supreme Court upheld a ban on transgender girls participating in school sports. Opponents of these initiatives argue that these laws would isolate the transgender community, as well as undermine the decision-making ability of parents and schools and lead to invasive personal exams for trans and cisgender students.

Additionally, Proposition 135 would ban gender-affirming care surgery for minors in Colorado. Voters in Missouri will vote on a similar amendment, which would ban gender-affirming surgeries in addition to hormone treatment and puberty-blocking drugs.

With the Trump administration continuing its efforts to undermine public confidence in election security by making false claims of voter fraud, multiple states will leave changes to voter ID registration rules up to their voters.

While it is already illegal for noncitizens to vote in federal elections, six states—Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, Kansas, South Dakota, and West Virginia—will vote to amend their state constitutions to include language that explicitly states that only U.S. citizens can participate in elections. Additionally, voters in Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, North Carolina, and Ohio will decide on election security measures that will require identification when people vote in person or by mail. Opponents of these measures argue that it will make voting harder.

For example, Proposition 144 in Arizona—a state where 75 percent of voters voted by mail in the 2024 election—would amend the state constitution to require voters to show valid identification before each election, regardless of whether they choose to vote in person or by a mail-in ballot. The measure does not explain how voters would be able to comply with such a rule.

Proposition 39 in California would similarly change voting rules by requiring citizens to present government-issued IDs before submitting their ballot, but the measure does not clarify which types of ID would be considered acceptable.

In Arizona, Proposition 142 could shape diversity, equity, and inclusion efforts at public institutions. If passed, the Republican-backed measure would amend Arizona’s constitution to prohibit the state from requiring anyone to endorse preferential treatment or discrimination against people on the basis of their race or ethnicity.

This debate fits within the larger national conversation about DEI initiatives, with President Trump working to dismantle DEI at the federal level through a series of executive orders.

“Arizona already bans racial discrimination or preferential treatment. That’s already in our constitution,” state senator Analise Ortiz said in a message shared to social media. “[Proposition 142] is not needed and it’s normalizing discrimination. Diversity makes Arizona great. Equity gives people a fair shot. Inclusion makes our community strong.”

The PBS Nature film "Tiger Island" captures rare glimpses of tiger behavior, including a mother, father and cubs spending time together ‪—‬ a first for these usually solitary cats.

A male tiger approaches his cubs and greets them with a head-bobbing motion while their mother rests nearby. The surprising interaction, captured in the PBS Nature documentary "Tiger Island," a two-part series that follows two big-cat families across Nepal.

The footage marks the first time a tiger "family" has been documented spending time together, and it could challenge conventional assumptions about how the seemingly solitary cats behave around their young, said Dan O'Neill, a wildlife biologist and filmmaker who contributed to "Tiger Island." Previously, it was thought that tiger moms raise their cubs for up to two years without any help from the male tiger.

"It just shows that we really know nothing about them," O'Neill told Live Science. "And they're so much more complex than people realize."

That's just one of the scenes in Tiger Island that challenges our understanding of the tigers' solitary nature. In another scene, a female tiger cares for another tigress's cubs.

"We have these very rigid ideas of how these animals live," O'Neill told Live Science. But the natural world is "diverse and ever-changing," and tigers are no exception, he said.

The footage of the female caring for another tiger's cubs could offer clues about how mother tigers protect their young. Male tigers, like male lions, sometimes kill cubs ‪—‬ a behavior known as infanticide ‪—‬ particularly when a new male takes over a territory. Allowing another female tiger to care for cubs might help more cubs survive to adulthood, O'Neill suggested. Previous observations of female tigers have shown females helping to co-parent each other's cubs, but it's a rare event.

To capture these scenes, the crew used drones with powerful optical-zoom lenses and quiet propellers to observe the tigers in places that were difficult to access without disturbing the big cats. Traditional camera traps often capture animals along trails, leaving much of their private lives unseen. O'Neill noted that the drone propellers were quieter than the local bees where the filming took place, thus giving the crew candid views of the tigers as they went about their lives.

Though tigers (Panthera tigris) are solitary animals, they often live close to or share habitat with human communities. That means tigers sometimes attack people and kill livestock, O'Neill said. "It would be absolutely naive and wrong to suggest that they [attacks on people] don't happen," O'Neill said. As a result, conservation efforts must account for the circumstances in which people and big cats encounter one another, rather than treating human communities as separate from wildlife, he said.

The same issue affects other big cats. O'Neill, a researcher with the wild-cat conservation organization Panthera, has spent years studying jaguars, pumas and snow leopards. He said the popular image of these animals living in remote wilderness can obscure how closely they overlap with human lives.

For O'Neill, conservation requires more than studying the animals' behaviors or protecting their habitats. It also means helping people who bear the cost of living alongside these apex predators.

"Helping those people is how you help tiger conservation," he said.

#4424. Everything Apple announced so far: M6 MacBook Pro, M6 iMac, and an OLED iPad mini

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apple's M6 Macs finally arrived on Tuesday, two weeks after the company's "Welcome Home" smart home launch. The announcement, part of another slate of press releases Apple dropped ahead of its private Pro and Tell event on Wednesday, Oct. 28, includes a new 14-inch MacBook Pro with M6, a refreshed iMac with M6, and a round of AI features for Apple Creator Studio, the company's subscription bundle of creative apps.

The timing isn't a surprise. When the M6 MacBooks were missing from the Oct. 13 home announcements, Mashable reported that Bloomberg's Mark Gurman still expected them in October. For the time being, though, outlets like MacRumors report that the expected touchscreen MacBook Pro won't be announced until Wednesday — so check back here after tomorrow for more details.

In the meantime, Apple's M6 chip is the main upgrade here. Both machines keep their existing designs, and Apple's pitch leans hard on running AI locally. According to Apple, M6 adds two CPU cores and two GPU cores over its predecessor, puts Neural Accelerators in every GPU core, and pairs them with a new dual 16-core Neural Engine and up to 170GB/s of memory bandwidth. Apple frames the result as a platform for running large language models (LLMs) on-device while keeping data private.

It’s the same AI push Apple made with its home devices. As Mashable noted earlier this month, Gurman has argued the AI boom caught Apple off guard. Both Macs ship with macOS 27 Golden Gate, which brings Siri AI, Apple’s rebuilt assistant. Apple says it can draw on personal context from messages, email, and photos, answer questions on virtually any topic, and act across apps.

Here's everything Apple announced for the Mac on Oct. 27:

The headline product is the 14-inch MacBook Pro, which gets M6's 12-core CPU. Apple says M6 features the world’s fastest CPU core. Apple claims the 12-core GPU is up to 30 percent faster at graphics and up to 25 percent faster at AI workloads than M5, while the Neural Engine is up to 1.9x faster. Owners of an M1 model could see bigger jumps: Apple cites up to 11x faster graphics and up to 13x faster AI performance.

Apple's AI benchmarks are the more telling numbers. It says LLM prompt processing delivers a first token up to 1.3x faster than the M5 model, with similar gains in Blender rendering and Xcode builds.

Battery life is rated at up to 24 hours, which Apple calls the longest ever in a Mac, and fast charging reaches 50 percent in 30 minutes with a 96W adapter. Memory starts at 16GB and tops out at 32GB. Wireless connectivity upgrades to Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6, and the laptop supports up to two external displays.

Speaking of displays, the MacBook Pro has a Liquid Retina XDR display (with up to 1,600 nits of peak HDR brightness and a nano-texture option), a 12MP Center Stage camera, six speakers, and the same ports, including HDMI, MagSafe 3, three Thunderbolt 4 ports, and an SDXC slot.

The 14-inch MacBook Pro with M6 starts at $1,999, or $1,899 with education pricing, and comes in space black and silver. Apple Upgrade leases start at $38.99 per month for 36 months. Pre-orders open Wednesday, Oct. 28, in the U.S. and 34 other countries and regions, with availability beginning Friday, Nov. 6.

The iMac skips M5 entirely, jumping from M4 straight to M6. Apple says the CPU and GPU each gain two cores, with configurations offering up to 12 cores apiece, delivering up to 2x faster graphics than M4. Again, Apple’s big claim is the iMac’s ability to run AI locally. The company says the iMac runs on-device AI up to 5x faster than M4, including prompt processing in LM Studio. For anyone still on an Intel iMac, Apple reports an overall performance gain of up to 18x.

Connectivity gets some of the more practical upgrades. Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6 come standard, available 2.5Gb Ethernet is up to 2.5x faster than before, and Apple says SSD speeds have doubled. Up to four Thunderbolt 4 ports support two 6K external displays.

The rest of the iMac is unchanged, including the 24-inch 4.5K Retina display, 12MP Center Stage camera, color-matched accessories, and seven colors: blue, green, pink, yellow, purple, orange, and silver.

The iMac with M6 starts at $1,499 ($1,449 for education) with a 10-core CPU and GPU, and $1,699 ($1,599 for education) with a 12-core CPU and GPU. That base price matches what the M4 iMac currently sells for after a $200 increase in June, according to MacRumors. Apple Upgrade leases start at $28.99 per month for 36 months. Pre-orders open Wednesday, Oct. 28, with availability beginning Friday, Nov. 6.

Apple also gave its new Macs more AI software to run. Apple Creator Studio adds intelligent assistants (in beta) to Logic Pro for Mac and to Keynote, Pages, and Numbers. Notably, Apple says those assistants are built on a combination of its own models and OpenAI’s. Keynote, Pages, and Numbers also gain Model Context Protocol (MCP) support, which lets third-party AI agents read documents, make edits, and run automated actions inside the apps.

Elsewhere, Final Cut Pro for Mac now includes MotionVFX's library of effects, transitions, and templates, and Pixelmator Pro adds Generate Fill for editing selected parts of an image using text prompts. Logic Pro's Stem Splitter can now separate drums.

Apple's week of announcements technically started Monday with the iPad mini, which Apple calls the tablet's "biggest update ever." The headline change is a larger 8.5-inch OLED display that fits in the same footprint, thanks to 24 percent thinner bezels. Apple says peak brightness reaches 1,200 nits, more than double that of the previous model, and a nano-texture option is available.

The A20 Pro chip follows the same local AI script as the M6. It has Neural Accelerators in each GPU core, and Apple claims up to 4.5x faster AI performance than the previous generation. The tablet is also thinner, at 5.4mm, and adds IP67 water and dust resistance, a 12MP front camera on the landscape edge, and Apple's N1 wireless and C2 cellular chips.

The iPad mini starts at $699 for Wi-Fi and $849 for Wi-Fi + Cellular (education pricing starts at $649), and comes in midnight and starlight. Pre-orders open Wednesday, Oct. 28, with availability beginning Friday, Nov. 6.

#1191. I Tried the Lip Stain That’s All Over TikTok—Does It Actually Work?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I love the idea of wearing lip products. A bold red lipstick, a shimmery lip gloss, a coat of moisturizing tinted lip balm—they instantly make you look more put together without requiring much added time to your makeup routine. But I 1) sip a lot of water throughout the day (via my at-home Stanley cup and my office Owala, ofc), and 2) hate reapplying. The second my lip product starts to fade or smear, I just wipe it off and forget I even tried to be that kind of person for the day. I’m convinced, for this reason, that lip stains were made for me.

In lieu of carrying around a bunch of lip products all the time, a lip stain should, in theory, be the answer to all my prayers. To see if it really stood up to the test, I tried one of the most-hyped lip stains out there: Wonderskin's Wonder Blading All-Day Lip Stain.

Anyone with a TikTok account has probably seen influencers wiping away the affordable-yet-terrifying blue metallic stain to reveal flawlessly pink (or brown, or red, or berry, or neutral) lips. Wonderskin claims the formula is smudge-proof, waterproof, and transfrer-proof. But does it actually work? I tried it out, so peep my full review below.

The “Wonder Blading” name isn’t just a cute play on words. This formula actually uses what the brand calls “Ionic Blading Technology” to temporarily dye the top layer of the skin on your lips for long-lasting color. But there is a special technique involved. First, you layer on the formula all over your lips. Don’t be alarmed by the blue color; this will go away. After 60 seconds, you can wipe away the blue color with a wet tissue or paper towel. You’re left with a stain that Wonderskin claims lasts up to 10 hours, even through eating, drinking, and kissing. But we’ll be the judge of that...

I had mindlessly applied some lip balm about 30 minutes before I sat down to do my makeup, so I wiped off that residue before carefully painting on the Wonderskin lip stain in the shade Whimsical (it's a lovely rosy pink, although you can't tell just yet!). It tingled slightly upon application and felt more like a liquid lipstick than a thick mask.

Btw, don't be afraid to layer up—my first few swipes were pretty sheer but I eventually discovered that it was better to dab on the mask little by little rather than making wide swipes across my mouth.

Although everyone on TikTok tries to peel it off, the formula is actually meant to be lightly wiped away with a paper towel dipped in water. This should get rid of any remaining blue residue.

And wouldn't you know it—I'm OBSESSED with the subtle, I-woke-up-like-this results. The alien blue stain left behind the most gorgeous tint that makes my lips look healthier and more flushed. It's the pouty, just-bitten lip color I wish I naturally had—the same results I imagine lip blushing would give me, minus the scary needles and the high price tag.

The Wonderskin lip stain is also a reeeeally great base for other products. I'm just wearing a touch of Aquaphor in the after photo, but a sheer lipstick or tinted gloss would look A+ over this natural stain.

“I really love this stain when I know I have an event or long meal ahead of me (filled with multiple drinks, greasy food, lip gloss applications, and maybe some smooches) and it's somehow still perfectly intact by the end of my night. It pairs seamlessly with any lip oil/gloss/balm, so you can layer on your favorite hydrating formula without needing to rely on drying lipsticks for bold pigment. The only downside? You need a wet tissue or cloth to remove the purple layer, so be sure to do this before your makeup routine to avoid accidentally wiping away other products.”

"Now, Wonderskin is definitely better if you want to cover your entire lip. It's also available in a gazillion shades, which is def helpful if you want a wide range, like a red lip that won't budge. I just wish this one had a guide color since all of them apply purple/blue, and I really gotta look at the bottom to see what shade I'm putting on."

I'll start with a con: I'm not gonna lie, after 12+ hours, the stain was a little crusty. There. I said it. The stain isn't inherently moisturizing, so I'd recommend wearing a lip balm or gloss on top. But to be fair, that's still wayyy better than solely relying on a mediocre lip oil that comes off every time I take a sip of my drink or a gooey lipstick that transfers onto everything I touch.

#1005. Elena Dudum on Her Searching New Memoir, ‘They Told Me Back Home Would Be Beautiful’

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

One of the most alienating experiences a young person can have is realizing that who they are—at least, as they’ve learned to understand it—diverges from how their identity is understood by the rest of the world. As Elena Dudum recalls in her debut memoir, They Told Me Back Home Would Be Beautiful, she had that experience early, raised in early aughts San Francisco by Palestinian-Syrian-American parents. Since then, she has endeavored to continue work started by her father to build an archive of Palestinian history, examining and ultimately uplifting her cultural heritage.

Vogue spoke to Dudum about drawing inspiration from writers including Sarah Aziza, Cathy Park Hong, and Tara Westover for her book and telling her very personal story at a fraught sociopolitical moment.

Vogue: What did the process of putting this book together look like for you?

Elena Dudum: There are words in the book that were written in 2019, but I would say the majority of the work of the book really happened between 2023 and now. In 2023, I had graduated from grad school, and I had 40,000 words of what would become my proposal. That process of [writing] the proposal took about a year, and then I moved to London in January of 2024. It’s very strange because I had actually completed the other 40,000 words of the book in London, and then I left London a week before the book came out. Being in London created all this distance for me; I was in a city where I didn’t know many people and wasn’t in the same old routine. But part of the process was writing a book that wasn’t going to be the book. I originally wrote a version of it that didn’t even include me or my father.

You write so intimately about your family. How did you approach talking about the book and preparing them to see themselves in print?

Well, I had seven years of allowing my family to kind of warm up to this idea that I was writing a book. When I started the book in 2019, my family was like, “Okay, what?” I think they were a little confused or curious, but, like, not curious enough. My parents in particular were kind of upset by the idea because they were worried I’d be airing our dirty laundry. What ended up happening is that I published a few sections of my my thesis, which were the bones of my book, and my parents were sort of able to come on board as the process evolved. I received a grant, and they were like, “Oh, it’s interesting that someone gave her money for this.” Then I received another grant and got offers from agents, and I think as accolades slowly began to trickle in, it helped my parents see this as a professional, serious thing and something they could get behind and be okay with.

Are there other examples of Palestinian memoir, or memoir from the MENA diaspora more broadly, that inspired you?

I loved Sarah Aziza’s memoir and Hala Alyan’s memoir. Those both came out rather recently and they were so beautiful; they were different, but they obviously engage with similar themes and questions, because diasporic stories tend to do that. I really enjoyed reading those, and before those books, Raja Shehadeh’s book We Could Have Been Friends, My Father and I was a good primer of, like, how do you write about a tense and complicated relationship with a parent? I love memoirs that make you feel like you’re sitting in a story, and this might be the most overused example, but I did think about Educated by Tara Westover often. I thought about that book in terms of how a reader might come to my story. I was unfamiliar with so many parts of Tara’s world, but she created a full story that I could really sit in and fully imagine. There’s a novelistic aspect of that book that’s similar to Heavy by Kiese Laymon; he has such a singular voice, and I looked a lot at his work. Another book that I thought a lot about was Minor Feelings by Cathy Park Hong. Structurally, I didn’t want to write essays, but what I loved about that book was how it got into what I call this icky feeling of not quite understanding why you feel the way you feel, or perhaps having shame around your feelings about your identity and your family. I really appreciated Cathy’s voice while trying to navigate that through line in my own work.

We’re in such a strange cultural moment where we are, perhaps, hearing more Palestinian narratives than we used to, but it’s set against the context of the war in Gaza. How do you navigate that tension?

It’s something that I really struggled with at the beginning, because while I started my book in 2019 and I had 40,000 words’ worth of work I was sitting on, I had been submitting to editors and small journals and no one was responding. Then they did respond, and quite quickly, in the fall of 2023. That was upsetting because these voices and these narratives have been important for forever. I really struggled with that because I felt as though my work was riding a wave that I didn’t want it to ride. I have a close friend who’s Palestinian and grew up in the Arab world, and I was bitching and moaning. I was like, “This is so awkward, this is terrible,” and she was like, “You’re a Palestinian who’s alive. What are you going to do with that?” I remember that so vividly because when she said that, I was like, “She’s right, I’m alive.” I did not choose to be part of the diaspora, and yet there is a hunger for these stories now, so are you going to sit and pity yourself? Or are you going to actually take advantage and try to share as many stories as possible and uplift as many other Palestinian voices as possible?

Another thing I will say is that I also had to come to terms with the fact that I am telling a Palestinian story that is a diaspora story. I am not telling a Palestinian story of living under occupation, right? All I can do is connect dots and uplift voices, but I am not going to speak on behalf of Palestinians who have their own story and their own voice. It’s my job to uplift those Palestinians’ voices, but I am never going to replace them.

What’s your biggest hope for this memoir?

I want to reach as many people as possible, and I want to reach those who are Palestinian or Arab and can feel like their stories are finally seen and centered, especially those who are like, I didn’t know how to talk about this terrible feeling of not coming to terms with my identity until later. I think this is a story that’s not just Arab or Palestinian; it’s so many diasporas. I wish there was a book like this when I was growing up that I could have read and that honored my experience in an important way. I also want the book to reach people who aren’t of the diaspora, and who can then see what a strange thing it is to have to constantly lie and navigate this big conversation from the age of five or six years old. I want people to feel less alone, but I also want it to serve as a wake-up call.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

A new report from the American Psychological Association, Stress in America 2026, suggests that ongoing stress is commonplace for most Americans. What is causing this increased stress?

All signs point to uncertainty as the key factor. Americans are concerned with the rising costs of goods and services due to ongoing inflation. In this research, 77 percent of American adults agreed that the daily cost of living was the most significant source of life stress.

Other causes of stress include global uncertainty at the economic, environmental, and political level and the ongoing political infighting that rages across the country. A large majority (74 percent) said they were concerned about the “future of the nation.” Not only is this uncertainty-caused stress being experienced during waking hours, but a majority of adults (60 percent) said that stress has affected their sleep, and that it has affected their optimism about the future (59 percent). Importantly, majorities of Americans report that stress has affected both their mental and physical health.

As these stress levels rise, interestingly, many Americans are reporting that it is becoming the “new normal.” Seventy-two percent of Americans say that “stress is something I have to live with,” and “bad news no longer shocks me the way it used to.” In other words, people seem to say they are becoming accustomed to living in this uncertain, stress-filled world. Of course, with the reported impact of these stressors on mental and physical well-being, it may be the case that Americans are simply engaging in rationalization, a very common psychological coping response, but one that may not, in the long run, be very effective in reducing the impact of stress.

Given the diversity of the U.S. population, there are important demographic differences in the survey responses. For example, younger adults (aged 18 to 29) were more likely than older adults to report stress was affecting their motivation, productivity, and sense of optimism for the future. Caregivers, especially those who are in the “sandwich generation” of having to care for both parents and children, report particularly high levels of stress, with concerns about health care issues and finances being prominent. Marginalized individuals, particularly members of the LGBTQ community, are being stressed by the sense of uncertainty caused by today’s supercharged political climate.

There are some even more alarming results from this recent report. Nearly half of adults (47 percent) reported that stress has caused them to pull back from social participation. They are less likely to attend social gatherings, community activities, and hobbies. This withdrawal from possible sources of social support (an important strategy for coping with stress) is concerning. Moreover, a good portion of adults are instead turning to AI for connections, turning to chatbot “friends” and seeking out AI to provide mental health advice and guidance.

The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) is reversing course on its previous efforts to plug those leaks. As part of President Donald Trump’s deregulatory agenda, the EPA plans to undo Biden-era rules that were expected to cut methane emissions by 80 percent over 14 years.

Methane has caused about 30 percent of global warming

The EPA teased the rule change earlier this month, saying in a press release that it was “days away” from making the proposal. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin says the rollbacks will save oil and gas companies $45 billion, while the previous rules would have incurred $18 billion in regulatory costs.

The agency is expected to step back standards for so-called “marginal wells” — old wells that produce little fuel but create an outsize amount of methane pollution from leaks. These wells are responsible for half of the industry’s methane emissions despite only producing 6 percent of US oil and gas, ProPublica reports. #3625. Dutch Art Historian and Curator Rudi Fuchs Dies at 84

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Dutch art historian and curator Rudi Fuchs has died at the age of 84 in Amsterdam. His family announced his death on Sunday to the Amsterdam daily newspaper Het Parool.

Born in 1942 in Eindhoven, Rudolf Herman “Rudi” Fuchs studied art history from 1967 to 1975 at Leiden University. In 1975, at the age of 32, he was appointed director of the Van Abbemuseum in Eindhoven; at the time he was the youngest museum director in the Netherlands. It was the beginning of a storied career as one of Europe’s leading museum directors. Related Articles Ursula von Rydingsvard, Sculptor of Gigantic Cedar Forms, Dies at 84 Jogen Chowdhury, 'Towering Pillar' of Indian Modern Art, Has Died at 87

In 1982 Fuchs gained international recognition as the artistic director of Documenta 7 in Kassel, Germany. Fuchs’s iteration of the quinquennial exhibition was marked by a rejection of 1970s art trends such as conceptual art, video, and performance—as Artforum noted at the time, Fuchs notably included only one video in the exhibition, Dara Birnbaum’s PM Magazine—in favor of painting and sculpture by the likes of Georg Baselitz, Keith Haring, and a 21-year-old Jean-Michel Basquiat. Widely criticized for being too Western-centric and conservative, it launched neo-Expressionism as one of the major art movements of the 1980s.

Between 1984 and 1991, partly in tandem with directorships of the Van Abbemuseum, and later of the Gemeentemuseum in The Hauge—a position he held from 1987 to 1993—Fuchs was also the director of the Castello di Rivoli museum in Turin. In 1993, despite having left the Gemeentemuseum with a deficit of over 4 million guilders—he would later publicly admit to “having no talent for administrative affairs”—he was hired as director of the Stedelijk Museum in Amsterdam.

At the Stedelijk, Fuchs—who famously disliked wall labels—was known for his impressionistic curation, which he compared to composing music or creating a play. A series titled “Couplets,” for example, paired historical works from the museum’s collection with pieces by contemporary artists such as Donald Judd, Jannis Kounellis, and Karel Appel. He also invited outsiders, including Queen Beatrix in 2000, to curate shows. His tenure, however, was marred by financial difficulties and an ill-fated expansion plan, and he stepped down as director in 2002.

After leaving the Stedelijk, Fuchs remained active as a writer, lecturer, and exhibition curator.

On the Stedelijk’s Instagram page, Rein Wolfs, the current director of the museum, wrote, “[A]s artistic director of Documenta 7 in 1982—the only edition ever led by a Dutch person—[Fuchs] emerged as a champion of new painting; as director of the Haags Gemeentemuseum, he transformed the way Mondrian was perceived; and as director of the Stedelijk, he surprised audiences with unexpected juxtapositions and innovative modes of presentation. . . . Through his acquisitions, presentations, and publications, Rudi made a significant contribution to the understanding of art; everything he did served as an encouragement to really look.”

#567. Aging’s “Zombie Cell” Problem May Have a Solution, Scientists Discover

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

“Zombie cells” become harder to clear with age, and a new study reveals why the immune system struggles to remove them.

Researchers at Albert Einstein College of Medicine have identified a breakdown in protein recycling that helps explain why senescent cells, often called “zombie cells,” accumulate in aging tissues. In experiments involving older mice, restoring that process reduced senescent-cell buildup, inflammation, and tissue scarring. The findings, published in Nature Aging, suggest a possible way to help the immune system clear these cells more effectively.

Senescent cells remain alive after they stop dividing, but their activity changes. Although they can support tissue repair, their prolonged presence can fuel chronic inflammation and age-related disease. Most of the new experiments involved mice, with human lung samples providing evidence that the biological changes may also occur in people.

“In older animals, age-related changes in both senescent cells and the immune cells responsible for removing them allow these zombie cells to accumulate and play a role in disease,” said study leader Ana Maria Cuervo, M.D., Ph.D. “By restoring cellular recycling, we may be able to help the body’s own defenses clear these cells more effectively.”

Some injured cells enter senescence and release substances that recruit other cells to support repair. Once that work is done, immune cells called macrophages normally remove the senescent cells.

Macrophages also fight infection, dispose of dead cells, and regulate inflammation and tissue repair. The researchers found that their ability to remove senescent cells depends partly on chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), a recycling process that uses specialized “chaperone” molecules to select damaged or unnecessary proteins for breakdown.

When the team genetically altered mice so their macrophages lacked CMA, senescent cells lingered at wound sites, and healing slowed compared with control animals. The experiment showed how a defect inside immune cells can turn a temporary repair response into a lingering obstacle to recovery.

Cuervo, a leading CMA researcher, is a distinguished professor of developmental & molecular biology and medicine, the Robert and Renee Belfer Chair for the Study of Neurodegenerative Diseases, and co-director of Einstein’s Institute for Geroscience. Her earlier research established that CMA declines with age. Subsequent work linked the resulting buildup of cellular waste to Alzheimer’s and other neurodegenerative diseases, as well as atherosclerosis and diabetes.

The age-related decline in CMA also affects senescent cells themselves. To examine this, the researchers collected fibroblasts, cells found in connective tissue, from young and old mice. They then used drugs to stop the cells from dividing and induce senescence in the laboratory.

Cells from young mice responded by increasing CMA activity. Cells from old mice failed to increase their already low activity, allowing certain proteins to escape proper breakdown. They consequently released harmful substances that encouraged neighboring healthy cells to become senescent and interfered with macrophages’ ability to recognize and remove them.

At the same time, macrophages from older animals had lower CMA activity than those from younger animals and were less able to engulf senescent cells. Aging therefore disrupted both the cells awaiting removal and the immune cells responsible for clearing them.

Age-related changes in senescent cells could affect drug development. Researchers are developing senolytics to eliminate these cells, but testing them on cells taken from young animals and induced to become senescent in the lab may not accurately predict their effects in older bodies.

The findings pointed toward another possibility: improve cellular recycling so senescent cells release fewer harmful substances and macrophages can recognize and engulf them more effectively. The team tested a compound it had previously developed, a small-molecule CMA activator called CA77.1.

Aged mice given CA77.1 orally every day for five months accumulated fewer senescent cells in several organs. They also showed fewer signs of inflammation and fibrosis, the buildup of scar tissue.

In a separate experiment, the researchers treated macrophages collected from aged mice with CA77.1. Their ability to engulf particles recovered to levels comparable to those of macrophages from young mice. That result demonstrated improved engulfing capacity, while the experiments in living animals showed reduced senescent-cell accumulation.

The researchers next investigated idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), an aging-associated disease in which scar tissue progressively builds up in the lungs, making breathing increasingly difficult. People diagnosed with IPF have a median survival of three to five years.

Lung samples from patients with IPF showed markedly reduced CMA activity, prompting the researchers to test whether boosting it with CA77.1 could help in a mouse model of lung fibrosis. Treatment with CA77.1 soon after lung injury reduced scarring, senescence, and inflammation.

“Our research connects two major drivers of aging—declining CMA and cellular senescence—and shows for the first time how their interaction allows senescent cells to evade clearance by the immune system in old organisms,” said Dr. Cuervo. “We’ve also found that instead of trying to kill zombie cells, we may be able to restore their interaction with the immune system so that the body can clear them naturally. The next challenge is determining whether this approach can eventually be developed into a safe treatment for age-related diseases in people.”
