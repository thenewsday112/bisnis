This full-length adventure documentary follows kayaker Dane Jackson and a small team down Chile’s remote and wild Rio Cuervo.

With an entire river to descend, the film wastes no time getting right into it. Dane Jackson launches into the complicated business of trip logistics, trying to find a way to get the team and their equipment to the beginning of the river. Besides him, the team consists of the trip’s mastermind, Kilian Astorga, and kayakers Tom Dolle, Eirik Hansen, Adrian Mattern, and David Sodomka.

Forty years ago, there was a road up to Lake Mayuin, the volcanic pool from with Rio Cuervo flows. It’s gone now, so they turn to helicopter transport. With their gear dangling below on a rope, the chopper carries them up into the mountain, depositing kayaks and kayakers alike on the lakeshore. They set up camp and settle in to begin the first descent of the Rio Cuervo.

The team scouts the head of the river, swelled with recent rainfall. Photo: Screenshot

The dense forest and sometimes steep banks add another layer of difficulty as they begin to make their way downstream. They have to plan ahead, as there are stretches where it is impossible to bail if the rapids become too much. This means more portaging, which is painfully slow due to the thick vegetation.

When they do hit the rapids, on-board cameras capture the frenetic energy of whitewater kayaking. But the film makes no attempt to conceal the reality of a first descent. Much of their time is spent scouting ahead by drone, poring over satellite images, portaging, and standing about debating logistics.

Sunset arrives before the end of their portage. Camping in the jungle makes for an uncomfortable night. Photo: Screenshot

Jackson and the rest of the crew soldier on, though, cracking nerdy jokes (the jungle is compared to both Dagobah and Fangorn Forest) and making the best of bad weather and rough camping. In the final few days, conditions improve, and they can spend more time on the water.

The contrast between their slow, grueling push forward and the wild speed on the river brings refreshing energy to the kayaking sections. When the river finally widens as they glide into the sea, it feels like a hard-won victory.

The spooky season is officially here! To celebrate, we've put together a list of five Halloween-themed skywatching targets that you can enjoy with nothing but the naked eye or binoculars and, of course, clear skies.

Over millennia, the night sky has helped shape human beliefs. In return, we have imprinted our myths, stories and perceptions upon it, seeing monsters and mythology in the twinkling lights of the stars and assigning sinister aspects to innocent cosmic objects.

Many of these ghostly impressions and meanings have survived to the present day. Here are a few of our favorites.

Don't be fooled by their beauty; the blue-white stars of the Pleiades are dripping in spooky lore. Ancient Celtic druids would hold the celebration of Samhain — the precursor to Halloween — when the star cluster reached its highest point in the autumn night sky at midnight.

The veil separating the worlds of the living and the dead was believed to be at its thinnest around this time, granting the departed a brief window to visit the living before the realms separated once again.

The Pleiades appears as a tight collection of six or seven bright twinkling stars to the naked eye, while a pair of binoculars will reveal dozens more of the 1,000-strong star cluster.

Stargazers in the U.S. can find the Pleiades using the three familiar stars of Orion's Belt, which glisten in the eastern sky in the hours preceding midnight. Trace an imaginary line upward through the trio of stars until you find the bright orange star Aldebaran, which represents an eye of the great bull represented in the constellation Taurus. The Pleiades can be found glowing less than 15 degrees above! (Reminder: Your clenched fist held at arm's length covers about 10 degrees of sky.)

Next we turn our gaze to the "Demon Star" Algol — a beautiful binary system that represents an eye of the monstrous Gorgon Medusa, whose severed head is depicted in the constellation Perseus.

The demonic eye appears to "wink" at us every 2.867 days, as one of a pair of co-orbiting stars eclipses the other, making Algol appear 3.3 times dimmer than usual at the climax, or "minima," of each 10-hour eclipse. Algol won't be eclipsed on Halloween night in 2026, though you may notice its light dim during the surrounding minima on Oct. 29 and Nov. 1, according to Sky and Telescope.

The night sky is often described as a sea of stars, so it's only fitting that a monster lurks within its depths. The constellation Cetus — also known as the sea monster, or the whale — can be found shining in the southeastern sky after sunset in late October, with the circlet of stars representing the monster's head glistening to the right of the Pleiades for viewers in the U.S.

In Greek mythology, Cetus was a mighty sea beast that terrorized the coast of Aethiopia under the command of the god Poseidon, according to the website for NASA's Chandra X-Ray Observatory. The sea beast was eventually killed by the hero Perseus, who in some tellings used the severed head of medusa to turn Cetus to stone!

Look to the lunar disk in the week surrounding the full moon on Oct. 26 and you may notice a bright network of spiderweb-like patterns surrounding the Tycho, Copernicus and Kepler craters.

Thankfully, these aren't the nests of some colossal moon-dwelling arachnids, but streaks of bright material cast across the lunar surface by the violent impacts that created the bright young craters. They're best viewed around the full moon phase, when the lunar disk shines opposite the sun in Earth's sky.

Our last pick is going to take a great deal of patience, and a good amount of luck! Look out for bright, slow-moving meteors belonging to the Taurid meteor shower, which appear to streak away from a patch of sky close to the Pleiades in the constellation Taurus (hence the name).

The Taurids are comprised of two separate meteor showers — the Northern Taurids and Southern Taurids — which are active in late October as Earth passes through the debris trails left by the 3-mile-wide (4.8 kilometers) Comet 2P/Encke.

Taurids are known for producing bright "fireball" meteors, which are often called "Halloween fireballs" because they tend to coincide with the annual celebration. They're spectacular and can appear anytime after the constellation Taurus rises above the horizon, which is shortly after sunset in October for stargazers in the U.S.. But they're also very rare, so it's best to treat the fireballs as a bonus if you're lucky enough to spot one, rather than a target to plan your nights around, at least outside of the shower's peaks in early November.

#3692. Cloud House by Peter Morris brings "fairytale flamboyance" to London

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Marshmallow-pink walls, sweeping arches and turquoise detailing influenced by a trip to Miami animate architect Peter Morris‘s self-designed home in Gospel Oak, London.

Named Cloud House, the eccentric project combines two offset residences, one for Morris and his partner, producer Emily Kennedy, and a second for a neighbour who shares their love of colour.

According to the architect, the goal of the project was to bring joy to passersby, while giving his “love of postmodernism a chance to reveal itself fully”.

“The key goal was to make a beautiful building which I hoped might create a little moment of joy for anyone walking past it on the street,” Morris told Dezeen.

“It’s easy to dismiss colour and pattern in architecture as frivolous, but there’s something about this visual language that triggers an emotional response that’s different from what you’ll get with elegant modernism,” continued Morris.

“For me, that alone tells us we should push for more variety in the built environment and be open – as Camden Council have been with this project – to the idea of more fairytale flamboyance on our streets,” he continued.

Cloud House occupies the site of a now-demolished Victorian home, adjacent to a distinctive church and school, where the couple have lived since 2016.

Its bold colour palette and cloud-like forms were inspired by the couple’s honeymoon in Miami and the city’s vast collection of art deco architecture.

“Emily and I honeymooned in Miami, where we loved the art deco curves of the buildings, and the pink and turquoise colours everywhere,” Morris explained. “In a way this building is an architectural love letter to my wife, so it felt appropriate to have some of that honeymoon spirit in the mix.”

According to Morris, the project also draws on the curved forms of the neighbouring Grade II-listed school and Grade I-listed St Martin’s Church, which Morris said are “fabulously eccentric buildings”.

“The school, which sits in St Martin’s Hall, has a frontage that looks like a Medieval castle,” he said.

“And the church – described by the historian Nikolaus Pevsner as ‘the craziest of London’s Victorian churches’ – has a knock-out interior, with a brightly coloured chancel ceiling and a nave with dramatic wooden arches.”

Cloud House’s main structure is composed of structural steel and blockwork.

This is clad in decorative arches made of polystyrene, sprayed with a waterproof layer and a textured top coat to match the bright pink render on the elevations, and positioned in place by the architect himself.

Morris’s home is the smaller of the pair, located on the right side of the plot. It measures 133 square metres.

The home is accessed from an entrance on its front elevation. It has a large, open-plan ground floor, comprising a lounge, kitchen and the architect’s studio.

Its two upper floors are each fitted with a bedroom and bathroom, while a roof terrace crowns the home, complete with an outdoor bathtub.

Next door, the adjoining 165-square-metre home is accessed by a side entrance and has an upside-down arrangement.

Its ground floor contains a home office with a kitchenette, and a guest ensuite bedroom at the rear. Above is the main bedroom.

The main bedroom has a dressing room and ensuite, and sits alongside another smaller ensuite bedroom.

Meanwhile, the social space with seating, dining and cooking spaces is on the top floor, with an adjoining terrace. A second larger terrace tops the home.

While the interiors of Morris’s house boldly match its vibrant exterior, the interiors for the neighbour’s home were left blank and ready for them to customise.

Interior highlights of the architect’s home include turquoise epoxy floors, colour-drenched bathrooms and an emerald-green striped kitchen.

Cloud House was featured on the TV show Grand Designs in September and has been shared widely on social media. Morris said the response to the project is sharply divided.

“I know that not everyone will love the Cloud House as much as we do – the reactions to it on social media have been either overwhelmingly positive or overwhelmingly negative, with nothing in between,” he reflected.

“My final goal, and hope, is that some of the people that do love it might one day be keen to commission me to create something colourful and joyful for them too.”

Other pink dwellings featured on Dezeen include a concrete “fun house” on the English coast by RX Architects and a guesthouse in the Azores contained within a converted stable.

By October, a jade plant (Crassula ovata) is heading into its slow season. Days are shorter, the sun rides lower in the sky, and the heat comes back on in many homes. Growth slows way down through winter, and the same routine that kept the plant plump all summer can start causing trouble if nothing changes.

Good jade plant care in fall is mostly about pulling back. Too much water is the most common problem, and the risk climbs as the weather cools, as soil in a cool room stays wet longer and a slow-growing plant takes up less of it. Fertilizer and big pruning jobs can wait too, while light is the one thing plants need more of.

There's a small reward for getting the season right, as jade plants do well with cooler nights. A mature plant given a cool, fairly dry rest sometimes produces clusters of small white or pale pink, star-shaped flowers in winter. Indoor blooms are fairly uncommon, but a plant kept in bright light with cool nights has a better chance than one kept warm all winter. Get these seven things right for healthy, happy plants.

A jade plant needs four or more hours of direct sun a day, and in October there's less of it to go around. Low light shows up in the stems – they stretch, the gaps between leaf pairs get longer, and the plant grows leggy and weak. Put the pot in a south- or west-facing window if you have one, but keep leaves off the cold glass at night, and well away from any radiator or heat vent.

If you don't have a good window, you'll need to use a grow light. The Sansi Clip-On Gooseneck LED Plant Light, available on Amazon, has the option of 1–4 flexible lights.

Let the potting mix dry all the way through, or close to it, before watering again. The surface dries well ahead of the soil around the roots, so a dry top inch (2.5 cm) doesn't prove much. Push a wooden chopstick to the bottom of the pot and pull it out. If damp soil clings to it, wait. Or use the probe on a soil moisture meter, like the XLUX moisture meter from Amazon, which gives a more definite reading.

When the mix is finally dry, soak the pot until water runs out the bottom, then let it fully drain. Soft, yellow leaves that fall at a touch mean the roots have stayed too wet; wrinkled, deflated-looking leaves mean the opposite.

Fertilizer doesn't do a jade plant much good this time of year. The plant hardly grows from fall through late winter, and whatever the roots don't take up gets left in the potting mix as salt. That buildup can make it harder for the roots to pull in water, and in bad cases it burns the root tips. Hold off until you see fresh growth in spring. When you start again, use a targeted liquid feed that's suitable for jade plants and other succulents, like Miracle-Gro Succulent Plant Food.

Jade plants do best with nights around 50–55°F (10–13°C). A plant that summered outdoors can stay out through the first cool nights. Bring it inside once nights regularly drop below 50°F (10°C). Colder nights can damage the leaves, and temperatures much below 40°F (4°C) can kill the plant.

Indoors, place the plant in front of the sunniest window in the house. If it sits lower than the glass, use a plant stand to raise the height, like Walmart's Mainstays metal plant stand. Some leaves may drop after the move, but firm, green leaves that fall off now are usually a reaction to the change, not a sign of disease.

Jade stems bend toward the window, and in weak winter light the lean shows up faster. A plant left facing one way from now until March can end up noticeably lopsided. Turn the pot a quarter turn every time you water so a different side faces the glass. It won't pull a badly stretched plant back upright; it just keeps new growth from piling onto one side.

Old jade plants get heavy, too, and a hard lean can tip a light plastic pot right over. A wide clay or ceramic pot is steadier, but switching pots is a spring job.

Inspect the plant before bringing it inside, since outdoor pests spread quickly indoors. The most common culprits are mealybugs and scale. Start with the stems and leaf joints, then turn a few leaves over and check the undersides.

Heavy pruning and repotting are easier on a jade plant once it's actively growing again, so save both jobs for spring. There's rarely any rush – these plants can live happily in a snug, root-bound pot for years. Rot or a broken branch is the exception.

Cut soft or damaged stems back to healthy tissue with clean tools. Fiskars micro-tip pruning snips, available at Home Depot, have a narrow, curved blade that fits into tight branch forks. Come spring, a jade that needs a bigger home does well in a clay pot with a drainage hole, like this Vigoro terra cotta clay pot from Home Depot. Fill it with cactus mix, like Espoma Organic Cactus Mix from Walmart, for fast drainage.

By March, a jade plant that spent the winter dry and in good light should look a lot like it did in October. That's a good outcome. Every so often a mature plant does more than that and blooms, with small clusters of white or pale pink flowers opening at the branch tips after a run of cool nights for an even better show.

The Nazca Lines are sprawling geoglyphs that were constructed on Peru's ground and hillsides around 2,000 years ago. But why were they built?

The Nazca Lines are the most famous geoglyphs in the world. Dating back around 2,000 years and reaching a combined length of more than 800 miles (1,300 kilometers), they depict a variety of motifs, including humans, animals, geometric shapes, plants and flowers. These designs were constructed on the arid hills and valleys of coastal Peru by the Nazca people between 200 B.C. and A.D. 500, long before writing was used in the region.

It's not clear exactly how many Nazca Lines there are, but they number in the thousands, with new geoglyphs continuing to be discovered with the help of AI.

Scientists have been debating this question since the lines were first formally investigated in the early 20th century. Although the definite answer remains unknown, experts have proposed various reasons for the famous geoglyphs.

There "are many ideas out there about their function and meaning," Ana Nieves, a professor of art history at Northeastern Illinois University, told Live Science in an email. "If you look at the area's geoglyphs closely, though, there are different styles of geoglyphs, some are relatively larger and some smaller, some are on hillsides while some are on the pampas. It is not likely that a single explanation would apply to all."

More Nazca Lines, including one that looks a bit like a person.

A vast array of theories for the lines' uses have been proposed over the years, with the most outlandish of these suggesting that they were sites for aliens to land spaceships.

More grounded theories link the lines to cultural and religious practices.

"The question of why the geoglyphs exist is difficult to answer, given that we lack any type of preserved oral or written testimony," Karsten Lambers, an art history professor at the University of Augsburg in Germany, wrote in an essay published in the book "Los geoglifos: imágenes y escenarios en el desierto de Nazca y Palpa [The Geoglyphs: images and settings in the desert of Nazca and Palpa]" (University of Augsburg, 2017). It's possible that the uses for the geoglyphs changed over time, he added.

One suggestion is that they were initially used to mark routes for travelers and caravans and later became places for gathering and feasting.

In a 2026 study, researchers noted that some of the earlier Nazca geoglyphs were constructed on hillsides, seem to be located on trails, and "were visible from considerable distances." This means the geoglyphs may have been used to mark travel routes and indicate areas that were good for temporary camps. The team suggested that the markings became places for gathering and feasting.

A geometric-shaped geoglyph from Nazca; the geoglyphs vary in size and shape and may have had different purposes.

Another idea is that the geoglyphs were used to match astronomical alignments to serve as a kind of calendar. In December 1941, historian Paul Kosok examined the lines and determined that they had astronomical alignments. Mathematician Maria Reiche followed up on Kosok's work, examining the Nazca Lines for more than 40 years to identify many geoglyphs she believed have an astronomical relationship to various celestial bodies, a research team noted in a 2021 paper. Yet after analyzing the geoglyphs, the modern-day team found that only a relatively small number had a possible alignment to a star or solar event such as a solstice.

"The results suggest that the geoglyphs, as a whole, certainly cannot be interpreted as a calendar system," the team wrote. However, this doesn't discredit the idea that the Nazca Lines had an astronomical significance, Christiane Richter, a researcher at the University of Applied Sciences in Germany and first author of the 2021 paper, told Live Science in an email.

Since the paper's publication, Richter and her team's research has indicated that around 8% of the geoglyphs have some form of lunar alignment. It is "highly probable that celestial bodies played a role in the creation of the drawings," Richter said in an email.

Offerings such as ceramics, crabs, corn and earrings made from Spondylus shells (a genus of bivalves known as thorny oysters) were also placed on low rock platforms near some of the geoglyphs, especially those shaped like trapezoids, Lambers noted in his paper. Spondylus shells are normally found farther north, in Ecuador, but during El Niño events, they can appear farther south, in Peru. These shells, alongside crabs and corn, tend to be associated with water and fertility, Lambers noted.

Another idea is that the geoglyphs themselves may have been used for ritual processions. Some of the geoglyphs are made with a single, uninterrupted line," Nieves said. "Those are the ones that would lend themselves for processions since people could walk them in a single file."

Some of the activities at the Nazca Lines may have helped foster a sense of community and equality, said Darryl Wilkinson, a professor of religion at Dartmouth University who has written about the geoglyphs. For instance, "walking the lines was a group activity, and participating in such processions would have likely created a feeling of community among those involved," Wilkinson told Live Science in an email. "I interpret the Nasca lines as geared towards the realization of a relatively more equal expression of community."

Whatever the reasons for their construction the Nazca Lines are awe inspiring today. Lambers wrote in his essay that the Nazca people who built them "succeeded in transforming one of the driest deserts in the world, over the course of many centuries, to serve the region’s inhabitants as an expression of their cultural ideas and a setting for their social activities."

“Now you know what it’s like to be in the eye of a storm,” said our hiking guide, Audun Rasmussen, with a grin.

I tried to nod, but the wind whipped it into a wobble. We were nearly 2,000 feet up a steep, multi-edged mountain face, and simultaneously getting pelted by rain. If views over picturesque towns and freshwater lakes below are what is typically promised in this part of Northern Norway, today there’d be none. Instead, fog clung to every nook and crevice of the glacial hulks surrounding us.

A room at Holmen Lofoten framing the wild ocean and craggy shoreline

There was a sense of achievement, certainly. Rain had turned much of our trails into sinking mud, and rock faces to gripless stone. We had used our hands to push past birch and skidded on moss. And we would still have to make our way back down. But for now, we were in the clouds, hiding under rocky overhangs and finding reprieve in reindeer-meat sandwiches, dry fish bites, and chocolate-covered wafers called Kvikk Lunsj that translates to “a quick lunch."

It’s ascents like these, over rock foundations that vastly predate the Jurassic period, that draw close to a million visitors each year to Lofoten Islands. The tiny Norwegian archipelago, 68 degrees north of the Arctic Circle and two flights from Oslo, is a distinctive and dramatic landscape with spiky mountains rising from the Norwegian Sea and sandy beaches fringed by fjords. In summer, buoyed by endless daylight, adventurers arrive from Norway’s mainland to camp, climb, and swim.

But summer was past its twilight when I arrived in late September, and I had come to discover an entirely different side of Lofoten: one found not on its peaks, but on its plates.

Lofoten's rocky shorelines feature a nutrient-dense ecosystem and seabird colonies.

In a wind-whipped corner of Sørvågen, a village in the southwestern reaches of the archipelago, a cluster of mustard-colored cabins on stilts juts out into the sea. Once home to a cod factory, these buildings have been transformed into Holmen Lofoten, a unique culinary destination and hotel that has welcomed food lovers and adventurers from around the world since 2007. The traditional fishing cabins, known as rorbuer in Norwegian, are distinguished by their corrugated roofs, some of which are topped with insulating grass. Walkways connect the buildings to each other and to wooden decks facing the sea. Inside the guest rooms, large picture windows frame the shoreline. With its simple local furnishings and hand-hewn charm, everything about this place feels elemental.

A cluster of traditional fishing cabins make up Holmen Lofoten, a hotel and culinary destination.

Locally sourced furnishings at Holmen are kept deliberately simple so the views take centerstage.

During my four-day stay, Holmen would play host to Kitchen on the Edge of the World, an extraordinary culinary adventure launched in 2018 that welcomes acclaimed chefs into its kitchen four times a year, and brings guests around the table—and out into the surrounding wild. This, the 30th edition, would be helmed by Matt Orlando, a California-born, Copenhagen-based chef who has worked at some of the world’s most influential kitchens like The Fat Duck in London and Per Se in New York. For our part, we would feast and drink to our hearts’ content, with the landscape that nurtures every dish spread out just beyond the windows.

Ingunn Rasmussen, the warm, chatty matriarch behind Kitchen on the Edge (and hiking guide Audun's sister), took over the property in October 2001 and now runs it with her son, Håvar Holmen. “I wanted to build a space that felt like home, where we could all log off and reconnect with our natural roots,” she said when she picked me up from the airport. A fisherman's daughter, she grew up around this very site—in fact, one of the cabins still bears her sister’s childhood scribbles on the wall. After a period abroad, Ingunn returned determined to breathe life back into this corner of Lofoten. What followed was a meticulous restoration focused on preserving the original structures and historical details, and eventually, the addition of a couple of new buildings in the same vernacular to accommodate larger suites.

In Lofoten, there are often four seasons in an hour.

Traditional bunk style beds in a larger room at Holmen

On the first night of the multi-day event, we gathered in the living room above the kitchen. Like all of Holmen’s shared spaces, it is defined by rustic interiors, exposed beams, and raw, sculptural elements—foraged flora, taxidermy, found objects—that echo the weathered landscape outside. That evening’s dinner would be helmed by Holmen’s own chefs, a young and talented assemblage from as far as Sicily and Piauí, in the northeast of Brazil.

Trays circulated with shrimp croustades and cocktails that looked like Negronis but told a more local story: the vermouth was infused with foraged blackcurrant leaves, and the Campari steeped with dried berries and yarrow flowers. Each sip seemed to capture the autumnal palette I’d seen blanketing the hillside on our drive in. The nonalcoholic version was just as heady: an elderflower tonic with a syrup made from freshly picked wild hops from nearby. “Holmen was created because we want to show our guests the Lofoten Islands,” said Ingunn. “And food is a critical part of understanding it.”

The kitchen is the heart of Holmen, and guests are welcome to pull up a stool and chat with the chefs at any hour.

I wandered over to Mia Hjorth Hansen, co-owner of Gedulgt, a cocktail bar in Aarhus, Denmark, and resident mixologist at Kitchen on the Edge. Shelves around her workstation held glass jars filled with foraged and dried ingredients—cornflower, dandelion, and fireweed—that she was transforming into elixirs. While visiting chefs rarely return, Hansen is an exception: this is her twelfth Kitchen on the Edge. Still, there's been a through-line among the chefs. Whether Ana Roš, the renowned Slovenian chef behind the three-Michelin-starred Hiša Franko, or Simon Rogan, the UK pioneer of the farm-to-table movement, each has shared a commitment to land stewardship. “We take time picking them,” says Ingunn, “because we want [chefs] who will be considerate about the people and wildlife that make this [our] home.”

The first night of the event is always designed to be an icebreaker, and Ingunn warned us we’d be surprised by how quickly we’d grow comfortable sharing close quarters over the four days. We were a motley crew, including a geologist from Brunei, a committed adventurer from Dubai, and a Melbourne retiree who travels for intimate food events like this one. Our conversations followed us outside for dessert, onto a deck where we savored ice cream made with black currant leaves and lovage, topped with mascarpone made from Lofoten cream and finished with a meringue charred over binchotan, a Japanese white charcoal. A tracking app suggested the Northern Lights were tantalizingly close, and Holmen kept watch on the skies. It was a taste of what was to come: an intimate experience shaped by live-fire cooking and an embrace of the Arctic elements.

Chef Matt Orlando working his magic by the tradition wood-fired oven

The next morning, encouraged by Ingunn's invitation to explore every corner of the property, I wandered into the kitchen to watch Orlando and his sous chef, Tom Siebeler, prepare for their first dinner. “Anything that comes out of that wood-fired oven is going to taste amazing,” Orlando told me, working his way through a pile of reindeer ribs from a sustainable farm run by Sámi herders just north of Lofoten. Originally expecting chops, he was improvising garnishes to suit the ribs. Reindeer is one of many ingredients Holmen takes care to source locally, alongside cheeses, Lofotlam (a Geographical Indication-protected local lamb), and vegetables. “For us, knowing where things come from matters, even with the objects we use," Ingunn said. The exquisite tableware we were dining on, for instance, was made by local ceramicist Gunvor Tangrand, whose organic forms pay homage to the surroundings and textured surfaces incorporate old fishing yarn.

Orlando had arrived in Lofoten a few days prior with a case full of his favorite building blocks—barley, Danish yellow peas, several kinds of miso—but was most excited to work with what he could find in the immediate surroundings. He invited me to step outside to forage for his dinner menu, and we returned with chickweed, corn shoots, and sheep sorrel. “I love cooking in remote places,” he said. “Thinking on the fly and exploring the potential in things you’ve never worked with is where the coolest ideas come from.”

The writer foraging for herbs with chef Matt Orlando

Fishbone noodles reflect Matt Orlando's zero-waste philosophy.

Orlando, like Ingunn, is a bit of a visionary. Some years ago, he opened Amass, a circular restaurant in Copenhagen’s industrial Refshaleøen neighborhood, where friendly service and an approachable, low-waste tasting menu were paired with a harborside produce garden diners could wander through. Amass has since closed, but last year, Orlando opened Esse in a restored Nordhavn warehouse, and it received its first Michelin star just a few months later. Signature dishes, including his fermented potato bread, made the move, along with his commitment to environmental responsibility. At Holmen, that philosophy is a natural fit.

Later that night, as we gathered around communal tables adorned with wildflowers and candlelight, we squealed with delight when the potato bread arrived with allium butter, then again for the fishbone noodles, a masterful creation made by pressure-cooking fish bones and puréeing them until smooth. The collagen-rich mixture gives the noodles their slippery texture, which Orlando complements with smoked chili, sunflower-seed miso, and fermented chili cream. Served family-style, the meal was messy and fun, with utensils set aside so we could savor the main course, the reindeer, with our hands. As a pescatarian, I happily dug into my dish: hedgehog mushrooms grilled with chili-strawberry barbecue sauce and served with Lofoten chimichurri.

Foraging for Rowanberries in the hillsides around Holmen

The weather changes quickly in the North. Quiet and calm one minute, then wild and Baroque the next. “Four seasons in an hour,” joked Holmen. It’s this unique confluence of climatic conditions that makes Lofoten such a productive landscape: the interaction between the warmer Gulf Stream and cold Arctic waters supports marine life; the long hours of sunlight send plants into hyperdrive; and the sea spray nourishes lamb grazing pastures. “This land and sea have nourished us for generations,” says Ingunn, who leads guests on foraging walks, returning with fireweed flowers for immune-boosting tea, cloudberries for jam, or lovage for a vibrant, savory butter.

But to really understand this landscape is to understand its enduring connection to one particular food source: skrei, or Arctic cod. Here, tepee-style wooden drying racks known as hjeller are as much a visual fixture as Lofoten’s mountains; their distinctive frames have dotted the terrain for more than a thousand years. The racks are empty when I visit, but come January they will be stacked with skrei that have been caught at the end of their annual migration, from the Barents Sea to Lofoten’s milder waters where they spawn. Transformed into tørrfisk (wind-dried stockfish), this seasonal bounty has sustained the local economy for centuries. However, like many other coastal societies shaped by the migration and economics of Atlantic cod, Lofoten has been no stranger to ecosystem pressures over the decades, from shifting migration patterns to offshore fishing fleets.

Perhaps, it is no surprise then, that a more diversified agricultural movement is taking root. One rainy afternoon, I departed from the cozy confines of Holmen and drove an hour north to the fishing village of Napp, home to Lofoten Seaweed Company, whose products supply Holmen’s kitchens—and line our bathroom shelves. Co-founded by Angelita Eriksen and Tamara Singer, this entirely women-led team dons their wetsuits and hand-harvests seven types of local seaweed, including sugar kelp and nori, from the strong tidal currents of straits like Nappstraumen, transforming them into prized seasonings, even toiletries. Singer showed us around the processing facility inside a traditional rorbu, where Eriksen once cut out cod tongues—"a common way for kids in this region to make a bit of pocket money," Singer explained—and where they now sort, store, and age seaweed for top kitchens like Noma and Geranium in Copenhagen.

Holmen’s prep table, heaped with treats gathered from the wild landscape outside

They’re not the only ones. Across the islands, artisanal cheesemakers and farmers growing root vegetables and edible flowers are cultivating the region’s rich ecosystem. Back at Holmen, I spoke with local Bjørg-Elin Kärn, who specializes in food and agriculture systems at Love Food, an organization that connects small-scale producers with local restaurants. “Lamb, stockfish, and seaweed are all remarkably sustainable ways of producing food,” she said. “But conservation only works when the people living on the land and by the sea can make a good living. When a restaurant chooses these products, it helps keep that whole system alive.”

Matt Orlando with Holmen Lofoten's kitchen team

Kärn also works to steer tourism toward local food and agriculture, rather than simply encouraging visitors to “hitch tents and climb mountains.” We talked about the growing toll of [the wrong kind of] mountain tourism—a sore point among residents. Holmen’s own mountaineering guide Audun prefers not to share the names or locations of ascents anymore. He fears that overexposure on social media will draw the wrong kind of attention, not least from underprepared climbers who underestimate both the difficulty of the routes and the whims of the weather. A few years ago, Lofoten banned wild camping, restricting campers to designated grounds.

It is clear that tourism has come knocking on Lofoten’s door. In 2025, inbound visitor growth in Northern Norway as a whole, and Lofoten in particular, outpaced the rest of the country, bringing with it a familiar tension: how to welcome visitors while protecting what makes a place worth visiting in the first place. “Our hope is that people come to Lofoten for the right reasons,” Ingunn told me. “Ultimately, that will be what helps us protect the islands and our way of life.” Food, increasingly an anchor to how people travel, offers a way to bridge that gap, inviting visitors in while helping them understand the place they’ve come to experience. “Here, you can’t separate food production and landscape,” said Kärn. “Much of what visitors come to see, whether they know it or not, is shaped by food.”

In this way, Kitchen on the Edge hopes to sound a call for travelers to rethink why and how they visit places like Lofoten. Hospitality that takes guests beyond the plate and into the fields isn’t a new concept, but here it’s more than a perk: it’s a way of life.

One morning, when the weather cleared just enough, we piled into inflatable boats that cut through choppy waters over to an uninhabited island. Once a thriving fishing village, the island was abandoned when motorboats became commonplace in the mid-20th century, and fishing communities no longer needed to live close to spawning grounds. Today, nature has reclaimed this idyllic spot—where Ingunn spent childhood summer days picking berries and swimming—with waterfalls and carpets of reindeer moss.

While others traced the shore on foot, I sat by the jetty and took it all in: the mountains disappearing into the sea, the pair of white-tailed eagles circling overhead, the tiny berries and subarctic flora underfoot. There’s something that happens to your brain when you’re constantly zooming out to take in the big picture, then narrowing your focus to the smallest details. I later learned there’s a name for it: attentional elasticity, a psychological framework that helps you anchor yourself in the present moment. It might be why I felt such a stillness, despite the fog of jet lag and the pull of the action around me.

...to Henningsvær, a fairytale fishing village two hours north of Holmen. Its main street is lined with shops selling handblown glass and cozy knits, and colorful boats bob along its winding waterways. Check into Trevarefabrikken, a photogenic boutique hotel and cultural hub in a mossy-yellow building that once housed a cod liver oil factory. Take an early morning stroll to the football field, famous for its dramatic setting, then browse contemporary art at the gallery in the old caviar factory. Warm up with fish soup at Klatrekafeen before hitting the seafront sauna back at the hotel.

#2861. Where to Shoot a Deer: Everything Hunters Need to Know About Shot Placement

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 24, 2026 8:34 AM EDT

Get everything from whitetail news and opinion pieces, to advanced hunting tactics and cutting-edge gear reviews.

Knowing where to shoot a deer for a quick kill is the responsibility of every deer hunter. Most of us were taught that the best place to shoot a deer is the “boiler room,” which means the top of the heart and the lungs. For the vast majority of hunting scenarios, that’s still the best option. Penetrating these vital organs (with a bullet or an arrow) leads to massive blood loss and a quick death. It also results in very minimal meat damage.

So, if you know you want to shoot a deer in the heart and lungs, where exactly should you aim?

Put simply, if the deer is broadside, follow the front leg up the deer and aim just below the halfway point on its chest. Look for the “crease” that the deer’s shoulder creates. Shooting with a rifle, you can edge tighter to the deer’s shoulder. Bowhunters want to hit just an inch or two behind the crease, which allows a little more room for error (more on this later).

There isn’t much of a difference in aim point for rifle hunters or bowhunters, as you can see in the photo below.

The video below, created by the National Deer Association, does an excellent job explaining where to shoot a deer on an anatomically correct deer target. Look closely at the bone structure of a deer’s shoulder. It is not one solid mass of bone. First the bone angles forward of the leg and then the shoulder blade sweeps back. Understanding this anatomy is especially important for archers who generally want to avoid hitting these large bones with their arrow (well constructed rifle bullets will easily blast through bones). This is a key difference for bowhunters: There’s simply a much smaller margin of error.

Now, the discussion on where to shoot a deer gets more complicated when deer are quartering away from you or angling toward you. You still want to hit the vital area of the heart and lungs, but now you must change your aim point. The best way to do this is to visualize where the heart and lungs are placed within the deer’s body cavity and aim so that your bullet or arrow angles through them. In general, this means holding farther back on quartering away shots and farther forward on quartering-to shots.

Many hunters prefer quartering-away shots to broadside shots. In some cases, the angle creates a larger margin of error for hitting the lungs. Plus, the deer is facing away from you so it’s easier to draw or shoulder your gun without being detected.

Visualize an angle that takes your bullet or arrow through the deer’s heart and lungs and then continues into its offside shoulder, or just in front of it. One way to do this is to look at the offside leg. Follow the front of the offside leg up to the body.

The photo above is the aim point for bowhunters on this angle. Rifle hunters should aim a few inches farther forward.

Quartering-to shots are not ideal for bowhunters. This is because you must hold into the shoulder or risk hitting guts. Lighter arrows and lower draw weight bows will not penetrate bones in the deer’s shoulder. But for rifle hunters shooting quality bullets, the quartering-to angle still presents a good shot opportunity. You must hold into the frontside shoulder now, with your bullet exiting the middle or back of the rib cage. This shot will damage more meat than a well-executed broadside shot.

Frontal shots can be incredibly effective but they offer a smaller margin of error than broadside shots. When a deer is facing directly toward you, aim between the two shoulders, just below the half-way point on its chest. Executed properly, this shot typically drops deer very quickly. Because your target is much narrower at this angle, however, this shot should only be taken at close ranges.

Bowhunters who hunt from the ground and rattle in or decoy bucks commonly see frontal shots. If you’re a bowhunter and intend to take frontal shots, you should opt for a heavy arrow with a high FOC and heavy, durable fixed-blade broadheads. But most importantly, you must have your setup tuned properly and be able to shoot baseball sized groups without fail.

You will see some hunters bragging online about the “Texas heart shot” which means shooting a deer that is facing straight-away, through the anus. It’s true that when this shot is made properly, with a rifle, it can be quickly fatal. The bullet passes through the rear end of the deer and takes out its vitals in front. Essentially, it’s the same as the frontal shot but in reverse.

However hunters (and especially bowhunters) should pass on this shot angle. Deer are often angling away, not walking perfectly straight away. So it’s very likely that you’ll hit the deer in the ham and miss the vital organs. Also, a deer presenting this shot angle is likely moving, which only increases the likelihood of a marginal hit. The one exception here is when making a followup shot. If you know you hit the deer with your first shot (and are unsure of how well) then it’s wise to follow up with a going-away shot if you can.

Shooting a deer with a bow is more complicated than shooting one with a gun. As we’ve already discussed, there’s less margin for error with archery gear. Plus, since bowhunters are often hunting from treestands and taking close-range shots, they have to account for the sharper angle of the shot. Lastly, deer commonly jump a bow string on farther shots, meaning they duck or spin the instant you loose your arrow. In many cases, bowhunters should aim slightly lower on their target, even if they intend to hit a deer in the heart/lungs. Here’s what you need to know about where to shoot a deer with a bow.

Many bowhunters advocate for shooting deer through the shoulder in the “vital V,” instead of the crease. By this they mean, holding into the deer’s shoulder, where its humerus and scapula make a sideways V shape. If you place your arrow through this window, it’s unarguably deadly. There are no bones (besides the ribcage) in this V. Plus, bowhunters shooting heavy arrows, stout draw weights, and durable fixed-blade broadheads are able to penetrate these bones without issue anyway. The YouTube personality “Ranch Fairy” has been one of the most prominent modern advocates of aiming for the Vital V, and you can see his explanation in the video below.

Aiming for the vital V is problematic, however, especially for new bowhunters. If you pull your shot low or forward, you’ll hit heavy bones in the shoulder, which your arrow might not pass through. Plus, the lungs narrow in the deer’s forward body cavity. Most bowhunter’s education courses, and this author recommend holding farther back into the crease of the deer’s shoulder, and not aiming for the vital V.

As long as you’re using quality archery gear, a well-placed shot through the crease will be deadly, almost without fail. Arrow weight, broadhead style, and draw weight aren’t as much of a factor here. Plus, you have slightly more room for error by holding behind the shoulder. By aiming for the crease, you’re holding for the area on the deer’s body where the lungs are the tallest and widest. If you miss a little forward, you’ll likely hit the vital V, if you miss a little back, you’ll likely hit the back of the lungs and liver.

It’s also easy to see the crease and use it as a specific spot to aim for. Picking a singular aim point is crucial.

When shooting from a tree stand, your arrow will be striking deer at a downward angle. The higher your stand and the closer the deer, the steeper this angle will be. For example, if you’re 20 feet up, shooting a deer that’s 15 yards away, you’ll be shooting downward at an approximately 60-degree angle. When your arrow strikes the deer, it will look something like the photo below.

So for close shots at a steep angle, you want to aim slightly higher. This ensures your arrow will pass through both lungs. But the keyword here is “slightly.” Generally speaking, hitting a deer too high with a bow is bad.

The best thing you can do to master elevated shot angles to is practice them. Use  a 3D target and shoot from an elevated position, as if you were in a treestand. This will help you get an instinctual understanding of where to hold for different shot angles. You want your arrow to pass through the top of the near lung and the bottom of the far lung.

All of this gets a bit more difficult for bowhunters because so often deer jump the string or duck at the sound of the bow releasing. This is typically only an issue beyond 20 yards. Some bowhunters solve for this by simply aiming a little lower for farther shots. For shots beyond 20 yards, it’s a good idea to hold a little lower, but still in the vital zone. However there is no rule or formula for outsmarting string-jumping deer.

“Their reactions are so dynamic and different in every situation that you cannot predict what the deer is going to do,” says Aaron Warbritton of The Hunting Public. “Over all the video we’ve watched, we noticed that some deer would duck straight down to the ground. Some deer would drop a foot at 30 yards. Others would drop four inches at 30 yards. Still other deer would not move at 30 yards. Some would duck and roll away. So, they would be broadside at 30 yards, duck six inches, roll away and by the time the arrow impacted, the deer is actually quartering away instead of broadside.”

Every scenario is slightly different, but here are some basic tips and takeaways to deal with deer jumping the string:

Because rifle bullets carry so much more energy and generally do more damage to bones and organs, rifle hunters have more options besides the heart and lung area. With a heart/lung-shot, deer often run off even though they die quickly. A heart-shot deer can still make it 100 yards. Some hunters want to drop deer in their tracks, so should these hunters aim elsewhere? We asked a number of deer cullers—sharpshooters whose job requires them to kill deer quickly, for their perspectives on bullet placement. Their advice, detailed below, is: “It depends” on distance, bullet type, shooting ability, and your goals for meat retention. Besides the heart and lung area, other options include high-shoulder, neck, and head. There are pros and (major) cons to each.

Pros: The ultimate shock-and-awe shot. A big, fast-moving bullet will likely snap the spine, short-circuit the nervous system, break bones, and anchor a deer where it stands.

Cons: The volatile, upsetting bullets best suited for this shot damage a lot of meat, from the shoulder through the neck and upper backstrap. Plus, it’s easy to miss high when aiming here.

When you sharpshoot deer for a living, as Grant Woods did for 21 years, “you can’t afford misses or wounded deer running around,” he says. Both cost you time and money—especially a wounded, bleeding deer, running for its life and spooking other deer.

Woods, a noted whitetail biologist, did much of his deer-­control work on golf courses. There, shots usually ranged between 200 and 300 yards. His first choice was the double-shoulder shot, with a .308 round entering a shoulder blade on one side, slamming through the body and into the far shoulder blade.

“If you watch slow-motion video of a deer being shot this way, its whole body flexes when the bullet hits,” says Woods. “That snaps the spine. That deer’s never going to move again.” This shot can damage more meat than the heart-lung approach, but the benefit is that the deer will likely drop immediately.

Pros: A deer dies instantly when its brain takes a direct hit. Plus, there is very little meat lost to a head shot.

Cons: The brain is a tiny target, and it’s easy to miss the deer entirely or, worse, to wound it through the jaw.

How do you guarantee a drop-it-where-it-stands shot? For Anthony DeNicola, owner of White Buffalo, a top deer-control operation, it’s all about the brain.

“Draw a line from tear duct to tear duct, then go 2.5 to 2.75 inches above that line, centered,” says DeNicola. “That’s where you want to place your bullet—first and best option.”

A bullet in the brain instantly incapacitates the animal; death follows in seconds. Of course, DeNicola and his team have an advantage over hunters: They shoot at night with infrared optics, from raised mobile platforms, over bait, at known distances (usually 50 to 60 yards), and (where legal) with suppressed rifles.

DeNicola uses .223-caliber rifles, firing 50- to 55-grain frangible varmint projectiles that expend all their energy into the brainpan. In the urban and suburban environments in which he works, DeNicola can’t afford to have a round exiting an animal.

The second best shot placement for headshots is a brain shot from the side. DeNicola’s third choice is a shot just below the back of the skull in the first four cervical vertebrae of the spine.

“The deer drop immediately,” DeNicola says of the vertebrae shot. “Heart and lung functions will cease. They lose consciousness and die in eight to 12 seconds.”

For hunters, however, taking head shots is ill-advised.

“There’s simply no margin for error around a deer’s relatively small brain (about 3 inches on the average deer, as much as 4 inches on a large adult buck),” NDA’s Lindsay Thomas wrote for OL earlier this year in his column, Don’t Aim for a Deer’s Head. “Plus, a deer’s brain is a deceptively small target. Yes, a big whitetail might look like it has a large head. But for reference, compare a deer shoulder mount to a skull mount. With the skull mount, you’ll see that the cranial cavity only makes up a small portion of the skull. It would be easy for hunters to misjudge the location of the brain, within the skull, on a live, moving deer.”

Head shots should be reserved for sharpshooters only.

Pros: A correctly placed bullet will kill with massive shock to the spinal cord and vertebrae while damaging very little meat.

Cons: The vital area on a neck shot is quite small. Hit low, and you will wound a deer with very little chance of recovery. Plus, this shot often merely paralyzes a deer, requiring a second shot.

Like the head shot, the neck is a deceptively small target. This is especially true for bucks with swollen necks during the rut. Unless the deer is at close range and you are extremely confident in your marksmanship, avoid attempting neck shots and wait for a better opportunity.

The best shot on a deer is tight behind the shoulder, through the lungs and heart.

Deer can survive a shoulder shot if the projectile doesn’t penetrate through the scapula or humerus bones. Deer that are hit high behind the shoulder often survive, too.

At the risk of sounding like a broken record, shooting a deer tight behind the shoulder, through the lungs and heart, is ideal placement, and does not ruin meat.

Unless you’re a professional sharp shooter, the old school deer hunters had it right all along on where to shoot a deer. The best shot placement for deer is through the lungs and the top of the heart, just behind the shoulder. With any standard deer hunting cartridge or any quality broadhead, a well-executed shot here will mean a quick death for the deer, plus a highly visible and short blood trail for the hunter to follow. Study whitetail anatomy before heading to the woods. And when you see deer while hunting, pay close attention to their body angles and the resulting shot presentations. It’s good practice to visualize your aim point on real live deer, even when your not intending to harvest that particular animal. That way when it is time to shoot, you’ll know exactly where.
