#4485. US Designates Helium As a Critical Material

An anonymous reader quotes a report from the Associated Press: The U.S. designated helium as a critical material on Friday because it's indispensable for making semiconductors that are essential to energy generation, and the global supply is at risk of disruption, according to the Energy Department. The designation by the Energy Department means that more federal financing will be available for helium producers and that the government will monitor it more closely. Helium is primarily produced as a byproduct of natural gas processing. The United States is the world's largest producer of natural gas and of helium. Qatar and Russia are the other top helium producers. [...] Helium makes balloons rise, but that's not why the DOE is interested in it. Helium plays an important role in wind, solar and nuclear energy production, space exploration, medical imaging and research. Assistant Secretary of Energy Audrey Robertson said helium's unique properties make it irreplaceable, such as the fact that it's non-combustible and has the lowest boiling point of any gas. The nation needs an ample supply for semiconductors, quantum and super computing, and superconducting magnets, added Robertson, who oversees the Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation. [...] Despite environmental concerns with recovering helium from natural gas, there are also opportunities, said Greeshma Gadikota, director of the Lenfast Center for Sustainable Energy at Columbia University. "If we burn the natural gas, there are a lot of CO2 emissions associated with that," Gadikota said. "But if you just extract the helium and use it, the uses of it don't contribute necessarily to greenhouse gas emissions. "So from a climate standpoint, if we are going to continue down the line of extracting natural gas and recovering helium," she added, "I would prefer to see natural gas being used in a form where we directly produce hydrogen and we produce solid carbon." Hydrogen is a clean burning fuel, and solid carbon can provide permanent carbon sequestration and can be used widely in industrial applications, including rubber and pigments.

#4144. Do You Compare Yourself to Your Partner? It’s Not All Bad

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

After venturing through the wasteland of modern dating, it would be natural to harbor disdain for social comparison. Dating apps require you to stand out, which means you can’t just be yourself; you need to sell yourself as more appealing than the 50,000 sexy singles in your area. Putting yourself on display and knowing you’re being compared to others can feel exhausting. But once you’ve swiped right, matched, and established a lasting romantic relationship, surely the social comparisons are all in the rearview mirror…right? That couldn’t be further from the truth. Research suggests we compare ourselves to romantic partners more than anyone else in our lives—on average several times per day. In my own studies, I’ve endeavored to unpack these daily partner comparisons to understand: What kinds of people make partner comparisons, and why? The answer, it seems, depends on the type of comparisons partners are making: either based on our unique abilities, or on the opinions we hold. The good news: Comparisons can offer a learning opportunity and even bring us closer together as partners. Who’s doing better? Many partner comparisons revolve around who is better at a given activity—also known as “ability-based comparisons.” These comparisons can range from trivial (Who’s the better bowler?) to weighty (Who’s the better parent?). In my latest study with collaborators Robin Edelstein and Stephen Garcia, we studied patterns in who makes more of these ability-based comparisons. We surveyed over 1,100 U.S. adults in romantic relationships, asking them questions about how regularly they compare themselves to their partners. For instance, one question asked them, “To what extent do you pay a lot of attention to how you do things relative to how your significant other does things?” Participants also completed measures of self-esteem, relationship quality, and demographics. The results were clear: People who were younger, those with lower self-esteem, and those in shorter and less satisfying relationships were particularly likely to make ability-based comparisons. Men in relationships with men also reported making more of these comparisons than any other gender pairing, but we only surveyed 37 men in same-gender relationships, so take this finding with a grain of salt. Does my partner think differently? A second type of comparison involves comparing judgments and attitudes—known as “opinion-based comparisons.” In these comparisons, people evaluate their own opinions by comparing with those of their partners. For instance, before enthusiastically locking in a major purchase, a partner might wonder, “Would my partner consider this a good deal, too?” Similar considerations may arise in the contexts of voting, child care, or anywhere judgments lead to decisions. When it came to who was most prone to making opinion-based comparisons, our results flipped: These were most common among people in longer and more satisfying relationships. The equity-assessor and the self-improver Taken together, these individual characteristics paint two portraits of people who frequently compare themselves to their partners. The first portrait is that of a young, insecure partner who is in a newer relationship and unsure whether that relationship will work out. Think Bella Swan from Twilight who worries endlessly about whether Edward is too good for her. And, indeed, when we asked participants why they made partner comparisons, two of their motives included assessing equity and predicting the relationship’s trajectory. For instance, participants grappled with questions like, “Am I doing for her as she is doing for me?” The second portrait is that of someone in a long-term relationship who respects the knowledge and expertise of their partner and seeks to harness it as a tool for growth and decision making. For this one, think Leslie Knope from Parks and Rec who regularly compares her own judgments to those of her long-term work wife, Ann Perkins, as a means to make better decisions. This theme, too, emerged from our participants. For instance, one participant lovingly wrote about how his partner outshines him in most areas, and this dynamic continually inspires him to grow. Understanding me and you Yet partner comparison isn’t limited to these two portraits. Whereas these portraits describe those who compare most frequently, we found most people make some comparisons sometimes. What motivates occasional comparers? The two most common motives for partner comparison were striving to gain a deeper understanding of oneself and one’s partner. With respect to self-evaluation, one participant shared that they compare regularly to their partner because comparison helps them benchmark their progress toward goals. Want to know if your cooking class has paid off? One of the best ways to evaluate your growth is to see if your latest dish can delight your family in the same way that your partner’s cooking always does. Partner comparisons can also help us understand our partners’ quirks, what we call partner evaluation. For instance, one participant shared how she compares her upbringing to that of her partner, which helps her understand why her partner thinks, feels, and behaves so differently than she does. Partner comparison is a tool It’s no secret that social comparison can sting. No parent wants to hear, “I like Mom better,” and no newlywed wants to hear, “How did you land such a catch?” We find in our studies that partner comparisons are often involuntarily thrust upon people who would have preferred not to be compared. It can be true what they say, “Comparison is the thief of joy.” But comparison does more than push our emotional buttons. Partner comparisons help us assess our relationship and calibrate our behavior, such as how we divide household labor. They allow us to make important decisions, like who should take ownership over responsibilities. They serve as sources of growth, and they teach us about ourselves and our partners. Comparing yourself to your partner isn’t necessarily a sign that something’s wrong. Rather, partner comparison can be a handy strategy for making sense of your social world. The key is to use it as a tool that stays in its place, rather than letting it undermine your self-worth or relationship. Serena D’souza contributed to this article.

#1964. Did ‘corporate speak’ and PowerPoint lead to complacency at GM?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

General Motors headquarters in Detroit. Jonathan Weiss Shutterstock.com

General Motors employees were taught to be careful when writing about safety issues.

A 2008 Power Point presentation had some helpful suggestions, according to a report by Jenner & Block chairman Anton Valukas on the automaker’s failure to respond to defective ignition switches.

A “problem” should be described as an “issue, condition, matter.” The word “safety” should be replaced with “potential safety implications.” The word “defect” should be nixed in favor of “does not perform to design.”

A defective ignition switch in Chevy Cobalts that could switch to the accessory or off position? GM personnel didn’t make the connection between the low-torque switch, which required less force to turn, and airbags that wouldn’t deploy when the switch changed out of the run position. They saw stalls caused by the switch as a “customer convenience” problem, rather than a safety problem, according to the report (PDF).

The Washington Post notes the proliferation of “corporate speak” at GM. “Idioms and corporate cliches are more than just buzzwords that threaten our linguistic sanity,” the story says. “At GM they stood to mask real meaning, obscured leaders’ responsibilities, and created labels that hindered the critical thinking needed to keep real problems from getting worse.”

The story cites another instance where words got in the way. A top GM lawyer asked a vice president of global quality to be a “champion” in the probe of the switch “nondeployment” problem. The executive believed he was supposed to be a person who helps the team remove roadblocks and obtain resources. After that executive’s retirement, a new executive stepped in who also did not understand the urgency of the safety issue.

A Wall Street Journal article says PowerPoint presentations were a substitute for meaningful communications at GM. In one instance in 2009, then GM CEO Rick Wagoner “may have viewed” a PowerPoint presentation that included a “back-up” slide about a change in design to the Cobalt’s key. The change was described as a move to reduce warranty costs as a result of customer complaints about the ignition switching out of the run position because of a heavy key chain swinging in a wide slot.

“What if someone had simply stood up,” the Wall Street Journal asks, “without a visual prop, and said: ‘People are dying’?”

#1126. Servers at Munich's iconic Oktoberfest call for more respect

Oktoberfest is famous the world over for beer, brass band music and Bavarian traditions. But staff are also reporting incidents of racism and sexism, and are taking a stand against discrimination.

"The first week went by peacefully," Oktoberfest Director Christian Scharpf said at the festival's half-way point. Munich's Deputy Mayor Verena Dietl praised the "good, harmonious atmosphere."

The city's police force also delivered a positive report half-way through the event in Munich.

But if you take a closer look, all is not peachy at the Wiesn, as as the event is popularly known. Service personnel are among those painting a very different picture. And they're the ones at the heart of the action. The situations they encounter often go unreported and don't appear in the statistics, but sometimes, a line is crossed with racist, discriminatory comments, for example.

In response, a group of service staff have launched an initiative calling for "mutual respect at the Wiesn."

In a video posted on Instagram and TikTok, staff say: "For years, we've been observing how it's suddenly acceptable to say certain things again, and that there's less of an outcry."

Service personnel say the election successes of the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) in Saxony-Anhalt, coupled with their personal experiences at Oktoberfest, were a catalyst for setting up the initiative. To them, the current climate is an indication that right-wing views are gaining social acceptance.

Hence the name of the initiative: "Staff Against the Right."

In an interview with Bavaria's public broadcaster, Bayerischer Rundfunk, a spokesperson for vendors at Oktoberfest insisted that "the entire Oktoberfest family has always rejected and continues to reject any form of discrimination, racism and sexism in the strongest possible terms."

But supporters of this latest initiative want to see more being done to address the problem.

There's certainly no shortage of surveillance: Oktoberfest is monitored by 56 police CCTV cameras and patrolled by around 600 police officers and thousands of private security staff.

Halfway through the festival, police had recorded around 470 offenses. Reports were also filed for assault, sexual harassment and "upskirting" ― secretly filming or taking photographs up someone's skirt.

Munich's Oktoberfest is the world's largest folk festival and attracts millions of visitors from all over the world every year. At the halfway point, organizers estimated visitor numbers at around 3.8 million people. Oktoberfest ends on October 4 after 16 days of revelry.

#920. '90s art scene in the UK: An international, edgy trendsetter

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The 1990s are remembered for Brit-pop and provocative fashion, but beyond the nostalgia, it was a decade of cultural experimentation and social change. Tate Britain's new exhibition looks past the familiar icons.

The 1990s in Britain are often remembered through a haze of nostalgia consisting of rave culture, Britpop and provocative art and fashion by the likes of Tracey Emin and Alexander McQueen.

Undeniably, the decade was a time of major change in British society — the long reign of Margaret Thatcher had come to an end and the West entered a period of relative post-Cold War stability.

Meanwhile, British cultural icons like Oasis, the Spice Girls and Vivienne Westwood garnered international fame.

But Tate Britain's new exhibition "The 90s: Art and Fashion," which opens on October 8 and runs through February 14, 2027, urges viewers to look beyond the familiar names and images.

Even though now prominent artists such as Damien Hirst and Juergen Teller are among the show's 70 contributors, the exhibition also aims to highlight the creative input of those in communities which were left out of the mainstream discourse.

After all, says chief curator Edward Enninful in the exhibition's program, "the energy of the 90s was not concentrated in a single movement, but distributed across communities, disciplines and geographies that have not always been equally represented."

For Enninful, the exhibition is personal: The Ghanaian-born British fashion editor, stylist and creative director became the youngest-ever fashion director of i-D Magazine in the 90s at age 18, helping shape the diverse and experimental London fashion scene of the 1990s. He later became editor-in-chief of British Vogue in 2017. Enninful writes that he and his co-curators aimed to present a body of work which "reflects a broader landscape of practices and voices that shaped the period, many of which operated beyond the structures of visibility at the time." The exhibition pays special attention to artists of color and women whose work was not necessarily showcased as prominently at the time.

Black masculinity became "a site of cultural negotiation" in Britain in the 90s, writes co-curator Helen Little in the program text: "Figures such as Chris Ofili, Steve McQueen, Keith Piper, Koto Bolofo and Ozwald Boateng reimagined the Black masculine, challenging the racist specter of Black men, who had been defined as part of a masculinity crisis linked to post-industrial decline and excluded from the narrative of 'Cool Britannia.'"

Just like "Swinging London" in the 1960s, "Cool Britannia" became a widespread phrase to refer to the 1990s as an era during which British youth culture, pop music, art and political optimism boomed.

The 90s also offered artists plenty of chances to create without waiting for support from traditional gatekeepers. Britain entered the decade in recession, but that economic uncertainty also left artists free to do what they wished.

Empty buildings and industrial spaces were turned into informal venues. A 23-year-old Damien Hirst orchestrated "Freeze," a self-organized student exhibition in 1988 in a disused London warehouse. It became a landmark early art exhibition for the generation of artists known as the "Young British Artists" or YBA.

Artists Sarah Lucas and Tracey Emin opened The Shop, a temporary art gallery and social space in 1992, as a place where exhibitions could develop outside established galleries. Lucas and Emin also paved the way for women artists thanks to their deeply personal works and their edgy expressions of female liberation. While the Spice Girls helped make feminism accessible to a younger generation of Brits in the 90s by popularizing the "girl power" slogan and shaping "identity through agency power and resistance," as Tate curator Helen Little argues, Emin and Lucas meanwhile "presented a new visual language of clothing as art to tackle societal taboos around female sexuality, vulnerability and desire" — from t-shirts with provocative slogans to a grunge look of scuffed Dr. Martens boots with razor blades. Their work "held a mirror up to gender, behavior and identity," Little writes.

At the same time, the British fashion industry came into the international spotlight.

In a feminine sensuality counter to the gender stereotypes dismissed by Emin and Lucas, waif-like Kate Moss came to represent the feminine ideal of the 1990s. Dubbed "heroin-chic," the style was an antithesis to the glamorous, athletic physiques of the supermodels who preceded her. Corinne Day's photographs of the model led to the raw aesthetic to be labelled "grunge" after the anguished style of rock music hailing from Seattle and led by Nirvana.

The trend has undergone significant cultural reexamination since then: "Along with the look also came criticism that the strung-out look of drug or alcohol addiction was glamorized while encouraging serious eating disorders in a generation of young women," points out Alex Farquharson, director of the Tate Gallery in his foreword to the exhibition.

The 1990s were also an exciting era for Britain in haute couture, as legendary designers John Galliano led Dior, while Alexander McQueen started directing Givenchy at the age of 27.

McQueen's runway presentations were more akin to performance art than commercial fashion shows — and they often included historical critiques. Among them was the famous finale of his Fall/Winter 1998 collection at a London warehouse, during which the model stood in a circle of flames. Titled "Joan," the show was a reference to the killing of Joan of Arc. McQueen designed several other runway shows that reflected on British history, with topics of conquest and punishment repeatedly coming to the forefront. His 1995 presentation "Highland Rape" referenced the history of Scotland's subjugation, with the designer describing it as "England's rape of Scotland."

As co-curator Jessica Vaughan points out in her exhibition text: "Art, fashion and photography were not simply transgressive reactions … but reflected a genuine public unease with Britain's global identity and its past." In a world that can now feel increasingly mediated by screens, algorithms and established platforms, the artwork of the 90s offers a reminder of the value of physical spaces and fresh DIY creativity of the last smartphone-free decade.

#849. Art for freedom: Maria Kalesnikava in Bonn

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Belarusian musician and civil rights activist Maria Kalesnikava held the keynote speech on the importance of art at the 2026 Beethovenfest in Bonn. She also visited DW.

Maria Kalesnikava knows all too well how authoritarian regimes repress art: The Belarusian flautist and activist spent five years in detention as a political prisoner, three of which were in solitary confinement. Speaking at this year's Beethovenfest, she said: "A person's external freedom can be taken away, sometimes almost entirely. Their freedom of movement, contacts with other people, and access to information can be limited." She pointed out that it is, however, much harder to control what has already become part of one's memory, imagination and inner experience. "And it is precisely in this that I find the explanation for why culture is so important for a free society and why authoritarian systems so often view it with distrust" she concluded during the keynote speech.

A key figure of the Belarusian opposition, the artist and political activist was arrested by the regime of Alexander Lukashenko in the summer of 2020, after the regime had violently suppressed the peaceful revolution for democracy and freedom. Across the country, mass protests and a democratic civil rights movement took place after the controversial presidential election on August 9, 2020.

Kalesnikava recalled how, during her imprisonment, she had carried her world and her experiences, with her all along. This enabled her to remain free, and not to feel like a victim. To Kalesnikava, this is precisely the power of art and also the reason why all dictators — whether Stalin, Hitler or Putin and Lukashenko — hate independent arts and culture, even if it's not overtly political.

During a panel discussion in Bonn, Kalesnikava stressed this point directly:

"The question often arises: what are we defending in Ukraine? Why do we need so much money and new weapons? For me, it is completely clear: the war Russia started is not just against Ukraine. It's a war against European values and European culture. It is a war against our civilization." Kalesnikava realized this immediately when she heard about the Russian war against Ukraine in February 2022, while she was imprisoned.

Kalesnikava was released and has been living in freedom again for ten months now — a miracle that she didn't think was possible and an achievement of international diplomacy. The fact that she emerged and returned to her life full of resilience is thanks in part to Beethoven and Bach, as Kalesnikava, who studied the flute and earned a degree in early and new music in Minsk and Stuttgart, recalled. "I spent a lot of time in complete silence. But this silence was almost never empty, because music constantly played in my head," Kalesnikava said in Bonn. "We musicians know this state very well — sometimes a melody or a musical phrase gets so stuck in your head that it's simply impossible to get rid of it. In normal life, this can even be annoying. In prison, I realized for the first time that this could bring great happiness."

During her visit to Bonn, the Belarusian activist also visited DW and appeared as a guest on the DW Russian language show "DW Novosti." Kalesnikava currently lives in Berlin following an invitation by the German government, where she is also recovering from the horrors she experienced. She sees it as her responsibility to remind the world about what is happening to more than 800 political prisoners in Belarus, as well as the importance and fragility of free artistic expression. On October 2, 2026, she held her keynote speech at the Beethovenfest in Bonn as part of a conference titled "Cultural Events as Political Spaces?," organized with the support of the German Federal Cultural Foundation.

More than 200 representatives from concert institutions and music festivals from across Germany came to the newly renovated Beethovenhalle in Bonn for the final weekend of this year's Beethovenfest. Together, they debated innovative forms and formats for music, as well as issues that stretch far beyond the music industry and urge on to think outside the box.

"Arts and culture are not responsible for taking on specific opinions from political parties, or for making explicitly political statements," Steven Walter, artistic director of the Beethovenfest and host of the conference, told DW. "What art always creates, however, is spaces for encounter, and places where people can tolerate ambivalence. And that is such an important part of our society, that we're able to handle making collective experiences." To Walter, this is precisely where arts and culture come in, as artists create spaces for independent thought, subjective interpretations, as well as doubts. "Perhaps it's true that there is something like a DNA of freedom in the nature of art," Walter explained in an interview with DW.

The festive closing of the 2026 Beethovenfest took place on 3 October, which is also German Unity Day. The BBC Philharmonic Orchestra, under the baton of conductor Anja Bihlmaier, performed Johannes Brahms' "A German Requiem," an iconic piece that evokes reconciliation and trust.

After four weeks of Beethovenfest 2026, the festival recorded the highest number of visitors in a decade. Around 35,000 people came to experience, celebrate and discuss art together at more than 100 concerts and events.

Short skirts, knee-high socks, white blouses: Girls' school uniform items are widely used as costumes in pornographic videos. As students experience harassment, a group wants to change that.

Aylin and Mae, both age 15, are members of their school's Little Feminist Book Club in Bicester, Oxfordshire, England.

During a group discussion, the club learned that teenagers at other schools were trying to have the sale of school uniforms banned in sex shops.

Schoolgirl costumes are also widely used in pornographic videos.

"All of us unanimously agreed. We wanted to take it a step further; we wanted to ban school uniform from all pornography," Aylin told DW. As a result, they started a petition to raise awareness of the issue.

#562. The Bundestag has debated the genocide Germany carried out in Namibia a century ago. But will it spur progress on addressing colonial crimes?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Opposition parties have forced a debate in the Bundestag on the early-20th-century genocide Germany carried out in Namibia. Will this finally spur progress in addressing colonial crimes?

Gaob Daniel Timotheus Frederick was sitting in the front row of the visitors' gallery in the Bundestag this week. Wearing a suit and hat, he had traveled all the way from Namibia to see this parliamentary debate unfold. For almost 120 years, his family had been waiting for the return of human remains of their ancestor, Cornelius Frederick (sometimes spelled Fredericks), who had led the Nama resistance against German colonial rule and died on Shark Island in Germany's first concentration camp.

In the name of his great-grandfather and as a member of the Nama Traditional Leaders Association (NTLA), Frederick was there to demand an "unconditioned apology" from Germany.

Sitting next to him, in uniform and white gloves, with a carved lion on the handle of his walking stick, was the head of the Ovaherero Traditional Authority, Paramount Chief Mutjinde Katjiua. Speaking before the Bundestag debate, the chief said: "We call upon the Bundestag to recognize unequivocally the genocides committed against the Ovaherero and Nama peoples."

Many people wonder why the German parliament hasn't already done this — and why the government has not yet issued an official apology. There is a draft joint declaration with Namibia in which the German government acknowledges that "from today's perspective, [it] would be called genocide." But there is no German-Namibian reconciliation agreement. The evidence is overwhelming: From 1904 to 1908, the German Empire waged a war of extermination against the Indigenous people of southwestern Africa, including the Ovaherero (also known as the Herero), the Nama, the Damara and the San. At least 100,000 people were killed, and just as many displaced. Many were subjected to forced labor and forced prostitution. The land seizures and expropriation carried out at the time continue to affect their descendants today.

Jürgen Zimmerer, a professor of history at the University of Hamburg whose research focuses on Africa, told DW that the Bundestag and government lack the will to confront this era in German history. "There are Bundestag resolutions on the Armenian genocide, one on the Holodomor [in Ukraine], and one on the genocide against the Yazidis [by the "Islamic State" in Iraq]. But there is no recognition of the genocide against the Herero and Nama by the German Bundestag." If there were any political will to do so, Zimmerer said, it could happen very quickly.

The socialist Left Party initiated the Bundestag debate by drafting a resolution calling for full recognition and compensation in collaboration with representatives of the descendants. The goal: "restorative justice." Paramount Chief Katjiua has rejected the existing agreement between the Namibian and German governments. "Development aid cannot substitute for comprehensive reparations," he said.

Since 2021, the governments have been negotiating a joint declaration that pledges €1.1 billion ($1.24 billion) in reconstruction aid to Namibia over 30 years. Once the agreement is signed, Germany's president is set to issue a formal apology.

But the Foreign Ministry warned: "Legal claims to compensation cannot be derived from the recognition of genocide. With these negotiations — which are unique worldwide — Namibia and Germany have broken new ground and are pioneers in confronting their colonial past."

Political scientist Andreas Mehler, the director of the Anton Bergstraesser Institute at the University of Freiburg, said the government's plan sounded like "checkbook diplomacy."

Zimmerer also said there was a financial calculation: "If we were to recognize an obligation to provide reparations to the families of those killed — given the millions of victims of World War II, the victims of the Germans — it would ruin us."

Four lines in the 144 pages of the coalition agreement of the current German government — between the conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU)/Christian Social Union (CSU) and the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) — summarize the extent of the government's commitment to reconciliation with Namibia.

"We will intensify our efforts to come to terms with colonialism," the text reads. "This includes transnational research into artifacts and the restitution of cultural property in dialogue with the countries of origin. Particular attention will be paid to establishing a dignified memorial site and the repatriation of human remains."

Compared with the efforts to address colonialism led by the SPD-led government from 2021 to 2025, "the issue has lost visibility," said Alexander Stroh-Steckelberg, a professor of political scientist at the University of Bayreuth who specializes in Africa.

Zimmerer has an even more critical take: "I wouldn't even call it a stalemate anymore. I believe this government is rolling back the progress that has been made in terms of raising awareness of colonial injustices."

Much remains to be done, as far the descendants of the Ovaherero are concerned: The remains of tens of thousands of people are believed to still be in Germany. Gaob D. Timotheus Frederick and other descendants of the victims have been fighting to heal these wounds for generations.

In March, Germany established a coordination council, headed by the Foreign Ministry, which stated that the government did not wish to "dictate to the countries and societies of origin which cultural artifacts and human remains they should reclaim, what priorities they should set or how restitutions should be handled."

Serap Güler, a state minister in the Foreign Ministry and the chair of the coordination council, defends the government's actions so far. Recounting her own official trips — she has already been to Togo, and next week she plans to travel to Rwanda and Burundi — she said: "Everywhere I go, I receive recognition for our sincere and trusting approach to the past — as well as for our determined commitment to a future of freedom, security and prosperity."

Zimmerer said Germany "has earned a reputation as a country that takes a critical view of its own history and learns from its mistakes" — which gives it "enormous cultural capital" on the world stage. But he is concerned that this capital is currently being squandered.

The government's memorial policy does not appear to address colonialism. Zimmerer even detects an attempt to "marginalize German colonial crimes" in the recent proposal by Minister of State for Culture Wolfram Weimer to create a dignified memorial site in Bremen. He notes that victim groups have not been included in Weimer's proposal and said the colonial capitals of the German Empire — Berlin and Hamburg — must also become sites of remembrance.

Ninety-five percent of the colonial troops set sail from Hamburg's Baakenhafen, including Commander Lothar von Trotha, who issued the orders to exterminate the Ovaherero and Nama. According to Zimmerer, the fact that a new opera house — rather than a memorial site — is being built at Baakenhafen constitutes a staggering case of "art-washing of history." Mehler also hopes that a productive debate on memorial sites will still take place and that, overall, "many more places will come to terms with colonial history."

Meanwhile, Gaob D. Timotheus Frederick continues to call for a collective effort to come to terms with the past: "Let us give opportunity to the affected communities that are being represented by us to have a seat on the table to discuss with them the rest of the way."

Release notes for Ghostty 1.4.0, released on October 27, 2026.

Ghostty 1.4.0 is a major release that includes large performance and memory improvements, a 100% complete Kitty graphics implementation including animation and relative placements (the first non-Kitty terminal to do so), SSH integration improvements, and hundreds more improvements and fixes across all platforms.

This release features 7 months of work with changes from 172 contributors over 2,889 commits. Thank you to all the contributors, maintainers, community moderators, translators, packagers, and users who each helped make this release possible.

These release notes are painstakingly and lovingly hand-crafted by human Ghostty maintainers, including the full changelog. They take a combined 16+ hours to write, so please enjoy. 🥰

Ghostty 1.4 remains fully compatible with prior macOS versions back to and including macOS 13 (Ventura). See the system requirements for more details.

Ghostty 1.3.1 works on macOS 27, but it has some of the visual issues listed above, such as the gray tab bar.

PRs: #13943 #13939 #13935 #13934 #13893 #13933 #13389 #13270 #13281

Ghostty now supports Kitty graphics animation and relative placements. These were the last major features of the Kitty graphics protocol that Ghostty didn't support.

Programs that display animated images with the Kitty protocol, such as an animated GIF shown with kitten icat, now animate in Ghostty instead of showing a single still frame. Frame transmission, composition, and playback control are all supported, and animations pause while the terminal isn't visible. Relative placements let a program position one image relative to another image's placement, so the child image moves along with its parent.

We also did a full conformance pass against the specification and Kitty itself, fixing dozens of behavioral differences. For example, images now scroll with text inside scroll regions (#4323 ), retransmitting an image ID replaces the old image (#6711 ), and invalid image data now gets an error response instead of silence, which could leave clients hanging. The full list is in the terminal capabilities section.

Kitty graphics are faster, too. Image payloads are now parsed in bulk with SIMD instead of one byte at a time, making large image transfers about 16x faster to parse in our benchmarks (64 MiB of image data, x86_64 Linux), and drawing images costs much less per frame.

Our Kitty graphics implementation now covers 100% of the published specification, making Ghostty (and libghostty) the first terminal to do this outside of Kitty itself.

PRs: #13264 #13473 #13240 #13245 #14130 #14137 #14138 #14017

Ghostty now transparently compresses scrollback that isn't visible, reducing the physical memory those pages use by 70% to 90% for typical text-heavy history. Compression only runs while the terminal is idle and pages are decompressed on demand when you scroll back or search, so it has no measurable impact on I/O throughput.

Thanks to compression, the default scrollback limit has been raised from 10 MB to 50 MB per terminal. On platforms that support compression, a full scrollback still generally uses less physical memory than the old 10 MB limit did.

Compression lowers resident (physical) memory, not virtual memory, so look at resident memory when measuring it. It is currently available on macOS and 64-bit Linux.

PRs: #13209 #13220 #13226 #13227 #13537 #13827 #13587 #14011 #13722 #14035 #12760

There are performance improvements across the board in Ghostty 1.4 that yield significant gains compared to prior Ghostty versions.

The biggest win came from a surprising discovery: on macOS, the kernel caps every read from a pty at about 1 KiB no matter how large the read buffer is. Previously, Ghostty processed each read as it arrived, so per-read overhead was paid every kilobyte, and the program writing output sat blocked while we parsed. Ghostty now drains the pty on one thread while parsing on another, raising end-to-end I/O throughput by 25% to 55% on macOS (M4 Max, plain and Unicode text) over Ghostty 1.3.x.

VT processing itself is also much faster. In parser benchmarks, plain ASCII text went from 128 MB/s to 725 MB/s (5.7x), CJK text got 3.4x faster, and a 2.6 GB dataset of real-world terminal recordings from asciinema is processed about 25% faster. Together, these changes took cat-ing a 150 MB text file on macOS from 1.5 seconds to 566 milliseconds.

The GTK app has a new rendering pipeline. It no longer uses GtkGLArea. Instead, the renderer draws with EGL on its own thread, exports each frame as a DMABUF, and hands it to GTK to display. Rendering is now triple-buffered, which should greatly reduce tearing, and no OpenGL calls run on the main thread anymore, which let us remove a number of workarounds.

The most visible result is crisp text under fractional scaling. Text and box-drawing characters are no longer blurry with fractional display scaling such as 125% or 150% (#1938 ). We fixed this once before, but it came back after upstream GTK changes. Ghostty now renders at the window's true fractional scale, so every rendered pixel maps to exactly one pixel on screen.

The new render surface needs a system EGL library (see Changes for Package Maintainers). On GPU drivers that can't export DMABUFs, Ghostty falls back to copying frames through the CPU, which is slower but works.

PRs: #13962 #13993 #13995 #13998 #14002 #14061

Ghostty now implements the Kitty clipboard protocol (OSC 5522) on both macOS and GTK. Ghostty 1.3 could parse the protocol but didn't implement it.

OSC 52 can only read and write plain text. The Kitty clipboard protocol lets programs running in the terminal read and write any kind of clipboard data, such as images or HTML, and lets them pick among the formats on the clipboard. Programs can also enable paste events (mode 5522), which tell the program what formats a paste contains so it can read the one it wants. This is how a terminal program can accept a pasted image, for example.

Clipboard access follows the existing clipboard-read and clipboard-write configurations. By default, reads prompt for permission and writes are allowed. The permission prompt shows a preview of the clipboard contents, including images. Programs that send a password can have your answer remembered for the rest of that terminal session. A new clipboard-write-limit-bytes configuration limits the size of a single write (default 64 MiB). A write over the limit fails and leaves the clipboard untouched.

You can now rearrange splits on GTK by dragging them, matching the macOS feature introduced in Ghostty 1.3. When a tab has two or more splits, each split shows a drag handle at the top. Grab the handle and drop the split onto any other split, including splits in other tabs and windows. An overlay shows which side of the target split it will land on. The terminal contents, working directory, and running processes all move with it.

A new drag-handle configuration controls when drag handles are shown on both macOS and GTK: auto (default), always, or never. With never, splits can't be rearranged with the mouse. Hiding the handles was a common request from users who found them in the way.

On GTK, splits can only be dropped onto other splits. Dragging a split out to create a new tab or window is macOS-only.

PRs: #14454 #13494 #14483 #14538 #14673 #14375 #14144

Ghostty 1.4 includes dozens of improvements to core terminal emulation. We now run esctest, Thomas Dickey's terminal conformance suite, against Ghostty's terminal core in CI. Alongside that, we fixed many differences from xterm in cursor movement within margins, tab stops, character sets, mode reports, and more. You can find the full list of related changes in the terminal capabilities section.

Ghostty also supports several new sequences, including soft terminal reset (DECSTR), extended cursor position reports (DECXCPR), backarrow key mode (DECBKM), and screen size reports in characters (CSI 19 t). Programs can also now ask whether the terminal is visible using visibility reports (mode 2033), so they can stop rendering while hidden.

Keyboard encoding also matches xterm more closely, with fixes for modifyOtherKeys mode 2 and legacy Alt-as-Escape encoding of non-ASCII keys.

PRs: #12582 #12814 #13533 #13844 #14472 #14854

Ghostty 1.2 introduced SSH shell integration features as a work in progress. In Ghostty 1.4, they're backed by a new ghostty +ssh CLI action: a drop-in ssh wrapper that prepares the remote session for Ghostty and then runs the real ssh with your arguments forwarded unchanged.

Before connecting, +ssh installs Ghostty's terminfo on the remote host so the proper xterm-ghostty TERM value can be used, and forwards COLORTERM, TERM_PROGRAM, and TERM_PROGRAM_VERSION. If the terminfo install fails (for example, tic isn't available on the remote), it falls back to TERM=xterm-256color. Flags such as --terminfo=false, --forward-env=false, and --verbose control each step.

The ssh-env and ssh-terminfo shell integration features (still opt-in) now call +ssh too. Previously, each shell integration carried its own copy of this logic, which made up roughly a third of our shell integration scripts. Now bash, zsh, fish, elvish, and Nushell all behave identically, and you can get the same behavior from scripts or anywhere else by calling ghostty +ssh directly.

The terminfo install itself is also more reliable. Ghostty now always installs its own bundled terminfo rather than accepting whatever xterm-ghostty entry the remote already has, and it reinstalls automatically when a Ghostty update changes the terminfo. It also no longer needs infocmp or Ghostty's terminfo on the local machine. Installs are cached per host and port, so two machines behind one address no longer share a cache entry. And ssh-env no longer silently overrides SetEnv entries in your ~/.ssh/config (#11518 ).

The +ssh-cache command that manages this cache now takes positional destinations (ghostty +ssh-cache user@example.com) and has a new --prune=30d flag. See the breaking changes if you script against +ssh-cache.

Over the past 6 months, the C API for libghostty-vt has grown from a parser and a few encoders into a complete terminal emulator API, adopted by dozens of free and commercial terminal emulators used by millions of people. It's hard to quantify exactly, but the number of people using a libghostty-based terminal is confidently several times the number of people using the Ghostty GUI.

libghostty-vt now builds for macOS, Linux, Windows (MSVC and GNU), FreeBSD, Android, iOS, and WebAssembly, and CI continuously verifies every one of these targets. The goal is for libghostty-vt to be generally usable everywhere, within reason, so if you want more target support, let us know!

#2897. Salkantay Trek: Machu Picchu and Much More

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

October usually spells the end of the alpine hiking season in my neck of the woods. But as the spooky season rolled around this year, I wasn’t quite ready to hang up my trail runners. Where could I still climb face-to-face with glaciers and not risk an unforeseen PNW blizzard (or a very foreseen PNW drizzle)? In that other hemisphere, of course!

And so I traveled south to Peru to hike the Salkantay Trek, a 35.7-mile route through the world’s longest and second tallest continental mountain range, the Andes. Starting near the former Inca capital of Cusco, the trail climbs up toward Salkantay Pass at over 15,000 feet above sea level before descending into Peru’s inland jungle region and ending at the legendary Inca citadel of Machu Picchu. This trek can be undertaken independently or with one of Peru’s many superb guiding companies. It takes from three days to over a week, depending on your desired pace and route. We went with a four-day guided and supported itinerary through Salkantay Trekking, since my partner is a big fan of glamping and not such a fan of camping. We spent three days in Cusco (elevation: 11,152 feet) to acclimate before starting the trek.

Peru is an enormous country – the third biggest in South America, more than triple the size of California. Getting from one place to another often involves waking up long before dawn to catch an epic bus ride. Thus we checked out of our hotel in Cusco around 4:30am to catch our shuttle to the trailhead at Challacancha. After a steep initial climb from the road, we settled into a stretch of trail that our guide described as “Peruvian flat” – gradually but steadily uphill. After a couple hours walking alongside a narrow aqueduct, which channeled glacial melt to the village of Mollepata and its surrounding farms, we reached the ascent to Humantay Lake. The ascent was an optional out-and-back (or more accurately, up-and-down) side quest from our trek, but you’d have to be crazy to miss this stunning turquoise lake. The Inca worshipped many mountains as Apus, living mountain spirits that provide life to surrounding communities, and this glacial waterfall-fed lake illustrated Humantay Mountain’s role as an Apu very clearly. Peru held elections just before our visit and the campaign posters were still everywhere – in Mollepata and along the roads near the trailhead, the slogans were all about water. “Water for All – Tourism AND Agriculture” was a common refrain. The blessings of the mountain were still well understood to be at the center of people’s lives.

The trail to and from the lake was bracingly switchback-free, which made it a great trial run for the ascent to Salkantay Pass that we’d have to do the next day.

We spent our first night glamping at 12,795 feet inside a cozy glass dome, with a view of the Humantay and Salkantay glacier-bejeweled mountains and of the brilliant milky starry night sky. Then it was time for another early morning, waking up before 5am to begin the ascent to Salkantay Pass. It was a beautiful, steady ascent, and when we arrived at the pass at 15,190 feet, our wonderful cook was waiting for us with a thermos of coca tea, which helped preempt any symptoms of altitude sickness. It was wild realizing that we were higher than the summit of Mt. Whitney. These altitudes were no big deal to the Inca who traversed this path over 500 years earlier, without the benefit of synthetic fabrics, nut butters, well-cushioned trail runners, etc. This was a trade route between different ecological and agricultural zones in the empire, between different cities and religious centers, and a place of worship to venerate the apu mountain spirit at a time when few peoples around the world were venturing so far above sea level.

From Salkantay Pass, it was a long, rocky, brutal descent – about 5,500 feet down, sharing a narrow dusty trail with the many mules who had packed our food and gear up the mountain. We routinely had to pull over the side of the trail to let a massive mule train pass us by – the largest we counted was over 30 mules. The ecological transition we witnessed was intense – from the drier alpine puna ecosystem of the high Andes, we descended into Peru’s inland jungle ecosystem. The forest grew thick, the bugs started biting, and the temperature and humidity rose quickly.

Upon finally reaching the village of Collpapampa, we checked into our most glam glampsite of all – the Mountain Sky Camp, where we stayed in glass domes with attached private bathrooms and hot showers (!). The restaurant in the campground even served delicious pisco sours, Peru’s signature cocktail and a welcome treat after 14.6 miles with a serious climb and a much more serious descent.

The fruit of all fruits … the one, the only, the custard apple / chirimoya.

After the first two fairly intense days, the trek mellowed significantly. We hiked out of Collpapampa at dawn, descending to the bottom of the scenic Santa Teresa River valley. We followed the river for a few hours, then stopped by a local fruit stand where we got to sample some fruits that don’t make it to typical U.S. grocery shelves, including lúcuma, pacay, and granadilla. It was here that I enjoyed my very favorite meal of the whole trail: a ripe custard apple, known locally as chirimoya. The juicy, fleshy white fruit tasted just like candy and was a great way to rehydrate after a few hours of steamy jungle hiking. Later in the day, we stopped by a coffee farm and got to sample Peru’s super-intense geisha coffee beans – much more potent flavor and caffeine punch than the standard arabica beans that we find in most U.S. coffee shops.

After hopping on a bus to the riverside dot on the map known as Hidroeléctrica (for the dam), we walked a few more miles along the Peru Rail train tracks until we reached Aguas Calientes, the base town for one of the wonders of the world, the Inca city-in-the-clouds of Machu Picchu.

It was another early morning to beat the crowds to the entrance to Machu Picchu. We made it to the entrance gate around 6am, and there was already a massive line. There is no low season here: visitors pour into this site all day, all year, from all over the world. But the crowds do nothing to diminish the magic of the ruins in the morning mist. We were astonished to learn that this citadel was inhabited for less than a century, completed around 1450 AD and abandoned by around 1530 after the Spanish invaded and toppled the Inca empire. It still isn’t clear why this stunning and seemingly impregnable citadel, built on a mountain ridge hundreds of feet above the valley floor, was so precipitously abandoned. As far as we know, the Spanish never made it here. There was no dramatic battle. Did smallpox or some other disease spread here, via Inca visitors from other towns that the Spanish had reached? Or did the residents heed the call to evacuate and join emperor Manco Inca in his last stand against the Spanish at Vilcabamba, about 40 miles away? Maybe we’ll never know. But the extraordinary craftsmanship of the Inca who built this place, and their knowledge of astronomy which informed the citadel’s design, will stand as an eternal testament to their civilization.

Peru is truly a hiker’s paradise – the eco-tourism sector is extremely well-developed, and you can find a broad range of guided and independent hikes throughout the gorgeous Andes. In addition to leading groups on the heavily trafficked Inca Trail and Salkantay Trek itineraries, our guide was preparing to lead a 7-day hike to Choqueqirao, another spectacular mountain complex of Inca ruins with much smaller crowds than Machu Picchu. The villages we encountered along our hike had a wide selection of accommodations and resupply options for independent hikers, as well as pay-to-use showers. And did I mention the dogs?! Everywhere we went in Peru, both in the cities and in the mountains, there were adorable, well-groomed, well-fed, well-socialized dogs of innumerable breeds ambling around the streets. We were especially delighted to encounter chakus, indigenous Andean herding dogs that were officially recognized as a breed during our visit in October 2026.  I couldn’t wait to return and explore more of this stunning country. In the meantime, I’d be searching the produce sections of Seattle’s mercados for some custard apples.

If personal growth comes from discomfort, you’ll grow a lot. With time to reflect, you’ll also (hopefully) be able to look back fondly at the uncomfortable days. Beyond that, if you consistently have a type three experience, quitting is probably the right choice.

In terms of fun, every job I’ve ever had was structured two, one, three. The thickness of the sandwich varied a lot, and getting through stage three required perseverance. During the final stage of my last job, reading The Trek posts each lunchtime was a good way to escape the daily grind. If you can relate, and you’re planning a thru-hike, consider becoming a Trek blogger. In a virtual world increasingly overrun by chatbots, your efforts will be appreciated.

Before starting the PCT in 2021, I intended to blog in real time. I soon discovered, however, that gazing at the scenery was preferable to tapping away on my phone. In town, I had neither the time nor the willpower to devote to writing. I admire the bloggers who make time. Their stories contain type two fun complete with all of its rough edges.

As usual, I’m posting post-trail, using my paper-and-pencil journal for reference. The downside of the delay is that time has already started to smooth those rough edges. It might, possibly, lead me to describe a rosier version of events than if I’d blogged in real time. I’m only mentioning it because I don’t want to inadvertently set unrealistic expectations for prospective thru-hikers.

I needed to get to the western terminus of the Ice Age Trail (IAT), so in the last week of July, I flew from Boise to Denver to Minneapolis. The flights were on time, my backpack arrived safely, but Frontier ended up being no cheaper than a non-budget airline.

On my second flight, my row had four empty seats. A few rows ahead, a guy asked if he could change seats, and the flight attendant said no. I waited to move to the window until she went to the back of the plane. The guy on the other side of the aisle moved to his window. The middle-seat occupants in the row behind me moved to the aisle seats in my row. When the flight attendant returned to give her safety demonstration, she knew people had switched seats. I started the next day with a one mile walk, doubling the number of miles on my new boots. My bus left just after midday, and dropped me about 30 miles north of the city in a commuter town called Forest Lake. I bought a five day resupply at the nearby Walmart, shouldered my pack, and walked another mile to offload my duffel bag at the post office. All I had to do now was find my way to the start of the IAT. At first I considered hitching, but couldn’t find a place I liked: somewhere with a pull-out, and slower vehicles that were obviously going in the direction I needed to go. While I was searching for a potential location, a passerby sized me up and took an educated guess.

“Weed store’s right there,” he pointed over his shoulder.

After that, I decided to take an Uber. The car picked me up about five minutes later, and my day’s journey ended at Interstate State Park.

At eight o’clock the next morning, I walked my final Minnesota mile. If not for the rush-hour traffic on Highway 8, the trail along the St Croix River would have been peaceful. I crossed the bridge into Wisconsin, walked a few hundred yards, and put a hand on the rock marking the IAT’s western terminus. Two trail-miles later, I celebrated with two donuts and a cold Mountain Dew. Six miles after that, I did my first tick-check. I found the first of many that I’d have to scrape off my skin in the coming weeks.

At the end of day one, at the edge of a small town called Centuria, I made camp. In the morning, I continued north along a rail-to-trail which is part of the State Trails Network. I stopped for a late breakfast at the second of two small towns, then disappeared into the backcountry for a few days. Here’s what I learned.

Day four, my phone informed me of a heat advisory. Temperature: 90℉. Humidity: 90%. That afternoon, during the road-walk to Grassy Lake Segment, my brain overheated. It suddenly occurred to me that the world needs a hiking-based romance novel. Here’s the first page of my masterpiece.

It was a torrid, sultry, steamy night amidst the swamps of western Wisconsin. The day’s heat advisory wouldn’t expire until midnight, still three hours away. In the corner of a remote trailhead parking lot, in the twilight, stood a hiker. He paused to look down, for a moment, with a certain satisfaction. Naked, but now fully erect, his tent was a monument to self-confidence. Without its fly, it announced to the world that there would be no thunderstorms tonight.

“Oooh, that’s hot,” he murmured, as he slowly pulled the tent zipper down. Then, he eased himself inside. Things were about to get sticky.

He immediately stuck to his sleeping pad. Its rubbery texture resisted the advances of his moist skin, issuing a series of squeaks and moans as he wriggled into position. He lay there for a minute, listening to the crickets and mosquitoes.

He removed his shorts. Not his compression shorts, just his actual shorts. “I’m not Donald Duck,” he thought. The corner of his mouth twitched into a half-smile at the memory.

It was pretty tight in there, and peeling off his t-shirt required real effort. As he squirmed and maneuvered, the sleeping pad complained again, but less convincingly. It was more amenable now that it was better lubricated.

The night air was still and stultifying. Beads of sweat trickled down his torso. Salt stung his legs. They still bore the marks of his previous tryst: a relationship that had started well, but ended badly.

He mopped his brow with the t-shirt. All he could do now was wait. The temperature and humidty would relent soon, he hoped, and he’d sink into a peaceful, restorative sleep…

Defensiveness is one of the biggest problems we face in close relationships. It’s really hard for many of us to hear anything negative about ourselves, something we said or did, or a way we behaved that hurt, angered, upset, disappointed, or failed someone else. We’re really bad at being told that we did something "wrong." It’s staggering, really, the degree to which we humans will battle to the death to prove that we’ve done nothing wrong and someone else’s experience is not true.

Often, when someone close to us tells us we’ve upset them in some way, almost immediately, we brace ourselves and go into an internal state of code red. We stop listening, disconnect from what they’re saying, and switch into a kind of fight-or-flight response. We're not able to hear their words or empathize with their experience because, consciously or unconsciously, we've determined that we're in grave danger and our self-esteem is at risk. It's at this point that our survival instinct kicks in and kidnaps the more evolved and wiser part of our mind.

For every situation we encounter in life, we may write a narrative, a story that answers three important questions: what is actually happening, why is it happening, and what it means. And specifically, what it means about (and for) ourselves. We experience everything through the lens of our personal “self,” a “me” that we've constructed and that must be continually maintained, shored up, and protected.

It’s not, however, because we’re all raging narcissists that we look at every moment from a self-centered perspective, to gauge whether it will serve or harm us, validate our self-image or destroy it. This ever-present question by which we live, “Does this moment feel good, does it make me feel good about myself?” is really all about survival. Our survival instinct is constantly waging a battle with life—vigilantly trying to ensure that our self-image, our story of “me,” doesn’t get harmed or destroyed. The problem, however, is that we imagine that the narrative we’ve constructed about ourselves, our “I’m somebody who” story, is who we fundamentally are. Anything, therefore, that goes against it holds the power to annihilate us at an existential level.

How this plays out in everyday life is that if someone tells me I’ve hurt them, whether intentionally or not, I can't listen, try to understand, or feel openhearted towards them. All I know is that I’ve done something wrong, which means that I am wrong and hurtful at my core, which, through the lens of my survival instinct, means that I will be cast out of the herd and left behind to die. All I can hear in that moment are deafening alarm bells; all I can feel is danger. Their experience puts my “me” story, namely, that I am a kind person, in jeopardy. As a result, their experience must be invalidated; their perception of me proven wrong. Because my "me" story doesn’t feel like just a story but feels like my very existence, I must get them to agree that I am not who they think I am, not the hurtful person they're portraying me to be. Once I can get this confirmation, then (and only then) can I return safely to my story of “me.” With the other's narrative proven wrong, I can relax, knowing that I am once again a kind person. Still, even with this current threat deactivated, I must remain hyper-vigilant and on watch for anyone's experience that might threaten my “me.”

Our sense of who we are is the sum of every experience we’ve ever lived, every compliment, judgment, and perception of us that’s ever been levied for or against us. Our self-image is therefore highly unsafe, transitory, and fragile: if we say or do something “wrong” (in our eyes—or someone else’s eyes), then who we are is fundamentally “wrong.” Our self feels dependent on and collapsed into the perceptions of our actions. This puts us in a terribly vulnerable position when it comes to other people’s experience, as well as all the other uncontrollable parts of life. After all, other people experience the world — and us — through the lens of their own stories, not ours. The value, meaning, and intention assigned to our behavior vary infinitely depending on the lens through which it’s being viewed. And yet, we award their perceptions with the power to confirm or deny who we are and can be.

Other people and life in general consistently force us to confront parts of ourselves and face perceptions of us that don’t align with our curated narrative on “who we are.” The way other people experience and perceive us, through their conditioning, beliefs, wounds, and everything else, is often inconsistent with the story of “me” we’ve constructed in our mind. And so we fight with this fundamental reality: the person we’re perceived as, how we’re experienced from the outside, is different from how we experience ourselves from the inside. Different and perhaps—also valid.

In part two of this series, I'll address the ways we can start to heal defensiveness and shift our relationship with other people and their experience. Then, we don’t have to be so defensive, self-protective and self-focused; we can truly meet other people, be curious, and empathize with other people's experience. We can stop feeling like everyone on earth is a potential threat to who we are. Ultimately, we build a sense of self that can tolerate the full range of realities we encounter in the world. I counted it as a learning experience for us — if a place is legally accessible by vehicle, we should expect vehicles to be in there.

#4063. The Relationship Mistake We Don't Notice Until It's Too Late

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

We know that relationships matter. Yet we consistently behave as though they can wait.

In the workplace, strong relationships can make the difference between a project's success, a secured promotion, or a closed deal. However, in our high-pressure, time-poor world, we tend to focus on what we’re trying to achieve now, doing the hard graft ourselves if need be.

We can address our longer-term goals another day.

But of course, that day soon comes, and we’re back to square one. Failing to invest in our relationships earlier makes the support or advocacy we need now harder to secure. Delaying nonurgent relationship-building activity carries real consequences, just ones we are not aware of until it’s too late.

It’s easy to assume that relationships will always be in place when needed. But trust, just like physical fitness or financial security, is built over time and only becomes apparent when we need it the most.

It’s natural to prioritise what is urgent, visible, and measurable. Particularly when we are held accountable for short-term targets. Nobody wants to explain why they have attended a conference or arranged a coffee meeting when the pressure is on.

Psychologists and behavioural economists describe this as present bias. We place greater value on what delivers results now than on benefits that may emerge later. Under pressure to meet targets, another hour at our desk feels productive. A conversation with someone who might be able to help us in six months feels like a luxury.

Relationship-building can be seen as a "soft" activity, a nice-to-have but nonessential way to spend our time. It doesn’t feel productive when we can’t demonstrate an immediate payoff, so we focus on activities we can count today.

Without relationships in place to guide, support, and advocate for us, however, it’s much harder to achieve those short-term goals. The insights and ideas other people can offer us can prevent us from repeating other people’s mistakes, while influential people in our network can open doors that would previously have remained blocked.

It’s a false economy to discard relationship-building for sweat and toil; if we put in the work up front to nurture the support we need, we can overcome challenges more easily. What feels like a distraction from achieving our goals may actually be an investment in achieving them more easily.

Failing to think strategically about relationships creates blind spots. Apart from the strongest relationships, which have almost become personal rather than purely professional, or those with people who work in immediate proximity, people fall off our radar if they are not immediately relevant to what we’re focusing on. It’s not necessarily that we wilfully neglect people when we don’t need them, but many people assume goodwill established earlier will remain available if we shelve the relationship for a while.

How many times have you been on the receiving end of a LinkedIn connection request from someone you haven’t been in touch with for a while, only to find it quickly followed up by a message asking for your help because they are looking for a new job? How did that make you feel?

Goodwill doesn't necessarily disappear when contact stops, but we shouldn't assume it remains unchanged. Regular, low-pressure interactions help maintain the trust and familiarity that make asking for support feel natural.

When we only reappear because we need something, our intentions may be genuine, but the approach can feel transactional. We are asking the relationship to deliver more than we have recently invested in it.

We can work with someone every day, collaborate on projects, and attend countless meetings without ever developing the trust that encourages them to challenge us, advocate for us, or offer support when things become difficult.

Regular connection is not enough on its own. Familiarity is not the same as trust. We need to find a balance between two key types of interaction:

If most of your conversations with a colleague are transactional, you may not be creating genuine relational depth and connection. We need curiosity, reciprocity, and the human touch. We need to find the space to understand the person behind the function.

This can be even harder when we are based in another office, or even another country, and most of our conversations happen remotely. But we need to proactively seek those points of connection, away from our respective agendas, if we are going to invest meaningfully in developing rapport and connection.

We need to think more strategically about our professional relationships and be willing to invest in them before we need to. But this doesn’t mean we approach other people to manipulate or trick them. This is about setting aside time, even during our busiest periods, to invest in meaningful and durable human relationships.

By doing this, we can ensure that support is there when we need it. The real mistake is not that people care too much or too little about relationships. It’s that they care too late.

#1938. Assessing the first round of oral arguments of the 2026-27 term

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson – typically the court’s most frequent speaker at oral argument – began the term relatively quietly. In Suncor Energy v. Boulder County, the first argued case, she spoke the fourth-most among the participating justices, accounting for only 12.1% of the court’s words. But that relative silence did not last. Jackson led three of the next four arguments and finished the six-case sitting with 6,254 words, significantly more than any of her colleagues.

The arguments produced other patterns, some less predictable. Jackson and Justice Sonia Sotomayor repeatedly concentrated their attention on the same litigating side, but Justice Neil Gorsuch did so as well, despite the justices’ ideological differences. At the same time, their questions did not necessarily point toward identical legal outcomes.

The cases themselves – and how they were treated – revealed something more. Disputes concerning climate litigation, veterans’ constitutional claims, retirement-plan investments, and environmental review all raised questions about when, where, and under what conditions judicial intervention is available. Indeed, that essentially became the theme of the court’s October session.

Jackson’s dominance and some unexpected cross-ideological alignment

As described above, Jackson’s relatively limited involvement in Suncor initially appeared to vary from one of the Supreme Court’s most consistent recent patterns. During the 2025-26 term, she accounted for 22% of justices’ total speech and led in 41 of 58 oral arguments. Yet in the first argument of this term, her 829 words represented only 12.1% of substantive justice speech, placing her fourth among eight participating justices. Across the other five arguments, however, Jackson’s share averaged 21.0%. By the end of the sitting, Jackson had spoken 6,254 words, more than any colleague, and accounted for 19.3% of all substantive justice speech.

Jackson’s questioning also revealed a recurring alignment with Sotomayor and, more surprisingly, Gorsuch. Across several disputes, the three justices repeatedly directed a disproportionate amount of attention toward the same side.

In Johnson v. United States Congress, for instance, Gorsuch questioned whether the veterans’ benefits system possessed the competence to adjudicate constitutional challenges, while Sotomayor emphasized the burden of forcing veterans through an administrative process prior to obtaining constitutional review. Jackson similarly challenged the government’s account of how constitutional questions were handled in practice. The three justices’ approaches differed, but all three explored weaknesses in the government’s proposed review scheme.

In Suncor, the justices’ shared focus covered up larger doctrinal disagreements. Jackson questioned whether the case was sufficiently developed for Supreme Court intervention. Sotomayor asked about the courts’ authority and whether specific arguments had been preserved. Gorsuch explored how the Clean Air Act affected federal common law and potential state-law restrictions.

These alignments should not be conflated with emerging voting coalitions, however. In Beaird v. United States, for example, a Federal Sentencing Guidelines case, Jackson and Gorsuch proved the argument’s two dominant speakers while developing substantially different accounts of the U.S. Sentencing Commission’s authority.

The lesson is this: The justices who identify pressure points in an argument do not always group along familiar ideological lines, and agreement about which position deserves examination need not produce agreement about the possible controlling rule.

Jackson’s individual prominence did not translate into liberal dominance of the court’s collective conversation. Across the six arguments, conservative justices contributed 54.3% of substantive justice speech, compared with 45.7% from the three liberals. Conservatives supplied the majority of justices’ words in five of the six cases, with Johnson the exception. This represents a somewhat different balance (so far) from the 50.9% conservative–49.1% liberal division reported in a previous Legalytics analysis of the 2025-26 term.

Perhaps more revealing was the distribution of participation within those blocs. Justice Brett Kavanaugh finished second across the sitting with 5,031 words, followed closely by Justice Elena Kagan (4,998) and Gorsuch (4,920). Yet the three justices occupied markedly different positions from argument to argument. Kavanaugh supplied 23.7% of the justice’ speech in Suncor but only 6.0% in Johnson. Gorsuch ranged from 25.7% in Beaird to just 1.2% in Salazar v. Paramount Global. Kagan, who averaged 21.2% across the opening-day arguments, averaged only 13.6% across the next four.

Overall, the six arguments revealed neither the conservative or liberal bloc dominating the conversation. Instead, participation shifted substantially with the legal questions presented, often placing different justices at the forefront of particular disputes. Those changes become especially consequential when examining the competing theories of judicial review and interpretive authority developed throughout the individual arguments.

Four arguments about access to judicial review

Four of the court’s first six arguments presented different versions of a recurring question: what must happen before a federal court can intervene in a case? The doctrines varied considerably, from Supreme Court jurisdiction and veterans’ constitutional claims to pleading requirements and administrative finality. Nevertheless, each argument required the justices to consider how procedural and institutional/justiciable boundaries determine whether a legal challenge can proceed.

The sharpest illustration came in Department of the Air Force v. Prutehi Guåhan, where the justices examined whether the Air Force’s decision to seek a territorial hazardous-waste permit constituted final federal agency action, and how that determination interacted with environmental review under a federal statute. The government received 67% of explicit question marks but accounted for only 52.7% of advocate speech. Its counsel, Sopan Joshi, experienced a 60% cutoff rate, which was the highest among the 15 advocates appearing during the sitting.

The exchanges revealed different understandings of when an agency decision is settled enough to justify judicial intervention. Kagan questioned whether an agency’s completed deliberations could be rendered non-final simply because another regulator’s approval remained necessary. Jackson, typically on Kagan’s side, focused on the opposite possibility: if Guam’s permitting authority could impose conditions materially changing the Air Force’s project, perhaps the application remained a proposal rather than an operative federal commitment. Justices Clarence Thomas and Amy Coney Barrett called out the unusual institutional arrangement, with the federal government appearing as a regulated applicant rather than the permitting authority itself.

Gorsuch and Chief Justice John Roberts approached the problem from another angle, exploring whether postponing review could leave challengers without an effective opportunity to challengee the federal government’s environmental obligations. Kavanaugh explored the practical consequences of requiring successive rounds of review.

The relationship to the opening-day cases is telling. In Suncor, Thomas and Jackson questioned whether the dispute was sufficiently developed for Supreme Court review, particularly with other federal issues and several factual questions still unresolved below. In Johnson, the justices confronted a very different institutional barrier: whether constitutional challenges involving veterans’ benefits must proceed through the specialized administrative-review system rather than federal district court. Jackson, Sotomayor, and Gorsuch each tested aspects of the government’s proposed route, including whether it provided a practical means of resolving constitutional claims.

Anderson v. Intel Corporation Investment Policy Committee brought up a related yet distinct procedural issue. There, the question was what a retirement-plan plaintiff must allege to advance a claim that an ERISA fiduciary failed to use the requisite “care, skill, prudence, or diligence” under certain circumstances. Kavanaugh and Jackson distinguished underperforming investments from allegedly imprudent asset-allocation strategies, while Kagan and Barrett examined how rigorous a “meaningful investment benchmark” must be.

These cases do not rest on a particular judicial ideology. But they all deal with the availability of judicial review: whether the litigation has reached a proper point, the question of an adequate forum, the sufficiency of the allegations, and whether the court’s intervention is appropriate yet.

Beaird: competing theories of legal authority

No argument during the sitting generated more speech from the justices than Beaird v. United States. The justices contributed 7,791 words, more than twice their total in Anderson, with Gorsuch and Jackson accounting for 25.7% and 22.4% of these, respectively. Together, the two justices provided nearly half the court’s substantive speech. Yet their exchanges revealed a profound difference in how they understood the authority of the U.S. Sentencing Commission and the legal status of its commentary.

The dispute asks whether Stinson v. United States, the court’s 1993 decision requiring deference to Sentencing Guidelines commentary under specified conditions, continues to supply the governing rule. The case’s unusual alignment further distinguished the argument. Both Beaird and the United States supported vacating the judgment, and the court thus appointed a “friend of the court,” Anthony Dick, to defend Stinson. The petitioner-aligned advocates consequently accounted for 68.3% of advocate speech and received 80.1% of justice words. This imbalance partly reflects the unusual distribution of advocates rather than necessarily indicating hostility toward their position.

Jackson, who once served on the Sentencing Commission, began by challenging the premise that the Sentencing Guidelines and their commentary can meaningfully be separated. In her account, sentencing judges rely on both parts of the Guidelines Manual to calculate the recommended sentencing range. Commentary helps determine how provisions operate together as opposed to consisting of an unrelated agency interpretation. She returned to Congress’ decision to establish a sentencing framework intended to limit disparities among similarly situated defendants, questioning whether importing limitations from administrative deference doctrine would undermine that design.

Gorsuch’s questioning approached the problem from a very different institutional perspective. He examined where Congress expressly authorized binding commentary, why the Commission should be able to accomplish through commentary what it could instead accomplish through formal Guidelines amendments, and whether deference would weaken the procedural safeguards associated with that process. His concerns extended to the incentives created by allowing the Commission to make authoritative interpretations without necessarily following the same procedures applicable to the Guidelines themselves.

Other justices explored possible consequences of either approach. Kavanaugh repeatedly returned to the stability of a system operating under Stinson for more than three decades, questioning whether revisiting the doctrine might introduce new uncertainty into sentencing. Kagan examined whether Congress’ express recognition of policy statements complicated efforts to distinguish them from commentary, suggesting that differences in terminology might not fully resolve the question of authority.

The exchanges suggest several possible routes to a decision: reaffirming the current system, limiting the circumstances under which commentary receives controlling weight, or requiring a more explicit statutory basis for binding interpretations. They also illustrate a theme from earlier research on the relationship between oral argument and eventual opinions: The most revealing material may not be which advocate received the most questions, but the competing legal frameworks the justices developed as they questioned counsel.

The first six arguments also revealed substantial differences in how advocates were able to develop their answers. Across 982 advocate speaking turns, 420 ended in an explicitly identifiable cutoff by a justice, a rate of 42.8%. That figure was somewhat higher on the first day (45.2%) than during the four subsequent arguments (41.4%), but the variation among individual advocates was significantly larger.

Joshi, representing the Air Force in Prutehi, experienced the sitting’s highest cutoff rate at 60.0%, with 66 of his 110 turns ending that way. Kannon Shanmugam followed at 56.9% in Suncor, while Paul Clement was at 55.2% in Salazar. These were not merely differences between government and private counsel but arose in disputes involving separate statutes, procedural issues, and judicial argument coalitions.

The clearest within-case contrast occurred in Salazar. Joshua Hammack received numerous questions about his interpretation of the Video Privacy Protection Act, yet only 21.5% of his turns ended in cutoffs. Clement, advocating the opposite position, was cut off in 55.2% of his turns. Similarly, in Prutehi, Caroline Flynn’s 41.1% cutoff rate (arguing on behalf of Prutehi Guåhan) was substantially below the government’s 60.0%, and her median response was more than twice as long.

Even advocates supporting the same position had different experiences. In Suncor, Shanmugam’s median response was 13.5 words, compared with 35 for Principal Deputy Solicitor General Sarah Harris, who supported the petitioner and experienced a 40.0% cutoff rate.

The differences illustrate how much the structure and subject of individual exchanges can shape an advocate’s opportunity to explain a legal position. These observations also complement earlier Empirical SCOTUS findings about an increasingly active oral-argument bench.

Jackson’s unusually limited participation in the first argument of the term, Suncor, proved to be an exception rather than the beginning of a broader retreat. Across six arguments, she remained the court’s leading individual speaker, even as the distribution of judicial participation shifted considerably from case to case.

More surprising was the relationship between the justices’ attention and their ideological positions. Jackson, Sotomayor, and Gorsuch consistently concentrated on the same litigating side. Called "The Light that Shines Through the Universe," the sculpture is the brainchild of Vietnamese American artist Tuan Andrew Nguyen. It is a tribute to the memory of the Buddhas of Bamiyan, a celebrated archeological site in Afghanistan. The Buddhas were carved into the massive cliff in the Bamiyan Valley 1,500 years ago. They are in the classic mudras (or postures) of fearlessness (Abhaya) and giving blessings and compassion (Varada). What is profound is that the artist takes the materials of war and destruction and turns them into objects of peace and resilience.

When we look at the sculpture, we are moved by the monumental presence, the heartbreak of loss, and the ubiquity of violence, but above all, the possibility of healing. The Buddha, in its component parts, holds the possibility that we have the imagination, energy, and courage to create a better future for all.

“May all beings be Safe and Healthy. May all beings be free of suffering and ill-will. May we all be Peaceful and live in Wisdom and Compassion. May we treat each other with kindness and respect.”

#3360. The Best Leg Day Workout to Become a Stronger Runner

Want to get faster? Then it’s time to strength train.

Runners often assume that “running builds strong legs,” but a better way to think about this is: “Running requires strong legs.”

If you want to run faster and more efficiently then hill repeats, speed workouts, and long runs will help—but doing leg exercises consistently will give you the edge you need to power up inclines, have a strong finishing kick, and end a long run feeling good.

Relying solely on running is a “less-than-optimal” training strategy that can eventually lead to injuries too, Dane Miklaus, C.S.C.S., founder of WORK Training Studio in Irvine, California, tells Runner’s World.

To gain those benefits from your strength training, plus stay healthy and pick up your pace, Miklaus developed the following leg day workout. Incorporate it into your current strength-training plan or pair it with an easy run. (New to strength training? Start with our bodyweight leg workout first.)

How to use this list: Perform 6 to 8 reps of each exercise (per side for the dumbbell swing, step-up, and Bulgarian split squat), resting up to 3 minutes between sets. Repeat the full circuit 4 times. Each move is demonstrated by Miklaus in the video above so you can learn the proper form.

You will need a heavy set of dumbbells and a loaded barbell. (If you don’t have a barbell, stick with the heavy dumbbells.) While you’ll want to start with lighter weights to get the form down, make sure to work up in weight. And don’t be afraid to go heavy: “Choose weights that are so heavy you literally cannot complete more than the prescribed reps,” Miklaus advises. “If you can do more at the end of the set, it’s not heavy enough.”

Why it works: The stiff-leg deadlift targets the hamstrings, glutes, and posterior core muscles, all of which tend to be neglected by runners who don’t strength train, Miklaus says. “This can lead to knee and hip injuries and decrease top speed,” he says. “Stiff-leg deadlifts help increase strength in those areas and can improve the power and speed of a runner’s ‘kick.’”

Why it works: Besides strengthening the posterior chain, dumbbell swings help develop a dynamic hip extension, which drives a runner’s forward propulsion.

Why it works: “Traditional deadlifts still have a very heavy quadriceps emphasis. The sumo deadlift allows the athlete to get deeper into their glutes and also target the oft-forgotten adductor muscles. For any runner who has ever experienced a ‘groin’ injury, selling them on the idea of strong adductors should be pretty easy,” Miklaus says. “On top of that, any training in the frontal plane (e.g. adduction, or moving toward the body’s midline) will help increase hip strength and stability.”

Why it works: Step-ups are tremendous glute strengtheners, but the box must be high enough, says Miklaus. “Ideally, your knee is higher than your hip joint when you begin the move and your foot is simply resting on the box. Make sure you extend all the way upright at the top before putting the other leg on the box in order to get the most benefit,” he says. “Also, make sure you return to the floor on the same leg in order to get the benefits of the eccentric contraction in the same leg.”

Why it works: “Bulgarian split squats directly target the quadriceps, hip flexors, hamstrings, and glutes, but they also add a great stability challenge as well. They’ll help strengthen pretty much every part of your leg, including down at your ankle,” Miklaus says.

Even if you do manage to avoid getting hurt, consistently skipping leg day could be robbing you of speed and power. “Fast-twitch muscle fiber types are primarily responsible for our fastest, most explosive, and strongest movements. Instead, they need direct targeting from heavy resistance training, and very fast, explosive exercises.”

#681. Eggplants have been secretly recruiting bacteria for thousands of years

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Scientists have discovered how eggplant genes influence the bacteria living around their roots, revealing a hidden connection between evolution and agriculture.

While farmers were selecting eggplants for bigger fruit and better flavor, the plants were doing something else entirely.

Their genes were helping determine which bacteria thrived around their roots. This process was not entirely random.

Specific genetic variations were linked to particular bacteria, and some of those variations appear to have been shaped by the eggplant’s history of cultivation and geographic expansion.

A new study caught them at it, and the findings are stranger and more useful than anyone expected.

The research comes from Nanjing Agricultural University and the Guangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences.

The team worked with 432 eggplant varieties from around the world. They sequenced their genomes and cataloged the microbial communities living around their roots.

This is the largest study of its kind in eggplant, and the scale was necessary.

These are not simple one-gene-one-microbe relationships. Most of the plant traits that influence microbial communities are controlled by dozens of genes acting in concert.

That’s exactly why the picture had been so hard to assemble before now.

Between 9% and 39% of the variation in individual microbial taxa comes down to host genetics. That is not a marginal effect.

The microbes most central to community stability – the core of the root microbiome – are under the tightest genetic control of all.

This suggests that eggplant genetics play an important role in determining which bacteria become established around its roots.

The team ran microbial genome-wide association studies and found 1,235 genetic variants linked to 46 core microbial taxa.

What those variants turned out to be is where things get odd.

The gene MYB113, which helps produce the deep purple pigment that makes eggplants recognizable, is associated with Stenotrophomonas.

This bacterial group includes species that produce growth hormones such as auxin, gibberellin, and abscisic acid.

The connection suggests that a gene associated with fruit color may also influence the root microbiome.

The gene BLH9, which governs organ development, is linked to Mycobacterium, which promotes root growth through its own auxin production.

A locus on chromosome 5 is associated with multiple bacteria from the order Rhizobiales. This is a group best known for fixing nitrogen in legumes.

“Plants are not passive recipients of their microbial partners – they actively manage them through their genetics,” the authors wrote.

“We found that eggplant has been shaping its microbiome not by accident, but as part of its adaptation to new environments.”

The sharpest finding in the paper is also the most conceptually satisfying.

The loci most strongly associated with microbiome composition sit in regions of the eggplant genome that show clear signs of directional selection.

These regions changed as eggplant moved from Southeast Asia into Europe and China over centuries of trade and cultivation.

Different geographic populations carry different combinations of alleles at these loci. Those combinations shape which bacteria show up at the roots.

So the spread of eggplant across the world was not just a story of farmers picking fruit they liked. It was also, invisibly, a story of plants assembling new microbial communities to match new soils and climates.

The agricultural history and the microbial history were running in parallel the whole time, and nobody knew.

“The same genes that helped eggplant spread across the globe also helped it recruit the right microbial allies along the way,” the authors explained.

“Plant-microbiome partnerships are not just ecological accidents – they are co-evolutionary outcomes.”

Crops that recruit beneficial bacteria could eventually require less fertilizer or fewer chemical treatments. But researchers still need to demonstrate those benefits under real farming conditions.

These are not small prizes. They sit at the center of every serious conversation about reducing agriculture’s chemical footprint without sacrificing yield.

Microbiome-assisted breeding would mean selecting varieties not only for what the plant does but for which microbial communities it assembles around itself.

The findings may also help researchers understand similar genetic relationships in other crops.

Gene editing, metabolomics, and field trials could help determine whether these discoveries translate into practical agricultural benefits.

But all of that requires knowing where in the genome to look. That, more than anything, is what this study provides.

The study is published in the journal Horticulture Research.
