#952. '90s art scene in the UK: An international, edgy trendsetter

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The 1990s are remembered for Brit-pop and provocative fashion, but beyond the nostalgia, it was a decade of cultural experimentation and social change. Tate Britain's new exhibition looks past the familiar icons.

The 1990s in Britain are often remembered through a haze of nostalgia consisting of rave culture, Britpop and provocative art and fashion by the likes of Tracey Emin and Alexander McQueen.

Undeniably, the decade was a time of major change in British society — the long reign of Margaret Thatcher had come to an end and the West entered a period of relative post-Cold War stability.

Meanwhile, British cultural icons like Oasis, the Spice Girls and Vivienne Westwood garnered international fame.

But Tate Britain's new exhibition "The 90s: Art and Fashion," which opens on October 8 and runs through February 14, 2027, urges viewers to look beyond the familiar names and images.

Even though now prominent artists such as Damien Hirst and Juergen Teller are among the show's 70 contributors, the exhibition also aims to highlight the creative input of those in communities which were left out of the mainstream discourse.

After all, says chief curator Edward Enninful in the exhibition's program, "the energy of the 90s was not concentrated in a single movement, but distributed across communities, disciplines and geographies that have not always been equally represented."

For Enninful, the exhibition is personal: The Ghanaian-born British fashion editor, stylist and creative director became the youngest-ever fashion director of i-D Magazine in the 90s at age 18, helping shape the diverse and experimental London fashion scene of the 1990s. He later became editor-in-chief of British Vogue in 2017. Enninful writes that he and his co-curators aimed to present a body of work which "reflects a broader landscape of practices and voices that shaped the period, many of which operated beyond the structures of visibility at the time." The exhibition pays special attention to artists of color and women whose work was not necessarily showcased as prominently at the time.

Black masculinity became "a site of cultural negotiation" in Britain in the 90s, writes co-curator Helen Little in the program text: "Figures such as Chris Ofili, Steve McQueen, Keith Piper, Koto Bolofo and Ozwald Boateng reimagined the Black masculine, challenging the racist specter of Black men, who had been defined as part of a masculinity crisis linked to post-industrial decline and excluded from the narrative of 'Cool Britannia.'"

Just like "Swinging London" in the 1960s, "Cool Britannia" became a widespread phrase to refer to the 1990s as an era during which British youth culture, pop music, art and political optimism boomed.

The 90s also offered artists plenty of chances to create without waiting for support from traditional gatekeepers. Britain entered the decade in recession, but that economic uncertainty also left artists free to do what they wished.

Empty buildings and industrial spaces were turned into informal venues. A 23-year-old Damien Hirst orchestrated "Freeze," a self-organized student exhibition in 1988 in a disused London warehouse. It became a landmark early art exhibition for the generation of artists known as the "Young British Artists" or YBA.

Artists Sarah Lucas and Tracey Emin opened The Shop, a temporary art gallery and social space in 1992, as a place where exhibitions could develop outside established galleries. Lucas and Emin also paved the way for women artists thanks to their deeply personal works and their edgy expressions of female liberation. While the Spice Girls helped make feminism accessible to a younger generation of Brits in the 90s by popularizing the "girl power" slogan and shaping "identity through agency power and resistance," as Tate curator Helen Little argues, Emin and Lucas meanwhile "presented a new visual language of clothing as art to tackle societal taboos around female sexuality, vulnerability and desire" — from t-shirts with provocative slogans to a grunge look of scuffed Dr. Martens boots with razor blades. Their work "held a mirror up to gender, behavior and identity," Little writes.

At the same time, the British fashion industry came into the international spotlight.

In a feminine sensuality counter to the gender stereotypes dismissed by Emin and Lucas, waif-like Kate Moss came to represent the feminine ideal of the 1990s. Dubbed "heroin-chic," the style was an antithesis to the glamorous, athletic physiques of the supermodels who preceded her. Corinne Day's photographs of the model led to the raw aesthetic to be labelled "grunge" after the anguished style of rock music hailing from Seattle and led by Nirvana.

The trend has undergone significant cultural reexamination since then: "Along with the look also came criticism that the strung-out look of drug or alcohol addiction was glamorized while encouraging serious eating disorders in a generation of young women," points out Alex Farquharson, director of the Tate Gallery in his foreword to the exhibition.

The 1990s were also an exciting era for Britain in haute couture, as legendary designers John Galliano led Dior, while Alexander McQueen started directing Givenchy at the age of 27.

McQueen's runway presentations were more akin to performance art than commercial fashion shows — and they often included historical critiques. Among them was the famous finale of his Fall/Winter 1998 collection at a London warehouse, during which the model stood in a circle of flames. Titled "Joan," the show was a reference to the killing of Joan of Arc. McQueen designed several other runway shows that reflected on British history, with topics of conquest and punishment repeatedly coming to the forefront. His 1995 presentation "Highland Rape" referenced the history of Scotland's subjugation, with the designer describing it as "England's rape of Scotland."

As co-curator Jessica Vaughan points out in her exhibition text: "Art, fashion and photography were not simply transgressive reactions … but reflected a genuine public unease with Britain's global identity and its past." In a world that can now feel increasingly mediated by screens, algorithms and established platforms, the artwork of the 90s offers a reminder of the value of physical spaces and fresh DIY creativity of the last smartphone-free decade.

#929. Art for freedom: Maria Kalesnikava in Bonn

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Belarusian musician and civil rights activist Maria Kalesnikava held the keynote speech on the importance of art at the 2026 Beethovenfest in Bonn. She also visited DW.

Maria Kalesnikava knows all too well how authoritarian regimes repress art: The Belarusian flautist and activist spent five years in detention as a political prisoner, three of which were in solitary confinement. Speaking at this year's Beethovenfest, she said: "A person's external freedom can be taken away, sometimes almost entirely. Their freedom of movement, contacts with other people, and access to information can be limited." She pointed out that it is, however, much harder to control what has already become part of one's memory, imagination and inner experience. "And it is precisely in this that I find the explanation for why culture is so important for a free society and why authoritarian systems so often view it with distrust" she concluded during the keynote speech.

A key figure of the Belarusian opposition, the artist and political activist was arrested by the regime of Alexander Lukashenko in the summer of 2020, after the regime had violently suppressed the peaceful revolution for democracy and freedom. Across the country, mass protests and a democratic civil rights movement took place after the controversial presidential election on August 9, 2020.

Kalesnikava recalled how, during her imprisonment, she had carried her world and her experiences, with her all along. This enabled her to remain free, and not to feel like a victim. To Kalesnikava, this is precisely the power of art and also the reason why all dictators — whether Stalin, Hitler or Putin and Lukashenko — hate independent arts and culture, even if it's not overtly political.

During a panel discussion in Bonn, Kalesnikava stressed this point directly:

"The question often arises: what are we defending in Ukraine? Why do we need so much money and new weapons? For me, it is completely clear: the war Russia started is not just against Ukraine. It's a war against European values and European culture. It is a war against our civilization." Kalesnikava realized this immediately when she heard about the Russian war against Ukraine in February 2022, while she was imprisoned.

Kalesnikava was released and has been living in freedom again for ten months now — a miracle that she didn't think was possible and an achievement of international diplomacy. The fact that she emerged and returned to her life full of resilience is thanks in part to Beethoven and Bach, as Kalesnikava, who studied the flute and earned a degree in early and new music in Minsk and Stuttgart, recalled. "I spent a lot of time in complete silence. But this silence was almost never empty, because music constantly played in my head," Kalesnikava said in Bonn. "We musicians know this state very well — sometimes a melody or a musical phrase gets so stuck in your head that it's simply impossible to get rid of it. In normal life, this can even be annoying. In prison, I realized for the first time that this could bring great happiness."

During her visit to Bonn, the Belarusian activist also visited DW and appeared as a guest on the DW Russian language show "DW Novosti." Kalesnikava currently lives in Berlin following an invitation by the German government, where she is also recovering from the horrors she experienced. She sees it as her responsibility to remind the world about what is happening to more than 800 political prisoners in Belarus, as well as the importance and fragility of free artistic expression. On October 2, 2026, she held her keynote speech at the Beethovenfest in Bonn as part of a conference titled "Cultural Events as Political Spaces?," organized with the support of the German Federal Cultural Foundation.

More than 200 representatives from concert institutions and music festivals from across Germany came to the newly renovated Beethovenhalle in Bonn for the final weekend of this year's Beethovenfest. Together, they debated innovative forms and formats for music, as well as issues that stretch far beyond the music industry and urge on to think outside the box.

"Arts and culture are not responsible for taking on specific opinions from political parties, or for making explicitly political statements," Steven Walter, artistic director of the Beethovenfest and host of the conference, told DW. "What art always creates, however, is spaces for encounter, and places where people can tolerate ambivalence. And that is such an important part of our society, that we're able to handle making collective experiences." To Walter, this is precisely where arts and culture come in, as artists create spaces for independent thought, subjective interpretations, as well as doubts. "Perhaps it's true that there is something like a DNA of freedom in the nature of art," Walter explained in an interview with DW.

The festive closing of the 2026 Beethovenfest took place on 3 October, which is also German Unity Day. The BBC Philharmonic Orchestra, under the baton of conductor Anja Bihlmaier, performed Johannes Brahms' "A German Requiem," an iconic piece that evokes reconciliation and trust.

After four weeks of Beethovenfest 2026, the festival recorded the highest number of visitors in a decade. Around 35,000 people came to experience, celebrate and discuss art together at more than 100 concerts and events.

#919. UK students call for school uniforms to be banned in pornography

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Short skirts, knee-high socks, white blouses: Girls' school uniform items are widely used as costumes in pornographic videos. As students experience harassment, a group wants to change that.

Aylin and Mae, both age 15, are members of their school's Little Feminist Book Club in Bicester, Oxfordshire, England.

During a group discussion, the club learned that teenagers at other schools were trying to have the sale of school uniforms banned in sex shops.

Schoolgirl costumes are also widely used in pornographic videos.

"All of us unanimously agreed. We wanted to take it a step further; we wanted to ban school uniform from all pornography," Aylin told DW. As a result, they started a petition to raise awareness of the issue.

Many of their fellow students, as the petition states, have already had unpleasant experiences while wearing their uniforms. Some reported being harassed as early as the age of 11. Many of these experiences had left them feeling frightened, ashamed and insecure. "I don't wear my school uniform outside of school," said Mae. "I don't like wearing it out."

In their petition, the girls also refer to a 2018 report by the charity Plan International. According to a survey in the report, 35% of the 1,004 respondents aged 14 to 21 had experienced unwanted sexual attention while wearing a school uniform. Eight percent said they had been filmed or photographed by a stranger without their permission, or that someone had taken a photo under their school skirt, known as "upskirting."

"Something that should actually be innocent, something that is supposed to symbolize a community, has now become discredited in society," said Mae. "And our perception of what a school uniform represents has been distorted by this disgusting media."

School uniforms are common in many countries around the world. They are meant to make class and financial divides less visible, which is also supposed to prevent bullying while strengthening a community feeling.

In the United Kingdom, uniforms are mandatory at almost all schools and preschools, which means essentially all children between the ages of 4 to 16 wear them.

Outside school, children have no way to escape unwanted attention.

"We just want to prevent people from thinking about children and school uniforms in that way," said Mae. "Hopefully, we'll be able to actually turn the ban into legislation."

Until that happens, they still have a long way to go, but the girls are optimistic. As of October 5, 2026, the petition has garnered more than 24,000 signatures. Thanks to an article in the English newspaper The Times, it's also gotten plenty of attention.

Now, Mae and Aylin hope to get parliament to debate the issue. Two Members of Parliament have already voiced their support.

But the girls also know some people believe longer skirts are the solution. "I believe that's wrong," explains Aylin. "We shouldn't blame the victims. We should blame those who think it is acceptable to sexualize them."

#751. The Bundestag has debated the genocide Germany carried out in Namibia a century ago. But will it spur progress on addressing colonial crimes?

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Opposition parties have forced a debate in the Bundestag on the early-20th-century genocide Germany carried out in Namibia. Will this finally spur progress in addressing colonial crimes?

Gaob Daniel Timotheus Frederick was sitting in the front row of the visitors' gallery in the Bundestag this week. Wearing a suit and hat, he had traveled all the way from Namibia to see this parliamentary debate unfold. For almost 120 years, his family had been waiting for the return of human remains of their ancestor, Cornelius Frederick (sometimes spelled Fredericks), who had led the Nama resistance against German colonial rule and died on Shark Island in Germany's first concentration camp.

In the name of his great-grandfather and as a member of the Nama Traditional Leaders Association (NTLA), Frederick was there to demand an "unconditioned apology" from Germany.

Sitting next to him, in uniform and white gloves, with a carved lion on the handle of his walking stick, was the head of the Ovaherero Traditional Authority, Paramount Chief Mutjinde Katjiua. Speaking before the Bundestag debate, the chief said: "We call upon the Bundestag to recognize unequivocally the genocides committed against the Ovaherero and Nama peoples."

Many people wonder why the German parliament hasn't already done this — and why the government has not yet issued an official apology. There is a draft joint declaration with Namibia in which the German government acknowledges that "from today's perspective, [it] would be called genocide." But there is no German-Namibian reconciliation agreement. The evidence is overwhelming: From 1904 to 1908, the German Empire waged a war of extermination against the Indigenous people of southwestern Africa, including the Ovaherero (also known as the Herero), the Nama, the Damara and the San. At least 100,000 people were killed, and just as many displaced. Many were subjected to forced labor and forced prostitution. The land seizures and expropriation carried out at the time continue to affect their descendants today.

Jürgen Zimmerer, a professor of history at the University of Hamburg whose research focuses on Africa, told DW that the Bundestag and government lack the will to confront this era in German history. "There are Bundestag resolutions on the Armenian genocide, one on the Holodomor [in Ukraine], and one on the genocide against the Yazidis [by the "Islamic State" in Iraq]. But there is no recognition of the genocide against the Herero and Nama by the German Bundestag." If there were any political will to do so, Zimmerer said, it could happen very quickly.

The socialist Left Party initiated the Bundestag debate by drafting a resolution calling for full recognition and compensation in collaboration with representatives of the descendants. The goal: "restorative justice." Paramount Chief Katjiua has rejected the existing agreement between the Namibian and German governments. "Development aid cannot substitute for comprehensive reparations," he said.

Since 2021, the governments have been negotiating a joint declaration that pledges €1.1 billion ($1.24 billion) in reconstruction aid to Namibia over 30 years. Once the agreement is signed, Germany's president is set to issue a formal apology.

But the Foreign Ministry warned: "Legal claims to compensation cannot be derived from the recognition of genocide. With these negotiations — which are unique worldwide — Namibia and Germany have broken new ground and are pioneers in confronting their colonial past."

Political scientist Andreas Mehler, the director of the Anton Bergstraesser Institute at the University of Freiburg, said the government's plan sounded like "checkbook diplomacy."

Zimmerer also said there was a financial calculation: "If we were to recognize an obligation to provide reparations to the families of those killed — given the millions of victims of World War II, the victims of the Germans — it would ruin us."

Four lines in the 144 pages of the coalition agreement of the current German government — between the conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU)/Christian Social Union (CSU) and the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD) — summarize the extent of the government's commitment to reconciliation with Namibia.

"We will intensify our efforts to come to terms with colonialism," the text reads. "This includes transnational research into artifacts and the restitution of cultural property in dialogue with the countries of origin. Particular attention will be paid to establishing a dignified memorial site and the repatriation of human remains."

Compared with the efforts to address colonialism led by the SPD-led government from 2021 to 2025, "the issue has lost visibility," said Alexander Stroh-Steckelberg, a professor of political scientist at the University of Bayreuth who specializes in Africa.

Zimmerer has an even more critical take: "I wouldn't even call it a stalemate anymore. I believe this government is rolling back the progress that has been made in terms of raising awareness of colonial injustices."

Much remains to be done, as far the descendants of the Ovaherero are concerned: The remains of tens of thousands of people are believed to still be in Germany. Gaob D. Timotheus Frederick and other descendants of the victims have been fighting to heal these wounds for generations.

In March, Germany established a coordination council, headed by the Foreign Ministry, which stated that the government did not wish to "dictate to the countries and societies of origin which cultural artifacts and human remains they should reclaim, what priorities they should set or how restitutions should be handled."

Serap Güler, a state minister in the Foreign Ministry and the chair of the coordination council, defends the government's actions so far. Recounting her own official trips — she has already been to Togo, and next week she plans to travel to Rwanda and Burundi — she said: "Everywhere I go, I receive recognition for our sincere and trusting approach to the past — as well as for our determined commitment to a future of freedom, security and prosperity."

Zimmerer said Germany "has earned a reputation as a country that takes a critical view of its own history and learns from its mistakes" — which gives it "enormous cultural capital" on the world stage. But he is concerned that this capital is currently being squandered.

The government's memorial policy does not appear to address colonialism. Zimmerer even detects an attempt to "marginalize German colonial crimes" in the recent proposal by Minister of State for Culture Wolfram Weimer to create a dignified memorial site in Bremen. He notes that victim groups have not been included in Weimer's proposal and said the colonial capitals of the German Empire — Berlin and Hamburg — must also become sites of remembrance.

Ninety-five percent of the colonial troops set sail from Hamburg's Baakenhafen, including Commander Lothar von Trotha, who issued the orders to exterminate the Ovaherero and Nama. According to Zimmerer, the fact that a new opera house — rather than a memorial site — is being built at Baakenhafen constitutes a staggering case of "art-washing of history." Mehler also hopes that a productive debate on memorial sites will still take place and that, overall, "many more places will come to terms with colonial history."

Meanwhile, Gaob D. Timotheus Frederick continues to call for a collective effort to come to terms with the past: "Let us give opportunity to the affected communities that are being represented by us to have a seat on the table to discuss with them the rest of the way."

#495. Albania's student protests are only getting bigger

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

As police try to suppress anti-corruption protests by young Albanians, Prime Minister Edi Rama is claiming that "antifa" is behind the unrest. Few are buying that explanation.

The fallout from the October 13 protests was still visible when students and faculty from the University of Tirana gathered outside the Education Ministry in Albania's capital on Tuesday to express their continued discontent. Smashed windows have yet to be replaced; the walls are still black with soot from fires that broke out during the protests.

Albania has seen nearly five months of peaceful anti-government protests, sparked initially when plans were made public that Jared Kushner — a son-in-law of US President Donald Trump — and associates intended to build a luxury hotel complex within a nature reserve.

Since then, anti-government and anti-corruption protests have escalated. Now, college and high school students clash with police forces almost daily, with the fiercest clashes so far occurring last week.

Protesters denounce corruption, mismanaged universities and the rising cost of education in Albania — and call for the resignation of Prime Minister Edi Rama, who has been in office since 2013.

Wearing a black hooded jacket and a scarf across her face to conceal her identity, a woman who asked to be called Sara because she fears repercussions for speaking to the press told DW that protesters "communicate via text messages — that's the only way."

She cut the interview short as some protesters began flinging stones at the Education Ministry. Police pushed demonstrators and journalists out of the way, using water cannons to disperse the crowd.

"Social media is the most important public space today — more so than physical spaces," Besmir Semanaj, a prominent Albanian cybersecurity advocate told DW. "Young Albanians no longer organize through traditional structures such as political parties, associations or public appeals. Instead, they organize themselves where they spend much of their daily lives: on their cellphones."

"The most important communication channels include Instagram, WhatsApp groups for academic-year cohorts, departments and dormitories, Telegram channels for general announcements, and TikTok for sharing videos," Semanaj said. He added that people who are especially concerned with security use the Signal messaging app.

"This is a horizontal organizational structure, without individual 'leaders' and without a central command structure," Semanaj said. "That is exactly why the government is having such a hard time understanding and controlling the situation."

Semanaj said the Telegram messenger app had proven particularly useful for protesters. "Telegram channels allow you to reach tens of thousands of people at once," he said. "Notifications are sent out within seconds, and external control is more difficult to establish than on traditional platforms."

Rama has called the youth uprising a "hybrid war." He said protesters thought they could "make Albania a better country through TikTok and without even being able to string together more than seven coherent words." Though not all are terrorists, he said, he believes that "they are hostages to a certain mindset, a new ideology that has only one goal: to destroy, to turn everything upside down, to raze everything to the ground."

Over nearly five months, Rama has accused various "true" masterminds of organizing the anti-government and anti-corruption protests. He has alleged that the governments of Greece, Russia and Iran are stirring up dissent.

Most recently, Rama said it was "antifa" — or a decentralized movement of global antifascists — that had stoked anti-government and anti-corruption sentiments in Albania. "These are young people who have been radicalized and ideologically indoctrinated and who are acting under the pressure or influence of a new, dark force that has emerged in Europe and the United States, a force that the President of the United States has designated as a terrorist organization: Antifa," Rama claimed.

Redion Qirjazi, a security affairs researcher who focuses on violence prevention and conflict resolution, told DW that Rama's claim was not likely to prove true. "That is a serious accusation — one that requires equally compelling evidence," Qirjazi said. "To date, no verifiable evidence has been presented to prove the existence of an organized Antifa structure in Albania, let alone its involvement in coordinating the protests."

Qirjazi said Rama's reference to Trump's calling antifa a terror group was problematic. "Antifa is not a unified international organization with a centralized structure or leadership," he said, "but rather an umbrella term for various movements and groups united by the fact that they are, at their core, anti-fascist."

Scientists estimate that around 60% of the Baltic coast's seagrass meadows have disappeared.

"That's the bad news," said marine ecologist Thorsten Reusch from the GEOMAR Helmholtz Center for Ocean Research Kiel . "The good news is that the decline has stopped."

Underwater seagrass meadows feed and shelter fish, crabs, shellfish and many other species. They improve water quality and slow waves, helping protect shores from erosion. And they store carbon in the seabed about 30 to 50 times faster than forests.

Around 70 species of seagrass can be found along the coasts of more than 150 countries. Some grow just below the surface, others at depths of 60 meters or almost 200 feet.

"Some countries have far more seagrass, particularly in the Indo-Pacific," said Reusch. "There, it can play a major role in their carbon balance."

But the value of seagrass goes beyond carbon storage. Its meadows in the Baltic are among the sea's most valuable habitats. They shelter wildlife, stabilize the seabed and act as natural filters by trapping particles and nutrients.

Planting individual seagrass shoots is painstaking work so people like Reinhard Helbig are trying to make it easier.

Through ZosteraTec, the start-up he co-founded, he helps restore marine habitats, including seagrass meadows damaged by offshore activity, such as wind farm construction. His work begins on the beach.

"Stormy weather helps us. Waves and currents wash seagrass out of the seabed. A day or two later, it washes up here, still fresh," he said.

The collected shoots are grown and multiplied in ZosteraTec's aquaculture. Several months later, the marine saplings are laid on a plant support. A specially developed machine binds the planted strands with biodegradable thread. The resulting lengths can be laid on the seabed with less diving work.

"We plant here on land to cut down on underwater work. That means fewer dives, and we can do the painstaking work on shore," Helbig said.

Researchers at the Geomar Helmholtz Center in Kiel are also testing new ways to restore seagrass across larger areas, including using seeds.

Marine ecologist Esther Thomsen reaches into an aquarium tank and pulls out a flowerpot containing seagrass.

"Ten percent of the plants in our meadows produce seeds. You can clearly see the seeds sitting inside the seed pod," she said.

"The big advantage of restoring meadows from seed is greater genetic diversity. And in the future, we should be able to sow seeds over much larger areas," explained Thomsen.

Reusch from Geomar said they currently "think the most promising way to grow meadows that can withstand climate change is to plant individual shoots as a foundation, then sow seeds between them to increase genetic diversity."

Whether a planting mission is successful is revealed months or even years later.

Thomsen and her colleagues are visiting the first seagrass meadow planted in Germany's Baltic waters in 2021. The divers count shoots, check how densely they grow and record how the meadow has developed.

They then compare it with the donor meadow that supplied the plants. Only that comparison reveals whether the new crop has merely survived or is beginning to function more like a natural one.

"We've just checked the seagrass meadow, and it's really dense. We counted lots of shoots per square," Thomsen said. "It's looking really good."

But Dr Corns warned the High Court of Australia was not simply another court where those convicted could "have a second go" at appealing.

"There are very strict criteria that the High Court applies to determine which cases they will hear or not," he said.

#677. The thunderstorm that arrives like clockwork at 3pm every day

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The northern wet season has officially commenced, coinciding with the annual arrival of the Top End's largest resident.

Looking north from Darwin, a marvel of meteorology, one of the largest storm clouds on the planet towers over the Tiwi Islands from about 3pm every day.

It is the world's only individually named thunderstorm, with locals calling it Hector.

World War II pilots labelled Hector, using its dependable location as a navigation beacon when travelling between Darwin and Papua New Guinea.

But you do not need a plane to spot Hector. The thundercloud is clearly visible from Darwin, and that is because he is no ordinary storm.

Hector in all his glory during a past Top End wet season. Note the flat top which is caused by Hector breaching the top of the troposphere. (Supplied: Paul Thomsen, Weather Obsessed Facebook page)

While the storm got the name Hector 80-odd years ago, the Indigenous communities of the Tiwi Islands were well aware of its occurrence with the traditional calendar tracking the changes in the land and sky across three major and 13 minor seasons.

The two major seasons which overlap with Hector are Tiyari and Jamutakari, which run from September to February.

During Jamutakari, pakitiringa (rain) falls consistently every day and the swamps, creeks and rivers are full. There is much pumurali (lightning) and thunder with the rain.

One of the minor seasons also references the arrival of a daily storm. Pumwanyingari is the season of thunder when humidity increases and clouds develop every afternoon.

Hector is a colossus, rising vertically up to 20 kilometres in altitude.

Hector is so tall he soars through the troposphere, the layer of the atmosphere where weather occurs, and punches into the lower stratosphere, a hostile environment that flattens his head.

Containing millions of tonnes of water, Hector is also known as Hector the Convector because he transfers massive amounts of heat and moisture from the warm tropical surface high into the atmosphere.

He can easily drop 50 millimetres of rain in a couple of hours and contributes to the Tiwi Islands being the wettest part of the Northern Territory with up to 2,000mm annually.

Forecasting the exact location and timing of an individual thunderstorm before it forms is one of meteorology's greatest challenges; just ask Doc Brown from Back to the Future.

In fact, it is nearly impossible because the three key ingredients for a storm rarely occur simultaneously in the same place each day.

So how does Hector overcome weather's chaotic, inherently unpredictable nature?

Hector on October 23 this year viewed from Darwin, as he awakens for the season. (ABC)

Hector's existence results from the unique geography of the Tiwi Islands.

The first ingredient for a storm is moisture, and since the Tiwi Islands are surrounded by warm tropical seas, water vapour is abundant.

Ingredient two is instability, which utilises the property of physics that warm air rises.

The largest Tiwi is Melville Island, the second-largest in Australia after Tasmania, measuring roughly 50 by 100km.

What is critical is that Melville Island is small enough that the centre is still inundated by moist maritime air, but it is large enough that the middle sees peak daytime temperatures up to 5 degrees Celsius warmer than the coastline.

The result is sufficient heat and humidity at the surface to ensure a parcel of air moving vertically upwards will remain warmer than the surrounding air and therefore keep on rising to form a towering cumulonimbus storm cloud.

The hotter interior of the Tiwi Islands draws in a daily sea breeze, completing the Goldilocks geography for Hector's existence. (ABC News)

The third and final ingredient, and often the missing link that prevents reliable storms elsewhere, is a trigger.

The trigger is the mechanism required to launch air from the surface and overcome gravity, and heat alone will not do the job, otherwise the entire layer of surface air over the sunlit side of the planet would simultaneously rise.

What you need is another force to initiate uplift, which could be a mountain range, a cold front, or perhaps converging winds.

Again, the Tiwi Islands has Goldilocks geography. Since the centre is warmer, and warm air is light, a sea breeze develops each day that carries in cooler, denser air from the ocean.

But unlike a mainland sea breeze which just blows in one direction from the ocean to the land, an island's sea breeze draws in air from all directions.

Eventually, all that inward-blowing wind meets in the middle where it is then forced to rise vertically (since it cannot blow into the ground), a process also aided by a slight increase in elevation towards Melville's centre.

Hector forms when converging sea breezes meet in the middle of the Tiwi Islands and force the development of a thundercloud. (ABC News)

And thanks to a lack of variation in the day-to-day temperature near the equator, this convergence occurs at the same time every afternoon during wet season.

The now rapidly upward-flowing column of hot, humid tropical air then cools and condenses, giving birth to a cumulus congestus cloud, which eventually rises high enough for ice particles to create the friction required for lightning.

And thus, Hector the Convector, now a mammoth cumulonimbus cloud, is born.

Meteorological advances mean living in the Top End is no longer a requirement for appreciating Hector's daily eruption in the wet season.

Hector can be viewed remotely using observational instruments like radars and satellites, and modern modelling can predict the localised temperature, wind and moisture conditions that will ensure his formation.

For example, the satellite loop above shows small cumulus clouds converging towards the centre of the islands as the sea breeze moves inland, and the resulting inland clash of airstreams which triggers a larger storm cloud.

High resolution modelling also picks up on the daily temperature contrast and wind fields which contribute to Hector's development.

During the next six months, as long as monsoon winds or cyclones are not blowing, Hector the Convector will spawn on most days.

That is before May arrives and the dry season south-easterly winds off the mainland send Hector back into a deep hibernation for the austral winter.

#755. Controversial goldmine subject of new Aboriginal protection application

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Belubula River originates at the mine site. (ABC News: Xanthe Gregory)

A group of Orange Aboriginal Land Council members is seeking emergency protection of a river it says is sacred and under immediate threat from the McPhillamys Gold Project.

The Federal Court recently overturned another Aboriginal protection order declared by former environment minister Tanya Plibersek on the same site.

The federal environment department will now assess the application.

A faction of the Orange Local Aboriginal Land Council (OLALC) has made a fresh bid to save a "sacred" river system, a move that could halt a billion-dollar New South Wales gold project.

It comes just weeks after the Federal Court threw out a protection order over the same area.

"Concerned members" of the council have submitted a section 9 application, seeking emergency protection for a section of the McPhillamys Gold Project near Blayney, in the state's Central West.

The application, submitted independently of the OLALC, describes the area as being under "imminent threat of desecration".

The concerns of a dozen members were raised in an email seen by the ABC that was sent to the federal Environment Minister Murray Watt this week.

"We respectfully urge you to consider this application as a matter of urgency and take all available lawful steps to protect these Sacred Waters from irreversible harm," the application said.

"The Bila Bula [Belubula] is far more than a River."

Regis Resources argued the Commonwealth's decision rendered its goldmine unviable in its current form. (ABC News: Lani Oataway)

On October 14, Regis Resources successfully challenged the former federal environment minister Tanya Plibersek's decision in August 2024 to grant an Aboriginal protection order over the site in question.

The project had been put on hold and the company said the minister's declaration meant the open-cut operation was "not viable".

The original application was made by Wiradyuri elder Aunty Nyree Reynolds and supported by the Wiradyuri Traditional Owners Central West Aboriginal Corporation (WTOCWAC).

The declaration was made under Section 10 of the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Heritage Protection Act to protect the headwaters and the springs of the Belubula River, where the tailings dam that holds mining waste was to be built.

But a fortnight ago the court ruled Ms Plibersek's decision was "invalid" due to procedural errors with how additional information about the area's cultural significance was assessed.

The Belubula River starts between Bathurst and Orange, stretching through farmland into the mouth of the Lachlan River.  (ABC News: Xanthe Gregory)

In considering the fresh application, the environment minister must now decide if he is satisfied that the site is a significant Aboriginal area that is under serious and immediate threat of injury or desecration.

The Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water (DCCEEW) has confirmed the application is being assessed, and no decision has been made.

OLALC CEO Annette Steele said she expected the department would ask OLAC to respond to the application submitted privately by a group of its members.

"Our response will be guided by the senior Wiradjuri elders who hold cultural authority for the area in question," Ms Steele said.

She said that all Aboriginal people within the OLALC's boundaries were eligible to be members, whether or not they were traditional owners.

"For this reason, cultural heritage matters are typically decided by those people with cultural authority, not the membership as a whole," she said.

"However, LACLs are democratic organisations and members are free to raise any concerns that they may have at meetings or to the board."

The new application highlights disputes playing out between the region's Aboriginal groups, including disagreements over who holds positions of cultural authority and who has the right to speak for country.

In 2019, before the mine was initially approved, OLALC made a submission in response to the McPhillamys Gold Project environmental impact statement.

It objected to the State Significant Development on the basis that "the proposed project will detrimentally and irreversibly impact the Aboriginal cultural heritage significance of the study area".

The Orange Local Aboriginal Land Council holds a "neutral" position on the mine.  (ABC Central West: Xanthe Gregory)

In 2023 OLALC changed its position on the mine from "object" to "neutral".

It has publicly questioned the accuracy and veracity of claims made about the area, including a Dreaming story.

The new section 9 application raises concerns that "Wiradjuri and other First Nations people with cultural connections and responsibilities to this country, were not given a meaningful opportunity to have our say".

"We were not consulted or asked for our views when the change in stance by the executive of the Orange Aboriginal Land Council was announced previously," it said.

In her statement of reasons for granting the protection order in 2024, Ms Plibersek found that development of the mine's tailings dam would cause "irreversible" and "permanent" loss to Aboriginal heritage.

She found the Belubula headwaters were sacred to the Wiradjuri people and their Blue Banded Bee Dreaming Tradition, which narrates how the springs were formed.

The site of the proposed McPhillamys goldmine near Blayney. (ABC Central West: Tim Fookes)

In his federal court judgement delivered in October, Justice James Stellios referenced a brief from DCCEEW that said the declaration was contrary to submissions and advice from OLALC.

The department concluded that "a consensus view amongst all Wiradjuri people is not necessary".

But it was the timing of the revelation about the Dreaming story that would ultimately unravel the legality of Ms Plibersek's declaration.

Justice Stellios's judgement said his decision to overturn the Commonwealth's section 10 was not "about the merits of the claim … whether it's a significant area or under threat of injury or desecration".

He found that "the decision-making process was fundamentally flawed because of the failure to treat the revelation of the Blue Banded Bee Dreaming story as a new application".

During the court case, Regis Resources argued that the Dreaming story did not exist and challenged why it was not raised in the initial protection order application.

In a statement provided to the ABC, Regis Resources said the project had been approved through both the NSW State Significant Development pathway and the federal Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act, "each of which considered Aboriginal cultural heritage matters".

The company said it "supports the protection of Aboriginal cultural heritage, through a planning process that provides certainty for all parties".

The ABC understands that a second section 9 application has also been lodged by a different group.

A federal judge has set a trial date for the man accused of trying to kill President Donald Trump at the White House Correspondents’ Association dinner earlier this year.

Jury selection for Cole Tomas Allen's trial is scheduled to start on Sept. 7, 2027, and the trial in Washington, D.C., is expected to last three to four weeks.

Prosecutors wanted the trial to begin in May 2027 but U.S. District Judge Trevor McFadden set the later date after one of Allen's attorneys requested more time to prepare.

Video showed Allen, of Torrance, California, allegedly rushing the media dinner with guns and knives and firing a shotgun at a Secret Service officer who tried to stop the April 25, 2026, attack at the Washington Hilton hotel. The officer was struck once in a bullet-resistant vest and fired five shots but didn’t hit anybody, authorities said. Allen was injured but not from gunfire.

White House officials, including Trump and first lady Melania, were rushed to safety in dramatic scenes inside the ballroom before the event, one of the most high-profile annual events in DC, was canceled.

“I was watching to see what was happening, probably should have gotten down even faster,” Trump said at a briefing shortly after the shooting. “Melania was very cognizant, I think, of what happened. I think she knew immediately what happened. She was saying, 'That's a bad noise.’”

“It’s a dangerous profession,” Trump said after being asked if he was concerned about his safety.

A new date was set at a different venue, and Trump returned to speak at the rescheduled dinner three months later.

Allen, a 31-year-old computer engineer from California, railed against Trump administration policies and referred to himself as a “Friendly Federal Assassin” in writings that he sent to family members before the attack, authorities said.

He faces an attempted assassination charge that carries a maximum possible sentence of life in prison. Allen also is charged with assaulting a federal officer with a deadly weapon and two additional firearms counts.

The assassination attempt at the correspondents’ dinner came after Trump was shot in another assassination attempt at a rally in Butler, Pennsylvania, in 2024. A bullet grazed his ear before the 20-year-old gunman was shot dead by security personnel.

The brave captain who fought off his co-pilot during the attempted hijacking of a passenger jet last month has revealed his injuries from the savage axe attack.

Smit Machchhar was awarded the UAE’s First Class Order of Zayed II on Wednesday for his bravery in helping to thwart what authorities believe was an attempted suicide attack targeting Israel’s Ben Gurion airport on September 30.

Flydubai Flight 1073 from Dubai to Tel Aviv plunged 16,000ft in 30 seconds before the attacker was overwhelmed and two other pilots who were on board were able to safely land in Saudi Arabia.

Capt Machchhar, still wearing a sling and with stitches in the back of his head, told UAE president Sheikh Mohammed during yesterday’s ceremony that it would still be another two months before he heals fully.

“I have an idea. When you heal, I want to have a session with you to train you how to fight,” the Emirati president told him, suggesting he would benefit from learning jiu-jitsu.

“It’s my biggest honour,” replied the captain.

The aircraft was in Saudi airspace when the co-pilot attacked Machchhar with a crash axe, typically kept in the cockpit for emergency use.

UAE public prosecutors have said the plan was inspired by the September 11, 2001, attacks on the United States.

Speaking from a hospital bed in Abu Dhabi earlier this month, Machchhar said that when the first blow landed, he thought something had gone “horribly wrong the aircraft structure”, before quickly realising he was being attacked.

“The next thought was I need to get help in here. My immediate action was to free myself from the seat belts and I tried to just block the blows as much as I could,” he said, pointing to his arm that was wounded as he defended himself.

“The blows were coming on the head. Immediately my hand went to the knob which opens the door and screaming for my colleagues, screaming his name. I believe that was the first time when I lost my consciousness.”

Machchhar was covered with blood and on the floor when he managed to open the cockpit door, a spokesperson for Israeli prime minister Benjamin Netanyahu said.

Yaniv Hayun, one of the men who incapacitated the attacker, described running to the cockpit as the plane nosedived.

“We pulled the assailant out, I pulled him out. After I pulled him out we took control of the plane, I pulled up the yoke (steering control of the plane), I straightened the plane out a bit,” he said.

Netanyahu earlier said that the co-pilot, whom he identified as an Omani national, had undergone radical Islamist indoctrination and was clearly suicidal, but that so far a motive for his actions had not been established.

Israel does not have official diplomatic relations with Oman, and Netanyahu has said pilots from such countries were not permitted to operate flights to Israel. It was not clear how the co-pilot came to be assigned to the flight to Tel Aviv.

Oct 29 - Venezuela's interim President Delcy Rodriguez said on Thursday the government has made progress identifying state assets to offer to the private sector and former owners, a move that could reverse nationalizations ordered by former President Hugo Chavez.

"I have instructed that non-essential assets held by the state be assessed to determine which can be offered to the private sector or returned to their former owners. This process is already well advanced," Rodriguez said in a televised address.

In April, Rodriguez appointed a government commission to audit and evaluate state-owned assets, including infrastructure in industries such as energy, power and mining. They have been working on asset classification since, according to people familiar with the matter.

She did not provide further details on the process. Under Chavez, the nationalization of key projects and industries left over a dozen international arbitration claims, which mostly remain unsettled by the government.

In her speech, Rodriguez said 44 energy contracts are being negotiated under the state's oil law approved in January. The government has stated that 61 oil agreements have been signed, without providing details.

"Similarly, important negotiations are under way in the mining sector, specifically regarding gold, aluminum, and iron," she added.

The president also said she has instructed law enforcement agencies not to arrest people without a warrant, and vowed to defend the right to "peaceful protest" in the country, which has a history of political repression and last week experienced its first nationwide opposition protest in nearly two years.

"The responsible exercise of political rights is guaranteed for everyone. Our doors are open," Rodriguez said.

In her address, Rodriguez said the government has planned four quarterly increases in the national minimum wage in 2027.

During a separate afternoon appearance on state television, Rodriguez said diplomat Oliver Blanco, previously the vice foreign minister, will take over as the country's top diplomat.

His predecessor, Felix Plasencia, will become presidential adviser for international affairs, Rodriguez said. REUTERS
