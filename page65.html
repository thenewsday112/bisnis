#1902. Beehiiv Brings Dynamically Inserted Podcast Ads To Its Publisher Platform

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Content discovery has moved away from the channels controlled by publishers and their audiences.

People just don’t go to websites like they used to, which has some media industry insiders declaring that the pageview era of digital publishing is dead. Meanwhile, search, social media and AI chatbots are beholden to the algorithmic whims of Big Tech.

Which leaves the email inbox as one of the last remaining bastions where users curate their own content preferences. Beehiiv wants to capitalize on the inbox’s direct connection to audiences as it builds an all-encompassing content and monetization platform, including services for newsletters, websites and podcasts.

To round out the podcast offering it introduced this Spring, beehiiv announced Tuesday it’s adding dynamic audio ad insertion to its programmatic platform.

Beehiiv’s platform serves the gamut of publishers, from single-operator newsletters and local publications run by relative unknowns to startups launched by nationally recognized reporters like Oliver Darcy’s Status. It also works with big media brands, including Newsweek and TIME.

Now, any of those publishers can open their podcast inventory to programmatic demand to complement their direct-sold host-read ads and more effectively monetize their back catalogs, Ainsley Rossitto, head of podcast at beehiiv, told AdExchanger.

The shift in content consumption has been a boon for newsletter-native platforms like beehiiv and its competitors Substack and Ghost. As some writers have recently noted, it feels like everyone has glommed onto the idea that audiences don’t want to engage with content unless it arrives in an email or gets served to them on social.

With that shift in mind, building a podcast offering has long been on beehiiv’s road map as a way to complement its newsletter, social networking and website development tools, Tyler Denk, the company’s co-founder and CEO, told AdExchanger.

The company’s decision to bring in Rossitto to head up its podcast business – she joined in May after stints managing podcast ad strategy for NPR and Paramount – shows how serious beehiiv is about this fast-growing part of its portfolio, Denk said.

Beehiiv currently has more than 13,000 publishers in its network and delivers about $1 million in monthly revenue to its clients.

More than 2,100 of these publishers rely on the platform to manage their podcast distribution across major platforms, including Spotify and Apple Podcasts. And the number of publishers using beehiiv for podcasting has grown about 20% each month since the company launched its podcast offering back in April, Rossitto said.

Beehiiv sees introducing dynamically inserted podcast ads as a way to coax more high-profile creators to its platform, Denk said. The lack of dynamic ad insertion has been cited by some big-name creators as a reason for why they’ve chosen other platforms in the past, he added.

“Most larger, serious podcasts are using some form of dynamic ad insertion to monetize, and we will not win their business to migrate their podcast over until we had a competitive feature set,” he said.

Although dynamically inserted programmatic ads tend to sell at lower prices than direct-sold ads, they offer publishers a way to continue to monetize their back catalogs and other inventory that gets left out of direct deals.

For programmatic podcast ads, publishers keep 50% to 60% of the revenue, with beehiiv taking the remaining cut. And there’s no impression or download minimum for publishers to participate in any of beehiiv’s programmatic ads. (For comparison, beehiiv takes about a 20% to 25% cut of programmatic display ads, Denk said, and it takes no revenue share on direct-sold ads.)

Beehiiv now gives publishers two ways to monetize their podcasts with ads, Denk said.

They can use beehiiv’s ads manager to manage their own direct-sold inventory. Or they can opt into programmatic monetization and have beehiiv and its audio ad serving partner, AdsWizz, fill the inventory for them. Either way, beehiiv offers analytics tools for tracking performance for both programmatic and direct-sold campaigns.

Publishers can place their own ad slots inside their podcasts at the episode level, or they can opt into allowing beehiiv to mass-apply placements across their catalog, Denk said. AdsWizz can serve ads into third-party podcasting platforms using the podcasts’ existing RSS feeds.

Beehiiv is not dictating the number of ad breaks publishers can insert per episode, Rossitto said. However, to keep the ad saturation in check, beehiiv is capping its podcast ad load to 10 different ads per break, she said. It’s also publishing some best practices and guidance for podcasters.

Publishers categorize their own programmatic inventory using the IAB’s standard content taxonomy, and also according to how they categorize their content on Apple Podcasts, Rossitto said.

They can also pick which categories of advertisers they want to see demand from, Denk said. And beehiiv offers a “prioritization waterfall” so that programmatic demand doesn’t take precedence over the publisher’s direct deals, he said.

Buyers can target ads contextually. But, in another evolution for beehiiv, Denk said, the company is expanding its ad targeting capabilities beyond its initial focus on contextual to also incorporate behavioral data, which is tied to the user’s email login. That way, for instance, advertisers can target people who are interested in technology while they’re also engaging with sports content.

In early use cases, beehiiv has seen publishers pursue a variety of podcast monetization strategies, Rossitto said.

For some, she said, programmatic has opened up an additional channel for them to earn revenue that they can set up quickly. For others, they’re incorporating podcast ads into larger direct-sold deals and charging higher CPMs for omnichannel sponsorship packages.

Speaking of omnichannel opportunities, the next move on beehiiv’s podcasting road map is to add distribution support for video podcasts, likely by the end of the year, Denk said.

It’s a tricky proposition, since emails do not support embedded video, whereas they can support audio formats, he noted. But beehiiv plans to manage video podcast distribution on YouTube, Spotify and other platforms, similar to how it currently handles audio distribution on third-party platforms, he said.

No matter how they reach their audiences, Denk feels strongly that publishers have to be omnichannel these days.

“The most successful content creators today are not single channel, but multichannel,” he said. “Rather than just being active on TikTok or having a podcast or having a newsletter, they usually have more than one of those. And that’s also why we’re launching community features and website digital products as well.”

It all goes back to helping publishers navigate a new online ecosystem in which they have to meet audiences where they are, Denk said, rather than pulling them into their preferred properties.

The deal is the latest sign that media buyers are moving closer to publishers to shorten supply paths and get at the audience data they need to train AI agents.

OptimizeRx, which has software that plugs into the systems doctors use to prescribe drugs and maintain patient records, has taken its next big step into the world of programmatic through a new partnership with The Trade Desk.

If AI agents can plan and buy media, who needs a holding company? Good question. It came up during Omnicom’s Q3 earnings on Thursday.

Netflix’s revenue grew 12% YOY to $12.9 billion in Q3, which it maintains is in line with its forecast. Its ads business, however, is lagging slightly compared with expectations, causing Netflix to tweak its forecast for the rest of the year.

Advertising supply chain intelligence company DecryptAds uncovered more than 2,100 AI-generated recipe sites that are monetized with programmatic ads and feature fabricated author profiles.

A century after Harry Houdini’s death, the magician’s name still draws visitors to a Los Angeles estate he never owned. The Houdini Estate, a terraced property in Laurel Canyon now used for events, originally belonged to developer Ralf M. Walker. Its connection to Houdini himself owes much to his wife and stage partner, Bess, who lived across the street after his death and entertained guests in Walker’s gardens.

The original mansion was destroyed by a fire in 1959, but surviving stone stairways, archways, and a carriage house became part of the property’s later restoration. With the centennial of Houdini’s death falling this Halloween, we’re taking a look back at the history of the Houdini Estate.

The estate began as a lavish home for Walker, who built a Mediterranean-style mansion in Laurel Canyon in 1915. Its 11 bedrooms and nine bathrooms were joined by a ballroom and a stage. Walker also owned a smaller guesthouse across the street, where Houdini and Bess are said to have briefly stayed while he was filming The Grim Game and Terror Island in 1919.

Bess lived in the guesthouse again with her manager Edward Saint after Houdini’s death in 1926. She also used Walker’s gardens across the street for entertaining, including a 1935 party for hundreds of visiting magicians and their spouses. According to Houdini historian Patrick Culliton, coverage of that gathering helped spread the “Houdini House” name to Walker’s mansion. Over time, the distinction between Bess’s residence and the larger estate blurred.

The name persisted long after Bess left. When the mansion burned in 1959, newspapers described it as the “old Houdini mansion,” reinforcing the association even though it had never belonged to him.

The original mansion and guesthouse were destroyed in the 1959 Laurel Canyon fire. The burned remains of the mansion stood until 1970, when they were demolished. A carriage house survived on the larger property, along with stone stairways, archways, and garden paths.

Owner José Luis Nazar later restored the grounds and developed the property as a venue for events and filming. His work followed decades in which the overgrown ruins had attracted visitors who believed they were exploring Houdini’s former home. The restoration features landscaped terraces, waterfalls, gates, and renewed use of the property’s natural spring.

The estate now has five acres spread across nine outdoor terraced levels, with trees, sculptures, a koi pond, and waterfalls. The terraces provide separate spaces for meals, ceremonies, and other gatherings, while paths and stairways connect the hillside gardens.

The current house has four bedrooms, according to the estate’s rental information, while the grounds include a swimming pool and indoor and outdoor spas. Large paintings of Houdini decorate the interiors. The property is privately owned and operated.

#452. Archaeologists Found a 3,200-Year-Old Warhorse—Buried as If It Were Still Charging Into Battle

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Egyptians valued their beasts of battle so much that they gave them ceremonial burials and posed them just right for the afterlife.

At a site now known as Tell Al-Abquain in Egypt’s Beheira Governorate, ruins of mud brick houses and other remnants from 3,200 years ago are scattered around an area that used to be an ancient fortress. In its day, it appears to have been a flourishing society. Along with weapons and barracks for the soldiers, archaeologists have uncovered what is left of grain silos, wells, and pottery ovens that also baked bread, a reminder of what daily life was like during the New Kingdom. Something unexpected had also been buried among these artifacts.

As archaeologists were excavating the fortress, they unearthed the intact skeleton of what appeared to be a warhorse. Whomever the young male horse had belonged to must have been a military official of high rank, as the horse had been buried with honor, still wearing its alabaster and limestone chariot harness. Even in death, the horse had been positioned as if it was galloping into battle, further indicating elaborate funeral rites at the time. Most likely used to pull war chariots similar to those interred with Tutankhamun, who ruled as pharaoh during the same time period, the horse is thought to have been bred for fighting. Preliminary studies led by Mohamed Abdel-Badie, head of the Egyptian Antiquities Sector at the Supreme Council of Antiquities, found it to be a medium breed whose strengths were the speed and agility needed for war.

“Among the most significant findings of the excavations was the complete animal burial of a young male horse,” the Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities said in a recent press release, adding that “This breed is likely used for training, military activities, or pulling chariots during the New Kingdom. The horse's complete body position, the direction of its head, and the regularity of the burial suggest a deliberate and organized burial, likely a ceremonial one.”

Who the horse belonged to is unknown. Thought to have crossed over into Egypt form the Levant with the Hyksos at the dawn of the New Kingdom, domestic horses were usually a luxury reserved for pulling chariots or carrying pharaohs. Most of what is known about the Egyptians’ relationship with horses is depicted on stelae, tomb murals, and temple wall hieroglyphics, but few actual remains of warhorses have been found. Most horses that appear in temple and tomb murals are decorated with military regalia and as they pull chariots carrying archers. These chariots were modified versions of heavier Levantine models. With the advantage of chariots that were lighter and faster than their predecessors, there are few existing images of riders, though some New Kingdom depictions that suggest riding survive.

Tell Al-Abquain arose to protect Egypt’s western border as a defense from the Libyans and Sea Peoples. This is the same fortress that revealed a bronze sword inscribed with the cartouche of legendary pharaoh Ramesses II. Faience scarabs and a bronze ring, engraved with the names of deities, along with necklaces strung with beads of faience and carnelian in the shape of pomegranate blossoms, may have also belonged to him. Horses often accompanied royals and became a symbol of royal power. In the retelling of the Battle of Kadesh, in which Ramesses II prevailed against the Hittites, the names of his horses are mentioned: “Victory-in-Thebes” was named for the capital city of Egypt then, and “Mut is content” revered the mother goddess Mut. This is just one example of how highly regarded horses were in Egypt.

Ramesses himself is said to have had 24,000 horses in his army. Other kings such as Thutmose III and Amenhotep II are pictured riding gloriously into battle, often compared to gods, and some, such as Amenhotep II and Tutankhamun were buried with their chariots. While they were associated with royalty, especially in royal propaganda that emphasized the king as a fierce warrior, horses were not exclusive. New Kingdom elites such as the royal family and high officials also owned horses and chariots. One relief shows the sons of Ramesses II riding, and horses in another transport Queen Nefertiti and the royal princesses of Amarna. Some officials were given titles such as “Master of Horses” or “Stable Master” to emphasize their rank. The burial of this warhorse is a reminder of how valued the animals were in Egyptain society.

Also discovered at the site were more artifacts to add to the trove that had been exposed in the last excavation—jewelry, amulets, looms, and pottery vessels in the image of everything from lotus flowers to fish and baboons. Some were covered in sculpted crocodile scales. The archaeological team even came upon kernels of unhusked grain, which had stayed intact for millennia in the shadows of crop warehouses. Fragments of limestone stelae also memorialized events and royal figures. Carved into one is a prisoner of war, possibly a Libyan, bound and bent in submission. The others glorify Ramses II with his multiple names and liken his son Merneptah to the formidable god Seth.

“This discovery adds a new dimension to understanding the system of military fortifications established by the ancient Egyptians to protect the country's borders,” stated Sherif Fathy, Minister of Tourism and Antiquities. “The residential structures, storage facilities, and diverse artifacts uncovered by the mission provide a comprehensive picture that extends beyond the military aspect of the fortress.”

#4429. When file infrastructure becomes the bottleneck

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Legacy NAS, VPNs, and file-sync workflows leave hidden costs in their wake. Is the way your organization manages files still fit for purpose?

Nobody sets out to build file infrastructure that makes employees less productive. Yet sensible technology decisions made years ago can leave organizations wrestling with systems that no longer suit how their teams work.

Take network-attached storage (NAS). It has long provided a dependable way to store and access files. But what happens when the people using those files are no longer sitting in the same building, or even the same country?

A project team can spend its morning waiting for a multi-gigabyte file to transfer over a VPN, then lose more time checking whether the copy they received is the latest. No single delay attracts much attention. Repeat that across projects and the cost starts to add up.

When shared access becomes difficult, employees find workarounds. They download local copies, duplicate folders, and transfer files between systems to keep projects moving.

Now imagine an architecture firm sharing a large building model between design offices. One team updates its copy while another works from yesterday’s export. Before anyone can move forward, someone has to reconcile the differences. That's time spent managing files rather than designing the building.

#470. Oct. 27, 1961: The first Saturn rocket launches

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Five months after President John F. Kennedy promised that America would put a man on the Moon, NASA successfully showed that they had a rocket to get them there. Under development with Wernher von Braun’s team since 1957, the Saturn I rocket consisted of a booster stage with a liquid oxygen tank at its core, surrounded by four tanks of kerosene and four additional tanks of liquid oxygen. It featured eight H-1 engines. Elements of the Saturn I were derived from the Redstone missile, the Jupiter rocket, and the Thor rocket and hardware was even reused, lowering costs.

After an array of testing, the Saturn program was transferred to the newly opened George C. Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC) in Huntsville, Alabama, and assembly began. In April 1961, the Saturn I boosters and engines were tested at the MSFC. Meanwhile, construction of Launch Complex 34 at the Cape Canaveral Air Force Station in Florida had been underway since June of 1960, and the facility was dedicated in June 1961. Components arrived at Cape Canaveral for stacking throughout the summer, eventually reaching 162 feet (50 meters) high and 460 tons. After further testing in September, the Saturn I was successfully launched on Oct. 27, 1961. The booster stage boasted 1.3 million pounds of thrust that lifted the rocket to an altitude of 85 miles (137 kilometers).

Saturn rockets would be launched a total of 32 times – including the Saturn V rockets that powered all the crewed Apollo flights – and per NASA, are “the only family of rockets to have had a 100 percent success rate.”

Will it move the needle on dating app fraud?

Last week, President Trump signed into law the Romance Scam Prevention Act that Congressman David Valadao (CA-22) introduced and that received bipartisan sponsorship. In short, this new federal law requires dating apps and services to notify users who have interacted with someone who was removed for fraudulent activity.

The scale of romance scams is enormous, with Americans losing $1.5 billion in 2025 as per the FTC and global losses coming in yearly at over $1 trillion. Dating apps play a significant role in the phenomenon because they present predators with the perfect pool in which to fish for victims. According to a year-long investigation whose results were released in recent weeks, U.S. Sen. Maggie Hassan concluded that Match Group has greatly lagged behind in its efforts to implement the best technological safeguards available. This is especially relevant because Match Group (which controls most of the best-known dating apps such as Tinder, Hinge, and OkCupid) continues to dominate the dating app market and hence its failures put many users at risk.

The new legislation may help users who are still in touch off-app with fraudsters who have been removed. That is a very narrow slice of users, however, and the system largely depends on whether dating apps effectively remove said fraudsters in the first place. The Romance Scam Prevention Act, however, places no obligations on apps to remove fraudsters or to prevent their joining apps in the first place. It is theoretically possible that the passage of even this fairly weak dating-app-related legislation, in conjunction with the issuance of Sen. Hassan's report, will put some pressure on apps and signal that stricter laws could follow if they don't improve their safety measures. As of now, however, the new law itself is unlikely to stop more than a minimal percentage of romance scams.

Last, it is worth mentioning that financial harm to dating app users, while very important, is far from the only damage resulting from dating apps' continued lackluster use of safeguards. For a crucial example, according to a Columbia Journalism Investigations survey, one third of women who use dating apps report having been sexually assaulted or raped by someone they met on an app. Neither the Romance Scam Prevention Act nor other congressional action so far addresses these many acts of violence or other related harms.
