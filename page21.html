Denver has been impacted by a drought brought on by record-low mountain snowpack and record-high temperatures

Officials in Denver, Colorado, have proposed a lawn patrol to enforce water restrictions amid worsening drought conditions.

Denver relies on mountain snowpack for its water supply — but record-low snowpack and record-high temperatures brought on by climate change caused the city’s water supply to dry up.

Local officials have placed strict restrictions on water usage because of the drought — and now they may enlist more than a dozen people to make sure residents are following the rules.

Denver’s Board of Water Commissioners has received a staff proposal creating a 15-person lawn patrol for next summer that would likely conduct “2,500-3,500 stops or violations per month,” according to the document reviewed by The Colorado Sun. The board would still have to vote to approve the proposal.

The goal is to “demonstrate that Denver Water is enforcing its rules,” the proposal reads.

Last March, the board declared a Stage 1 drought, seeking a 20 percent reduction in water use by the end of April 2027.

At the time, water reservoirs were 80 percent full, lower than the historic median for storage during that part of the year.

The board approved several water use rules for residents, including watering grass just two days per week, washing cars with a bucket or hand-held hose with an automatic shut-off nozzle and power washing homes only on assigned watering days.

Denver Water said that while many residents are conserving water, the city has yet to reach its 20 percent water savings goal.

At the start of the month, the city banned lawn watering altogether. Local officials warned that any violations will cost residents up to $2,000 in fines, and they may have their water shut off. The ban is in place indefinitely.

“From Oct. 1 through Oct. 18, under the post-October 1 violation structure, we issued 448 fines totaling $265,000,” a Denver Water spokesperson told The Colorado Sun.

The drought in Colorado is so bad that the old U.S. Highway 6 beneath the Dillon Reservoir has resurfaced.

“Believe it or not, I'm walking on a highway that's spent most of the last 60 years underwater,” AccuWeather Meteorologist Tony Laubach said while surveying the land.

Dillon Reservoir is currently just 58 percent full, according to the weather outlet.

#97. Hong Kong’s leader apologises for residential fire that killed 168 people

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Blaze was world’s deadliest residential fire since 1980, but some survivors and bereaved families say justice has yet to be served

Hong Kong’s leader has apologised for government shortcomings that contributed to a fire that killed 168 people in the city last year, a rare sign of public contrition that nevertheless failed to impress some victims’ families.

The chief executive, John Lee, and seven other officials bowed in apology at the release of a report that pointed to “decades of failure” by government bodies.

Four departments “should bear part of the responsibility” for the high casualties, the report said. So far one official, housing secretary Winnie Ho, has resigned.

Last November, a small fire probably started by a cigarette grew into an inferno that enveloped seven of the eight towers in Wang Fuk Court, a housing complex that was shrouded in bamboo scaffolding and netting for renovations.

Former residents and victims’ families have been waiting to see how the government will address who is to blame for the ignored warnings, substandard building materials and disabled fire alarms that made the fire the world’s deadliest in a residential building since 1980.

Authorities have filed charges, including manslaughter, against seven individuals and two companies over their roles in the fire. At least 35 people have been arrested.

The independent report released on Thursday found the “main culprits” of the fire were private contractors but also accused officials of “lax enforcement”.

Had the four departments better recognised the risks and acted accordingly, “there was a real possibility that the misconduct of the private-sector parties and the fire hazards in WF Court could have been rectified or removed before the fire”, it said.

Lee said a three-month investigation would now begin into the government’s role.

“I am deeply saddened. I would like to apologise to the affected residents and the general public,” Lee said on Thursday, adding that housing minister Ho should “bear political responsibility”.

But Yip Ka-kui, who lost his wife and home in the fire, told reporters he did not consider the government’s acceptance of accountability adequate. “All the secretaries for the departments named should step down,” he said, according to AFP news agency. “Administrative responsibility falls short” and political accountability even more so, Yip added.

Phyllis Lo, whose mother died in the fire, told AP news agency that progress had been made but that justice had yet to be served.

She added the officials’ apology was late, though it was better than nothing.

Hong Kong’s government has previously been accused by critics of being more concerned with clamping down on criticism than addressing the causes of the disaster.

Since a national security law was imposed on the city in 2020 by Beijing, the city’s once vibrant civil society groups and political opposition have been all but quashed.

In the weeks after the fire, authorities warned against crimes that “exploit the tragedy” and reportedly arrested at least three people for sedition.

China’s national security agency in Hong Kong also summoned international media representatives, saying they had smeared the government in their reports on the fire.

On Thursday Lee promised that “similar incidents will never be allowed to happen again”.

Some observers had called for a more legally powerful inquiry commission to be set up, rather than the judge-led independent review committee that compiled Thursday’s report.

Lee told reporters that authorities had proposed “systematic reforms”, including criminalising bid-rigging and overhauling the complaints system.

The report stated: “The fire has been put out, but the reform has only just begun.”

#389. Albanian opposition storms parliament, sets off flares and smoke bombs

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Opposition lawmakers disrupt parliament session, accusing the prime minister of downplaying ongoing antigovernment protests.

The leader of Albania’s opposition has branded members of the government as “criminals”, while he and fellow lawmakers disrupted a parliamentary session by setting off flares and smoke bombs.

Sali Berisha and other opposition members stormed parliament in the capital, Tirana, on Thursday in support of demonstrators, who are demanding Prime Minister Edi Rama’s resignation. Berisha and his colleagues unsuccessfully tried to prevent a vote on a new law governing the country’s central bank.

“These [ruling lawmakers] are criminals and ⁠they deserve tear gas, rubber bullets, cracked skulls and broken bones, not the young people,” Berisha, leader of the Democratic Party of Albania, said.

The protests began in May and were triggered by Rama’s preliminary approval of a luxury resort linked to Jared Kushner, the son-in-law of United States President Donald Trump.

The student-led protest movement has since evolved into something far bigger, with demonstrators citing economic stagnation, alleged corruption and poor education standards, coupled with high tuition fees.

The anti-government protests are Albania’s largest in decades and have turned violent, injuring hundreds of people, including police officers. Government buildings, banks and private buildings have also been attacked and set on fire.

Rama has been in office for 13 years and has refused to resign after winning a fourth term last year.

As Berisha addressed parliament on Thursday, his colleagues surrounded him while holding photos of protesters allegedly injured by rubber bullets.

Rama has introduced fines of more than $6,000 for anyone who attends a protest that turns violent, even if they did not participate in such acts. That is six times Albania’s average monthly salary of around $1,000.

Rama has announced a series of measures aimed at easing the protests. These include increasing the monthly scholarship stipend for students from low-income families, according to Albania’s public broadcaster, Radio Televizioni Shqiptar. Scholarships have also been extended to master’s degree students. Rama has also announced subsidised rent for students, the broadcaster reported.

WASHINGTON – A lobbying group that says it represents farmers and meat companies is helping to fund US$3 million (S$3.8 million) in attack ads against Republicans in some of the most competitive US midterm election races, criticising the party’s candidates on cost-of-living and animal-welfare issues.

That sum is a small slice of the billions being poured into the Nov 3 midterms. But President Donald Trump’s Republican Party already faces headwinds among typically stalwart rural supporters due to concerns about tariffs and high fuel costs driven up by the war in Iran.

The ads in Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, Colorado and Iowa are paid for by the political action arm of the American Meat Producers Association (AMPA), an organisation founded in 2025 that represents farmers who support high animal welfare standards.

The group has just one listed employee on its website: its president Holly Bice. It does not disclose its donors or member lists.

But farmers are one source of cash for the midterm ads, according to Reuters interviews with farmers associated with the AMPA.

The ads criticise four incumbent Republicans for supporting a Bill in Congress, the Save Our Bacon Act, which would overturn California and Massachusetts laws that require higher farm animal welfare standards and which have for years divided the pork industry.

The Bill has been endorsed by nearly 400 state and national farm groups.

The Bill has been endorsed by nearly 400 state and national farm groups. AMPA says it represents farmers who oppose the bill.

But the ads also attack the candidates on other topics, like their support for Trump’s tariffs on Canadian goods.

One ad against US Representative Gabe Evans, a Colorado Republican, says he “voted for Trump’s tariffs and the war in Iran, sending prices skyrocketing.”

Evans did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Many farm groups have remained allied with Republicans this midterm cycle.

For instance, the political action arm of the Iowa Farm Bureau, a branch of the national Farm Bureau lobby group, in September endorsed all Republican candidates for House, Senate and state secretary of agriculture.

Bice, AMPA’s president, said the organisation is running ads on issues beyond its focus of animal welfare because “farmers and ranchers don’t experience policy one Bill at a time”.

“These ads point out that certain lawmakers have voting records that have hurt farmers,” Bice said in response to e-mailed questions, declining to name the major donors to its political action committee running the ads.

Bice said AMPA and its PAC are supported by farmers, meat companies and “people who care about family farming”, but declined to provide more details.

Russ Kremer, a Missouri hog farmer who is on AMPA’s leadership council, said there are roughly 5,000 farmers involved with AMPA and that many had contributed to its PAC, but he did not provide details.

Farmers have been hard hit in 2026 by record diesel prices, higher fertiliser costs and reduced exports to China.

Trump plans to give farmers US$8 billion in diesel aid, bringing government payments to farmers to a record high of US$55.4 billion in 2026, though the funds will not arrive until after the election.

AMPA has spent US$2.9 million to date on digital and radio ads, according to federal election filings. Of that, about US$2.5 million has funded ads opposing US Representative Ashley Hinson, an Iowa Republican, in her Senate race, the filings show.

The ads target Hinson’s sponsorship of the Save Our Bacon Act, which Hinson has said would support Iowa’s hog farmers.

“She don’t give a damn about Iowa farmers. She will never feel the pain her votes have inflicted on people like me,” says a man in the ad.

Billy Fuerst, Hinson’s campaign communications director, said: “Iowa farmers strongly support Ashley Hinson because she delivers for agriculture.

“She has even bucked her own party to get year-round E15 passed out of the House, a critical priority for corn farmers and ethanol producers.”

The Bill supported by Hinson would expand sales of E15, a higher-ethanol gasoline blend.

Dean Frazer, an Iowa hog farmer, told Reuters he supports Hinson and the Save Our Bacon Act in part because it is costly for farmers to convert their operations to comply with the higher animal welfare standards.

Hinson has been endorsed by the Iowa Corn Growers Association, as well as the state Farm Bureau chapter.

Trump won Iowa by double digits in the 2024 presidential election, but polls show Democrats tied with or leading Republicans in many of the state’s midterm races.

Rural approval of Trump hit a new low in October of 45%, according to Reuters/Ipsos polling.

AMPA’s spending is not a straightforward effort to elect Democrats. The group has also backed some Republicans who share its views on animal welfare standards.

AMPA has spent US$100,000 on ads supporting Republican incumbents Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania and Andrew Garbarino of New York, who have opposed the Save Our Bacon Act.

#788. Bangladesh nuclear plant gets power generation nod

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Bangladesh’s Rooppur Nuclear Power Plant has received the final regulatory clearance, paving the path to commence electricity generation.

Rooppur, the country’s first nuclear power plant, was given the nod to advance to Stage C by the Atomic Energy Regulatory Authority (BAERA), the Daily Star reported on Thursday.

During this stage, the reactor’s power will be increased to produce steam, boost the turbine, and generate electricity. Capacity will gradually be increased in phases.

“Now Unit 1 will proceed with the power startup to begin the generation process,” BAERA Chairman Mahamudul Hasan was quoted as saying by the news outlet. He added that the authority would determine the startup date.

Rosatom head Alexey Likhachev earlier this month told media that the plant is on track to be connected to the national grid by end of next month.

The Rooppur plant, located 160 kilometers from Dhaka on the banks of the Padma River, was built at a cost of $12.65 billion, with Russian financial and technical support.  With two VVER-1200 Russian-designed reactors, it will have a combined capacity of 2,400 megawatts. Moscow is financing $11.9 billion in state loans for the project.

Rosatom, which is providing equipment, nuclear fuel, and engineering assistance, expects to commission the second unit in 2027.

Once fully operational, the plant is expected to deliver 12% of Dhaka’s power demand.

Likhachev recently disclosed that Moscow is in talks with Dhaka to build a new phase of Rooppur’s high-capacity units.

“We are primarily talking about a possible additional phase of large units,” Likhachev said on the sidelines of the Russian Energy Week forum, adding that Dhaka was also exploring the possibility of small-capacity units and a research reactor.

#617. Why students in ancient China honoured teachers by gifting wild vegetables

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Students gifted only water celery or white wormwood, choosing these fragrant plants to symbolise a pure pursuit of knowledge

While it is common for Chinese people to hold banquets for teachers upon graduation as a way of honouring them – a traditional value – in ancient China, students expressed their respect for teachers differently: by presenting them with vegetables.

The tradition began in the Zhou dynasty (1046–256 BC). On the first day of school, students wore blue gowns and brought their teachers wild vegetables they had gathered themselves. They then bowed reverently before memorial tablets honouring ancient sages. The ceremony was followed by a group dance, which also expressed the students’ respect for their teachers and their dedication to their studies.

Not just any vegetables could be presented to teachers on this day. Water celery and white wormwood were preferred because they were seen as clean, fragrant and ethereal, symbolising the students’ pure pursuit of knowledge.

#720. Trump isn’t just facing a backlash. He is caught in something much bigger than that

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Look at this political moment a certain way, and you can see the establishment coming back to life. The most anticipated case is next week’s US midterms, whose polling looks severe enough for the Republicans to have commentators musing the end of the Trump era. Presidents almost always get a whacking in the midterms, of course, but Donald Trump seems on course for a historic one. Historic enough to return not just the House to the Democrats, but the Senate, which was frankly meant to be unthinkable. That’s on the cards because suddenly Democrats are in potential winning positions in places as red as Texas and Kansas.

But if the establishment is finding comfort anywhere, it will be in the Democrats’ numbers. That party, you will recall, is facing its own insurgency in the form of avowedly left-wing candidates – occasionally self-identified democratic socialists – who dispatched the Democrat establishment in some high-profile primaries. Now we have a spread of Democrats the political coverage variously designates as either “progressives” or “moderates”. And right now, the polling is showing that the moderates are significantly outperforming the progressives. Put another way, the progressive candidates currently seem a drag on the Democrat vote. If they win, it will be because the Democrats had enough margin for error. The closer you are to being the establishment candidate, the better your chances seem.

Now look to Britain, where gravity seems to have taken hold of the insurgents. A suite of polls this week has Reform sinking to third place, with the major parties eclipsing them after two years of running behind. The Greens, meanwhile, have lost half their vote since their peak in February. The result has Labour riding its “Burnham bounce” into a clear first place. The Conservatives are now second. To be clear, that merely has the Tories returning to somewhere near their disastrous 2024 election result. But the direction of travel has them chuffed about that.

If all this is as it seems, we’re looking at something significant. You couldn’t say the underlying conditions that have been driving the populist surge have drastically changed. The cost of living is still an enormous stress everywhere, and public attitudes to immigration remain sceptical. And yet somehow the insurgencies seem fractionally out of puff. Whatever we’re seeing in these societies, it’s not a sudden contentedness with life. It’s more likely the discontentedness remains, but is slowly decoupling from the politicians who once profited from it.

Hereabouts, exercise caution. You probably don’t need me to tell you how slippery US polling has been in the past decade. Sometimes they underestimate the Republican vote – especially Trump’s personal vote in presidential elections – and sometimes they undersell the Democrats. The Economist carried a fascinating analysis last week that found that heavily red states are consistently redder than the polls suggest, and heavily blue states bluer. Normally that doesn’t matter because the result is foregone. But if it holds true this time, we’ll see Democrat hopes dashed in places like Iowa or South Carolina. And Democrat hope restored in places like Maine and New Hampshire.

Similarly, British politics is experiencing some strange conditions. Partly, that’s about Reform’s season of scandal: suspect donations, and elected representatives calling Asians “parasites” and black children “animals” – all of which led to high-profile sackings and suspensions. There’s good reason to believe this pushed once-disillusioned Tories back home. But Reform might be suffering in another way too. One theory is that its recent influx of Tory conversions has completely undermined its outsider status. Reform never pitched itself as a kind of Tory party-in-exile. Once it becomes that, there is less reason for its existence.

Meanwhile, Labour has a prime minister who seems newer than Nigel Farage. Perhaps even more of an outsider. Andy Burnham wasn’t even in the parliament until June. He was parachuted in via a byelection for the express purpose of unseating Keir Starmer and becoming prime minister. It would be too much of a stretch to call Burnham an insurgent. But he’s not continuity, either. He gives the disillusioned a way of listening again, and hearing things afresh. You see the hints of this in the polling of people who didn’t vote in 2024. Until recently, Reform owned these people, having support from 63 per cent of them. Now they have only 26 per cent of them, while Labour now claims almost as many, on 22 per cent.

None of this is quite the empire striking back. It is more the desire for change assuming a different shape. In America, the anti-incumbent mood is clear. Trump, as the chief incumbent, is now a lightning rod for dissent. The looming swings are starkest in places he won comfortably, and are dampened in seats the Democrats are meant to win. In Britain, the title of incumbent has become far more ambiguous. But Labour’s fortunes seem to have risen the less incumbency it exuded, and Reform’s case is the reverse.

That’s less a picture of stability returning than one of restlessness. Clearly, insurgencies have a half-life, determined by how proximate they become to power. This has been one of the clearest trends of Trump’s political career: he builds momentum in opposition, storms to sensational victories, then promptly repels voters when in office. But when he or his fellow travellers are ejected, the disillusionment that put them there hasn’t suddenly been extinguished. It merely reconfigures itself.

Sometimes, under certain conditions, that reconfiguring happens around the establishment. But it doesn’t seem to happen for establishment reasons. And that lays down a particularly wicked challenge: incumbents must exude a sense of constant renewal, a kind of perpetual motion, but one that doesn’t look like chaos or incompetence. Perhaps here Burnham will provide a useful case study. He could hardly begin with a cleaner slate. Now we’ll see how long it takes for the smog of high office to sully it.

It’s exhausting, I admit. But it’s worth observing what it isn’t. For instance, it isn’t politics driving itself off a cliff. It’s not things moving in a linear direction towards authoritarianism, as many fear. It seems more a series of convulsions to no particular end. That’s not to advise complacency, of course. It’s more a case of: strap in. Because in our age, things don’t so much return to normal as pass through normality on the way to somewhere else.

Waleed Aly is a broadcaster, author, academic and regular columnist.
