#2864. I Survived the Taco Bell 50K, Barely

📅 Oct 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Dan England put his journalistic and ultrarunning chops where his mouth is, consuming a menu item at 10 different Taco Bells over the course of running 50 kilometers. Along the way, he spoke with the Taco Bell 50K race director and dozens of participants to understand the unique allure of this race, which has expanded from a quirky local event to a beloved race format across the country.

I’ve run 100 miles, climbed all 54 of the 14,000-foot peaks in Colorado, owned a heeler, attended at least a dozen death metal shows, and raised a son and twin daughters. But on Saturday, October 3, I  faced one of the biggest challenges of my life: I had to eat an entire Nachos BellGrande eight hours into a 50-kilometer race.

I was already full of Pintos ’N’ Cheese, a Doritos “Locos” taco, several glasses of Baja Blast, spicy potatoes, Nacho Fries, lots of cinnamon things and, for breakfast, a Chalupa Supreme. I’d run 25 miles through some of the best and worst parts of Denver. It was 88 degrees Fahrenheit.

But this is what it takes to finish the Taco Bell 50K. You have to dig deep. You have to go hard. You have to Live Más.

What initially was by the race organizers’ own admission probably the stupidest event they could dream up is now, by numbers at least, one of the most popular ultramarathons in the country.

One of those organizers and this year’s race director, Dan Zolnikov, is flabbergasted at the attention the event has received, even if he still doesn’t quite understand it. Just two years ago, about 250 hungry runners showed up to Denver, the race’s origin, and 120 finished. Last year nearly 750 came to run. It’s turned into an epidemic: Using the Denver race as a guide, many other Taco Bell races have cropped up across the country from Tennessee to California, organized by other runners who want to inspire people.

Maybe inspire isn’t the right word. The goal, after all, is to eat 10 Taco Bell items at 10 different Taco Bells across the course of a 50K.

And yet, maybe inspire is the right word. It’s safe to say that many, if not most, of the 1,200 or so who showed up Saturday had never run an ultramarathon before and would likely never consider doing one otherwise. More than a few didn’t even know that a 50K is, in fact, five miles longer than a marathon. Even more of them had probably spent more time in a Taco Bell than on a trail, running.

I have proof: The longest any of my three running partners had run was a half marathon. And yet, they were among those ready to run farther than they ever had in their lives while eating food you normally consume when you’re drunk or high.

“Back in 2019, the second year we did it, we had 11 show up,” Zolnikov told me, “and I was blown away by that.”

The race is now enjoying a moment. This year, Denver TV stations swooped down upon the event, and instead of shaking their heads, residents were both horrified and supportive of the idea. Hundreds of cars honked at us as we ran, and dozens of residents sat in their front lawns to cheer us on. One group set up their own aid station with small cups of Modelo, which went down smoother than I expected. I hadn’t received cheers like that in a race since the year I ran the Chicago Marathon in 2013.

The thing is, as goofy and fun and ridiculous as the race is, it’s also hard, and that’s probably the combination that fascinates people. The DNF rate, over 60%, is higher than the Leadville 100. Not only do you run a 50K through a major city, one without many cushy trails, a well-marked route or closed streets, you have to stomach all that Taco Bell. And not only are Tums, Pepto, or Rolaids not allowed, if you puke, you DNF.

This is why I was staring down at the Nachos BellGrande at our eighth Taco Bell. Nachos BellGrande is one of my favorite foods. Now it looked like a pile of compost. We’d already faced many other challenges that day, including overworked soda stations that had run out of ice to cool us off, crowded bathrooms (and a strict edict not to go behind a bush), and the cruel pavement pounding our feet into hamburger.

The clock was also working against us: The 11-hour cutoff nipped at our heels all day. We weren’t sure we were going to make it. There was no time to waste.

I took a deep breath with my first gooey bite.

The origin story of the Taco Bell 50K is actually, against all odds, kind of endearing.

The idea started as most good ideas do: on a group long run. This one, in 2018, was with the Denver chapter of Achilles International, which organizes guided weekly training runs and weekend races for disabled runners. Jason Romero, who is blind, had a bunch of ultrarunning buddies, some of whom had guided him in big races.

Heartwarming, right? Yes, except now this turns into a hard lesson of what happens when you allow ultrarunners to go on group runs together. They ran through a Denver farmers market, where one of the runners loved to eat, and they began to joke about fueling up at the most opposite place they could think of. With apologies to the franchise, that turned out to be Taco Bell.

They joked about it for a few weeks, until Romero, using coarse language, challenged the group. Zolnikov plotted out a 50K course through Denver with 10 Taco Bell stops. It’s the same course now, years later, with a few alterations.

The idea was only sort of batshit crazy. Unlike at a road marathon, a good ultramarathon aid station isn’t just stocked with Gatorade and gels—it’s full of real food. This was my answer when my friends, even other runners, called me insane for signing up for the 2026 Taco Bell 50K.

What do you think I eat at aid stations? Salad?

Still, Taco Bell is infamous for challenging even the most iron of stomachs. My Latina wife, who spent several hours on the bathroom floor after one late-night trip, hasn’t eaten it in years. After the inaugural event, Romero admits he and his friends were wrecked and spent far too many hours in a bathroom.

So, against my better judgment, I did what I do when I sign up for any ultra: I trained. Over the course of three months, I ate at Taco Bell an embarrassing number of times. Kelly, one of my best friends and a former co-worker who is “mostly” a vegetarian and had the idea to sign up for it, took several taco trips herself. She also tried to follow a training plan I put together, but her toddler and infant, along with a demanding job, prevented her from completing many of her longest runs, other than a tough trail half marathon.

When I picked her up, at 4:30 a.m., a couple minutes from my home in Greeley, she asked me how I slept before interrupting me and saying her kids screamed at her all night.

We found a place to park close to the start of the race, a Taco Bell with beautiful arches to run through, a design so clever you’d think it was built for the race (it wasn’t). We pulled in at 5:45 a.m. and grabbed a bib from an unmanned table, with Sharpies so you could write your name. Some wrote things like “Beefy Runner,” “Macho Supreme” and “The Little Dog Who Says ‘Yo Quiero Taco Bell’.”

We hooked up with her two friends, tried to use a bathroom at Safeway. Kelly got in, but the men’s line was too long, at least a half-hour wait. This was a theme all day because there weren’t many women running the race: Women are generally smarter than men. The females couldn’t help but gloat at us as they swept past.

“My my my,” one said. “How the tables have turned.”

A few minutes before the huge crowd gathered to listen to Zolnikov’s pre-race briefing, I unwrapped one of my two “big” items, a Chalupa Supreme, an overstuffed fried taco—I decided to start big while my stomach was empty. Last year, they allowed participants to eat during the 7 a.m. briefing, and I figured a few minutes couldn’t hurt. Kelly vehemently disagreed.

Fine. I wrapped up my fried taco and waited for Zolnikov to talk to us about the rules—keep your wrappers, eat two big items, don’t be feral to the Taco Bell workers—safety (don’t run in front of cars) and no shitting in alleys before taking my first bite.

The race had four waves, from fastest to slowest, all by their own determination, and began with AC/DC’s “Thunderstruck,” probably the most appropriate song for this occasion. We were one of the last to line up. We’d stay near that position for much of the race.

Kelly chided me for my serious demeanor as we approached the first stop. Everyone was having so much fun, she said. Yes, I thought to myself, that’s because they don’t know what’s coming. I’d done ultras. They always hurt.

But, OK, fine, let’s focus on the whimsy, for now.

At the first stop, a group of five dressed in tacos practiced cannibalism. The group leader, who had run a 100K earlier this year, found the costumes on Amazon and then made taco hats. The farthest her best friend (also in the group) had run was 6 miles, a smidge less than 50K.

The group of five all grew up together in Tennessee. They were a handful of the hundreds—yes, hundreds—who traveled out of state to take part in the original and hence superior Taco Bell 50K.

I got their names, but I spilled Baja Blast on my phone screen and panic-wiped it with a napkin and erased the note on my phone.

The Taco Bell 50K doesn’t sell race shirts or any merch at all—in this era of trail and ultrarunning commercialization, it’s heartening to see how the race organizers refuse to make a buck off it—so runners, just like the taco worshippers, wore their own fun. Many, if not most, wore some kind of goofy shirt, many with Taco Bell themes. I wore a colorful, button-up skull shirt. Kelly wore a hat that she made, something about how those who get diarrhea from Taco Bell have weak bloodlines. We saw one runner with a Del Taco button up shirt, which made me laugh.

At the third Taco Bell, Kelly ate her Burrito Supreme, so she’d already consumed her two required big items. She thought going big early would pay off. I needed a break after the Chalupa and Doritos taco and ate some small Cinnabuns instead.

Kelly and I had purchased our first four items the day before and kept them in the fridge overnight. This was not only legal but encouraged by race organizers to help prevent us from overwhelming the stores.

But in Taco Bell’s defense, they were ready. Maybe that’s because Zolnikov admittedly harassed many store owners in advance. Maybe it’s because nearly all runners used the mobile app to order ahead, as we did. (Technology is awesome!) Plus, at most stops, there were crates of free bottled water for runners. It was a life saver on a hot day: I was unable to carry much water because my pack was either full of food we carried for the first four stops or the wrappers and containers you have to keep until the end to prove to race organizers that you consumed 10 items.

We get our first taste of dirt, a trail around Sloan’s Lake, a Denver centerpiece, after the third stop, and the route begins to change from questionable urban areas to parks and neighborhoods. It’s actually beautiful.

I pass by a runner who has a plastic bag full of Cinnamon Twists. They will be eight of his meals, he told me. THAT’S CHEATING, Kelly, I say to myself, and kinda bullshit. That’s why they require runners to eat two big items.

We aren’t abusing the rules as much. So far for us, everything is staying down.

At the eighth stop, I use my vast experience at eating when I don’t feel like it, a common problem at ultras, and hork down the nachos. My stomach whines.

I take solace in the fact that I have actually felt better after eating at every stop, not worse. That’s usually my experience in ultras, and it remains true after every Taco Bell stop.

Still, the nachos were tough to swallow. When we start to run again—I’d generously call it a lope—I have a frightening burp, the wet kind that could go either way, like a fart. (In case you’re wondering, shitting yourself is, as far as I can tell, allowed) We agree to walk to let our food digest.

We also continue walking because the real race, as they say, has now started. I have a blister the size of a half dollar on the bottom of my right foot. Kelly says her feet are covered in them. One of Kelly’s friends, Ana, took a digger hours ago and gashed her knee. Everyone is hurting.

A goofy 50K is still a 50K, and many of the back of the pack are experiencing, for the first time, the kind of suffering that comes with an ultra. When I ask a group of 30 in the 10th Taco Bell if anyone has run an ultra before, they all shake their heads.

“I don’t think anyone here has even run a marathon,” says one woman who ran “maybe five miles” for her longest training run.

This is the beauty and brutality of this race. Many of its participants will push themselves harder than they ever have before and will be able to call themselves ultramarathoners by the end. Maybe, and probably, a few will continue. Amanda, Kelly’s other friend, said in the race, “I’m just so grateful to be able to move my body,” which is exactly what an ultrarunner would say.

There are fast people who do this event. Professional runner Sage Canaday runs it every year. This year he came in second behind Aquinas Lasnoski, who finished in 3:44:37 to break Canaday’s course record. (“Running fitness wasn’t there,” Canaday wrote on Strava. “Eating fitness kind of was.”) But this is a race for slow people like me. If you run too hard, your stomach doesn’t have a chance to digest all that food, and again, puking means you’re out.

The only problem with being slow is cutoffs haunt you. This race only has one cutoff, and it comes faster than we expect. We forget that our GPS watches add mileage for everything, even trips to the bathroom, and when we look at the course on our phones, we are unpleasantly surprised to discover that we still have 2 miles to go and only 35 minutes to do it.

When we start to run, our bodies protest in different ways. Our stomachs, thankfully, are fine, but our feet and joints and knees and spirits are beat up. We are tired and hot and ready to be done. But this is what it takes to finish the Taco Bell 50K. You have to dig deep. You have to go hard. You have to Live Más.

We bang out a mile in 11 minutes, and all of a sudden, the arch, that glorious purple arch full of other runners and spectators, appears in our view. We finish with 10 minutes to spare.

We present our wrappers, get a taco sauce packet attached to a gold ribbon for our finisher’s medal, and limp back to the car for the drive home.

The next day, we report how we’re feeling in our new group chat. We’re proud of ourselves: Less than half the field, about 600 runners, finished the race, according to the somewhat official stats.

The consensus is that everyone feels better than they thought they would. (Maybe all that mid-race fueling actually helped with recovery?) It was also more fun than they thought it would be. The underlying message is one that no one wants to say out loud just yet: Maybe we will join all the crazy fools again next year.

#1484. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

📅 Oct 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

While you could take your bike to the shop for everything it needs (or worse, avoid maintenance entirely), you’ll save money and time while avoiding frustration once you understand these basics. Here’s what you need to know to keep your bike rolling smoothly.

When you think of bike safety, helmets, bike lights, and obnoxiously bright jerseys may come to mind, but you need to ensure your bike is in good working condition, too. “No matter what brings someone into our shop looking for service, we always start with a safety check,” says Ian Hughes, co-owner of Just Riding Along in Basking Ridge, New Jersey.

Ideally, you’ll inflate your tires before every ride, or every week at a minimum.

How much tire pressure you need varies according to the terrain, your weight, and tire width. Every tire has manufacturer’s guidelines printed on the sidewall, which is a great starting point.

Use a floor pump to fill your tires to the desired pressure. This item is a must-have, as it allows you to add a bit of volume at a time to get the exact pressure you’re looking for, says Park Tool master bike mechanic and technical expert Truman Purdy. Most floor pumps accommodate both a Presta or Schrader valve (the two types of valves you’ll find in a tire or tube). If you’re not sure exactly how to use your pump, Purdy suggests exploring the manufacturer’s website or YouTube channel.

Check the tightness of your thru axles or quick release skewers (i.e. the cylindrical part that slides through your wheel to hold it in place between the fork or frame). If they’re not secure, you risk your wheels wobbling.

If you have a quick release skewer, pull on the lever to release it, then push it closed again. If you start to feel some resistance when it’s about halfway closed, and it’s a little challenging to close it fully, you’re in good shape. If it’s loose, tighten the nut and try again.

If your bike has thru axles, you may need an Allen wrench (a.k.a. Allen key or hex wrench) or a switch lever tool to check the tightness. Since most of us don’t have a torque wrench at home, Hughes suggests making sure it feels snug without cranking on it. “You don’t have to kill yourself to get it tight enough,” he says.

You should also check your handlebars before you roll out. To do this, stand facing your bike with the front wheel secured between your legs, place your hands on either side of your handlebars, and try twisting them and pushing down on them, says Hughes. If they’re not secure, use an Allen wrench to tighten the bolts in the faceplate, which is the part of the bike that connects the handlebars to the stem.

You’ll also want to make sure your saddle is securely in place. Give it a shove in every direction (up, down, and side to side) to make sure it isn’t going anywhere. If you find that it’s loose, position it, and use an Allen wrench to tighten the relevant bolts.

Last, but certainly not least, check your brakes before you hop in the saddle. Standing next to your bike, lift one wheel off the ground at a time, give it a spin with your hand, and engage your brakes to make sure they’re doing their job.

“Your brake levers should begin to connect with the rim/rotor at no more than halfway through the lever motion (from fully open to the handlebar),” Hughes says. “I want brake adjustment to be strong enough to start slowing you immediately upon the pads contacting the rim/rotor in a controlled manner.”

You should be able to control the rate you slow down and have the ability to initiate a skid (even if you don’t want to actually skid), Purdy adds.

If you find your brake levers go all the way to the bar, it’s a good idea to get to your local bike shop and skip the ride until your brakes work properly, Hughes says. #4341. Got the birthday blues? 7 tips to feel better on your big day

📅 Oct 07, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

You're turning another year older, and instead of wanting to celebrate, you feel like canceling everything. Happy birthday texts are piling up, your friend bought a cake, yet somehow nothing is helping.

If you’re feeling sad, flat, or anxious—or you can’t stop overthinking—you might have the birthday blues. And it's a lot more common than the cake-and-confetti version of this day makes it seem.

Here’s everything you need to know about birthday blues, and some tips to make your day feel lighter.

The birthday blues are a dip in mood that some people experience around their birthday. They can show up as sadness, irritability, anxiety, low energy, or just feeling “off.” And they can happen in the days leading up to your birthday, on the day itself, or both.

This isn't a recognized diagnosis in the DSM-5 (the diagnostic manual used by mental health professionals), and there's no direct research on the subject. But clinicians notice common birthday blues patterns, such as unmet expectations, time pressure, social comparison, and grief.

Birthday blues tend to be short-lived. They come up around your birthday, and typically ease off within a few days. Birthday depression is more pronounced: persistent low mood, trouble functioning in your day to day, or distress that lingers well beyond your birthday.

Neither is a formal clinical diagnosis. But if what you're feeling lasts longer than two weeks, or feels heavier than you'd expect from a birthday, it’s worth talking to a doctor or mental health professional. It could be a sign of depression or an adjustment disorder (an intense stress reaction to a specific event). Both of these conditions respond well to treatment.

You may be feeling birthday blues due to personal, cultural, or situational factors (often a mix).

Birthdays can invite comparison: Another year passing naturally makes you want to take stock. How am I doing? Where did I think I'd be by now? Evidence shows that comparing yourself upward—measuring your life against people who seem ahead—tends to produce feelings of shame, envy, and lower mood.

Unmet expectations can sting: When your social media feed constantly shows you big celebrations and birthday shoutouts, it can raise expectations for what your own birthday should look like. If your day doesn’t match that, it can leave you feeling disappointed, even if nothing went wrong.

Grief and memory can surface: Your birthday can remind you of something you’ve lost: a parent who always called, a relationship that ended, a version of life that didn't work out as planned. There’s research on this too — it's sometimes called an anniversary reaction, when a specific date stirs up old emotions.

Aging can feel like pressure: Each birthday can remind you that you’re getting older. It can also bring up fears about health, identity, or what comes next. Those fears tend to weigh heavier as milestone birthdays come around (like turning 30, 40, 50).

Change in routine can throw you off: Anxiety may spike when your day feels different. The extra attention and the pressure to make the day special can feel overwhelming, especially if being in the spotlight isn’t your thing.

If you’re feeling down on your birthday, there’s no need to force a good time. These tips are just here to help you feel a little steadier. The first few are for preparing before the day arrives, and the rest are for the day itself, whenever you need them.

A lot of birthday stress comes from your expectations leading up to the day. Take a moment to think about how you’d like to mark the occasion, rather than what you think a birthday's “supposed” to look like. Maybe you prefer a low-key day at home over a grand celebration. There’s no blueprint, only what works for you.

The birthday blues often come with confusion about why you're feeling this way in the first place. Once you can name the specific emotion—for example, "I'm feeling lonely" or "I'm anxious about getting older"—you can start to process it.

You don't have to try to stop the emotion or talk yourself out of it. Give yourself room to feel what you feel, without adding self-criticism on top. Treat yourself the way you'd treat a friend going through the same situation.

Sadness doesn't always have an obvious cause. Your birthday marks another year passing, which can stir up feelings that have been sitting in the back of your mind. You don't need a specific reason for that to be valid.

Birthday blues are temporary and related to the day itself, while depression is a persistent condition that affects your daily life. If your low mood is sticking around well past your birthday, it's worth checking in with a doctor or therapist.

Crying on your birthday is completely normal, and it’s usually your body’s way of working through big emotions — whether that's grief, exhaustion, relief, or just the weight of a special day. It doesn't mean anything is wrong, and it doesn't need to be justified.
