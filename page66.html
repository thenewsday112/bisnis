#2785. Best Lever Action Rifles of 2026

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Published Oct 27, 2026 12:56 PM EDT

The latest for gun hunters and competitive shooters.

When I was a kid, there were three reasons I wanted a lever action rifle. First, they were cool. Second, because I grew up just outside New Haven, Conn., the historic home to Marlin and Winchester, they were my hometown guns. Third, they were cheap.

Only one of these things is still true. Lever guns always have been and always will be cool, though they will never again be cheap, nor—barring divine intervention—will they ever again be produced in Gun Valley.

Writing about the top lever action rifles is a bit tricky. Do you focus on the best of all time, which is great for us nostalgic sorts, or do you talk about great lever action rifles that you can buy new?

I tilted this list of the best lever action rifles in favor of those you can order new. Though if you have a hankering for one of the old-time classics, there are plenty to be had on the used market. Just don’t expect to find any bargains. Nearly all quality lever actions command a premium.

This list is divided into those guns that are being currently produced (or soon-to-be) in significant numbers and those that are either specialty orders or that must be sought out on the used gun market.

With the exception of the rifles that have just been announced, Senior Staff Writer Tyler Freel and I have shot, handled, and/or owned all the models listed. Beyond our hands-on experience with the rifles over the years, however, many of them are no-brainers based on their well-documented history and legacy.

The good — actually, great — news here is that we’re seeing more lever actions made with each passing year. About 15 years ago, Remington screwed the pooch with Marlin and those were some dark days indeed. In those years it seemed only Henry was making quality lever guns in meaningful numbers, but things have taken a turn for the better.

Under Ruger’s ownership, Marlin seems poised to flourish. Henry is still innovating hard and trotting out new models in new cartridges, like the 360 Buckhammer. And we have a couple interesting and innovative newcomers in the mix.

When Ruger resurrected Marlin, the first model they brought back was the 1895 SBL. This was a smart move because the SBL, with its gray laminate furniture, stainless steel metal, threaded barrel, oversized lever loop, and picatinny rail was one of the sexiest and most sought-after iterations of this classic lever action.

I had good things to say about the Marlin 1895 SBL when it was reintroduced in late 2021, and that opinion hasn’t shifted. It is the best lever action rifle produced under the Marlin brand—at least in the modern era. Packing 6+1 rounds of .45/70 Gov’t in its magazine, it brings a formidable level of firepower to the party which is why it has garnered a lot of interest from hunters in bear country, as well as with everyday sportsmen in more sedate parts of the U.S.

Marlin has also introduced two new versions of the 1895 worth considering, a Model 1895 Trapper and a Model 1895 Guide Gun. The Trapper comes with a Skinner peep sight, no picatinny rail, and is a bit more compact (34 ¼ in. vs. 37 ¼ in.) than the SBL. It, too, is chambered in .45/70 and has a 5+1 magazine capacity. The Guide Gun (model GBL) is slightly less expensive than the SBL ($1,239 vs. $1,479), has traditional semi-buckhorn sights, blued steel, is chambered in .45/70, and a 6+1 capacity.

In 2024, Smith & Wesson made a big splash in the lever-gun world with their model 1854. It was first introduced in .44 Magnum, and .45/70 models followed suit in 2025, along with options in the venerable .30/30 Winchester and .45 Colt.

We were immediately impressed by the first Model 1854 rifles we got our hands on. These lever guns, which are relatively complex pieces of machinery, were superbly executed — with excellent fit and finish and smooth operation. They feature both side-gate and magazine tube loading, and are available in blued/wood and stainless/synthetic trims. Both are generally classic-looking styles, but the synthetic version offers modern perks like a picatinny rail, M-Lok slots on the fore-end, and a threaded muzzle. Since introduction, Smith & Wesson have also introduced a Stealth Hunter model with black-on-black finish, a longer optic rail, tactical-style handguard with multiple M-Lok slots, and threaded muzzle — specifically for optics, lights, and a suppressor.

We’ve tested several of the Model 1854 rifles as they’ve rolled out and have been consistently impressed with the quality of build, smoothness of operation, and reliability of these guns. We rank them among the finest factory lever guns made today.

One of the slickest, funnest lever guns we’ve ever run is the Lever Action Supreme from Henry. This rifle combines the nostalgia and classic good looks of a lever gun with some features that a lot of modern shooters will find appealing. It’s offered in both 5.56/.223 and .300 Blackout, and its most notable characteristic is that it feeds from standard AR-15 magazines. Since it’s introduction, it’s also been updated to include the popular .450 Bushmaster. This isn’t simply a design that Henry adapted to work with these mags and cartridges. They started from scratch.

The Lever Action Supreme cycles very smoothly and uses an AR-style rotating bolt head and the bolt body does not protrude from the rear of the receiver as with many other lever guns when cycled. It also sports a floated barrel that provides good accuracy and while it has a classic look when bare, it’s equipped to accommodate modern accessories like suppressors, scopes, and red dot optics. You can read Snow’s full review of the Lever Action Supreme for a more detailed look.

It didn’t seem that long ago that Henry Repeating Arms was only making Golden Boys chambered in pistol cartridges and .22s. But these guys have been busy, and as the company embarks on its second quarter century, they have added an impressive number of products to the lineup.

One of the flagship hunting rifles is the All-Weather Picatinny Rail .45/70 Side Gate, which is Henry’s version of a do-everything guide gun. As the name implies, it is built for the elements. The metal has a hard-chrome satin finish. The stock is a stained hardwood.

It has an aperture peep sight mounted at the rear of the substantial Picatinny rail. Henry outfitted it with an oversized lever loop. With the 18.43-inch barrel, the overall length is 37.5 inches and it has a 4+1 capacity. The rifle can be loaded either through the side gate on the receiver or by dropping rounds into the tubular magazine.

This is a versatile and capable thumper that is equally at home in bear country or in a deer stand overlooking a food plot.

The Winchester Model 1873 might have been The Gun that Won the West—a great bit of marketing by Winchester executives—but the Model 94 is undeniably the rifle that won the hearts and minds of generations of deer hunters. If you polled long-time hunters regarding their choice as the top lever action rifle, there’s little doubt the Winchester 94 would win the vote.

This was the most prolific of Winchester’s lever actions, being the first sporting rifle in the U.S. to sell a million units. Prior to the end of its domestic production in 2006, more than seven million Model 94s were produced.

Model 94s are still being made for Browning/Winchester by Miroku Corp. in Japan and are available in several different grades. These run from the basic Carbine (1,309)withitsplainwalnutstockandbrushedpolishedbluedmetal,totheDeluxeSporting(2,230), which has case color hardened metalwork, upgraded wood, nicely executed checkering, and a half-round, half-octagonal deeply blued barrel.

Currently, the 94 is being offered in .30/30 Win., .38/55 and .450 Marlin.

The Browning BLR is one of the most versatile lever actions on the market. It is available in both short- and long-action lengths and because it feeds from a detachable box magazine it is able to run pointy spitzer-style bullets in cartridges usually associated with bolt guns.

The Lightweight ’81 Stainless Takedown is the most utilitarian BLR of them all, making it the best lever action rifle in the BLR family. It breaks down into two pieces for easy transport and is built on a lightweight aluminum receiver and sports a stainless steel barrel for excellent weather resistance. At about 7.25 pounds (depending on the specific chambering) it has a very nice balance and feel. Combined with its smooth lever system, which uses a clever gear and pinion design, the BLR embodies the lithe, fast-handling characteristics that lever action fans admire. (I’d be remiss if I didn’t give a nod to the legendary Winchester Model 88, which was a game-changing lever action from which the BLR drew lots of inspiration.)

The Lightweight ’81 Takedown is currently chambered in .243 Win., 6.5 Creedmoor, 7mm-08 Rem., .308 Win., .300 WSM, .30/06, and .300 Win. Mag. Several other cartridges are available in other BLR models as well.

It’s difficult for me to express how happy I am to include the Marlin 336 among the top lever action rifles currently made. I know I’m not alone in this. I cut my teeth hunting deer with a Marlin 336 in .35 Remington so, as with countless other hunters, the 336 was my first love.

We’ve handled and put a few rounds through one of the new 336’s, which will initially be chambered in .30/30 Win., and its quality was spot-on. As with the 1895s, the 336s have excellent fit and finish, run smoothly, and are aesthetically pleasing.

As you history buffs probably know, the original Henry lever action was the first commercially successful lever action rifle. Originally chambered in .44 Rimfire, it was one of the most advanced firearms to see action during the Civil War and was fielded in limited numbers by Union Troops.

Confederate Colonel John Mosby is credited with cursing it as “that damned Yankee rifle that can be loaded on Sunday and fired all week,” after fighting against troops wielding it in battle. That unintentional endorsement stuck and helped propel the Henry to everlasting fame.

Henry makes a “New Original Henry” chambered in either .44-40 WCF or .45 Colt, though the most popular (and affordable) homage is the Henry Golden Boy rimfire, which can be had in .22 S/L/LR, .22 Mag., or .17 HMR.

In .22 LR, the most popular configuration, the rifle holds 16 rounds, the same as the original. It has a 20-inch octagonal barrel that is nicely blued and contrasts sharply with the bright “Brasslite” finished receiver and brass buttpad and barrel band.

The receiver comes drilled and tapped for a scope, but before you mount an optic on yours spend some time running it with semi-buckhorn sights, as the Creator intended.

These Golden Boys run like a scalded squirrel and can digest ammo by the brick over the course of an afternoon.

As a first gun for a youngster, it has no real equal. But its appeal is so strong that no one will fault you for buying one for your grownup self first.

In 2024, Savage introduced the Revel, a rimfire lever-action chambered in .22 LR, .22 WMR, and .17 HMR. Considering their modest price, these are great little lever actions. They come in three trims: Classic, DLX, and Tactical.

I’ve had one of the classic Revels for a couple years and it’s a wonderful balance of traditional looks, performance, and modern features. It’s got handsome walnut furniture with a couple of modern lines and nice ergonomics. The steel has a matte blued finish, and the aluminum receiver is coated in black as well. The DLX has wood furniture with some modern M-Lok slots and the gun has a threaded muzzle. The Tactical version has black synthetic furniture.

The classic version I’ve been shooting has traditional dovetailed sights and the slightly heavy weight of the barrel, in conjunction with the buttstock design, makes the rifle quick to the shoulder and steady pointing. The receiver is unique in that it features two push pins that can be used to take the rifle down in seconds for stowage or cleaning. A lot of our .22 LR ammo is excessively waxy, and that can gum up lever guns pretty easily so easy takedown is helpful.

The Tactical Revel comes with a Picatinny rail for mounting an optic, but the other models are drilled and tapped to accommodate a scope. These are some of the nicer rimfire lever guns you’ll find for the money today.

The Marlin 1894 was hot stuff when it was introduced back in the day. It was the first lever gun with a flat top, side-eject design that anticipated the eventual wide-spread adoption of telescopic sights on rifles. This innovation gave it a leg up (on paper anyway) compared to the Winchester 1894 which debuted at the same time, though the fact that the Winchester came in the potent .30/30 versus the less powerful offerings in the Marlin gave Winchester’s 1894 a decisive advantage.

The Marlin 1894 has been chambered in a variety of mostly pistol-caliber cartridges over the decades, the most common of which (in the modern era) included the .44 Spl./.44 Magnum. .38 Spl./.357 Magnum and .45 Colt. I have a North Haven, Conn., built 1894 in .44 Magnum and it is one of my favorite carbines. (Though if I could snap my fingers and conjure any 1894 it would be one chambered in the quaint and now obsolete .218 Bee.)

Ruger announced the return of the Marlin 1894 this spring, so we will hopefully have hands-on range time with one soon for a full review. Expect to see the first offerings in the cartridges mentioned above, but as details become available we’ll update this information. (Note: the rifle pictured above is one of the older Marlin 1894s).

Rossi has recently, with the R95, become a true contender in the .45/70 lever gun space. Their stainless-steel R95 Laminate .45/70 is a high-quality gun that incorporates some modernized features and is better quality than even some of the previous Marlin models. This model has a polished stainless receiver — that is honestly a bit over-polished — and laminated wood furniture. They seem durable and weather-resistant in our testing, but we’d prefer the fit of the fore end to be a bit better. The sides of it aren’t fully against the magazine tube and can flex and potentially crack.

This new .45/70 lever gun features a modernized M16-style extractor and a threaded muzzle for accepting a suppressor. Staff writer Tyler Freel put a lot of ammo through his sample and it ran beautifully. You can read his full review here. Priced below some of the top-shelf lever guns like the new Marlin, Henry, and Smith & Wesson rifles, it’s affordable but still very good quality.

Another great Rossi lever gun is the Rio Bravo .22 LR. This is available in a number of configurations, but we really like the wood/gold variant. This is a lever gun with good looks that isn’t priced to be a safe queen. Staff Writer Tyler Freel put one of these to the test with his son and you can read his full review here.

The Rio Bravo has an aluminum receiver with a top cover that’s held in place with four Phillips-head screws. It’s easily removed for cleaning and maintenance — something that’s important to keep rimfire lever guns working correctly. It’s an accurate rifle that works well with a variety of ammunition and holds 14 rounds in its tubular magazine. If you want to mount an optic, you can use the narrow dovetail atop the receiver with tip-off rings, but this gun is most fun with iron sights.

This list could have been much longer, but in the interest of brevity I’ve only highlighted a few notable classic lever guns. Before getting to those, however, I do want to highlight a couple important honorable mentions.

The Spencer repeating rifle is one. It was the first military repeating rifle that used self-contained metallic cartridges and is the forefather of all lever actions. Though the Spencer was produced for only a short time (1860 to 1869) and in relatively limited numbers, it was the steppingstone upon which the lever action rifle, specifically Winchesters, was built.

The Winchester 1895, which gained prominence as Teddy Roosevelt’s “Big Medicine” in .405 Winchester is an iconic lever action whose fame outstrips its actual benefits. From a technical standpoint, its most distinguishing element is that it was the first Winchester lever action fed from an internal box magazine.

This was the first big-boy lever action in history. The Winchester 1886 changed the game because its strong locking-block action was able to handle proper rifle cartridges, rather than the pistol rounds that previous lever action designs were limited to.

It was originally chambered in .45/70 and .45/90, two rounds you can still get it in. Over the years it was loaded for other hard-hitting rounds like the .50/110 Winchester and .33 WCF.

Those rounds, combined with its excellent handling characteristics, made the Model 1886 the first true big-game capable lever action. Today, you can order it in a Saddle Ring Carbine configuration (1,630)witha22-inchbarrel,in“ShortRifle”trim(1,450) with a 24-inch round barrel, or go all out with the fancy Deluxe Case Hardened model ($1,930) with a 24-inch octagonal barrel, upgraded walnut and color case hardened receiver.

Arguably the finest lever action ever (and if there’s one thing lever gun fans like to do its argue about which is best), the Savage Model 99 was a revolutionary addition to the American sporting scene when it was trotted out in 1899.

It had an elegant, hammerless design, used an innovative rotary magazine that allowed the rifle to safely chamber cartridges with spitzer-style pointed bullets, and a stock silhouette that oozed sexiness and speed decades before such aesthetics became the hallmark of art deco industrial design.

First called the Model 1899, it officially changed to the Model 99 in 1921 when the .300 Savage was introduced. Initially, the 1899 was chambered in .303 Savage and .30/30 Win. Soon, Savage added other cartridges to the lineup—the most iconic of which was Charles Newton’s speedster, the .250-3000 Savage, in 1915. Later in life, the Model 99 added the .243 Win., .308 Win., 7mm-08 Rem., .375 Win. and others to the mix.

My Model 99 is a particularly lovely rifle. It was given to me by my father-in-law before he passed away and is a takedown model chambered in .300 Savage that includes a .410 bore barrel that it shoots single-shot.

The rifle was sold by Abercrombie and Fitch to legendary car maker Carroll Shelby, who eventually gave it to my father-in-law as a gift. It’s still in its original case of leather and wood lined with purple velvet. I rarely shoot it—but admire it frequently.

Though it never gained the following of the Savage 99, the Winchester Model 88 is similar to the Savage in that it incorporated a lot of technical innovation in its design and caused hunters and shooters to reassess the lever action platform.

Winchester unveiled it in 1955. It had a short lever throw and used a rotating three-lug bolt that smoothly stripped rounds from the detachable box magazine. The Model 88 was chambered in a handful of cartridges that were new at the time, specifically the .308 Win., .243 Win., .284 Win. and the .358 Win., which was a particularly terrific brush round that is sadly all but forgotten these days.

The Model 88 has a hammerless design and side eject, which allows for easy mounting of an optic on the receiver. One particularly clever feature is how the trigger moves with the lever loop, eliminating the possibility of a pinched finger or snagged glove when working the action.

I don’t think there’s a rimfire lever action as beloved as the Marlin 39A. It tops many shooters’ lists as the best .22 LR ever produced. The legacy of the 39A is fascinating. It started as the Model 1891 and was the first .22 LR lever action produced. Its legendary accuracy was demonstrated by Annie Oakley who used one in her shooting exhibitions. The Model 39A held the distinction of being the longest continually produced shoulder-fired firearm in history. That streak was broken when the dimwits who purchased Marlin and placed it under the Remington umbrella decided to move Marlin’s production facilities from North Haven, Conn., to Ilion, New York—at which point the production of all Marlins ground to a (predictable) halt.

My sincere hope is that we’ll soon—as in the next couple years—be able to reclassify the 39A under the “Best Lever Action Rifles Being Currently Made” category since it seems Ruger is doing such a fabulous job reviving Marlin since they purchased it.

When you think “cowboys” and the “Old West” the first rifle that comes to mind is this beauty. The Winchester 73 really was the right gun for the right time—being the first rifle chambered for the .44-40, which is said to be the first centerfire cartridge developed. Whether it deserved to be called “The Gun that Won the West” as Winchester touted, there’s no denying that the Model 73 was used to send a whole bunch of men—both good and bad—to their maker, and accounted for innumerable deer and other game to boot.

The Model 1873 was produced until 1923 but has enjoyed a revival since the sport of cowboy action shooting sprang up. Uberti makes replicas chambered in .357 Mag. and .45 Colt, while Winchester, which worked with their Japanese partner Miroku, has been importing ’73s since 2013. Those are available in .357 Mag., .45 Colt, and .44-40 WCF.

Since the barrel of a lever action is longer than that of a handgun, cartridges like the .357 Mag. and .44 Mag. get a significant performance boost in terms of velocity that makes them more capable on animals like deer and hogs.In addition, a lever-action chambered in .357 can run .38 Spl. rounds, which are economical and have little recoil making it a great option for plinking and practice.

Yes. They are fast-handling, quick-shooting, easy to wield and carry, hold good quantities of ammo, are available in a wide range of cartridges for different applications, and are legal in most all jurisdictions. Whether you’re on horseback with one in a scabbard or have a take-down model in a bug-out bag, lever-actions are an excellent choice.

Are lever-actions effective on dinosaurs?

Absolutely. The Marlin 1895 SBL carried by Chris Pratt’s character—dinosaur whisperer Owen Grady—in Jurassic World, would be a top choice as a dino stopper, provided it was loaded with heavy, hard-hitting ammo like Buffalo Bore’s 380-grain mono-metal WFN dangerous-game load.

Certainly. A carbine-length lever action chambered in a pistol cartridge like .357 Mag. or .44 Mag. would be a highly effective option for home protection. Even a rifle chambered in .22 Win. Mag. can be a formidable home-defense gun when loaded with ammo like Federal Punch.

Usually when one compiles a “best of” list—whether talking deer rifles, elk cartridges, or sniper rifles—there is significant push-back with the selections. Everyone has deeply-held convictions on such matters.

With lever action rifles, it is a bit different. There are many rifles that are universally agreed upon. No one in their right mind is going to say that a Savage 99 isn’t one of the best lever action rifles of all time. The same goes for the Model 94, Marlin 336 and others.

I think we rally around lever actions because they are the quintessential American rifle. Yes, we make the best bolt-actions in the world, and the same can be argued with other firearms platforms—but the lever action is distinctly ours. Though some are made abroad and imported, the lever action is, and always will be, America’s rifle.

#4220. This tool can recover deleted AI chats from your phone – how it works

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This new tool proves it's still best to limit the info you share with chatbots.

Researchers at Georgia Tech have created a tool that can recover deleted conversations with AI assistants from Android phones. That has potentially promising implications for getting other types of erased information off phones during investigations, for example. But it also means your on-device AI chats, which are supposed to be private, may have a longer shelf life than intended.

Many privacy-conscious AI users prefer to run assistants locally on their devices, because their data isn’t sent out to the cloud, where it could be vulnerable to prying eyes or data breaches. Instead, the chat session uses your device memory.

But, as it turns out, some information from those sessions can stick around in your phone’s working memory even if you’ve deleted the chats themselves. Georgia Tech researchers created ORISA, a tool that searches that memory for clues to deleted conversations. They designed a fake note-keeping AI assistant for their experiment using the ChatterUI Android app, gave it a set of 100 fictional pieces of information about a project, and then deleted that information.

Also: You can run local AI on your Android or iOS device

Using ORISA, the researchers then searched the device memory for remnants of that information that could be used as context to restart the session. ORISA found enough usable information to create a new working session with 26 fictional facts from the original set of 100. That’s more than the 14 the original assistant session had access to, “because the rest had fallen outside its active memory,” the researchers explained.

“It can help establish what information someone shared with an AI assistant and what assistance they received,” Ph.D. student Ken Xu, and lead author of the study, said of the experiment.

ORISA was developed to fill a cybersecurity gap. The researchers noted that a downside of local AI is its lack of “centralized logging and enforcement.” That means bad actors can fine-tune models for crimes, for example, and then make the evidence untraceable by deleting them after they’ve used them.

Until now, that reality has made it difficult for investigators using forensic memory techniques to recover useful evidence of that activity.

“ORISA leverages the batch tokens and key-value (KV) caches preserved in process memory to reconstruct token sequences and restore session-level attention states,” the researchers wrote.

That means a tool like this is most relevant to those trying to stop cybercrime rather than to those digging into your personal data. That said, if hackers recreate a technology like this or get their hands on a tool like ORISA itself, they could theoretically use it to gather deleted information from your chats and recreate your queries using that information as context. But ORISA’s efficacy also depends on time — the researchers noted that it’s harder to recover deleted information as time goes on, because your phone’s memory gets overwritten by newer activity.

Either way, our long-standing advice remains: Don’t share sensitive information with chatbots, even in a local session.

#1912. This Startup Makes Sure That AI Tools Aren’t Out Of Reach For SMBs

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Small and medium-size businesses are often the last to gain access to new marketing technologies.

After all, complex tech is often expensive, and SMBs don’t have the same budgets as Fortune 1000 companies.

Pie, an AI-powered growth platform for SMBs, was built to give smaller businesses access to tools that historically felt out of reach, CEO and Co-Founder Syed Ali told AdExchanger.

Pie emerged from stealth in June, alongside a $19.5M Series A funding round led by Lightspeed Venture Partners. However, it was originally founded in August 2025, when it also raised its seed round.

Both Ali and his co-founder, Akhil Mantripragada, previously worked at restaurant point-of-sale platform Toast, and Ali spent several years before that at Square, another POS company.

Toast and Square both primarily serve SMBs. The pair’s goal was to use their experience to bring “affordable and accessible” AI solutions to those same businesses, Ali said.

Between Toast and Pie, Ali’s resume is starting to read like a menu. (What’s next? Brioche?)

The official reason behind the name reflects the company’s goal to “increase the share of the pie” for small businesses by providing them with affordable marketing resources, Ali said. But, he admitted, the word “pie” also lends itself to “so many bad puns.”

Pie’s clients pay between $300 and $500 monthly, according to Ali – a sharp contrast to the multiple thousands of dollars a larger agency might charge. Pie also fronts the cost of an initial campaign that serves as a free trial. It’s not cheap, Ali said, but, so far, the strategy has paid off.

Before emerging from stealth, Ali said, Pie had already surpassed 1,000 customers thanks to its free trial offer, and then satisfied customers – many of whom hadn’t previously been able to access or afford the latest AI marketing tech – would spread the word.

Pie offers three products: paid advertising, AI search optimization and a “front desk” service that answers phone calls.

The paid ads product feeds transaction data from a brand’s CRM, as well as incoming calls, into Pie’s growth agent. The agent identifies trends, such as what time of day most people call and which types of people most often buy from the brand, and uses that information to determine what ads should say and when to run them.

But as more consumer discovery happens through AI search, running ads isn’t enough on its own. Its AI optimization tool is designed to improve the way that brands show up in AI search.

Most of Pie’s clients are service- and appointment-based businesses, like salons, auto repair shops, gyms and restaurants, so Google and Yelp are the main sources that LLMs pull from for information about them, Ali said.

Pie queries the major LLMs daily, asking which prompts result in a specific brand being mentioned. It also tracks broader query trends so marketers know what questions potential customers are asking. Then, Pie creates AI-generated brand websites that are specifically formatted to be “readable and accessible to crawlers,” said Ali, so LLMs can more easily pick up the brand’s message and mission.

The front desk tool, meanwhile, can take bookings over the phone and answer other routine questions, like inquiries about a business’s hours of operation and parking availability. Pie’s agent can handle roughly 90% of incoming questions, Ali said. But anything that requires judgment or a custom answer, like a large group booking or a question about allergy accommodations, for example, gets transferred to a human.

Since Pie connects to each client’s CRM, it has access to customer data and sees the types of questions customers ask and how the brand responds, said Ali. It also sets up a conversion tracking phone number for when people call via Google or Yelp, he added, and has access to the written transcripts from those calls. Pie trains on all of those sources to develop a voice agent that knows both what customers generally ask and the tone in which the brand responds.

The result, Ali said, is a big-time saver for many SMBs.

Hair salon franchise Supercuts, for instance, doesn’t have separate receptionists; instead, the hairstylists juggle phone calls as well as their styling jobs, Ali said. Since partnering with Pie, Supercuts has saved roughly four hours of talk time per day. The agent takes phone calls and manages bookings, which allows stylists to take on more customers and focus solely on cutting and styling hair.

The deal is the latest sign that media buyers are moving closer to publishers to shorten supply paths and get at the audience data they need to train AI agents.

A history of a planetâs volcanic activity is contained in its rocks. And Marsâ rocks - or at least many of the meteorites that have made the journey from it to Earth - were telling a contradictory story. They appeared to be highly oxidized, but our understanding of the internal volcanic forces on Mars meant that they should have been formed in a reducing environment - the exact opposite of what the chemical makeup of the rocks themselves indicate. Now, a new paper published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth from planetary scientists Hideharu Kuwahara and Ryoichi Nakada of Ehime University provides an answer as to why - and it involves a gemstone.

Thatâs not to say itâd be the type of gemstone that a jeweler would set in a ring. When the planet first formed four and a half billion years ago, its heavy metals were pulled to the center and formed an iron-rich core, similar to that of Earth. When in the presence of pure metal, like a planetary core, chemical reactions usually have oxygen stripped away immediately, creating what is called a reducing environment. More recently, NASAâs InSight mission also confirmed a 150-kilometer deep layer of molten silicate rock directly above the core - what we would typically call a âmantleâ. That is the central contradiction the researchers set out to solve, and they suspected majorite might be involved. On Mars, it forms all the way at the base of its mantle - right above the heavily reducing core - at pressures around 18 gigapascals, or 180,000 times the atmospheric pressure at sea level. So, in order to see how this crystal behaves under those conditions, the authors had to subject it to the same environment.

To do so, they enlisted a massive hydraulic press that squeezed synthetic Martian rock between tungsten-carbide blocks inside tiny diamond capsules. Since the rocks were made of silicates, they would form majorite, a type of silicate crystal, at extremely high pressures and temperatures. So they also cranked the temperature of the system up to around 2,150 to 2,200 degrees Celsius, and saturated it with metallic iron to try to mimic the âreducingâ conditions near the planetâs core.

Once the samples were sufficiently heated and pressurized, the researchers allowed them to cool then took them to the SPring-8 synchrotron facility, about 250 km away. Inside the synchrotron, the samples were blasted with X-rays to explore how the iron was bound into the mineral grains that had formed under the intense heat and pressure.

According to the paper, the majorite acted almost like an iron sponge, gathering plenty of oxidized ferric iron directly into its crystal structure. Between 11% and 21% of the iron locked inside majorite crystals was oxidized, which was a much higher percentage than the surrounding silicates that had also formed during the experiment. And therein lies the answer to the contradiction.

As Marsâ interior began to crystallize, majorite began precipitating out of the molten rock around it. It then captured some of the ferric iron that would have otherwise been dissolved in the reducing basal layer. Eventually, the constant movement of the planetary mantle would have forced those majorite-rich rocks up closer to the surface, sometimes spewing oxidized volcanic rocks onto the surface, and even into space itself, when volcanoes like Olympus Mons exploded.

This research also has implications for Earthâs own internal workings as well. Since Earth is about twice the diameter of Mars, it has a much deeper mantle, which allows a different mineral called bridgmanite to dominate its lower reaches near the core. Scientists already know that bridgmanite can hold vast amounts of iron - even more so than majorite - so Earthâs deep mantle is noticeably chemically stratified. However, as the mantle rises, the bridgmanite is forced to transform into majorite, and thereby release some of its ferric iron into the surrounding rock, essentially oxidizing that transition zone of the mantle from below.

Ultimately this paper ties together the chemical signatures we see in meteorites with how crystals react under pressure in the presence of molten metal. While these two might not seem directly related, a type of gemstone that only forms deep within the mantle within rocky planets provides a link that can last billions of years.

#460. Scientists Made an 80-Year-Old’s Cells Look 19 Years Old. Could It Mean Aging Is Reversible?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

By reprogramming red blood cell precursors into neural stem cells, researchers effectively reset the cell’s molecular clock.

The human body contains more than 200 major cell types, but all of those differing cells—whether blood cells, brain cells, muscle cells, and on and on and on—originate from just one fertilized zygote. In other words, dig deep into any cell’s past and it was once an undifferentiated stem cell.

The trillions of cells that make up the body aren’t terribly unlike the billions of people who make up human civilization. In a perfect society, children would be free to develop into a supportive role that enhances a healthy civilization, and in a similar sense, stem cells also hold this differentiating potential, as they can form virtually every cell in the body. Now, a new study led by scientists at the University of Bonn in Germany finds that if red blood cell precursors undergo some “job retraining” (continuing with our civilization-focused analogy), then, perplexingly, they can also rewind their molecular clock by more than 60 years. The results of the study were published in the journal Aging Cell.

In a previous 2024 study, University of Bonn neurobiologist Oliver Brüstle and his team used transcription factors (proteins that operate “on” and “off” switches for gene expression) to create induced neural stem cells (iNSC) from blood cells and found that they successfully matured within the organism. However, in this new study, Brüstle wanted to focus closer on a strange quirk of these cells.

“In our current study, however, we focused on a different phenomenon: namely, the observation that cells become significantly rejuvenated during reprogramming,” Brüstle, who is senior author of the new paper, said in a press statement. “We also know that this epigenetic rejuvenation ensures that the cells actually behave like young cells.”

Using a molecular clock which tracks DNA modifications that are closely linked to aging, the new study showed that blood cell precursors from an 80-year-old exhibited a molecular age of less than 20 years old after reprogramming. Typically, these cells are reprogrammed by first converting cells into pluripotent stem cells and then developing into iNSCs. This time around, however, authors skipped that middle step and used a virus to deliver two transcription factors. After 12 days, the precursor blood cells began to resemble iNSCs, and by using DNA methylation and RNA sequencing, the team could track aging effects on the cell.

“In contrast, we converted blood cells directly into neural stem cells—that is, without first passing through the pluripotent cell stage,” Brüstle said in a press statement. “In our approach, rejuvenation occurred gradually and could be tracked for over 100 days.”

This slow rejuvenation process is key because in the typical, two-step process, changes occur very quickly, but this longer period of rejuvenation allows scientists to investigate what exactly drives these changes. Using that benefit, the authors tracked the cells getting progressively younger until they reached roughly 60 years younger across just 50 days. As Science Alert notes, the cells also acted their (youthful) age by displaying less mitochondrial toxicity, and their de-aging occurred even when cell proliferation was inhibited. And it doesn’t take an adventurous imagination to see how turning old cells into young neural cells could have huge benefits, especially as many neurodegenerative diseases are linked to aging.

It might be true that you can’t teach an old dog new tricks, but that long-held maxim doesn’t quite hold up in the cellular world.

#4225. In the Age of Borrowed Thinking, How To Become More Valuable as AI Gets Smarter

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Publish AI, ML & data-science insights to a global community of data professionals.

A framework for staying relevant in the age of artificial intelligence

I was recently reading about the evolution of cognition that lets humans make decisions through the history of time. In my understanding of the eras we, humans, have lived through, the definition of intelligence has changed throughout history.

If we look back at the Renaissance era, an intelligent person was someone capable of reason, judgment, moral reflection, eloquent communication, and meaningful contribution to society. Intelligence was synonymous with the cultivation of human potential.

In the Industrial Age, intelligence became associated with specialization, expertise, and efficiency. Then came the Information Age, where intelligence increasingly became linked to access to knowledge.

Humans have always looked for ways to extend their capabilities beyond the limits of their own minds. Sometimes I think the earliest seed of AI may have been planted with something as simple as the calculator. Someone must have wondered:

If a machine can perform arithmetic, can it perform reasoning?

As computers became more powerful, researchers began exploring whether intelligence itself could be recreated by encoding human reasoning into machines.

At first, they tried teaching machines everything. The real breakthrough came when machines started learning from data instead. Rather than being told every rule, they began discovering patterns on their own.

Today, we live in a world where artificial intelligence exists because humans want to remove friction from thinking.

Which often makes me wonder: why are humans so obsessed with creating intelligence outside themselves?

Perhaps because intelligence has always been humanity's greatest advantage.

Learn this step by step with the interactive AI Engineer roadmap.

We live in an age where social media tells us what we need to like, dislike or even hate. Influencers tell us what success should look like, how to impress your boss, what to wear, what to buy, where to go, you know it! On top of it, AI agents are increasingly helping us decide what to learn, how to work, and even what to think about.

Today, a person can generate a strategy, create a presentation, draft an article, summarize a book, analyze data, or formulate an opinion in seconds.

The distance between question and answer has nearly disappeared.

Yet in that process, something else seems to be disappearing: the habit of thinking.

AI was created to extend human intelligence but now it feels like because AI exists around us, we have limited ourselves from developing our own intelligence.

This week, my organization is teaching us how to prompt in Copilot like a pro as different personas in the analytics and AI space, bringing the AI results together, drafting a story that AI has given you, etc. and as I was hearing these sessions, I realized that we live in a world flooded with answers. We have a minimum of three different answers for one question (INSANE, I know!) So then what answer would you choose?

I think judgment becomes the ultimate competitive advantage here.

With the evolution of AI we’re seeing, I think we could very well go back to the Renaissance age where intelligence was never just about having answers. It was about judgment, wisdom, and the ability to think independently.

That realization led me to develop the Human Relevance Framework™.

I arrived at this framework (which is uniquely a brain child of me) based on six soft skills or capabilities that I found to be the most valuable to practice and exercise as technology becomes more capable.

My hope is simple: to help people continue to challenge their cognition, become better decision-makers, create original insights, and remain relevant in an AI-powered world.

We all have gradually outsourced more thinking to AI, even more than we realize.

I started using AI at work to help debug code faster for my problem rather than scrolling StackOverflow pages. That usage has now evolved to using AI as my first analyst; it looks at the data before I do, determine the quality of that data, helps write code, troubleshoot problems, and accelerate work that once took hours.

I say no because I noticed that I was spending less time wrestling with the problem statement. I cared less about the time I previously spent exploring the different alternatives, wondering what would happen if I looked at the problem differently.

My curiosity has quietly moved from being the engine of discovery to being an optional step.

In an AI world where answers are abundant (regardless of its accuracy), curiosity makes you interesting. With the skills we have as a data professional, the hard skills will very well be taken care of by AI so then how do we stay relevant and in jobs? Be the one to ask questions nobody else thought to ask.

Practical Habit: Before asking AI a question, spend five minutes generating three different ways to frame the problem. Often the breakthrough comes from framing the question, not the answer.

If your curiosity has generated multiple options in your head, use your judgment to evaluate these options and then make a choice among them.

My grandfather used to tell me this whenever I asked him questions anywhere about “what should I do?”:"I can recommend actions, but only you can evaluate the context, risks, trade-offs, and consequences. I'm just a signpost. Whether you take the next turn is your judgment."

I never imagined how important and relevant that advice would become.

AI can generate responses endlessly, in seconds. But no workplace allows for AI to carry the responsibility for the outcome yet. As leaders, analysts, and professionals, our value increasingly comes from making sound decisions when the answer isn't obvious.

Practical Habit: Whenever AI gives you a recommendation, force yourself to document one reason to follow it and one reason not to. This strengthens independent evaluation.

As I talk to people in and outside my workplace whose career path I admire, majority of them say that one of the most valued skills in analytics is the ability to connect seemingly unrelated ideas.

We know that data, business context, stakeholder expectations and industry trends are rarely a neat package. It is the analytics & reporting teams who must connect the dots.

Information alone cannot create a meaningful actionable insight. Insights can be shared with the leaders only when we, the data analysts, connect the dots in the data we have to the context, experience, and consequences.

Practical Habit: After every project, ask yourself: What is the story connecting these facts? Don't stop at reporting information. Explain why it matters.

With the amount of information we get from AI today, the challenge today is no longer accessing information. The challenge is filtering it.

As we keep asking AI to clean up our data, identify the patterns, generate hypotheses, create compelling narratives from the analysis, we create more content, more data, and more opinions. While AI makes all of it very easy and quick to generate, it is also very easy that the “insight” could very well be noise.

Workplaces using AI all advise to use discernment and I say the same to build a competitive advantage, to be able to separate:

Practical Habit: Whenever a hypothesis sounds convincing, ask: What evidence would change my mind? If you can't answer that question, you're likely evaluating confidence rather than truth.

Early in my career, my mentor advised me to carve out a niche for myself, become a trust agent in that and ensure that my personal brand communicates that. Over the years, with much work, I believe that my strength isn’t simply analyzing data. It is creating new ways of thinking about complex problems.

My leader often says that creation requires courage. Most people spend their careers reacting to what others have already produced. Only a few create something genuinely new.

I love to create things that I can call my own product and now AI can remix existing knowledge at extraordinary speed. That's why originality becomes more valuable as AI becomes more capable.

For me, breakthrough ideas often come after I have applied all my experience, intuition, and imagination to experiment with different hypotheses (and when I have exhausted all of it, I take a coffee break and it all magically works!)

Practical Habit: Create one thing each week that didn't exist before: a framework, perspective, visual, workflow, or idea. Train the habit of building, not just responding.

Of them all, reflection is the most valuable skill to have in my honest opinion.

As data & analytics professionals, we should be used to reflecting as we examine our data, models, outcomes and adjust when new information emerges. It is the same reflecting but on your own thinking instead.

Reflection creates self-awareness. Once you have self-awareness, it improves your judgment. Improved judgment produces better decisions and people who cannot challenge their own beliefs stop growing.

As data professionals, I strongly believe and encourage to regularly examine and reflect on our assumptions, skills, and toolkit to remain adaptable because in the days to come, adaptability is relevance.

Practical Habit: Keep a simple "changed my mind" journal. Once a month, write down one belief, assumption, or approach you've updated because you learned something new.

If you could do one thing today, ask yourself:

Where are you currently outsourcing your thinking?

How often do you formulate the question before seeking the answer?

Can you recognize a bad answer from a convincing one?

How much original synthesis do you produce?

When was the last time you changed your mind because you learned something new?

What do you know that isn't easily searchable?

What do people come to you for that isn't contained in your job description?

These are some questions I collected to think myself from hearing them at a conference, meetup or instructor-led session. When I answered these questions, I had a good understanding if I was developing capabilities that technology enhances or capabilities that technology replaces and my hope is that you get a similar understanding.

We've seen technology change the way people work many times before. But AI feels different to me maybe because it isn't just taking over tasks. It's starting to take on parts of the thinking behind those tasks.

To me, that is the real challenge. The future isn't simply about humans learning to work with AI. We already work well with AI but for when AI will work for us, we better prepare ourselves to not hand over the very thinking that makes us valuable.

Because in the age of borrowed thinking, the most valuable skill may simply be thinking for yourself.
