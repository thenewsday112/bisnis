#592. From 54% to 100%: Scientists Reveal a Potential Breakthrough in Quantum Technology

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Scientists have proposed a surprisingly simple way to improve photon absorption using specially engineered waveguides, potentially making future quantum technologies far more efficient.

Quantum technologies depend on a process that remains surprisingly unreliable: transferring a single photon between two quantum bits, or qubits. With conventional methods, the receiving qubit successfully absorbs the photon only about 54 percent of the time. This inefficiency creates a major obstacle for quantum computers, secure quantum communication, and future quantum networks.

Researchers at TU Wien have proposed a way to overcome this limitation by changing the shape of a photon’s wave as it travels between qubits. Their calculations and computer simulations suggest that the method could achieve 100 percent absorption under ideal conditions.

Two qubits (two atoms, for example) can exchange quantum information when one emits a photon and the other absorbs it. But photons do not behave simply like tiny particles moving between two points. Their wave properties determine how efficiently they can be transferred.

“When a qubit emits a photon, you must not picture the photon as a tiny particle that is simply shot out,” says Dr. Zeyu Kuang from the Institute of Theoretical Physics at TU Wien. “The photon is a wave, and a wave has a certain shape and a certain extension.”

The researchers compare photon emission to striking a bell. The sound begins strongly and gradually fades, much like the wave emitted by a qubit. Unfortunately, this waveform is poorly suited to absorption by another qubit. A pulse that gradually increases in strength before reaching its peak would work much better.

The solution relies on time-reversal symmetry, a fundamental principle of quantum mechanics. Under ideal conditions, a quantum process can run in reverse, meaning a photon emitted with a particular waveform can be perfectly absorbed if that waveform is reversed in time.

“This follows from time-reversal symmetry in quantum mechanics,” Oliver Diekmann explains. “Under ideal conditions, quantum dynamics are reversible. If a qubit perfectly emits a photon with a particular waveform, the time-reversed process tells us which waveform that qubit can absorb perfectly.”

To achieve this reversal, the researchers propose using a specially engineered optical waveguide. Unlike light traveling through a vacuum, different frequency components of light inside a waveguide can move at different speeds. This behavior, known as dispersion, can be controlled to reshape the photon pulse during transmission.

By adjusting the waveguide’s dispersion, the team calculated that an emitted photon could arrive at the receiving qubit with precisely the waveform needed for optimal absorption. Under ideal conditions, the theoretical success rate could reach 100 percent.

“We calculated how this goal can be achieved and simulated the process on a computer,” says Prof. Carlos Gonzalez-Ballestero. “Our results indicate that the required setup should be technically feasible. This passive approach could significantly improve the absorption of photons by qubits and thereby increase the efficiency of many quantum technologies.”

Although the approach has not yet been demonstrated experimentally, the simulations suggest that carefully designed waveguides could make photon transfer substantially more reliable, potentially improving the performance of future quantum computers and communication networks.

Reference: “Passive Quantum State Transfer in a Dispersion-Engineered Waveguide” by Zeyu Kuang, Oliver Diekmann, Lorenz Fischer, Stefan Rotter and Carlos Gonzalez-Ballestero, 4 September 2026, Physical Review Letters. DOI: 10.1103/m2md-rxkv

InfoQ Homepage News How Anthropic Redesigned Source Control for AI-Driven Development

Oct 27, 2026 2 min read

Anthropic recently detailed how it migrated its monorepo from Git to Scion. Scion is a source control system that moves repository operations to the server side and uses content-addressed storage and tiered caches for agent and CI workloads. The design sacrifices offline independence for less local data and shared infrastructure.

In its 13 October engineering post, Anthropic reported that daily merged changes rose from around 2,500 in May to more than 30,000 in September. Its CI systems perform approximately 25 million checkouts daily, against a monorepo with nearly 100 GB of main-branch history and a compressed working copy exceeding 25 GB. The company had used shallow clones, sparse checkouts, promisor remotes, repository snapshots and several hundred Git mirrors, but said these became harder to operate as merge rates increased.

Daily merged changes in Anthropic’s monorepo from January to September 2026. (Source)

Scion puts commits, merges, rebases, history and blame queries on the server. Clients maintain bounded object caches and virtualised checkouts materialise files on demand. Tools can query diffs and perform some writes through APIs without checking out code.

A Scion changeset records the files that have changed relative to its first parent, with content-addressed pointers to the compressed file contents in blob storage. Creating changesets is separate from updating references, so accepting changesets requires no cross-server coordination. For history and blame queries, Scion builds additional structures called "derived data", computed recursively from the parent changesets, either eagerly or on demand. This server-side implementation lets derived data evolve without changing clients.

Scion changesets record changed files and references to their contents; tree structures are derived separately by the server. (Source)

For checkouts, clients ask for the missing immutable trees and blobs rather than negotiating a custom packfile. A node-local daemon shares cached objects across jobs, and fetches from regional caches, which in turn fetch from the authoritative servers (backed by Spanner and cloud storage). The regional caches stream the objects in the daemon's storage format so there's no per-request delta compression.

The tiers expose the same checkout-protocol RPCs, but Anthropic enforces their ordering to isolate failures and prevent a regional cache failure from overwhelming authoritative servers. Sparse and virtualised checkouts only request the objects that they need.

Anthropic reports cache-hit rates above 99% at each tier, a CPU and memory infrastructure footprint 200 times smaller than its previous Git mirrors, and checkout speedups of two orders of magnitude for CI and research jobs. These are company-reported results for its internal workload, not general benchmarks against Git.

InfoQ previously covered Uber’s GitFarm, which centralises standard Git operations without replacing the source-control system. Scion goes further by changing the underlying data model. Its design also builds on established work: in a 15 October update, Anthropic explicitly credited Mononoke and its Bonsai data model with pioneering many of the techniques used.

Anthropic cautions that Scion is not a general-purpose Git replacement. It requires its own code-review, code-search and CI systems, and the company’s implementations are highly bespoke. Instead, it recommends bringing selected ideas, including comprehensive server-side APIs and improved fetch protocols, into existing Git platforms.

For architects, the key takeaway is to reconsider where work happens as workloads change. Repository queries don't necessarily need local checkouts, and immutable objects can be shared via caches. This can reduce redundant work, but Scion depends on server availability, and the required integration is substantial.

The Department of Justice announced today that it has entered into a non-prosecution agreement with Extreme Networks, Inc.  The company agreed to pay criminal penalties totaling $975,721.84 to resolve the Department’s investigation of sanctions and export control offenses.

The Department reached the agreement under its 2026 Corporate Enforcement and Voluntary Self-Disclosure Policy (“the Policy”), which incentivizes companies to voluntarily disclose discovered misconduct, cooperate with Department investigations, and timely and appropriately remediate the wrongdoing.

This agreement with Extreme Networks marks the Department’s first non-prosecution agreement in a “near-miss” case under the Policy, meaning a case that involved full cooperation and appropriate remediation but did not qualify for a declination of prosecution.  While Extreme Networks’ disclosure to the National Security Division (NSD) was not timely because it occurred under imminent threat of disclosure by a news publication, Extreme Networks nonetheless fully cooperated with the Department’s investigation and timely and appropriately remediated the misconduct. As a result, its disclosure qualified as a “Near Miss” Voluntary Self-Disclosure under the Policy.

“This non-prosecution agreement and million-dollar criminal penalty mark the successful resolution of our investigation into the illegal sale of advanced networking equipment and services to a Russian weapons manufacturer, in clear violation of U.S. sanctions and export controls,” said Assistant Director Roman Rozhavsky of the FBI’s Counterintelligence and Espionage Division. “The FBI remains dedicated to keeping sensitive technology out of the hands of the Russian military, and we're grateful to Homeland Security Investigations and the Bureau of Industry and Security for their partnership on this case. Let this announcement serve as a clear warning: if you attempt to arm our adversaries by violating U.S. law, justice will find you.”

“HSI remains committed to investigating companies and individuals who evade U.S. export controls and sanctions to provide goods or services to foreign weapons manufacturers,” said Acting Special Agent in Charge Jeremy Pierczynski of Homeland Security Investigations (HSI) Detroit. “These laws protect our national security and prevent U.S.-origin products and services from supporting adversaries’ military capabilities. HSI will continue working with our law enforcement and regulatory partners to identify and hold accountable those who undermine these critical safeguards.”

The agreement arose out of an investigation into Extreme Networks’ sales of products and services to Moscow Machinery Building Plant Avangard (MMZA), a Russian weapons manufacturer that was owned by JSC Concern Almaz-Antey (Almaz Antey), which was added to the Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons List by the Department of the Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) in July 2014.

Pursuant to the non-prosecution agreement, Extreme Networks will forfeit $487,860.92 in proceeds from the sanctions and export control offenses and pay a criminal fine of $487,860.92, for a total criminal penalty of $975,721.84. Extreme Networks will also resolve a parallel administrative action by OFAC.

Between around March 2018 and March 2022, Extreme Networks, through its then-Russia based subsidiary, Extreme Networks Rus LLC, processed and fulfilled 11 purchase orders for networking equipment and services to MMZA without the required license or authorization from OFAC. MMZA was a primary producer of the S-300 and S-400 anti-aircraft missile systems used by the Russian army. To facilitate these sales, certain Russia-based Extreme Networks employees concealed the identity of MMZA from Extreme Networks’ global trade compliance group, causing Extreme Networks to file false electronic export information via the Automated Export System and smuggle goods from the United States. Through this scheme, Extreme Networks deprived the U.S. government of its ability to carry out its lawful function of enforcing sanctions and export controls designed to protect national security and economic interests. As a result of its unlawful conduct, Extreme Networks earned approximately $487,860.92 in revenue from the sales of goods and services.

Although Extreme Networks’ self-disclosure to NSD was not timely pursuant to the Policy, Extreme Networks nonetheless cooperated with the Department’s investigation by timely providing relevant facts and documents and making employees available for interviews. Extreme Networks also appropriately remediated the misconduct by terminating its contract with the third-party distributor it used to facilitate these sales, improving screening procedures, hiring additional compliance personnel, and implementing diligence procedures in connection with mergers and acquisitions. Given these steps and the lack of aggravating circumstances, the Department agreed to enter into a non-prosecution agreement.

The FBI Charlotte Field Office, the Department of Commerce’s Bureau of Industry and Security, and Homeland Security Investigations investigated the case.

This case was prosecuted by former Trial Attorney Maria Fedor of the National Security Division’s Counterintelligence and Export Control Section and Assistant U.S. Attorney Adam Hulbig for the Eastern District of North Carolina.

Videographers Claim ASAP Rocky Used AI to Rip Off Their Work

ASAP Rocky has been accused of using AI to directly steal the work of two video artists and a parkour athlete for his latest music video.

The video for Shroom Clouds shows a brief sequence (about 2:56) where the rapper is somersaulting through a kaleidoscopic effect.

But that six-second segment is identical to a 2019 Instagram video that was made by creative director and VFX artist Cache Bunny.

The striking similarity was pointed out by Emonee LaRussa (@em0n33y on Instagram). “There is practically no difference: the grass is the same, the shadows are the same,” says LaRussa, who asks, “At what point does AI-generated content cross the line into copyright infringement?”

A post shared by Emonee LaRussa (@em0n33y)

The original clip is actual footage of parkour athlete Jason Paul that was filmed on a 360-degree camera and later transformed into artistic footage.

“I was quite surprised to see how similar his version was,” Paul says of ASAP’s video. “When I thought about AI stealing my job one day, I was worried about those damn Boston Dynamics robots that keep getting better at parkour, not rappers.”

Paul explains that he made the video with Cache Bunny and an L.A.-based video artist called Ari Fararooy seven years ago.

“We found ourselves on a football field with a little monopod and a 360 cam,” Paul says. “Ari and Cache Bunny had a bunch of concepts ready for me, and I added my unique spin of action to it. We took an afternoon to shoot this, and then Cache Bunny worked her VFX magic and the result blew me away.”

“I ask myself: how could this happen? And who can I be mad at? Like, I’m not mad at ASAP Rocky. I love the dude’s music. We even used it for one of our videos before,” he adds. “I’m sure he got his share of pay for clicks on that. It’d be nice if it went the other way.”

“I’m just thinking, okay, it’s not ASAP Rocky’s fault, it’s probably not his label’s fault; they don’t know every clip that was uploaded to Instagram and can spot a copy right away. Who can? But somebody along the line took that clip and thought it was fine to leave it that way.”

Paul says that he even asked the director of Shroom Clouds, who told him that he shot the music video five years ago and it’s “out of my hands.”

Fans of ASAP Rocky were disappointed to see that listed among the music video credits were two AI artists.

Paul says he is considering his options, while Cache Bunny says that she doesn’t know what to do, but wrote on her Instagram story that “stealing artists’ work is not ok, and that’s not the precedent we want to set.”

“I can’t understand why they didn’t just reach out to license the clip, or make their own version inspired by it,” she adds.

ASAP Rocky recently shared his thoughts about AI with Complex magazine, in which he suggested he eschews using AI to create music from a prompt but doesn’t judge others who do.

#417. Low testosterone may make sugar far more harmful to your liver

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Low testosterone can contribute to several health problems, and an unhealthy diet may make some of those risks even worse. One major concern is metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), a condition that affects approximately 40% of adult men worldwide and has become a significant global health issue.

Fatty liver, the earliest stage of MASLD, occurs when excess fat builds up in the liver. Several factors are associated with the condition, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, low testosterone levels, and excessive fructose consumption from sugary drinks and processed foods. However, scientists have not fully understood how these factors interact or whether their combined effects are more harmful than each one individually.

How Low Testosterone and Fructose Affect the Liver

To investigate this connection, a research team from the Graduate School of Agriculture, led by graduate student Hiroki Takahashi and Associate Professor Naoki Harada, examined how reduced testosterone and high fructose intake influence liver health.

The researchers used eight-week-old male mice and divided them into six experimental groups. Some underwent castration to lower testosterone levels, while others received a sham operation. The groups included Sham/Control, Sham/Fructose, Sham/Fructose + Antibiotics, Castration/Control, Castration/Fructose, and Castration/Fructose + Antibiotics.

To determine how these conditions affected the animals, the team analyzed liver cells and tissues, blood plasma, organic acids from the cecum, and samples of gut bacteria.

The results revealed that mice with reduced testosterone levels developed heavier livers when they consumed fructose. However, this increase in liver weight was smaller in mice that also received antibiotics.

When examined separately, low testosterone and fructose intake produced only modest changes in liver triglyceride levels. When combined, however, the two factors had a much stronger effect, significantly increasing fat accumulation and worsening fatty liver.

The researchers also identified changes in the composition of gut bacteria, altered liver gene activity, and elevated levels of pyruvate in the cecum among mice exposed to both reduced testosterone and fructose.

These findings suggested that the gut microbiome could play an important role in explaining why the combination was particularly harmful.

Further experiments helped the researchers identify a possible biological explanation for the increased liver fat.

"Upon examining this mechanism, we found that changes in the gut microbiota led to increased levels of pyruvate within the intestine. Furthermore, experiments using mouse-derived primary hepatocytes revealed that pyruvate acts synergistically with fructose to promote neutral lipid accumulation in hepatocytes," explained Takahashi.

In other words, changes in gut bacteria appeared to increase intestinal pyruvate, a compound involved in energy metabolism. Laboratory experiments with liver cells isolated from mice showed that pyruvate and fructose worked together to increase fat storage in those cells.

The discovery suggests that interactions among testosterone levels, dietary fructose, and gut bacteria may help explain how fatty liver develops and worsens.

Potential New Ways to Prevent Fatty Liver Disease

Understanding this relationship could eventually help researchers identify new approaches to preventing or treating fatty liver disease. In particular, targeting the processes through which pyruvate contributes to fat accumulation may offer promising possibilities.

"Going forward, we hope to clarify the mechanism by which pyruvate promotes triglyceride accumulation to pioneer the development of therapeutic drugs and establishment of preventive methods through dietary interventions," added Professor Harada.

Although the findings come from experiments in mice and isolated mouse liver cells, they provide new insight into how hormonal changes and diet may interact to influence liver health.

The study was published in American Journal of Physiology-Endocrinology and Metabolism.

A knife is perhaps the most useful tool for anyone who loves the outdoors, and learning how to sharpen a knife is an important skill that will take time to perfect. But with some practice and concentration, anyone can learn to keep their knives sharp and ready for the next cutting task. Through years of experience in my knife sharpening business, I’ve developed a system for quickly getting a sharp edge that I’ll share with you in this article. I’m going to give you a step-by-step guide for two methods of knife sharpening: First, with a traditional sharpening stone. Second, with sandpaper and a mousepad. Yes, you read that correctly, a mouse pad sharpener.

There are many great knife sharpeners on the market that are easy to use and effective. But, learning how to sharpen a knife with a stone is a skill everyone should know. Now, there are many options out there for stones which range from cheap synthetic stones that wear out with one or two uses, all the way to high-end Japanese water stones that cost hundreds for each grit. For this knife sharpening tutorial, I’ll be using an inexpensive, but effective, medium natural Arkansas stone that I’ve had for years.

I like to use water on my stones, so I begin by submerging my stone in a Tupperware container filled with water while I prepare everything else. It’s not a good idea to use a stone dry because the metal shavings will “clog up” the stone and make it ineffective. During the sharpening process you may rinse the stone and soak it again. Too much water being drizzled on the stone during the process won’t be a problem, but too little can be. Make sure your stone has plenty of water on it during the entire process.

Take your marker and color the sharpening bevel. This will allow you to see where the stone is contacting the edge so that you don’t go too shallow or too deep. When the marker is gone, you’ll know that you’ve gotten to the apex of the edge.

Hold your knife close to the blade and lay it on the stone with the edge facing you. Place the fingers of your free hand on the spine (back/non-sharpened) of the knife and let the tips hang over and rest on the stone. Keeping your fingers on the same spot and letting them barely drag across the stone will help you keep a consistent angle until you really get the feel for it.

Hold the knife’s spine off the stone at about a 20-degree angle. Only the edge should touch as you make a light pass starting from the knife’s tip then moving across and away on the stone until the heel touches the stone.

Then check where the marker is worn off. If only a narrow line is worn off right at the very edge, your angle was too steep, and you need to lay the blade slightly lower to the stone. If the marker is only worn on the other side of the bevel, you were too shallow, and you need to bring the blade a little higher off the stone. Make a few passes until you find the sweet spot where the entire edge is contacting the stone, then go to Step 4.

Tip: Finding and maintaining a consistent angle is the most difficult part of sharpening with a stone. An angle guide is a handy accessory to buy with your sharpening stone. The guide will keep your angle consistent and act as training wheels as you get used to freehand sharpening.

Now that you have your angle, make passes on the stone until you feel a burr on the edge. Carefully (and lightly) “wipe” your thumb or finger across the edge. Make sure the motion is perpendicular to the edge, not parallel to it. One will allow you to feel the burr, and one will likely slice you.

It should feel “rough” going from one direction and smoother from the opposite direction. Sometimes you can see the burr. It may just look like a micro-bevel that will catch the light, but it’s a tiny “flap” of metal that has been folded over the edge.

When the whole edge has a burr, you can go back to Step 3, and do the same thing on the other side. (When you raise the burr on the other side, the burr may begin to come off and look like a super fine piece of wire as it detaches from the edge.) For most people, the hardest thing about this process is switching hands with the knife. Training your non-dominant hand may take some time. If you can’t get the hang of switching hands, you can flip the knife over so that the edge is facing away from you, and keep it in the same hand. But this will require that you learn the motion backwards. Either way works fine as long as you keep the angle consistent.

After you raise a burr on each side of the bevel, you will want to refine the edge. Make alternating passes on the stone, always with the blade’s edge trailing the spine. I usually do ten passes on the left, then ten on the right, then five, three, two, and one pass on each side. After that, I’ll check the sharpness by slicing a piece of paper. If it goes through it cleanly, then you’re all done. If there are some hang-ups or it won’t slice well, it may need some more refining, or you may not have raised the burr correctly.

Sharpening with a stone will definitely take some practice, and you will mess up more than once. But this is a great skill to develop that will absolutely be worth the time it takes to learn. One last note on using stones: Some people will progress through several stones to go finer and finer. If you do that, just raise a burr on both sides on each stone until your final stone. When the final grit is reached, you can raise your burrs, then refine the edge. Don’t bother refining on any stones other than your final grit.

Sharpening a knife with this sandpaper method will give you a convex edge on your knife. The good news is learning how to sharpen a knife will be easier since you don’t have to keep the angle perfect the entire time. As for the Sandpaper, I recommend the automotive wet/dry type. You can buy them in variety packs with a range of grits that will suit sharpening quite well.

Cut your scrap of wood to 3- to 4-inches wide, and as long as your mousepad. Cut the mousepad to fit the width of the wood and glue the fabric side (the surface the mouse moves on) to the wood. You can do this to both sides of the wood so it won’t slide around on your table. Having the rubber side out helps hold the sandpaper in place. I have used a general spray adhesive and had good results.

Just as with the sharpening stone method, I recommend using a permanent marker to mark the edge bevel on your knife. Color the whole edge bevel so that you can easily see where the paper is removing material. This will help you determine the correct angle in the next step.

The angle is slightly less important with a convex edge because the edge has a curve to it due to the flex in the mousepad base. Even if you are off by a couple of degrees, the pad’s give will cover your mistakes. Lay a piece of 600-grit paper on the block, and hold it in place with your fingertips at the bottom. Hold the knife with the edge facing you and at a 15- to 20-degree angle off the paper. Starting with the tip at the bottom corner, push the knife away from you while sweeping towards the opposite upper corner of the block. Now look at your marker to see where you’re removing material. The same adjustments apply here that I described in the sharpening stone method.

Just as with the stone method, once you find your angle, you will make passes on one side until you raise a burr, then switch sides and raise a burr again. Don’t start with any grit lower than 320 unless you have deep chips or rolls in the blade. The lower grits will remove material quite fast, and you can mess up your blade if you aren’t careful. Also, you don’t need a lot of pressure for this method. Too much will cause a lot of deformation to the pad, and make your convex edge too steep. A lighter touch works best. Remember to be careful with your fingers that hold the paper.

Once you have raised a burr on both sides, you can change the grit and begin to refine your edge. Take the next grit and do ten passes per side (or until you have removed the scratches from the previous grit), then move up and do the same until you’ve reached your final grit, then do ten strokes on each side, and reduce the number as described in the stone method. If you go up to 2000 grit, you will start to get a mirror-polished finish on your blade. If you are diligent about maintaining your angle and hitting the apex, you will have a very sharp knife by the time you reach 2000+ grit. You can get grits of 6000 and above, which will really polish and refine your edge and I can easily get an edge that will whittle hair with this method.

Remember that using this method always requires the edge to be trailing the spine. You can’t make passes where the edge is going into the paper. That can work on a stone, but not here. Also, the paper can be taped to the edges of the wood so that you don’t have to hold it with your off-hand. I generally just hold the paper myself, but you just have to stay aware of where the blade and your fingers are. Although this method is more forgiving than a stone, a consistent angle will yield the best results.

This method should really be called “How to maintain and touch up a knife with a honing rod,” because a honing rod is best for, well, honing an edge. Whether the honing rod is metal (think butcher shop) or ceramic, it’s designed for keeping an edge sharp. They are best when used frequently, between uses of the knife. There are better options for sharpening a dull knife with a damaged edge.

Honing rods can be handheld, or mounted in a tabletop holder. If you’re new to sharpening, or not very confident with holding the knife and hone at the same time, it’s best to go with a tabletop version.

As you make a pass on the hone, you will start off very shallow and gradually increase the angle until you feel the edge just begin to “bite.” This means you have reached the apex, and you will actually be refining the edge, not the material behind it. Repeat this for the other side of the knife, and take note. Keeping the angle consistent is very important.

Once you have found the correct angle, make alternating strokes on either side of the blade. Be sure that as you make your stroke, you are beginning at the heel of the blade, and finishing at the tip. For a handheld hone, your stroke will begin at the far end of the hone, with the heel of the edge, and as you move toward the handle of the hone, you will be pulling the blade back to get to the tip. The motion will be down and outward. Then bring your blade back up, cant it the opposite way, and make a stroke the same way for the other side. Take your time and move slowly. With experience will come speed. For a horizontal table mounted hone, the motion will be very similar. Away from you and outward, heel to tip, then flip the knife so the edge is pointing toward you, and make a stroke toward you from heel to tip.

If you are finding that your blade has lost some sharpness, you may have gone too steep with your angle, or you may be “rolling” your wrist during the motion, which changes the angle. If you have gone too shallow with your angle, there will be no change to the sharpness, because you weren’t reaching the apex of the edge. If you aren’t getting the results you want, try making small changes to the angle you’re using. If you aren’t sure which way to go with the angle, color the edge with a marker, and make a few strokes. You will see where the marker is worn off, and that will let you know how to change the angle. If you have chips or damage to the edge, you may need to use a stone or other method before honing.

Work Sharp makes a wide variety of sharpeners, but this will cover how to sharpen with one of their belt style sharpeners. Using the Work Sharp will give you a convex edge. This is a great way to go as long as you get your technique down. When done properly, it’s not hard to get a near mirror finish on your edge with one of these versatile tools.

Depending on the model you have, you will be able to select from a few different preset guides that will determine the angle of your edge. For thinner knives, like kitchen or fillet knives, choose a lower angle in the 15- to 18-degree range. For pocket knives and general use blades, 20 degrees is my favorite. If you plan on using a bigger blade for heavy chopping or just want a really strong edge, 22- to 25-degrees will work well. Select the guide that will suit you best and clip it on, or rotate it into place, depending on the model.

Is your blade still pretty sharp, but just needs a touch up? A fine grit belt should be your choice. Do you have some small nicks in the edge? Maybe a slightly blunted tip? Start with a medium belt. Does the edge look like you chop rocks for fun? Start with the coarse belt to get those repairs done more quickly. Wherever you start, you will progress through the belts getting finer and finer until you finish.

Turn your Work Sharp on, and if there are different speeds available, select the lowest speed while you are learning. Going too fast, especially with a coarse belt, can remove a lot of material in a hurry, and end up making a mess of your knife. Choose a side of the belt, set your blade against the angle guide, and lower the knife until it contacts the belt. Starting at the heel, pull the knife back toward you, and as you get toward the tip, raise the back of the knife up to keep the belly of the blade parallel to the ground. Be careful though—raising the back too high can result in rounding the tip of the knife. Avoid putting more pressure near the tip than you do at the heel. Go lightly, and let the belt do the work.

It may take a few passes on the same side before you raise a burr. You will be able to feel it with a fingernail, and usually see it on the edge. Once the whole edge has a burr, you’re ready to raise one on the other side of the edge. Repeat Step 3 on the other side of the edge until you have raised a burr.

Once you have raised a burr on both sides of the edge, you can change the belt to the next grit higher (finer). Begin alternating strokes, one per side. Make several passes on each side until you begin to see the scratch pattern from the previous grit disappear. When all you see are the finer scratches, move to the next belt, and repeat. By the time you are done making these alternating passes, and you have worked through the grits, you should have one very sharp knife, with a durable convex edge.

Stropping a knife is the final step in really getting your blade super sharp, and it’s also a great way to maintain your edge between sharpenings. Most strops are made of leather, but there are some cotton and even paper strops available, too. Since a wood block mounted leather is the most common, and, in my opinion, the most effective strop, here’s how to use one to get the most out of your edge.

There are many different compounds available for strops. Like sandpaper, they are rated by their grit. When you buy a leather strop, it’s usually bare, and has no polishing compound in it. You will need to “load” the strop before you use it. There are tons of choices ranging from paste, to bars of compound, to diamond emulsion sprays to load your strop. I generally use the bars, and rub each strop with a different compound from coarsest to finest. You don’t need multiple strops, but most have two sides for a coarse and fine compound. Load your strop with some compound by rubbing it in, or spraying it on, then grab your knife and get ready.

While it’s fine to use a sharpening stone with the edge “biting” into it, strops can only be used with the edge trailing in your stroke. Otherwise, you end up with a sliced up piece of leather and a duller knife. Start with the edge facing you and the heel of the blade fully on the strop. Push the knife away from you and move it outward so that you finish your pass with just the last inch or so of the knife on the strop. Be careful not to roll your wrist as you do this because it will increase the angle and dull the end of the blade. Keep the angle the same as how you sharpened it. After your first pass, lay the blade flat on the strop, roll it over the spine of the blade so that the other side of the edge is now on the strop. Then make a pass with the knife moving toward you, with the edge trailing behind. Be sure to move from heel to tip as you make the pass. This will keep the tip from biting into the strop and damaging it.

When you figure out the motion of stropping, and you see how it takes your edge to the next level, you may get addicted to it. I like to listen to podcasts while stropping my knives. Take your time, and make alternating passes, back and forth and back and forth. You will begin to see that edge really shine, and you can get a near perfect mirror polish on there with good compound, and a little elbow grease. Don’t use too much pressure, maybe slightly more than you do when you’re writing. Let the compound do the work, and enjoy the results. Remember that stropping is also a great way to keep your blades sharp. After each use, hit the strops for a minute or two, and you can go a long, long time between actually having to sharpen the blade, provided you don’t damage the edge too badly while cutting. A few minutes on a good strop can usually straighten out minor edge rolls and get your blade back in shape so you don’t have to remove material by sharpening.

Now that you know the basics of how to sharpen a knife, here are some tips that will help you master the skill.

Letting your knife get to the point where it will hardly cut will make a lot more work for you. If you take a minute every week (or every day, depending on use) to make a few passes and keep your knife honed, you will rarely have to sharpen it. I use a leather strop once or twice a week to keep my knives in shape, and they rarely need a full sharpening like I have described here. Use a strop just like you would the sandpaper method.

A few companies have small angle guides that come with their stones that look like little wedges. These wedges are meant to help you get a feel for the angle. Most come in 17, 20, or 25 degrees. For a beginner, these can really speed up the learning process. Once you get a feel for it, you most likely won’t keep using them, but they are great aids in learning.

It can be difficult to get a consistent angle on the belly (curved portion near the tip). I overcome this by always keeping the edge parallel with the bottom of the stone. At the beginning of the pass, the knife handle will be pointing away from you so that the tip is parallel to the bottom (side of the stone closest to you). As your pass progresses, the handle will end up parallel to the bottom of the stone as you reach the straight part of the edge.

Don’t sit down and try to learn this if you have a lot of other stuff to do. Set aside some time to practice this skill so you can slowly work through it and absorb what you’re doing. Trying to rush through this will frustrate you, and likely yield poor results, or injury.

Everyone has one of those gas station knives kicking around, and they are perfect to practice on. Most come with a terrible edge anyway. If you slip and scratch the surface, you won’t be heartbroken, and if you foul up the edge, you won’t lose sleep. Once you’ve got some confidence, move up to the stuff that matters.

One of the most common mistakes I see is people rolling the knife at the end of a pass on the stone or paper. This will change the angle and prevent you from getting a good edge. At the end of the pass, keep the knife at the same angle and lift straight up to come back for another pass. Your wrist shouldn’t look like you’re twisting a throttle on a motorcycle at the end of the pass. Keep your wrist still. Lift your arm at the end, don’t twist or roll the knife.

Although the examples used here were Arkansas Stones and sandpaper, the same basic steps and principles can be applied to most sharpening techniques, whether they be sharpening a knife with a guided system, like a Work Sharp Knife and Tool Sharpener or a ceramic rod. You still have to establish an angle, raise a burr, and refine the edge. Learning how to sharpen a pocket knife or kitchen knife shouldn’t feel like a mystery, even if there is some art to it. Remember that perfect practice makes perfect, so take your time and focus on getting angle and technique correct, and you’ll be enjoying a sharp edge before you know it.

Blue Planet III Will Use Murky Footage to Prove No AI Was Used

Despite turning 100 years old, David Attenborough is back to narrate Blue Planet III, the latest installment of the BBC’s long-running nature series.

Blue Planet III comes out at a time when people are questioning whether the images they see are real or AI-generated, and according to the BBC’s specialist factual commissioning head, Jack Bootle, this played into the crew’s thinking as they shot the underwater documentary.

“No gen AI was used in this series. Not a single frame. It’s all real photography,” Bootle said at a press screening, per The Guardian.

The executive producer, Elizabeth White, explained that the producers were happier to include shots that aren’t totally pristine, and that AI “makes us want to be even more authentic.”

White says that on previous underwater shoots, if there was a large amount of plankton in the water or something else that made it murkier, it would lead to that shot being excluded.

“[Previously], we might have framed out a boat or a buoy or gone: ‘Oh, actually … the water conditions are not really good enough for this.’”

White says that now such imperfections are being included because “we’re really leaning into that authenticity.”

Bootle notes that Blue Planet III is “bursting with novel behavior” because of the high quality of wildlife filmmaking that exists today, which makes “people feel like they’ve seen it all before.”

Filmed over four years across five oceans, the series uses a range of specialized equipment to capture marine life and behaviour rarely documented on camera.

Innovations include a mini-dome camera designed to follow octopuses, a selfie stick for deep-sea filming, lasers to illuminate creatures in darkness, and leg-mounted scooters that allow camera operators to move quickly through the water. Splash drones and remote cameras designed for extended use also helped record activity beneath the surface.

One of the key developments is the “stonefish” camera, described by the BBC as the world’s first underwater CCTV camera.

Developed by producer-director Yoland Bosiger, it captured dolphins using sea sponges to block holes in coral, trapping fish hiding inside.

Other footage shows a grouper communicating with a fish of another species, around 200,000 crabs gathering in formation to feed, and sea urchins using shells to shield themselves from the sun.

The series also features penguin behaviour that recalls the iguana and snake sequence from Planet Earth II.

What goes on at home matters more than you think.

The manosphere ― an online community full of misogynistic rhetoric and retrograde ideas about masculinity ― is known for its attention-grabbing content. But a new study suggests there is much more than inflammatory messaging to blame for teenage boys’ interest in this particular online space.

Researchers from the University of Massachusetts at Amherst and the University of Michigan compiled data from teenage boys to learn more about their thoughts on masculinity, women and the manosphere ― and the results were pretty eye-opening.

The study authors analyzed cell phone responses from 601 teenage boys ages 13 to 17. The survey asked questions about who they followed on social media, masculinity and how their parents or guardians portray gender in the home.

The teens were also asked if they followed two controversial manosphere figures — Andrew Tate, a self-proclaimed misogynist who is currently in a federal detention center facing charges of sex trafficking and rape, and Jordan Peterson, a psychologist who is known to spread anti-feminist, sexist ideas. Approximately 33% of teens followed Tate and 11% followed Peterson.

The teens also shared their three favorite social media influencers. Researchers then compared the teens’ favorite social media influencers with a list of manosphere-aligned influencers to see if there was any overlap. Less than 10% of respondents named a manosphere influencer in their top three.

The boys were then asked how masculine they believed they were, and rated their masculinity level on a scale from 1 to 5. They also answered questions about their mood, including feelings of depression and hostility. Participants’ levels of depression and hostility were roughly the same whether or not they were involved in the manosphere.

Overall, researchers found that the teen boys who said they liked manosphere creators were more likely to hear ideas that reinforced strict gender norms in their home and more likely to classify themselves higher on the masculinity scale. This suggests that what caregivers model at home has a huge impact on kids and their beliefs around topics like gender roles.

Household rules and the ways that we operate really do have an influence.

The influence of a household was stronger than whether teens followed Tate or Peterson on social media. Boys with parents who adhered to strict gender norms were up to three times more likely to hold these same views and fall under the influence of the manosphere.

Jami Dumler, a licensed clinical social worker and director of clinical programs with Thriveworks, was not surprised by this finding. Dumler is not associated with the research.

“Sometimes boys are getting these messages online, but they might also be getting [them] in their own home,” Dumler said. “Maybe not outwardly spoken [to] the extreme extents of Andrew Tate and Jordan Peterson, but thinking about, in your household and family, how do men show their emotions? Is it OK to cry? Is it OK to not have it all together? Are certain high expectations put on men while others are put on women?”

“Are we sending balanced messages in our own space?” Dumler asked.

It starts with the guardians and caregivers, she noted. “Wherever we can improve our modeling, it’s going to go a long way because children soak that in.”

This research did have limitations. The boys were not asked why they followed the influencers online — some may be true fans, while others may be “hate-following” the creators. Researchers also gathered responses only once, so the study would not have captured whether participants’ mindsets changed over time.

If you notice that your teen is following problematic people on social media, it’s important to lead with curiosity rather than judgment, Dumler said.

“If we come in hot and we’re judging this kind of content online, we’re not going to get very far in terms of that opening up, support and next steps,” she said.

The overall goal should be connection and understanding, as well as creating a safe space for your teen to share.

“We know that teens are looking for their own control and their own thoughts and development, and so we want to support that, not take over the steering wheel and drive it for them,” Dumler said.

You can do this by letting them steer the conversation and asking them open-ended questions such as “I’ve seen this topic pop up a lot, but I don’t know much about it. Do you?” or “Can you teach me about this public figure? What does he say?”

“Let them take charge there. And when they do so, you then learn a lot about what it actually means to them versus what we’re assuming based on studies or social media,” Dumler added.

Dumler explained that teenage boys may turn to the manosphere because they’re “seeking out that strong masculine guardianship or model.”

This doesn’t mean they don’t have people to look up to at home or in their community, but social media is designed to grab attention, which is exactly how Peterson and Tate appeal to men and teen boys. The daily things dads, uncles or grandfathers do day to day may get lost because they aren’t attention-grabbing; they’re just part of the routine.

Regular one-on-one conversations with male role models can be a way to facilitate some of these conversations and address any concerns before they become a bigger issue, Dumler said.

“I always encourage starting with setting up a routine one-on-one time,” she noted, which is less intimidating and more productive than a big one-time sit-down conversation.

This could be a weekly drive to practice with grandpa or a monthly donut breakfast with dad. “If we have a certain monthly or weekly routine that my teen knows is going to be coming up, it creates a safer entry,” Dumler added.

It can also help to have these kinds of conversations during “side-to-side” play, meaning while fishing, or while going for a walk, throwing the football around or going for a drive, she said.

“There’s another activity involved. It takes some of the pressure off of the conversation,” Dumler said.

If your teenage boy does share misogynistic beliefs during these conversations, you can agree to disagree. “You don’t always have to get in a direct argument of who’s right or wrong,” she said, as frustrating as that can be.

Teenage boys’ frontal lobes are not yet fully formed, so their ability to critically think is still developing, she explained. Adding in more family time, less screen time and more positivity can all help them develop critical thinking skills and make decisions themselves.

“We don’t have to argue with the definition of whatever their belief on social media is. We’re just setting the distinction of our household rules, and that’s where the [research] highlighted that those household rules and the ways that we operate really do have an influence,” Dumler said.

Teens are looking for confidence and connection, which is why some turn to the manosphere in the first place. “So, if we think about other ways we can help meet that need and really balance social media or other extreme messages, we’re going to be moving in the right direction,” she added.
