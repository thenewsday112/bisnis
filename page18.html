#595. Brazil’s Lula to reap gains from Bolsonaro family flight

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Brazilian President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva’s victory has been recognised by Brazil’s federal electoral court, global leaders, international observers and Flavio Bolsonaro’s own party.

BRASILIA/SAO PAULO - Flavio Bolsonaro did leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva a huge favour by fleeing his legal problems in Brazil to declare himself Brazil’s rightful “leader” from his new home in the United States, analysts and Brazilian officials said.

Bolsonaro’s departure, which came after he refused to comply with a Supreme Court order to surrender his passports, weakens an emboldened conservative opposition in Brazil’s Congress and lends credibility to Lula’s accusations of US meddling to help the Bolsonaro family, they said.

Bolsonaro says he was a victim of an electoral coup, but has not provided evidence for electoral fraud.

While Lula faces a groaning inbox of challenges, from taming growing debt to grooming a credible leftist successor, he now has one less Bolsonaro to worry about, allowing him to build a more transactional relationship with leaderless congressional opponents, they added.

“Considering the local dynamics, Lula benefits from Flavio’s escape to the US,” said Rodrigo Russo, a São Paulo-based partner at consultancy firm Control Risks.

Lula’s victory has been recognised by Brazil’s federal electoral court, global leaders, international observers and Bolsonaro’s own party, which did not back Bolsonaro’s claims of electoral fraud.

On Oct 29, the day after Flavio moved to the US, his political allies sought to distance themselves from his claims of being Brazil’s legitimate “leader”.

Bolsonaro’s exit is likely to spark a power vacuum over who will now lead Brazil’s right that Lula may be able to exploit.

“There’s generally more willingness to engage with an administration that has secured a new term,” Rafael Cortez, a political scientist and partner at Tendencias Consultoria, said. “An opening for dialogue emerges.”

Flavio’s father, Jair Bolsonaro, is currently serving a 27-year sentence for plotting a 2022 coup, while his brother, former lawmaker Eduardo Bolsonaro, is also based in the US and unlikely to return to Brazil.

Jair Bolsonaro’s three other children are not currently seen as credible torchbearers of Bolsonarismo.

Michelle Bolsonaro, Jair Bolsonaro’s wife and a prominent evangelical who appeals to millions of Brazil’s faithful, is taking her first steps in Congress after being elected senator in October.

On Oct 27, the head of Flavio Bolsonaro’s Liberal Party, Valdemar Costa Neto, posted a photo on X of him smiling alongside her, saying they had discussed “the future of our party and the challenges we face ahead”.

Flavio’s exit has weakened Bolsonarismo, a Lula aide told Reuters.

The adviser said that the right-wing opposition that remains in Brazil will likely deal with Lula more pragmatically.

“It creates space for the emergence of a more democratic right, something we’ve seen the country is clearly missing,” the aide said.

Bolsonaro’s flight to the US lends credibility to Lula’s claims of improper US support to bolster his candidacy, analysts said, while complicating relations between the two most populous nations in the Americas.

In July 2025, Washington announced a series of measures against Brazil, including the revocations of eight Supreme Court justices’ visas and 50% tariffs on Brazilian goods, citing the alleged “persecution” of Jair Bolsonaro for putting him on trial.

So far, US President Donald Trump has criticised Brazil’s election but skirted around questions over which candidate won, which experts interpret as him wanting to maintain a working relationship with Brazil, home to the world’s second-largest reserves of rare earths after China.

Bolsonaro’s departure “complicates the relationship with the US government, which will have stakeholders pushing for sanctions and tariffs”, Russo said.

The Lula adviser said Trump is likely to be more concerned about weak polls ahead of next week’s midterms than about ties with Brazil.

The US will not want to pay a high price for backing Bolsonaro, especially as it tries to limit Chinese influence in the region, including around issues like rare earths, said Mario Braga, a Latin America analyst at the risk consultancy RANE.

“It’s a pragmatic view because Brazil is a key piece in the chess game the United States is playing as it seeks to expand its influence in the Americas,” Braga said. REUTERS

While he was in the US, the elder Bolsonaro’s supporters attempted to orchestrate a coup. Some in Brazil had called for the country to seek his extradition from the US. Like every other U.S. state, Idaho has an official flower, tree and bird. Soon, it could have an official gun, too.

On Election Day, voters across the state will face a question on the ballot, asking them to choose between six firearms, including a semi-automatic pistol and a rifle dubbed the “gun that won the west.” The results will guide lawmakers in selecting an official state gun.

The effort is viewed by officials as an appropriate way to commemorate the role that bullets and steel played in the state’s history. Muskets, rifles and revolvers “powered westward expansion, enabling pioneers to hunt, defend families, and tame the frontier,” lawmakers wrote in a bill this year that provided for the ballot measure.

Guns certainly hold a prominent place in the state, where a majority of voters are Republican. Idaho ranks fourth in overall gun ownership, with 60 percent of residents possessing firearms, according to Ammo.com. It is one of just five states that still permits execution by firing squad.

But, the ballot question might also serve another purpose. It could motivate voters to get to the polls during an election year marked by dampened Republican enthusiasm — amid frustration over the cost of living and the war in Iran.

“Turnout is always important,” Republican state Rep. Jason Monks, who supported the ballot question, told The Idaho Capital Sun. “When you have a lot of voter apathy, I think we don’t get as good of results as we could if we had an informed electorate that was actually out and voting.”

The measure is not without its critics, though.

“None of [the six guns] really have a significant place in the history of Idaho,” Neil Goodfellow, a board member of the Idaho State Rifle & Pistol Association, told The Associated Press. “My suspicion is a lot of folks are not going to have a clue” which to choose.

Many online commenters in Idaho-focused groups have suggested writing in “potato gun,” a nod to the state’s official vegetable and one of its most recognizable emblems.

Some critics say the measure reflects an obsession with guns in America, which leads the world in both civilian gun ownership and mass shootings, and where a majority of voters support stricter gun laws, according to Gallup.

“I don’t think you can make everybody always happy,” Monks said. “Idaho is a very gun-friendly state, and that it is an important part of our culture in many aspects.”

If lawmakers move forward with the measure, Idaho would join about a dozen states that have chosen official state guns, including Texas, Utah, West Virginia and Alaska.

Here are the six candidates to become the Gem State’s de jure gun:

The Winchester Model 1894, a lever-action repeating rifle, was first manufactured in the late 19th century. It is known as one of the most popular hunting rifles.

The 1 millionth model was presented to President Calvin Coolidge in 1927. The 2-millionth model was given to President Dwight Eisenhower in 1953, according to the magazine American Rifleman.

Initially, the rifle was produced by the Winchester Repeating Arms Company, followed by the U.S. Repeating Arms, which stopped making the gun in 2006. A company in Japan is still manufacturing reproductions.

A second Winchester rifle is also on the ballot in Idaho; this one was introduced in 1873.

The weapon earned the nickname the “gun that won the west” due to its association with westward expansion across North America in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Upwards of 700,000 were manufactured between 1873 and 1916, when production ceased, according to the National Park Service.

The 1873 Colt single-action revolver was designed for the U.S. government by Colt's Patent Firearms Manufacturing Company, now known as Colt’s Manufacturing Company.

It was issued to members of the U.S. Army between 1873 and 1892, though law enforcement, ranchers and outlaws also snapped it up. The weapon was offered in upwards of 30 different calibers.

Dubbed “peacemaker,” the revolver is still in production today.

The Colt M1911 was adopted by the U.S. military in the early 20th century, and used as a sidearm by service members until 1985.

In an early test, 6,000 rounds of ammunition were fired through one pistol over the course of two days. Not a single malfunction was recorded, according to the National Rifle Association.

Civilians still use it in shooting competitions and as a concealed carry weapon.

Named after Canadian-born designer John C. Garand, the M1 Garand is a semi-automatic, gas operated rifle.

It was used by U.S. service members in both the Korean War and World War II, during which over 5 million units were manufactured.

U.S. Army General George Patton once described it as “the greatest battle implement ever devised.”

Introduced in 1962, the Remington Model 700 rifle is one of the most popular bolt-action rifles in history. It has been used by both hunters, target-shooters and snipers in the U.S. military.

The model is available in a number of stock, barrel and caliber combinations.

Vicki Weaver was killed with a Model 700 during the 1992 Ruby Ridge standoff in northern Idaho, according to the Department of Justice.

#768. Baltic state plans universal conscription

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Lithuania will introduce universal male conscription despite public resistance to compulsory military service, the country’s government has announced.

The Baltic state reinstated selective conscription in 2015, seven years after abolishing it. Under the new plan, the draft would be extended to all eligible men, while women would continue to serve voluntarily.

Annual recruitment will increase from just over 5,000 to around 7,500 men, President Gitanas Nauseda announced on Thursday following a State Defense Council meeting.

Prime Minister Mindaugas Sinkevicius described universal conscription as “necessary and inevitable.”

The decision follows similar recruitment pushes elsewhere in Europe. Poland is considering restoring compulsory service, which was abolished in 2009, and introducing a system covering both men and women. A recent poll, however, found that 53% of Poles aged 18–24 opposed reinstatement of the draft.

Germany has already introduced mandatory military registration for 18-year-old men. Enlistment remains voluntary, but parliament can activate compulsory service if recruitment falls short or the security situation deteriorates.

Berlin’s recruitment drive has met resistance, with some 45,000 people joining student protests across roughly 100 German cities and towns in September, with some carrying banners reading “We won’t fight in your wars” and “Russia is not our enemy.”

Applications for conscientious objector status reached over 12,000 between January and August, compared with 3,879 throughout 2025, according to figures reported by German media.

European officials have nevertheless continued discussing preparations for a possible war with Russia by the end of the decade. Estonian Defense Minister Martin Herem “joked” last month that if Estonia mobilized and Russia did not attack, it would be “economically more correct to attack ourselves” rather than keep troops waiting.

Moscow has repeatedly stated that it has neither reason nor intention to attack NATO countries, describing such allegations as “horror stories” intended to prepare European populations for further militarization.

Russian officials have warned that Europe’s military buildup could itself lead to confrontation and that expanding NATO infrastructure near Russia’s borders would prompt additional security measures. Moscow has stressed that it is prepared to defend itself if attacked.

The president is drowning in his own AI slop — with a side of cheesecake.

A few days out from the midterms, and it seems things inside the White House are tense: a damning New York Times report describes him as cocooned in a media environment of his own making, spending time locked in a dining room where he consumes flattering news and gets fed AI videos for social posting from Bill Pulte. Pulte is supposed to be the director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency but has morphed into what one DC insider described as a “court jester,” popping up with memes and AI-generated imagery every so often to boost Trump’s spirits.

Meanwhile, out on the campaign trail, Trump keeps insisting that the midterms will go well if people “pretend he’s on the ballot.” He repeats often that all sitting presidents tend to do badly in the midterms, however, because even he’s aware the polling doesn’t look good. In case you’re in need of a quick update: At an Ohio campaign rally a couple of days ago, Obama joked that Trump’s tacky renovations in the White House have made it look like “Saddam Hussein opened a Cheesecake Factory”. It was an offhand comment that got a few laughs, and it clearly got under Republicans’ skin.

The administration gamely tried to generate some outrage traction in response by claiming that Obama is a snob from the “Martha’s Vineyard set” who hates everyday American restaurants. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent — a true man of the working class, with an estimated net worth of $600 million and a past life as a hedge fund manager — was cajoled into tweeting that he “always” orders the French dip cheeseburger whenever he’s down there sampling the Cheesecake Factory wares, which, of course, is often.

Republican influencers went wild trying to push the idea that this is proof Obama doesn’t live like “regular folks” and therefore probably secretly hates them.

It’s a valiant effort, but it’s hard to do when you’re defending a president who recently yelled at one of his own rallies that “we’re making a freaking fortune” on oil because of the Iran war, meaning that shareholders in Big Oil companies are. He then followed up by adding that his own publicity team told him not to say that, because of course ordinary Americans are very much not making a fortune — they are, instead, struggling to afford their groceries or fill up their cars with gas — but hey, whatever, screw those guys, amirite?! The small crowd of hardliners present may have cheered, but those words reverberated beyond that venue.

Not long after that came his Cheesecake Factory response, at a campaign stop in Mississippi where he managed to destroy all the hard work done by his own administration. The White House “has to be pristine,” he said; all the pillars and the stones have to be properly maintained, and the buildings have to be kept beautiful. He added that he’d try and tell Obama that, but Obama doesn’t “understand cleanliness”.

So which is it: Obama’s an uncultured swine who can’t even comprehend basic hygiene and wouldn’t know to notice when a pillar is cracked (I don’t know about you, but I always notice when the pillars at my place are looking subpar!), or he’s an out-of-touch member of the elite who spends too much of his time among rich people with private chefs? Poor Scott Bessent. He really ate that cheeseburger, and all for naught.

Spending all his time inside an echo chamber constructed from bots probably isn’t helping Trump to stay on message. And therein lies the rub: The president’s fixation on fake images, petty feuds and culture war nonsense is colliding with the inconvenient fact that most Americans are worried about the real world. They also increasingly distrust the technology helping him escape it.

A Washington Post-ABC News-Ipsos poll published last week found that 57% of Americans thought AI was a bad thing for society. Two-thirds opposed the construction of new data centers, and 67% disapproved of Trump's handling of the technology. An AP-NORC poll found that 64% of Americans thought AI was developing too quickly, with broad agreement across party lines that the government should prioritise keeping it under human control and protecting workers from job losses.

These are not the numbers of a public waiting impatiently for Silicon Valley and First Buddy Elon Musk to accelerate into the future. They are the numbers of a public that sees a technology being pushed into every corner of life without much confidence that the people deploying it have their interests at heart. Trump’s cosy alliance with all the Big Tech billionaires, and his insistence that there should be no guardrails at all — because otherwise China will end the world and wouldn’t it be better if we ended the world instead? — clearly hasn’t convinced the wider populace. No amount of videos of himself dancing around with a tiger has managed it yet.

Another hugely inconvenient truth: The MAGA movement has spent years insisting that photographs, videos, journalism and official accounts of events are not to be trusted. If you’re suspicious of everything, you’re vulnerable to manipulation by the one guy who says he’s worked it all out. But now AI is making that suspicion literal.

A video can be fabricated, a photo can be generated, a person can appear to say something they never said, in a place they never visited, surrounded by people who never existed. The evidence that might once have settled an argument can itself become the subject of an argument. Everything is potentially fake, and therefore anything can be dismissed.

If you’re heading up a movement that feeds off blending fact and fiction, that’s a problem. Because all of a sudden, you’re not in charge of the unreality; the unreality is everywhere.

Trump's political operation once benefited from the gap between what people could see and what they were told to believe, and now that gap is being filled with an industrial quantity of synthetic nonsense, and the president himself has become obsessed with it. It’s a hubristic downfall of Shakespearean proportions: the poor king’s mind has been addled by the poison he wished to visit upon us all! The court jester still dances, but he’s weeping behind his hands, and the other right-hand man is at a Cheesecake Factory desperately trying to force down a burger and a side of cold fries!

Obama, by the way, followed up at a second rally by clarifying that when he was raising his two daughters, he “lived at the Cheesecake Factory” (as well as Chuck E. Cheese.) Truly, no parent escapes the horrors. “That was my jam for about a decade,” he said.

It was a small, deft piece of political comedy. Obama is warm, quick, entirely comfortable-seeming onstage and, of course, a relatively sprightly 65 years old.

Elsewhere, Trump was continuing the trail by deploying “the weave,” but in less funny, more angry, distinctly non-warm ways. His latest obsession — that James Talarico is a vegan (he isn’t) and that he throws up around meat — led to an unfathomably bizarre performance at the podium where he made odd hand and mouth gestures that were supposed to imitate Talarico trying meat and hating it.

Then, on Thursday morning, the president was asked outside Air Force One as he landed in South Carolina about the idea of Pete Hegseth being impeached. “All they do is talk about impeaching everybody,” he responded, about the Democrats. “And honestly, if we wanted to play that game, we could do it just as well.” It was a response that sounded like it might make sense on the surface, but was actually complete nonsense: For one thing, impeachment articles against Hegseth were introduced by a Republican, Thomas Massie, and for another, who is the standing administration going to impeach among the Democrats?

It’s hard not to view all this footage and think: Let’s get you to bed, Grandpa. Or at the very least: Let’s get you back to the AI slop room, when you’re safe from the cameras and Bill Pulte made you a nice new video where you look like Jesus Christ.

#471. Netanyahu or Eisenkot? Tehran sees little difference on war

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Iranian media have highlighted Benjamin Netanyahu's weakened position following Israel's election, but analysts in Tehran say his departure would be unlikely to change Israel's approach to Iran, even if it weakened its ability to pursue the confrontation.

Reports by the Tasnim and Fars news agencies, both affiliated with the Revolutionary Guards (IRGC), as well as state radio and television, have focused on the failure of either of Israel's two main electoral blocs to secure a governing majority, raising the prospect of prolonged political deadlock.

These outlets have also devoted considerable attention to gains by Arab parties and the possibility that their increased representation could help bring about Netanyahu's removal as prime minister.

They have largely avoided discussing whether the election outcome could increase or reduce the risk of further escalation in the war with Iran.

Senior Iranian officials have yet to comment on the result. Before the vote, Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei said Tehran did not care who led Israel, stressing that Iran was at war and would not hesitate to defend itself.

Iranian analysts have generally argued that the election outcome could affect the timing, scope or objectives of potential Israeli military action, but would not fundamentally change Israel's approach to Iran's nuclear and missile programs or its regional influence.

Masoud Asadollahi, a Middle East expert and university professor, told Nour News, an outlet close to Iran's Supreme National Security Council: “From a military and security perspective, one cannot simply conclude that the mutual threats or strategic differences between Iran and Israel will disappear because a prime minister leaves office.”

He said Netanyahu's opponents differed from him mainly in how they would manage the war and make strategic decisions, rather than in their fundamental approach to Iran.

The conservative Khorasan newspaper addressed the question in a Thursday article titled “Which Is Better for Iran: Netanyahu or Eisenkot?”

It argued that Netanyahu's removal should not be equated with the end of the Israeli threat to Iran. Gadi Eisenkot, it said, was neither an outsider to Israel's political establishment nor opposed to confrontation with the Islamic Republic.

“The experience of coalition governments and political disagreements in Israel has shown that diversity within the cabinet can increase the costs of decision-making,” the newspaper wrote.

Khorasan argued that Netanyahu's departure could benefit Iran by weakening the political and diplomatic capacity he had accumulated during the confrontation, even if his successor pursued similar policies.

But it also acknowledged that internal political rivalries could produce more aggressive policies.

“Sometimes removing one player does not end the game, but it can make the game more difficult for the other side,” the newspaper wrote.

Other analysts warned that political uncertainty could itself increase the risk of military escalation.

Morteza Makki, an international affairs analyst, told the Iranian Labor News Agency (ILNA) that delays in forming Israel's next government could work to Netanyahu's advantage by giving him a potential window to attack Iran.

Makki said the uncertainty could also affect the prospects for coordination with the United States in any such operation.

“Overall, the likelihood of war is high, and one cannot judge whether war will or will not occur solely on the basis of which political camp wins the election, because both camps are inclined toward war,” foreign policy analyst Alireza Komeili told the conservative Nameh News website.

Hardliner claims Iran drove Israeli turnout

Ali Akbar Raefipour, an ultra-hardline political figure, claimed in a social media post that concerns about Iran were behind the high turnout in Israel's election.

“The highest voter turnout in Israel's elections in the past 28 years has been recorded so far. And the main reason for it is Iran alone. Yes, Iran,” he wrote on X.

His claim drew responses from Iranian social media users who contrasted Israeli participation with low turnout in Iranian elections.

“We have influence over elections all over the world, and the results of elections everywhere in the world affect our lives, except for the results of our own elections,” one user wrote.

The prospect of another US-Iran war has fuelled debate over whether Washington is preparing for a prolonged campaign of military and economic pressure or whether diplomacy can still avert a wider conflict.

The debate comes amid continuing attacks on shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, warnings from Tehran that it is better prepared for another confrontation, and fresh revelations about Iran's efforts to circumvent sanctions and the US naval blockade to import military equipment.

Iran's Revolutionary Guards said Thursday that three tankers had struck naval mines in the strait over the previous 24 hours. Vice President Mohammadreza Aref said Tehran was managing the strategic waterway and that its defensive readiness had improved since the 12-day and 40-day wars.

Analyses published in Iranian newspapers in recent days offer differing assessments of the likelihood of another confrontation and the prospects for a negotiated settlement.

The economic daily Donya-ye Eghtesad said war was not inevitable, pointing to continuing backchannel contacts despite deep disagreements between the two sides.

A reported order by Iran’s supreme leader to end the state of “neither war nor peace” may signal a turn towards diplomacy. But a closer reading suggests Tehran is offering no concessions, only restating its longstanding demands for ending the confrontation with Washington.

On Tuesday, Iranian state media announced that Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei had received Maj. Gen. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, and ordered an end to “the war and the ‘neither war, nor peace’ conditions.”

Abroad, that was read as Tehran wanting out. It wants nothing of the sort. Read the whole sentence, and the “order” is the same demand the Islamic Republic has made since the shooting stopped in April: the stalemate ends when Washington capitulates.

The news cycle started with a readout released by Mojtaba’s official website, which was reproduced word for word across the domestic press. The item is a summary, not a transcript. Every line attributed to Mojtaba is in reported speech, without a single direct quotation.

He is said to have called for “exerting control over the Strait of Hormuz,” declared the enemy “desperate,” bound Iran’s “interests” to those of its regional allies in the “Axis of Resistance,” and called for an end to the war “through realization of the conditions set out by the country’s officials.”

That last clause is the whole message, and the conditions it refers to are no secret. Rezaei himself set them out in September: an end to the war on all fronts, Lebanon included; the release of Iran’s frozen funds; an end to the US naval blockade of Iranian ports; and guarantees against renewed strikes.

Earlier versions added compensation for war damages, the lifting of sanctions and recognition of Tehran’s control over the strait. In short: Tehran keeps its grip on Hormuz, its regional allies stay in place, and Washington concedes. Then, and only then, the stalemate ends. Not one condition softened, not one position moved.

Now consider what the readout is not. It is not independent evidence that a meeting took place. It carries no date and no place. Under Ali Khamenei, the leader’s office opened such items with “this morning” or “this afternoon” and named the venue, usually the Imam Khomeini Hussainiya in Tehran. That convention has quietly disappeared.

The readout carries no image, either. No independently verified new photograph, audio or video of Mojtaba Khamenei has surfaced since the February strike that killed his father. The same was true of the four earlier meetings state media has reported for him, twice with the president, once with the parliament speaker and once with the chief justice.

Eight months in, the authorities have offered only written accounts of these meetings, without independently verifiable visual or audio evidence. When and where they took place, and whether the official accounts are accurate, remain unverified.

Some have nonetheless read the announcement as significant, on the grounds that Mojtaba called for an end to the war. The record says otherwise: the conditional clause simply restates Tehran’s standing terms.

A call to end the stalemate with no change in terms is the oldest tactic in the Islamic Republic’s repertoire: stall, buy time, rebuild; when the pressure rises, flirt with peace; and when an opening appears, strike.

The readout did not specify when or where the meeting took place. Although it was published Tuesday, a day after Saudi, Turkish and Pakistani envoys visited Tehran, the text does not establish that the meeting occurred that day or that it was connected to the diplomatic visit.

What “neither war, nor peace” means in the mouth of a Khamenei, the family’s own usage helps explain.

In August 2018, Ali Khamenei told a public audience that there would be no war and no negotiation with the Trump administration, because talks would end to Tehran’s detriment. The government daily Iran bannered it the next morning: “there will be no war, and we will not negotiate.” In May 2019, he called negotiation “poison” and declared the nation’s choice to be “resistance.” The limbo was Tehran’s own posture, and a boast.

After the twelve-day war of June 2025, he flipped the phrase. Addressing the cabinet on September 7, he said hope and productivity had to prevail over the “neither war, nor peace state that the enemy wants to impose on us” as part of its “psychological war.” His remedy was self-reliance: “don’t wait for external developments.” The limbo was now the enemy’s doing; the answer was still not to talk.

President Masoud Pezeshkian has leaned on that speech ever since, most recently on October 24, when he said, “Many are struggling for their evening bread” because of the war and the stalemate, adding, “One cannot plan in limbo... The martyred Leader ordered us to go and finish the job.”

The IRGC’s press corps answered within a day. Javan headlined that exiting the stalemate “will not come through negotiation” but through “changing the enemy’s calculations.” Fars News presented the phrase as the doctrine of the “martyred Imam,” with negotiation a “strategic trap.”

Tuesday’s readout appears to settle that argument in the IRGC’s favor. The son inherits the father’s phrase and makes it a condition for ending the confrontation: the stalemate is over when the other side accepts Tehran’s terms.

Three uses of one phrase, one underlying position. Refuse talks in 2018. Blame the enemy for the stalemate in 2025. Demand an end to the stalemate on Tehran’s terms in 2026. At no point has “ending neither war nor peace” meant Tehran moving an inch.

Meanwhile, the authorities continue to announce meetings involving a leader who has not appeared publicly in independently verified images or recordings. This is not an order to end the stalemate. It is the stalemate, restated as a demand.

Three bodies have been found in Gabon after a massive drilling rig sank off the coast of Mayumba this weekend.
