Mt. Whitney is the highest mountain in the United States outside of Alaska (Photo: Gary Coronado / Contributor via Getty)

Search and rescue personnel recovered the body of a missing hiker from California’s Mount Whitney on October 25 following a nearly weeklong effort complicated by early winter storms.

In a press release posted to Facebook, the Inyo County Sheriff’s Office (ICSO) said a motel in nearby Lone Pine had notified law enforcement on October 19 that one of its guests had not checked out and had left some of their possessions behind. The office attempted to contact the missing hiker through a law enforcement messaging system, but did not receive a response.

While hotel staff knew that the guest had planned to hike, deputies were unsure of their route until the following day, when they discovered the guest’s car parked at Whitney Portal. Upon checking with the U.S. Forest Service, ICSO found that the missing hiker had secured a permit to climb Whitney on October 18.

That same day, a helicopter search found a hiker’s body along the Mountaineer’s Route at roughly 13,600 feet. Shortly after that grim discovery, an early-season storm moved into the area, buffeting the Lower 48’s highest peak with strong winds and snow and forcing authorities to postpone the recovery mission.

On October 25, after the storm cleared, a California Highway Patrol helicopter was able to carry a SAR team from Inyo County Search and Rescue to Iceberg Lake, a glacial cirque located at about 12,600 feet. From there, rescuers climbed to the hiker’s body and assisted the helicopter in hoisting it off the peak before descending back to the lake for pickup.

The Inyo County Coroner’s Office has yet to publicly identify the deceased hiker.

The harder of the two most commonly climbed lines up Whitney, the Mountaineer’s Route is 5 miles one way and requires class 3 scrambling in places.

The fatality is the second to occur on Whitney this month. In early October, Kalem Peter Kazarian, a contractor who had appeared in a pilot for HGTV, was descending from Whitney’s summit when he fell and suffered fatal injuries. In January, another climber passed away on the “Final 400” section of the Mountaineer’s Route after continuing up the mountain solo while their partner turned back.

#4265. Python's experimental JIT to be removed

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Python's experimental just-in-time (JIT) compiler, which was added to CPython in 2024 for the 3.13 release, is on its way out. In June, the Python steering council announced that it was pausing work on the JIT in CPython's main branch until there was an accepted Python Enhancement Proposal (PEP) that addressed concerns about maintenance, compatibility, and set clear success metrics as well as a timeline for completion. After creating PEP 836 ("JIT Go Brrr: The Path to a Supported JIT Compiler for CPython") to try to meet the council's request, and after months of discussion, the JIT maintainers have withdrawn the PEP. For now, it appears that development on the once-promising project has come to an end.

The JIT was added to CPython by Brandt Bucher in early 2024. Generally, such a major feature would be expected to land only after the council had approved a standards-track PEP. Bucher had given a talk (YouTube video, slides) about the JIT at a Python core developer sprint, and announced his intent to merge the feature—but he had not taken the step of authoring or submitting a PEP for its approval. He was prodded into doing so by steering council member Thomas Wouters, who was also serving as the release manager for Python 3.13.

Bucher and Savannah Ostrowski drafted PEP 744 ("JIT Compilation"), which was an informational PEP—meaning it only provides information to the Python community and does not represent formal consensus to proceed on a feature or project. LWN covered the discussion of PEP 744 in April 2024, and later covered Bucher's talk on the JIT at PyCon US 2024, as well as the follow-up talk at PyCon US 2025.

Work on the JIT had been proceeding on the basis of PEP 744 until this June, when the council had put forward its formal request for the JIT maintainers to create a standards-track PEP. One might wonder, as Antoine Pitrou did, why the council had decided that it was time to speak up and ask for a new PEP. Wouters said that there was no "single triggering event for this request", but that the council had been discussing the topic for a while and indicated that it was looking to ensure the JIT work did not interfere with other projects. "There's been work trying to get the JIT to play nice with instrumentation, debugging, and profiling. There's also external JITs (like CinderX), JIT-adjacent things (like Numba and PyTorch's compile), and discussions about a tier 3 JIT." The council had waited until after the Python 3.15 beta release "to be clear that we're OK with the status quo for 3.15".

The council asked for a PEP that would make the case for the JIT "as a supported, non-experimental part of CPython: its guarantees, its maintenance commitments, and its impact on redistributors". The council set a deadline of six months for the PEP to be submitted and accepted; failing that, the council would require the JIT code to be removed from the CPython main branch.

Bucher, Ostrowski, and Ken Jin drafted PEP 836 and published it on the Python forum for community feedback on July 3. It generated quite a bit of discussion, some favorable and some critical. One of the key themes in the comments, such as this one by Wouters, and this comment by Mark Shannon, was that the draft felt like it should be two separate PEPs. Shannon, for example, thought that the PEP should only cover goals for accepting the JIT work and felt that it should leave out implementation details. There were also quite a few calls for additional information.

Bucher pushed back a bit on the suggestion that it should be two PEPs, and Ostrowski defended the structure of the PEP as it was. "Outlining goals like we have in the PEP without being able to share the plan and everything we've thought through alongside it would make me uncomfortable. To me, goals without plans behind them aren't really goals at all."

When we last looked at this topic in September, the discussion about the PEP had slowed down without a commitment to develop new PEPs or a formal statement from the council. The last word from the council at that time, in the form of a summary of its August 6 meeting, was that it had met with the PEP authors and was "discussing the state of the PEP and whether it answered the questions the SC asked in its request for a PEP".

On September 21, Wouters posted a statement from the steering council, minus Ostrowski (who is currently a member of the steering council but did not participate since she is a co-author of the PEP), that summarized its position. The statement said that PEP did answer all of the questions that were highlighted in the council's request, "although perhaps some less convincingly than others".

The council wished to make clear that it was not looking for the criteria that the JIT should meet to be a supported feature, or whether it should be considered such. Instead, the council was evaluating whether the JIT should continue as an experiment in CPython's main branch.

The council said that the PEP was not specific enough about support plans, performance criteria, and too vague or too optimistic about a number of other areas. The statement included a ten-point list of items that the council wanted "to see either spelled out in the PEP, or at least discussed and rejected". The council's list expressed concerns ranging from memory impact to security. For example, on the topic of platform parity, the council wanted to ensure that the JIT was designed to be easily adopted for new platforms. It wanted assurance that the JIT's design would not be tied to specific platform or require platform-specific knowledge to maintain.

Another area that the council called out was user impact. It said that the PEP was "overly optimistic" about user-visible differences with the JIT enabled. There were many cases where users depend on idiosyncrasies in the interpreter design, "as we've seen with other Python implementations or even just between major Python versions", and it wanted clarification on how those differences would be handled. Would they be considered bugs, how would situations be handled if a difference in behavior was unavoidable, and so on.

Security was "an underserved area in the PEP", according to the council; it suggested that a discussion with the Python security response team was in order and that a security review should be part of the criteria for supported status as well.

The council also worried about the JIT's design decisions being made "without critical review by the larger group of developers ultimately responsible for [CPython] development, maintenance and support". The bottom line, it said, was that PEP 836 "mostly answers the questions we asked" with no missing pieces, but it wanted more discussion on those topics so it could "gauge community support and consensus before we're asked to pronounce on this PEP".

Jin replied a few days later to thank the council for its detailed feedback and said that the group would work on its suggestions.

After some consideration, it appears, the PEP's authors have decided not to continue pursing the council's approval. On October 19, Ostrowski (who is also release manager for Python 3.16 and 3.17) announced that the PEP was being withdrawn:

Barry Scott asked if that meant that work on the JIT was stopped. Brett Cannon replied that the work would be stopped in the main repository, but it would be up to the community whether the work would continue elsewhere. Dunc Graham suggested that there might be time for an alternative PEP to be submitted before the council's deadline. Cannon said that was not likely, "as all the people actively driving the JIT were either authors on that PEP or contributors to it".

With the PEP withdrawn, the JIT will likely be removed from CPython main fairly soon to ensure that any problems caused by its removal can be addressed early in the Python 3.16 development cycle. It is unclear whether the original drivers of the JIT work intend to continue working on it at all, or if it will simply be left for others to pick up if there is enough community interest. I have emailed the PEP authors for clarification, but have not yet received a reply.

Red bookshelves fill the multipurpose interiors of this city library, created by Dutch studio MVRDV in a former department store in Essen, in collaboration with architecture practices Includi and Meyer Architekten.

Named Essen Central Library, the 11,000-square-metre civic building was designed as a “vibrant cultural and educational hub” for the German city and to offer more than a traditional archive.

MVRDV said this aims to ensure the building’s longevity and usefulness in the information age, while also providing opportunities for social interaction in Essen.

“Of all the building functions out there, the library is possibly the one that has had the biggest challenges to adapt to the new millennium,” said MVRDV partner Fokke Moerel.

“Essen Central Library offers a response to this challenge. The library remains an important civic building, yet it offers much more than just a place to find books – it becomes a living room that is open and inviting for all of Essen’s diverse citizens,” she continued.

“It offers a solution to the social atomisation and isolation that has also been growing in the past decades.”

Essen Central Library, which opened to the public on Saturday, relocates a former library to a more central location. It spans all six storeys of Boeckerhaus, the former department store on Kennedyplatz that it occupies.

MVRDV and Meyer Architekten said their design draws on a theory by sociologist Ray Oldenburg about third places, which are free and informal social environments for people to enjoy beyond their homes, schools and places of work.

As such, it is hoped the library can feel inclusive to everyone in the city, whether they are visiting “without a specific purpose” or looking for a specific collection. To achieve this, the team collaborated with third place design specialist Includi on the interior.

Externally, the building is marked by large bands of glazing, allowing views of the library’s interior, including bands of study spaces on the perimeter of each floor.

MVRDV said this allows “the activities taking place within the library to become part of its expression towards the city”.

“The activated facade acts as an interface between the city and the library, connecting the public realm with the everyday experience of the library users,” said the studio.

“The different heights and uses create a lively view of people reading, working, talking, or relaxing, with visitors adding even more character to the space.”

Throughout the interior, bookshelves are used to define different spaces and combined in clusters on each floor, making collections “concentrated and identifiable”.

These clusters, named “media villages”, also define soft play spaces on the first floor, gaming pods on the second, and hobby zones on the third floor. They are all red in colour, but lighten in tone with each level.

The bookshelves are complemented by a palette of warm and natural wood finishes, plant pots and seating areas suited for socialising or focusing. Blue signage helps with wayfinding on each landing.

Completing the project is a ground-floor cafe intended to entice passersby and a rooftop events space with panoramic city views. There are also “makerspaces”, music rooms and areas to use technologies such as 3D printers, virtual reality and robotics.

MVRDV was founded in 1993 in Rotterdam. Its other recent projects include a proposal for a “new world wonder” in the Netherlands and a Bordeaux housing scheme with people-shaped portals.

Other libraries recently featured on Dezeen include a demountable timber structure in Amsterdam that was also conceived as “a living room for the neighbourhood” and a former commercial building in Norfolk that was overhauled and wrapped in a skin of cement shingles.

#492. Do spiders dream? Scientists just found a surprising clue

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists at the University of Bonn have discovered that spiders experience distinct stages of sleep, including a REM-like state that shares surprising similarities with human sleep. During this stage, the spiders move their eyes and twitch their muscles, behaviors also associated with REM (rapid eye movement) sleep in humans and other vertebrates.

The findings provide the first comprehensive experimental confirmation that spiders experience genuine, regulated sleep rather than simply remaining inactive. They also raise intriguing questions about how sleep evolved and whether animals with very different nervous systems might share some of its fundamental features.

Researchers at the University of Bonn's Institute of Organismic Biology made the discovery while studying the Australian jumping spider Portia fimbriata, a small but remarkably sophisticated predator.

A Tiny Spider With Remarkable Hunting Skills

The Australian jumping spider Portia fimbriata is only about the size of a one-euro coin when fully grown, but its hunting abilities make it particularly interesting to scientists.

Unlike many spiders that primarily capture insects, members of the genus Portia specialize in hunting other spiders. They use a variety of sophisticated strategies to catch their prey, including imitating the vibrations made by struggling insects trapped in spiderwebs.

These deceptive signals can lure web-building spiders toward what appears to be an easy meal, only for them to encounter the hunting Portia instead.

Such complex behaviors suggest that these spiders have unusual learning abilities, making them valuable subjects for investigating the relationship between sleep, nervous system function, and cognition.

Scientists Discover Two Distinct Stages of Spider Sleep

Although sleep has been extensively studied in humans and other vertebrates, scientists still know relatively little about how it works in animals with very different nervous systems.

To investigate whether spiders experience genuine sleep and whether that sleep includes different stages, the researchers closely monitored Portia fimbriata over several days and nights.

They discovered that the spiders exhibit two clearly distinguishable sleep stages. One of these resembles REM sleep, the phase in humans associated with rapid eye movements, muscle activity, and vivid dreaming.

During this REM-like stage, the spiders displayed characteristic eye movements and muscle twitches. These observations suggest that some features of sleep previously studied mainly in vertebrates may also occur in spiders.

The researchers emphasize that identifying REM-like movements does not establish whether spiders dream. However, the discovery provides important evidence that sleep in these animals is more complex than previously understood.

How Researchers Confirmed That Spiders Really Sleep

To study the spiders' sleeping behavior, the team placed individual Portia fimbriata spiders in plastic dishes measuring 12 centimeters in diameter. They monitored the animals under different lighting conditions to examine their daily activity and rest cycles.

Infrared cameras allowed the researchers to observe the spiders throughout the night without relying on visible light. Detailed recordings captured their sleeping positions, periods of quiet rest, and episodes of REM-like movement.

The team also conducted experiments to determine whether the spiders were genuinely asleep rather than simply sitting motionless.

Using sound stimuli, the researchers measured how quickly the spiders responded to disturbances. These tests helped distinguish sleep from ordinary inactivity.

Another important part of the investigation involved sleep deprivation. The researchers examined whether the spiders displayed "sleep rebound," meaning they compensated for interrupted sleep by increasing their subsequent sleep.

Together, the behavioral observations and experimental tests provided strong evidence that Portia fimbriata experiences regulated sleep with distinct stages.

Studying Spider Sleep Presented Unusual Challenges

Determining whether a spider is truly asleep is considerably more complicated than observing a sleeping human.

Because spiders sense their surroundings differently from people, the researchers first had to identify a sound stimulus that would produce a consistent response during their experiments.

They also needed to avoid assuming that the observed eye movements and muscle twitches necessarily represented the same processes seen during human REM sleep.

One major limitation was that the researchers could not measure the spiders' brain activity. Instead, they relied entirely on carefully documented behavior to identify different sleep states.

This required extensive high-resolution video recordings collected over many days and nights, producing enormous amounts of data for analysis.

Despite these obstacles, the combination of behavioral monitoring, response testing, and sleep disruption experiments allowed the researchers to build a detailed picture of how these spiders sleep.

What Spider Sleep Could Reveal About Evolution

The discovery has implications that extend well beyond spiders.

Most scientific knowledge about sleep comes from studying vertebrates, particularly mammals and birds. Finding comparable sleep features in animals with radically different nervous systems could help researchers understand when and why different types of sleep evolved.

The results also open new opportunities in behavioral science, neuroscience, and chronobiology, the study of biological rhythms.

By investigating sleep in a wider variety of animals, scientists hope to identify features that are shared across species and uncover the fundamental biological functions that sleep may have served early in evolutionary history.

The researchers argue that future investigations should move beyond traditionally studied animals and include species with unusual adaptations or much simpler nervous systems.

Such comparisons could reveal whether certain sleep mechanisms are widespread throughout the animal kingdom or developed independently in different evolutionary lineages.

Even after decades of intensive research, many basic questions about sleep remain unanswered, including important mysteries surrounding human sleep.

Could Spiders Dream? Scientists Have More Questions

The University of Bonn team is now expanding its research to investigate how sleep, particularly REM-like sleep, varies across different spider species.

Scientists are also comparing spiders sleeping in natural environments with those observed in laboratories to determine how surroundings influence their behavior.

Another major goal is to investigate the relationship between sleep and cognitive abilities. Researchers want to understand what happens inside the brains of spiders and insects during sleep, and whether different sleep stages contribute to learning and other mental processes.

Environmental changes are also becoming an important focus. The team plans to examine how climate change and human activities, especially artificial light at night (ALAN), may interfere with the sleep patterns of spiders and other arthropods.

For now, the findings establish that even a tiny jumping spider can experience a surprisingly complex form of sleep. Whether spiders actually dream remains unknown, but their nighttime behavior is offering scientists new clues about one of biology's most enduring mysteries.
