#2727. The Overlooked Muscle You Need to Strengthen For Better Knee Health This Ski Season

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Including eccentric work in your exercise routine is essential to making sure your muscles are balanced. (Photo: Tory Powers)

Strong legs are essential for skiing without injury. But many traditional strength training programs focus on the bigger muscles in your lower half, including the quads, hamstrings, and gluteus maximus (your largest butt muscle).

That means the smaller guys often get neglected. And one small guy in particular–the gluteus medius, which forms part of your side butt–is worth paying attention to if you actually want to reduce your risk of knee pain and injury this winter.

Because ACL tears are among the most common ski injuries, Dr. Gregory Lichtman, an orthopedic surgeon in northern California and a physician for the U.S. women’s ski team, puts heavy emphasis on strengthening the glute medius. In his view, it’s one of the most important muscles for staying safe and skiing well.

Unfortunately, even high-level athletes can have a weak glute medius, according to Lichtman. That’s because unlike the quads, hamstrings, calves, and glute max–which can naturally get strong from activities like road running and hill climbing–the glute medius typically needs to be intentionally strengthened.

Read on to learn why this small muscle is so important, Lichtman’s favorite way to strengthen it, and other essential fitness tips for injury prevention.

The glute medius is a small muscle on your outer hip that fires up when you bring your leg out to the side, a motion known as hip abduction. It also helps control the knee by preventing it from caving inward (a position known as valgus) as you perform various movements. And it assists you in balancing on one leg, which is important when loading your downhill ski, Lichtman says.

In skiing, if you get into the back seat (meaning, you shift your body weight backwards, instead of staying centered over your skis) and the knee goes into valgus, the tibia can internally rotate, “and that’s where the ACL tears occur,” Lichtman says. Even if you don’t get a full-blown ACL tear, going into valgus when you ski can contribute to patellofemoral pain syndrome, a fancy term for pain at the front of the kneecap, Lichtman says.

By strengthening the glute medius, you can make it more resistant to valgus in the first place, which decreases your chances of both ACL tears and general knee pain.

There are also performance benefits: “A strong gluteus medius also really allows you to stand on your downhill ski a little bit more aggressively and really make a more aggressive turn,” Lichtman says. That’s because when you place most of your weight on your downhill ski (the one that’s closest to the bottom of the hill), your G-forces will want to throw you down the mountain. But having a strong glute medius–in addition to strong quads and core muscles–helps form a strong pillar on your outer hip, which ”really allows you to withstand those G forces the best,” Lichtman says.

One of the classic ways to target the glute medius is to strap on ankle weights, lie on your side, and lift and lower your top leg. Lichtman says. This exercise can absolutely strengthen your glute medius, but it’s not Lichtman’s go-to. That’s because it primarily just isolates that muscle and doesn’t mimic how we move in sports.

A better bet, Lichtman says, is to focus on exercises that simultaneously activate the gluteus medius and other muscle groups because that better resembles how we use this muscle during athletics. His favorite exercise that ticks that box? The monster walk. Not only does this move really fire up the gluteus medius, but it also works the core, hamstrings, quads, and glute max, he says.

Incorporate this exercise into your workouts three days a week, with at least one day off in between each session so that you give the muscle the downtime it needs to recover and build back stronger. To stave off boredom–and also work different parts of your hips and quads–incorporate different variations of the monster walk throughout the week. For example, instead of stepping straight out to the side, step forwards and backwards, or diagonally forward and diagonally backwards. As long as you have good form and keep your legs spread apart so that you feel tension from the band the whole time, “all directions can be effective,” Lichtman says.

Lichtman suggests doing monster walks during both pre-season and throughout ski season. “One of the big pitfalls is that we will all get ready for ski season, and then taper off our dry land training,” he explains. “If you’re one of the fortunate few who can ski five or six days a week, you’re probably getting enough exercise, and you probably don’t want to stress your body out any more than that. But for those of us who are skiing two to three days a week, finding some time to do some cross-training and continue to work on our core and our hip abductor strength is really important because those are the less commonly used muscles during skiing.”

Once this exercise becomes easy, level up the challenge by using a stronger resistance band.

A strong glute medius can keep you safe on the slopes. But it’s not the only fitness-related aspect of injury prevention.

Having a strong core and quads, along with good overall endurance, is also key. That’s because one of the essential components of an ACL tear pattern in skiing is getting kicked into the back seat. And while the gluteus medius helps guard against knee valgus in this position, it doesn’t prevent you from getting into the back seat in the first place, Lichtman explains. What does help? Having strong core and quad muscles, plus overall endurance. This trifecta, Lichtman says, is “fundamental” to staying out of the back seat.

On the endurance front, the goal is to be as fresh after lunch as you are after breakfast, Lichtman says. “Fatigue is definitely a risk factor for ACL tears,” he says, explaining that fatigued skiers more frequently get into the back seat and sustain injuries than those who aren’t zapped. So make sure your dryland training doesn’t just focus on lifting weights: “A balance of endurance and strength training is integral for a skier preparing for ski season,” Lichtman says.

#3972. How to Deal With an Annoying Person

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Feeling annoyed by someone can occupy a lot more mental space than is desirable.

But, we can’t always do much about having to interact with that person.

For example, you can’t opt out of your exposure to them if they’re a work colleague who’s in most of your meetings, or a classmate who wastes everyone's time on tangents or who tries to show they're smarter than the instructor.

When you’re in this situation, you may realize that you need an attitude change toward the person for your own sake. You might no longer want to allow them to interfere with your daily experience to such an extent.

Here are five questions you can use to attempt that mental shift. The purpose isn’t to gaslight yourself into believing the person isn’t annoying; it’s to jiggle free your perspective just enough that you can see another view is possible. You're doing it to relieve your own tension.

Even if only one or two of these questions appeal and the others feel naff, that may be all you need.

1. It’s 10 years in the future. You’re looking back on now because it’s when you accomplished what, 10 years later, is still the most significant achievement in your life to date. The annoying person was part of that significant achievement, in a collaborative way. What happened? Tell a few different stories, including a couple of unpredictable ones.

2. Knowing X about [annoying person’s name] would cause your feelings and thoughts about them to change. What’s X?

You don’t need to know or expect that X is true. The question is simply an exercise in realizing something could change your perspective and therefore your perspective isn’t fixed.I don’t mean a sob story. For example, perhaps you find out they’d been a whistleblower in a prior organization, at significant personal cost.Or, you find out something else they did that was extremely generous, ethical, considerate, or open-minded.

3. You find out that you have X in common with the person you’re finding difficult. It changes your view of them and helps you create a non-forced bond with them. What’s X?

Example: You found out you’ve both donated gametes (egg or sperm) to friends who needed donation to have the children they wanted. Or, you’re both pursuing “FIRE” (financial independence/retire early). You don’t have many other people to talk about it with, but now you have someone who is trying to do it while navigating the same systems at your company you are.

4. You find out the annoying person did something unexpected in relation to you that causes you to realize they respect you more than you knew. What might it have been?

Examples: They referred you for an opportunity, spoke positively about you to an influential person, used advice you’d given them to solve a problem, changed their mind because of something you said, or gave you full credit for an idea that they’d contributed to.Again, the point here is just to consider that one data point or experience can shift our view and our relationship. This can help us not see the future as stagnant, or see escalating frustration as the only trajectory for the relationship.

5. It’s six months in the future and you realize you underestimated the annoying person. You hadn’t seen them as smart or valuable. What could happen to make you recognize their annoying aspects have more positive offsets than you’d previously seen?

Perhaps they create and share a resource with you that makes you significant money or saves you significant time. You didn’t think they were smart enough to create it or generous enough to share it, but they did.

If you find yourself bracing whenever you have to be around a particular person, consider if the questions offered here could help release that stress valve.

You don’t need to make the rosiest interpretation of the person’s behavior. Without that, it can still be useful to see that perhaps your annoyance isn’t the full story. When we see one side of someone repeatedly, and that experience is aversive to us, that can feel like comprehensive knowledge. Going through the process of considering these questions can help you recognize it isn’t.

COLLINS unites Ultimate Guitar, MuseScore, and Audacity under a rhythmic branding system for music software powerhouse Muse Group.

Most musicians know the tools, not the parent company. Muse Group quietly grew into a global leader in music software by backing platforms like Ultimate Guitar, MuseScore, Audacity, and MuseClass for more than 400 million users across learning, practicing, composing, and performing. Still, the parent brand stayed completely silent. Each product had its own look, voice, and loyal community, creating internal dissonance that teams described as "the gap" between creative ambition and real expression. COLLINS didn't just slap a corporate wrapper over those products. Instead, the team dug into what actually keeps everyday players picking up an instrument: fluency matters much more than virtuosity. Most people don't want to become famous rock stars; they just want music to flow without friction, so COLLINS repositioned the business as a Creative Fluency Company built around momentum rather than perfection.

The visual system hits that note right away. Drawing directly from the abstract geometry of Suprematism, the horizontal structures of sheet music notation, and the physics of sonic vibration, COLLINS built a modular branding language that pulses and resolves on screen. Sharp vector forms crafted in Illustrator lock into rhythmic staff lines before expanding into expressive motion behaviors in After Effects. Nothing in the grid feels static. Every geometric block acts like a visual chord that can compress for a tiny app icon in Figma or stretch across wide festival stages and product launch decks.

That balance between structure and improvisation is hard to pull off. Typography anchors the layouts with crisp grotesk headlines and clean editorial margins, giving the geometric shapes plenty of breathing room to move without overwhelming the interface. When the system rolls out across Audacity, MuseScore, and Ultimate Guitar touchpoints, it doesn't erase what makes each tool distinct. It gives them a shared center of gravity. By turning musical notation and Suprematist form into living software branding, COLLINS shows how an umbrella identity can unify cross-functional teams and guide millions of creators across the creative gap.

Are we creating a new problem for AI search by forgetting the lessons we’ve already learned from SEO?

Why would an AI search provider have a long-term incentive to trust information that a brand deliberately keeps separate from its public-facing website?

This is a question I’ve been coming back to as more conversations about optimizing for AI search seem to involve creating content specifically for agents, rather than people.

The logic is understandable: AI systems need information, sometimes in formats or structures that aren’t particularly useful to a human visitor. If we can make that information easier for agents to consume, surely that’s a good thing?

Yes, but there is a significant difference between making information more accessible to AI and creating an alternative version of your website, or even your brand, that exists primarily to influence what AI systems say about you.

We’ve spent decades dealing with versions of this problem in SEO, and I’m not convinced we’re taking those lessons seriously enough.

Anyone who has worked in SEO for long enough will have seen search engines evolve in response to the means used to manipulate them.

Early SEO was full of practices designed to influence search engines rather than help users: hidden text, keyword stuffing, doorway pages, cloaking, and eventually increasingly sophisticated content and link manipulation.

Search engines didn’t develop rules against these practices because they disliked SEOs (mostly). They did so because allowing websites to falsify relevance and authority undermined the quality of their results.

Google’s history gives us a fairly clear picture of this evolution:

This is a very condensed version of things, and the reality was far more complicated, but the pattern is important.

As search engines became more sophisticated, so did the methods used to influence (spam) them. Search engines responded by investing more effort into deciding what information was trusted and rewarded.

Google still explicitly identifies cloaking as spam. I am not saying that cloaking’s definition is as simple (or east to detect) as that, but the parallels are too close to ignore.

We’re now seeing a collection of approaches designed to make information available to AI systems and agents.

The llms.txt proposal is one example, providing a structured way of directing language models towards useful website content. There are also agent-specific markdown pages, dedicated information endpoints, structured feeds and increasingly sophisticated methods of supplying information directly to agents.

Cloudflare’s Markdown for Agents is a good example of serving an alternative version of existing content. The information originates from the same page, but is presented in a format that’s potentially easier for agents to process.

That’s fundamentally different from creating new information that exists only for AI consumption.

Making the same information accessible in different formats isn’t the problem.

Making different information available to different audiences is where things become considerably more complicated.

I can understand why brands might be attracted to this as getting content onto a website has never been particularly easy. Content ownership, CMS limitations, design requirements, development resources, governance, approvals and the competing priorities of different teams all play a part.

If you can bypass all of that by publishing a text file, or hosting a collection of AI-specific content somewhere else, you have an appealing shortcut.

There’s another important difference between traditional search and AI search: Google isn’t the only significant actor here. As I’ve explored previously, digging into ChatGPT’s search sources, there isn’t a single, consistent retrieval process underpinning every AI-generated answer as well.

Different systems, search providers, tools, and retrieval pipelines can all play a role in what information is discovered and ultimately used.

Some of these processes inherit established “search” infrastructure, including the systems those that evaluate the relevance and reliability of information. But beyond Google or Bing, other sources behave differently.

That doesn’t mean ChatGPT or other AI systems automatically trust everything they’re given. But there is a considerable difference between having mechanisms to identify reliable sources and having the experience that traditional search engines have dealing with people attempting to manipulate those mechanisms.

We also need to separate the ability to retrieve information from the decision to trust it. When I looked at how curious agents actually are, I saw how they use the least effort to retrieve, summarize, and move on. Just because the right answer was available doesn’t mean it would always be found and used.

I don’t think it is fair or truth to claim that every AI-specific content format is a form of cloaking, or that making information available to agents is manipulative. Structured data, XML sitemaps, APIs, and product feeds have been part of the web for years. There are entirely legitimate reasons to give machines information in different formats – in fact, I’d insist you do it!

But there’s a difference between helping machines understand your business and creating content just to influence AI output. Especially when that content isn’t represented anywhere people can readily access.

The incentive to manipulate is obvious, and whilst AI search isn’t traditional search, the incentives of AI search providers are opposed to this. They need to return useful, accurate answers and maintain user trust. Why would they want to encourage an ecosystem of increasingly fragmented, difficult-to-verify sources that exist primarily to influence their answers?

Perhaps these systems will become better at validating information against other sources. Maybe agent-specific information will be treated with greater scrutiny, or its influence limited without independent corroboration.

We don’t know exactly how this will evolve, but assuming that today’s emerging practices will remain acceptable, effective, or even useful in the long term seems worryingly optimistic or naive.

We’re “solving” an old problem with a new shortcut, and if it is too good to be true, it may well be!

For years, content strategies have been constrained by what businesses want to publish and what their internal processes allow them to maintain. Often this creates a gap between the information customers want and the information brands make available.

Generative AI has made this more apparent and has been a significant multiplier here! People can now ask detailed, specific questions about products, services, properties, experiences, and practically anything else, expecting useful answers without having to navigate a website to piece the information together themselves. That creates a much greater demand for clear, accessible, and specific information.

If that information isn’t available, AI systems may have to look elsewhere or simply fail to answer the question correctly. So we do need to reconsider the information we’re publishing, how it’s structured and how different systems access it.

But why should the solution be to create another content estate? One that requires separate maintenance, governance, potentially another tech stack, and additional processes to ensure it remains consistent with the information customers can actually see?

Most struggle to keep one version of their website accurate and useful… I’m not sure creating a second, less visible one is the obvious solution.

I don’t think the answer is to reject agent-specific formats or insist that AI systems should consume websites in exactly the same way as people. That would ignore the genuine differences in how these systems operate, and the opportunities they create.

Equally, assuming the solution is simply to create content specifically for AI is avoiding a problem we’ve been trying to solve for years.

The goal should be to make genuinely useful information available to both, from a consistent, authoritative source that can be maintained, validated, and, where appropriate, independently verified.

Information that’s useful only to AI creates a governance problem and raises questions of trust.

Information that’s useful only to people increasingly creates a visibility problem.

There is a sensible middle ground here, and it doesn’t require us to invent another version of the web. I’ve written previously about why Google’s reluctance to endorse an entirely separate set of AI optimization practices makes sense. There’s little incentive for search providers to legitimize another industry of techniques they will then need to police.

The more we encourage brands to create information specifically for agents, detached from the public-facing web, the more we risk recreating problems that search engines have spent decades trying to solve.

I’m not convinced that building a shadow web for AI is progress. It may just be another shortcut we’ll spend the next decade trying to undo.

Scientists are still learning about a cosmic "string of pearls," a unique celestial relic left by a supernova explosion that humanity witnessed around 850 years ago.

That is thanks to the most detailed observation of the dandelion-shaped chains of gas and supernova debris that comprise Pa 30, the only supernova remnant of its type observed in the Milky Way. What makes Pa 30 even more unique is that it has a rare "zombie star" at its heart.

Supernovas like the one that created Pa 30 are called Type Ia supernovas; they happen when a dead star called a white dwarf overfeeds on a companion star like a cosmic vampire. This overfeeding triggers a runaway nuclear explosion that usually destroys the white dwarf. But on rare occasions, the supernova doesn't completely destroy the star, and a shattered stellar body remains, called a zombie star. These events are called Type Iax supernovas.

Pa 30 was first identified in 2013 by a citizen scientist in 2013, but scientists soon linked it to a cosmic explosion recorded independently in the year 1181 in Japanese, Chinese, and Arabic historical documentation. That makes this one of only five supernovas seen before the invention of the telescope.

"Reliable historical records of stellar explosions extend back only about a thousand years. For all we know, there might well be similar remnants still lurking in the dark in our own galaxy," team member Ilaria Caiazzo, assistant professor at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria (ISTA), said in a statement.

"Today, we are lucky to study an intriguing object we know was observed by astronomers who made three independent historical records of the event centuries before the invention of the telescope."

Pa 30 is a good laboratory to study supernovas because, at around 7,500 light-years away, it is relatively close to Earth on cosmic scales. For instance, the heart of our galaxy is around 26,000 light-years away. "We can see this much detail because of Pa 30's proximity to Earth," said Caiazzo. "This proximity makes Pa 30 uniquely valuable for study."

It is also an important subject of study because it is the only supernova remnant in which we can see a zombie star.

Using the Gemini North telescope, Caiazzo and colleagues zoomed in on Pa 30, discovering knots along the strands of gas radiating out from the site of the cosmic explosion, like pearls on a string.

These pearls are each wide enough to encompass the orbit of Neptune, the most distant planet from the sun, ten times over.

"The planetary region of our solar system could fit in each knot about ten times with room to spare," said Caiazzo. "The knots are quite strikingly uniform. We are excited to try to model them."

The team also discovered that the zombie star sits almost perfectly at the heart of Pa 30. When these cosmic explosions happen, they should provide any debris, including zombie stars, with a kick. The fact that this "living dead" star still sits dead center of this wreckage suggests that when this explosion happened, it was roughly symmetrical and the kick was relatively light.

"Explosions conserve momentum. If the explosion itself was perfectly symmetric, the ejecta should also be symmetric, and the surviving star should not receive a substantial kick," Caiazzo said. "We were able to measure a precise upper limit of the remnant star's transverse kick speed, showing that it was surprisingly small. We practically don't see the star moving away at all."

While there are still many mysteries surrounding Pa 30 and its zombie occupant, one of the most exciting prospects emerging from this research is the possibility it will help identify the wreckage of other Type Iax supernovas in our galaxy.

"While we can see Type Iax supernovas in distant galaxies as point sources of light, resolving the remnants of such explosions is only possible in our own galaxy and in very few nearby ones," Caiazzo concluded. "Eventually, we plan to use Pa 30's distinctive properties to search the Milky Way and nearby galaxies for similar objects."The team's research was published in The Astrophysical Journal.

#557. The Ocean’s Mysterious “Snow” Could Be Hiding a Powerful Climate Secret

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A surprising property of the tiny particles sinking through the ocean could help determine how much carbon is stored in the deep sea. Scientists have discovered that the stickiness of marine snow affects how easily bacteria can break it down, potentially changing how much carbon reaches the ocean depths.

Researchers at UC San Diego’s Scripps Institution of Oceanography and the University of Lincoln found that particularly gooey marine snow resists bacterial degradation. Their findings, published October 8 in Science, suggest that highly viscous particles can transport more than six times as much carbon to the deep ocean as less viscous particles.

Marine snow consists of tiny clumps of dead plankton, waste, mucus, minerals, and other debris that continuously descend through the water column. As these particles sink, they carry carbon from the sunlit surface ocean toward deeper waters, where some of it can remain stored for hundreds or thousands of years.

Scientists estimate that marine snow transports more than 10 gigatons (11 billion U.S. tons) of carbon away from the surface each year. However, only about 2 gigatons (2.2 billion U.S. tons) reach depths where the carbon can remain stored for extended periods. Much of the sinking material is broken down or recycled before reaching those depths.

The new research identifies particle viscosity, a measure of resistance to flow, as an important factor in this process. Marine snow particles vary in composition and consistency, and those differences influence how bacteria colonize and consume them.

Understanding these interactions required a technique that had previously been unavailable: directly observing individual living bacteria as they navigate the microscopic structure of mucus.

“Observing individual bacteria interacting with the physical structure of mucus had never been possible,” said Bryce Inman, who began investigating the problem in 2021 while working as a postdoctoral scholar at Scripps Oceanography.

After four years of unsuccessful attempts, Inman and his colleagues, including Scripps microbiologist and professor emeritus Farooq Azam, developed a working approach in 2025. By combining 3D confocal microscopy with specialized fluorescent molecules, they could map the intricate physical structure of marine snow and track how bacteria interacted with it. The imaging technique is described in a companion study published in Nature Communications.

The images showed that bacteria colonize less viscous regions near the surface of marine snow particles more easily than the thicker, more resistant material inside. These internal regions present a physical barrier that bacteria must gradually break down before penetrating farther into the particle.

“Bacteria can latch on to less viscous portions of each particle, but cannot reach more viscous regions until they break it down,” Inman explained. “Think of it like a worm in an apple: the flesh is easy to penetrate, but the core is tough to chew through”

Laboratory experiments confirmed that more viscous marine snow takes longer for bacteria to degrade and fragment. Computer simulations further suggested that these resistant particles can sink to greater depths before breaking apart. Together, the findings indicate that viscosity can substantially influence how much carbon escapes recycling in shallower waters and reaches the deep ocean.

The newly developed imaging method could also help researchers investigate interactions between bacteria and mucus in other environments. Similar relationships occur in coral reefs, kelp forests, plant root systems, and the digestive tracts of humans and other animals.

“Mucus, slime, whatever you want to call it, has a surprising amount of structure,” Inman said. “There are bulges, crevasses, all this sort of structure to it that we really had no idea about at a scale smaller than bacteria. This is the first time we’re really examining this physical habitat.”

By making these microscopic environments visible, the technique could support research into how bacteria colonize mucus, interact with its physical structure, and influence processes ranging from ocean carbon storage to coral reef recovery and human health.

Hackers from the UNC5537 group claimed responsibility for the 2024 breach, part of a larger hacking spree targeting the Snowflake databases of more than 160 companies. UNC5537is a close affiliate of ShinyHunters, a notorious hacker group that frequently targets corporations with large stores of customer information. Two individuals were arrested in connection with the case.

While the exploits used in 2024 have been thoroughly documented and patched, hackers continue to target Snowflake servers through third-party partners, as seen in the recent Rockstar server hack.
