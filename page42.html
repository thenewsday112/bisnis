A clash on the restive border between Afghanistan and Pakistan killed one person and injured five, Pakistan officials said Wednesday (October 28, 2026).

The countries went to war earlier this year and, with no permanent resolution reached on security issues, there have been sporadic attacks since a March truce.

Pakistan accuses Afghanistan of harbouring militants responsible for deadly attacks on its territory, an allegation the Taliban government in Afghanistan denies.

Homes and shops were targeted in the market town of Angoor Adda during the cross-border fire from Afghanistan, according to the office of the deputy commissioner in Pakistan's South Waziristan district in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Province.

It blamed the "unprovoked" attack on Afghanistan's Taliban authorities.

A senior administrative official in South Waziristan, speaking on condition of anonymity, told AFP the casualties were caused by mortar fire from Afghanistan.

Pakistani forces responded with a "brief operation targeting the Afghan side with artillery fire", targeting an Afghan checkpoint, the official said.

They said they could not confirm any casualties on the Afghan side of the border.

A security official in Angoor Adda also confirmed the attack.

The governor of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Province, Faisal Karim Kundi, condemned the "unprovoked and blatant aggression by the Afghan Taliban regime against civilian homes".

Hundreds of civilians have been killed in fighting between the two countries since October last year, according to figures from the United Nations' mission in Afghanistan.

#69. Flávio Bolsonaro calls himself the ‘leader of Brazil’ after fleeing to Miami

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Far-right senator and family land in US after crossing from Brazil into Argentina and then flying via Ecuador

Flávio Bolsonaro declared himself the “true president” of Brazil after losing last Sunday’s presidential election and fleeing to the United States.

After landing on a private jet with his family in Miami, Florida, on Wednesday morning, the far-right senator published the claim in a letter – even though his own party has recognised the legitimate victory of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, as have all of Brazil’s institutions, and international observers who monitored and endorsed the vote.

Brazil’s three main newspapers – Folha de S Paulo, O Globo and O Estado de S Paulo – all described Bolsonaro’s declaration as “coup-mongering”.

The senator later deleted the letter and published a new one in which, instead of calling himself the “true president”, he described himself as “the leader of Brazil”.

After losing Sunday’s runoff vote to Lula, Bolsonaro disappeared for more than 24 hours, until Argentina’s migration authorities said he had entered the country by land on Tuesday night.

From there he boarded a Gulfstream IV, registered LV-JQW, which had been parked at Puerto Iguazú airport since the early hours of the previous morning, when the first reports emerged in Argentinian media that he intended to flee on it.

Bolsonaro, the son of the jailed former Brazilian president Jair Bolsonaro, took off on the jet with his wife and two teenage daughters at about 11.30pm, bound for Guayaquil in Ecuador, reportedly to avoid Brazilian airspace. They made a brief stop in Ecuador and continued on to the Miami-Opa Locka airport, a private aviation hub about 11 miles north of downtown Miami catering to corporate executives and wealthy travelers.

Bolsonaro, wearing a black bulletproof vest and looking somber, and his family emerged from the plane into a postcard-perfect sunny south Florida morning at 9.24am. They briefly remained inside the airport before leaving, shielded by black umbrellas, climbing into a dark gray Ford Expedition Max and speeding away under police escort.

Bolsonaro said nothing to reporters before boarding the vehicle and the mood was low-key, without any supporters present to welcome him and only two dozen journalists who were kept at a distance by two Customs and Border Protection (CBP) agents.

In the letter, published about seven hours after his arrival, Bolsonaro also thanked Argentina’s president, Javier Milei, “for his courage, sense of justice and legality”, and Donald Trump “and the wonderful American people for welcoming me”.

He again repeated, without evidence, his claim that there had been “electoral fraud” – a claim rejected by Brazil’s electoral court and supreme court, as well as the presidents of the lower house and senate. Bolsonaro’s party said on Monday: “We recognise the presidential result.”

At least three different versions of the letter were published by Bolsonaro and his allies, including one that used the country’s pre-1968 name and the English spelling “Brazil” rather than the Portuguese “Brasil” – which some saw as another sign of the Bolsonaro clan’s subservience to the US.

Brazilian newspapers reported that Bolsonaro and his family had been granted humanitarian visas in the US. On Tuesday night the US president said he would approve their move to the country.

“They’re fine people. … I didn’t take sides, you know. I know them both. I know Lula. I know Flávio a little bit. I know the father very well,” the US president said.

On Wednesday, however, when asked about Bolsonaro’s claim, Trump said he would stay out of Brazil’s post-election turmoil.

“Well, I’m not going to make a comment about that, but I know that we gave him a pass to come into the country, so we’ll see,” he said. “But I won’t get involved. It was a very strange election, that’s for sure.”

Lula called Bolsonaro’s flight “shameful”. “This is what the Bolsonaro family are like. Brazil is their second homeland. Their first homeland is licking the boots of the US, as they’ve become accustomed to doing,” he said at an event celebrating his 81st birthday in Brasília.

Some in the Brazilian government suspected Bolsonaro allies had spent Tuesday privately telling the press he was already on his way to the US to distract from his real plan to go through Argentina. The federal police are facing criticism for failing to prevent him from leaving the country.

There is a gap between the last time he was seen by allies, at about 6pm on Monday, and the moment he entered Argentina late on Tuesday. It remains unclear how or when he travelled to the border, or where he was during that time.

Footage from a television channel there shows two vans crossing the border checkpoint and then being escorted by airport security officers to the airport.

The Argentinian newspaper Página/12 reported that the aircraft belonged to the businessman Sergio Daniel Mastropietro, who was investigated by local police in one of the biggest scandals of Javier Milei’s administration, involving the alleged payment of bribes linked to the purchase of medicines for people with disabilities.

Bolsonaro is under investigation in at least two criminal cases, one of them examining suspicions that the senator was involved in a money-laundering scheme worth about £680m ($905m) involving betting companies.

It was in connection with that case that, shortly after the election result, Brazilian police asked the supreme court to order Bolsonaro’s passport to be retained over concerns he could flee, citing an interview given last Friday by his brother, the former congressman Eduardo Bolsonaro.

Speaking on a podcast in the US, where he is in self-imposed exile and lobbying the Trump administration against Lula, Eduardo said if his brother were to lose the election, he should not concede and should go to the US.

O Globo newspaper reported that the escape had been planned for weeks as a contingency in case he lost, and that Eduardo had been its main organiser, holding talks with US officials about receiving the senator.

Fleeing Brazil has almost become standard practice among Bolsonaro supporters facing criminal charges or convictions in the country: about a dozen politicians or influencers closely aligned with Jair Bolsonaro have done the same in recent years.

The former president himself spent three months in Florida shortly after losing the 2022 election. He later returned to Brazil and, while facing trial over the attempted coup, spent two nights at the Hungarian embassy in Brasília, then run by his ally Viktor Orbán.

While under house arrest after being sentenced to 27 years in prison, the patriarch of the Bolsonaro clan used a soldering iron in an attempt to destroy his electronic ankle monitor.

#283. New book makes fiery claims about Jared Kushner’s finances in Trump’s first term

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Kushner threatens to sue, denying allegations that two slush funds were set up to influence his Middle East role

A new book containing incendiary claims about Jared Kushner’s financial dealings during Donald Trump’s first term has ignited a political and media firestorm.

The book by author Matthew Cole is called Princes and Thieves: Jared Kushner and Mohamed bin Zayed’s Secret Plot to Remake the Middle East and was released in the US this week. An excerpt was published by Vanity Fair last Thursday, and immediately drew a furious response from Kushner, who called its claims “completely insane”. The book contains numerous allegations about Kushner, whose father-in-law is Trump and who has served as one of his top diplomatic envoys in the Middle East – the most explosive of which are claims linking him to huge bank accounts allegedly funded by Gulf states.

Kushner denounced the book on his site last week, and writing on X: “We denied every one of these claims on the record, yet the author failed to provide a single shred of evidence to substantiate any of them.” He has demanded that Vanity Fair retract the excerpt and has threatened legal action against the book’s publisher, Simon & Schuster.

He added: “During the period in question, I was one of the most scrutinized people in the world, subjected to intense media coverage, multiple congressional investigations, and a special counsel investigation. I voluntarily cooperated with these investigations, providing over 30 hours of testimony. None of these allegations ever surfaced, because none of these events ever happened.”

The Guardian contacted Kushner’s legal counsel from Clare Locke as well as Chad Mizelle for comment and received no response.

Cole’s sources are predominantly anonymous and he does not print images of emails or other documentation to support his reporting. Extensive footnotes indicate sources of information, including confidential interviews.

In the book, the top intelligence official in the United Arab Emirates, Ali Al Shamsi, is reported to have called Kushner “the most expensive employee we’ve ever had”. The book details alleged payments into two “slush fund” bank accounts allegedly set up for Kushner by Emirati prince Mohamed bin Zayed (MBZ) and Saudi Arabian prince Mohammed bin Salman (MBS) during Trump’s first term. The Guardian contacted the Saudi Arabian and Emirati embassies for comment and did not receive a response.

In the book, Cole claims the accounts were partially managed by Kushner on an iPhone hacked by Russian and Dutch intelligence.

But despite the Emirati spymaster’s opinion of Kushner, Cole alleges that the New Jersey real estate scion was not entirely trusted. Cole describes a dangerous world in which blackmail is as common a tool of statecraft as torture and murder, as in the cases of the Saudi journalist Jamal Khashoggi, killed on the orders of MBS in Turkey in 2018, and the Emirati journalist and poet Habib Al Sayegh, whose killing by agents of MBZ in 2019 is described by Cole.

Kushner continues to steer US foreign policy despite having no official role, with questions about his business interests accompanying him as he travels the world with New York real estate magnate Steve Witkoff, this week seeking peace in Ukraine, if on terms favorable to Russia.

In response, an attorney “who has represented President Trump in multiple defamation lawsuits”, Alejandro Brito, told Cole that, “during his time working for President Trump, Kushner ‘always represented and sought the interests of the United States and the President of the United States and did not make any agreements or arrangements with leaders or officials in foreign countries that were not agreed by the President of the United States and in the best interests of the United States.’” He further said that “[a]ny allegation that Kushner knew of, had access to, had authorization for or engaged in or directed any conduct related to any government-funded or government-linked bank account is totally false and defamatory”.

Brito told Cole that “‘there are no accounts and never were.’” Cole writes that the Dutch government refused to comment in response to his questions about the alleged hacking of Kushner’s phone.

According to Cole, MBZ and MBS wanted Kushner to help achieve their “vision of a new Middle East, one in which Israel would become an integral part of the region, recognized diplomatically by Gulf Arabs today and eventually by the rest of the Arab world; the Palestinians would end their conflict with Israel and accept a peace built on prosperity; Islamic extremism would be defeated; and Iran’s expansionism and militancy would retreat, either by persuasion or force.”

Nearly 10 years later, the US and Israel are planning new strikes in their eight-month war with Iran; Saudi Arabia is at war with Houthi rebels in Yemen, and Israel has killed more than 74,000 Palestinians in Gaza since the Hamas attacks of 7 October 2023.

Kushner entered Trump’s first administration with his wife, Ivanka Trump, as close presidential advisers. MBZ and MBS, Cole claims, saw a chance to secure the best access money could buy. Cole alleges that in early 2017 the two princes “proposed to give Kushner a fund, a bank account, which the two leaders, working with Jared, would use to identify and pay for projects and foreign policy goals to advance for President Trump’s benefit”.

According to Cole, an idea to fund Trump’s proposed wall on the US border with Mexico apparently did not work out but the author claims that “MBZ and MBS initially, and then MBZ alone, put at least $2bn into at least two banks for Kushner.” Classifying the accounts as “Alpha” and “Beta”, Cole claims the Alpha account was held at First Abu Dhabi Bank, the largest in UAE, and contained “roughly $750m” to be “used as a foreign policy fund” overseen by Kushner and MBZ and meant to help end the Israel-Palestine conflict.

Cole quotes a source who says he knew of the UAE’s alleged deal with Kushner as saying: “‘Jared was looking for shortcuts in his diplomacy.’” Cole writes: “This person held discussions with both senior UAE and Israeli officials about the secret financial arrangement between Kushner, the UAE, and, at least initially, Saudi Arabia. ‘Jared promised MBZ that he’d push their agenda with Trump if they participated in a fund. He wanted normalization with Israel so that business could take off,’” the source alleged.

The same source claimed that “a slush fund – a shared pool of money for off-the-books diplomatic objectives – was a common form of Gulf Arab government corruption … ‘The Emiratis and the Saudis do this once a year. A billion or two is nothing for them, and if it gets them what they want, it’s money well spent.’”

Citing “a person briefed on the Beta account”, Cole claims it contained “roughly $1bn” and at the suggestion of the Israeli prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu, was moved by MBZ to the Israeli Central Bank via Azerbaijan, a favored conduit for laundering money.

“It is … unclear how Kushner used the money the UAE deposited into the Beta account for him,” Cole alleges, “but according to the person with knowledge of the money, its allocation was for Kushner’s personal benefit.”

Kushner’s attorney, Brito, told Cole his client “had no financial relationships with any foreign countries or leaders”, adding that his “complete financial disclosures are publicly available in his OGE [office of government ethics] form 278e filings during this period.”

Cole alleges that the “Alpha” account established for Kushner in Trump’s first term was drained by failed schemes and Kushner’s access was cut off. “It is unclear if the Israeli government allowed Kushner access to the Beta bank account” after the end of Trump’s first term, Cole writes.

Cole also examines the widely reported story about how Kushner ultimately secured Qatari investment in 666 Fifth Avenue, a marquee Manhattan property, thereby profiting from interests on both sides of a bitterly divided Middle East, given Saudi Arabia and UAE’s rivalry with Qatar. (Kushner has denied engaging in any conflicts of interest.)

In Trump’s second term, Kushner’s diplomatic work has been dogged by questions about another massive financial asset: a $2bn investment by the Saudi sovereign wealth fund in Kushner’s Affinity Partners private equity firm, made after Trump left power in 2021. Cole quotes an unnamed Arab official and Affinity investor who claimed: “We don’t pay Jared because he’s a savvy investor. We pay him to keep him happy. That keeps his father-in-law happy.”

Cole writes: “Deep relationships with MBZ, MBS, Emir Al Thani [of Qatar], and Bibi Netanyahu were useful as [Kushner] shuttled between capitals, trying to find a way to end the daily slaughter in Gaza. But as it had been when he was a government employee, there was little record of what his investors were negotiating with him.”

In his epilogue, Cole writes that Kushner “declined multiple requests for an on-the-record interview for the book”, adding that he “first contacted him with a list of questions in May 2026 and followed up via email twice more, asking additional questions.”

Through his lawyers, Kushner denounced all of Cole’s reporting. Also on X, Chad Mizelle, a Trump administration lawyer who identified himself as “Jared’s attorney”, said: “The central allegation in this book is categorically false. These are not questions of interpretation or competing recollections: there are no bank accounts. They don’t exist. These are egregious and defamatory claims without a shred of evidence to substantiate them.”

The EU has backed away from its offer to Canada to start talks on becoming an “associate member” after disagreements between member states just as an EU-Canada summit was set to begin in Montreal.

A draft of a joint statement to be issued after the end of the summit, which runs Thursday and Friday, showed a watered-down version offering “an ever closer association of Canada to the EU, short of full membership itself”.

The issue was triggered after the European Commission president, Ursula von der Leyen, surprised nearly everyone by announcing in last month’s state of the union address that the EU would consider “associate membership” for Canada.

The declaration had been hailed as a landmark in EU history, offering a non-EU country such close ties to the bloc, which has not allowed any state membership since Croatia joined in 2013.

It was also seen as a sign that the call by Canada’s prime minister, Mark Carney, at the Davos summit in January for “middle powers” to work together was paying off.

But instead of announcing a work programme on developing “associate membership”, the draft communique, seen by the Guardian, states the two sides would “work to define by the 2027 EU-Canada summit the concept of an ever closer association of Canada to the EU, short of full membership itself, to reflect, through this even closer association, our common endeavour and cooperation towards our shared goals of prosperity, economic security and strategic autonomy”.

Associate membership had been mooted, and rejected, previously for countries such as Ukraine.

Von der Leyen’s declaration caught EU capitals by surprise and led to pushback politically and legally. Given “associate status” has no definition, diplomats wondered about such issues as whether Canada could join the European Council, legislative bodies or decision-making processes without a vote.

At a meeting on Monday, ambassadors in the EU raised concerns about the dramatic offer, but vowed to make efforts to seek a diplomatic path to rescinding the offer of “associate” membership without embarrassing Von der Leyen. It is understood that Italy suggested “associate membership as proposed by the commission”, with the definition to be thrashed out later by member states.

Germany walked back further, suggesting the wording “an ever closer association of Canada” to the EU.

“Nobody was consulted about associate membership apart from [Von der Leyen’s] closest staff, it would seem. Many member states, including Germany, were not happy. This is all about fixing the plane while it is flying,” said one diplomat.

Germany had argued that “associate membership” should be reserved as an interim step for prospective candidate countries, such as Ukraine or Montenegro, noting that the status has no formal grounding in current EU treaties.

Donald Trump, the US president, previously called the EU’s proposal to make Canada an associate member “laughable”, and said if he ​interpreted the move as a “hostile act” the US might stop trading with Europe “on many things”.

A bitter trade war with the US, a longtime ally, has seen Carney seek out new trading partnerships around the world. Ahead of the summit in Montreal, one of Canada’s largest banks released a report suggesting there was “meaningful” upside for Canada if it were to deepen economic ties with Europe. Tens of billions of dollars in additional trade would be possible if barriers were removed in areas of overlapping interest such as defence, energy, critical minerals and digital markets, TD Economics said.

Carney has spoken specifically about the need to lessen reliance on Elon Musk‘s Starlink satellite network, following a decision to throttle its usage over Ukraine.

Earlier this week, Carney posted on social media that Canada would join the popular music competition Eurovision. But despite fears raised by political rivals, Carney has said Canada has no interest in formally joining the EU. Changing Canadian laws to bring it in line with European nations would probably prove unpopular, despite public support for stronger ties.

Carney told reporters on Tuesday that the summit this week was the launch of further formal negotiations that will take place another year.

“The good news is it doesn’t matter about ⁠specific names or nomenclature,” Carney said. “What matters is the difference it will ​make to the lives ​of Canadians.”

A high-speed passenger train slammed into the back of a stationary train in Spain on Wednesday morning (Spanish time), in one of the deadliest train crashes in Spanish history. As authorities investigate what went wrong, here’s what we know about the rail disaster.

The crash occurred near Urda, in the province of Toledo, about 130 kilometres south of Madrid, in central Spain.

It involved two high-speed trains travelling in the same direction from Madrid to Seville. One train was run by Spain’s state-owned railway company, Renfe, while the other – which was stationary – was run by Iryo, a private operator.

According to Spain’s Transport Minister, Óscar Puente, a computer failure at 8.30am on Wednesday brought six trains to a halt along a 63-kilometre stretch of track, including the two trains involved in the crash.

Shortly afterwards, a rail control centre worker ordered the drivers to start up again and continue “as normal” (meaning at high speed). But the driver of the Iryo train reported he was having difficulty starting back up and so was stationary.

This was reportedly not communicated to the other drivers, and at 8.53am, the Renfe train slammed into the stationary train, derailing several carriages and sending two down an embankment.

According to Spanish media, the two trains were carrying a total of 544 passengers.

At least 68 people have been confirmed dead, and authorities warn the toll could rise as rescuers search the wreckage.

The latest reports indicate at least 108 people were injured, including 58 seriously. A major rescue operation is ongoing at the crash site near Urda as hundreds of anxious families await news.

The Spanish government’s early statements indicate that as many as six trains on the same line had been stopped at some point on Wednesday morning due to problems with the signalling system.

Puente described the decision to allow the Renfe train to proceed, despite a red signal, as inexplicable. He said the train drivers were not to blame.

Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez travelled to the scene within hours of the crash.

“It’s not possible for a country like Spain to suffer two high-speed train accidents in one year,” he said.

He said that while Wednesday’s accident and a fatal January crash on the same line seemed unrelated, the country’s rail systems needed to be investigated and improved.

Sánchez declared three days of national mourning, saying all of Spain was grieving. He promised a thorough investigation and pledged support for victims and their families.

In January, 46 people were killed near Adamuz, in the province of Córdoba, when a high-speed Iryo train derailed and collided with an oncoming Renfe service, which triggered a debate about the level of investment in railway maintenance in recent years.

The accident is still under investigation. A preliminary report by Spain’s Civil Guard in February pointed to defects in the track, rail or weld between two sections of track as the main hypothesis for the accident, but kept seven other theories open.

The second deadly accident in less than a year will probably raise further questions over the country’s train infrastructure ahead of November’s snap election.

In July 2013, 80 people died when a train derailed on a sharp bend near Santiago de Compostela in north-western Spain while travelling at excessive speed, slamming into a concrete wall and bursting into flames.

A Transport Ministry commission concluded that accident was caused by excessive speed and the driver being distracted by a call from a superior, but a victims’ association blamed the accident not only on human error, but also on the railway’s security systems.

Accidents, including derailments and collisions, increased from 45 in 2016 to 57 in 2024 and 69 last year, according to Spain’s railway safety authority.

In the same period, problems such as deterioration of, and breaks in, the rails rose from 494 to 667.

Spain spent an average of about €1.5 billion ($2.38 billion) a year from 2018 to 2022 on its high-speed network, more than any other country.

However, the vast majority of the money went to new infrastructure, with only about 16 per cent earmarked for maintenance, renewal and upgrades.
