In a letter to Bard leadership, Rep. Jamie Raskin said he had "damning evidence that Botstein likely enabled and possibly even participated in Epstein’s abuse while serving as Bard’s President." He also said Botstein may have interfered with Title IX proceedings at Bard and protected "sexual predators" on its faculty, citing whistleblower allegations.

Botstein, who helmed Bard for over five decades, resigned as its president this summer amid intense scrutiny over his association with Epstein.

The same day as Raskin's letter, Bard Board Chair Brandon Weber announced the college was "undertaking a full review" of the lawmakers' allegations. Botstein, who had still held a professorship at Bard and positions within the colleges' music programs, stepped down from those roles effective immediately, Weber said.

Both Raskin and Weber said Bard has been cooperating with the committee's investigation, and Weber said the college’s leaders would continue to do so.

A few weeks later, Bard announced it had retained FTI Consulting as one of its first steps to review the allegations in Raskin's letter.

The firm's Oct. 19 audit found that the 2014 email exchange was not on Bard's servers as far back as 2017. FTI Consulting also highlighted inconsistent formatting and timestamps in the conversation as it appears in the DOJ's files and noted that the responses attributed to Botstein only appeared as in-text threaded messages, rather than as independent emails.

The audit did not address legislators' Title IX or faculty abuse allegations.

"Our review of the other claims in Representative Raskin’s letter remains ongoing, and we are committed to establishing the facts," Bard College said in an Oct. 22 statement. "In the meantime, we will continue our work to provide a secure and respectful learning environment for everyone at Bard."

Botstein's relationship with Epstein — and his downplaying of it to Bard's board and campus — have been a source of consternation for the college for months.

An independent investigation from law firm WilmerHale earlier this year found Botstein and Epstein were in frequent contact, both remotely and in person, from 2012 to 2019. During that period, Botstein made a trip to Epstein's private island and visited his townhome more than two dozen times.

Epstein made donations to the college — the details of which Botstein obfuscated from the board — and visited Bard twice, according to WilmerHale. He also paid Botstein under a consulting agreement.

The WilmerHale investigation found that Botstein did nothing illegal, and its summary did not address the emails central to Raskin's Oct. 7 letter.

Under the agreement, the graduate institution will continue operating separately, led by its own board and senior officials.

According to a Pomona FAQ on the deal, the college will provide "financial and strategic" expertise to CGU, which is restructuring its academic portfolio and cutting costs to address its budget deficit. CGU will also gain access to "favorable terms" for financing through its affiliation with Pomona, the FAQ said.

Pomona is not taking on CGU’s debts and liabilities, nor is it planning to use its operating budget to fund the graduate university’s operations, according to the FAQ. CGU will also pay for administrative services from Pomona.

The graduate university has been struggling financially, with a $7.8 million budget deficit projected for this fiscal year. Earlier this month, it announced it would offer voluntary buyouts to faculty and staff to help close the gap.

The voluntary buyouts come on the heels of an academic overhaul at CGU, which plans to consolidate five schools and two centers into just three academic units. Those will be organized around three subject areas: management and technology; education, health and psychology; and arts humanities and mathematics.

According to the FAQ, Pomona serves as a “thought partner” to CGU’s leadership on the academic transformation and has made outside advisers available to the graduate university. Both institutions must accept the academic overhaul plan that CGU is developing before the deal closes.

CGU has struggled with enrollment. In fall 2024, it enrolled 1,771 students, down 18% from a decade prior, according to federal data. Pomona is similarly sized, with 1,700 students in fall 2024. However, that marks a 3% increase from a decade before.

Pomona also has the stronger finances of the two. In fiscal 2025, it logged a $16.9 million operating surplus on $258.5 million of revenue, according to its latest publicly available financial audit. It also ended the year with $3.8 billion in assets.

CGU, on the other hand, had an operating surplus of $4.6 million in fiscal 2025 on $68.8 million in revenue. It ended the year with $290 million in assets.

Both institutions are part of The Claremont Colleges, a consortium of seven higher education institutions on 600 acres of connected land.

“As the founding member of the Consortium, Pomona has a lasting interest in its strength and stability,” the college said in the FAQ. “This affiliation keeps graduate education in Claremont connected to an institution committed to the Consortium’s mission and collaborative model.”

The deal is pending regulatory and accreditor approval, as well as sign off from Claremont Colleges for CGU to continue being a member of the consortium.

#18. Ceasefire extended across Ukraine's Kherson region, officials say

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The people of Oleshky have had no electricity for years and no access to food or water

A ceasefire that began with the aim of getting food and medicine to the Russian-occupied Ukrainian city of Oleshky has been extended across the wider southern region of Kherson, according to sources.

Several thousand residents are trapped in the city with no access to food, water and electricity. On one side is the Dnipro river, while Russian forces are on the other and all the roads out are too dangerous.

However, no breaches of the truce that began early on Tuesday have so far been reported.

US First Lady Melania Trump, who helped broker the ceasefire along with Pope Leo XIV, said on Tuesday that the area involved had been expanded to provide "safe passage" for humanitarian convoys.

What we know about Ukraine-Russia truce in Oleshky, brokered by Melania Trump

That was later corroborated by the press secretary to Ukraine's public ombudsman, and by Oleksandr Tolokonnikov, deputy head of Kherson, who said Russian shelling had stopped both in Kherson city, which is in Ukrainian hands, and the wider region.

Under the deal, the ceasefire will be followed by deliveries of humanitarian aid to Oleshky and then evacuations, planned for Friday, for anyone wishing to leave.

An estimated 1,700 to 2,000 people remain in Oleshky itself and a few thousand more in surrounding areas.

Many residents have resorted to cooking garden weeds to survive, telling the BBC they are suffering extreme hunger and want to leave.

Both sides said the ceasefire was being observed. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said everything was proceeding "according to plan", and paid tribute to US mediation and in particular representatives of Melania Trump.

Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha said if the ceasefire produced results, it could "pave the way for other steps in the peace process".

A key official in President Volodymyr Zelensky's parliamentary party, David Arakhamia, said it would continue until 22:59 Ukrainian time on Sunday 1 November, and described the truce as the "largest de-escalation since 2022".

Before the war Oleshky was home to 24,000 people, but it was seized by Russia during the first days of its full-scale invasion in February 2022.

No shops are open, and the city has had no electricity, gas or water since the Kakhovka Dam was blown up in 2023, flooding the area and killing many people in Oleshky itself.

Conditions have become so desperate that not only is getting supplies into the city extremely difficult, but escaping is too risky because the routes out of Oleshky have been mined and subject to drone attack.

One woman told the BBC she had stepped on a landmine and was currently in hospital where she had undergone two operations: "I don't know how long I will have to spend there," she said, worried about not being able to join the evacuation.

Pope Leo appealed earlier this month to Russia's President Vladimir Putin to allow food and medical aid into Oleshky as well as a safe evacuation, and urged his Ukrainian counterpart Volodymyr Zelensky to co-operate with Moscow over a ceasefire.

Then the White House said last week that Melania Trump had secured a ceasefire.

On Tuesday, the US first lady said on X that the area covered by the ceasefire "has expanded beyond Oleshky, within the Kherson region, to provide safe passage for the humanitarian convoys".

These residents were able to get out of Oleshky in spring this year

The current truce is supposed to enable roads to be cleared of mines for supplies to get into the city possibly on Thursday, and for residents to have safe passage out in an evacuation planned for Friday.

Russia's defence ministry said units from the army's Dnepr group had been conducting "engineering reconnaissance" and clearing main access roads into the village of "explosive and booby-trap devices".

Many details on the evacuation plans are unclear, and residents are wary of trusting officials with their names and addresses. However, a local volunteer told the BBC that about 500 people had already expressed interest by Tuesday afternoon.

Vladimir Saldo, the Russian-installed head of occupied areas of Kherson, said temporary accommodation had been prepared in the region itself as well as Russian-annexed Crimea and across the Russian border in the Rostov, Krasnodar and Stavropol regions.

Saldo told Russian media that most Oleshky residents wanted to leave the city and stay in Russia, although his account has not been corroborated on the ground.

Oleshky has been under Russian occupation since 2022 and its people are now trapped

It is hard to make contact with people there as communication is patchy and many are reluctant to speak of the situation in fear of reprisals from the Russians.

Those residents or relatives who have spoken to the BBC have expressed fears that Russia may not allow them to return to Ukraine, or might examine their phones and online activity for signs of Ukrainian support, which could put them in danger.

Ukraine's military general staff urged people not to trust unverified reports or spread rumours about "the routes, time and place of the movement of people and humanitarian convoys".

Zelensky has warned that routes out of Oleshky that are demined by Russia will ensure that those civilians leaving Oleshky will "head towards Russia and stay there".

He said some residents would get to Ukrainian-controlled territory "one way or another", and that ideally anyone trapped would be able to reach their destination and evacuate to Ukraine.

One resident called Serhii, who fled Oleshky through minefields around three weeks ago, said he had eventually returned to Ukraine via Russia and Belarus. He called for a route across the River Dnipro that avoided Russian-occupied territory, saying "you simply cannot trust the Russian Federation".

The Westerly tower at Hilton Aruba offers exclusivity, resort amenities and ocean views at a premium price.

I’ve stayed at several upscale hotels in Aruba, but the personal service and incredible views from The Westerly at Hilton Aruba Caribbean Resort made it one of my favorites. Private check-in and one of the warmest concierge teams I’ve encountered gave my stay the intimate feel of a boutique hotel with the amenities of a large resort.

Opened in May 2026, The Westerly is a 161-room luxury tower within the historic Hilton Aruba Caribbean Resort and Casino on Palm Beach. Guests have access to the larger resort’s pools, restaurants and activities, plus perks such as complimentary use of the sauna and other wellness amenities at Eforea Spa. Cash rates start around $660 or 235,000 Hilton points per night. There are no standard rooms at The Westerly, so you won’t be able to use Hilton free night certificates for a stay.

For an additional fee, guests can access Terrace on 10, an adults-only rooftop club with meals, an open bar and sweeping Caribbean views.

I was invited to experience The Westerly during a three-night stay in September 2026. Although the hotel covered the cost of my room and food, the opinions expressed in this review are my own.

The Westerly shares the Hilton Aruba Resort’s 15-acre beachfront property in Palm Beach’s high-rise hotel district. Dozens of restaurants, shops and nightlife venues are within walking distance.

The hotel is about a 15-minute drive from Queen Beatrix International Airport (AUA). Rideshare services don’t operate in Aruba, and a taxi to the resort will cost $40. One of my favorite things about Aruba is the fixed prices for taxis across the island. Prices are set by the government, and you can look up the cost of a ride from one location to another at Taxi.aw.

If you rent a car, parking at the resort is included for registered hotel guests.

Cash rates start around $660 per night for a garden-view room and $1,000 for a swim-up room. Oceanfront and swim-up suites start around $1,800. These rates include the $79 nightly resort fee, which is waived on stays booked entirely with Hilton Honors points.

Hilton doesn’t publish an award chart, and the number of points required for a free night varies but starts at 235,000 points per night. At that price, a comparable cash stay would need to cost $940 to match NerdWallet’s valuation of Hilton points at 0.4 cent each.

The Westerly’s rooms are currently categorized as Premium Room Rewards, making them ineligible for Hilton free night certificates, which require standard accommodations. If you want to stretch your points balance or use a free night certificate, check out the standard rooms at the Hilton Aruba Resort or the Embassy Suites Aruba Resort.

The Westerly’s rooms and suites feature coastal colors, light wood accents and abundant natural light. The accommodations are larger, and the common areas feel brighter and more inviting than those in the resort’s other towers.

I stayed on the fourth floor in an oceanfront suite with a king bed. The room was massive, the bed was extremely comfortable and the bright colors and natural light were so inviting that I found myself not wanting to leave the room.

The views from the balcony were outstanding. It was such a peaceful place to watch the sunrise and get some work done.

All rooms feature a Lavazza coffee machine, a mini-bar and a wellness kit with a canvas bag and local Aruba Aloe products.

The Westerly also has the resort’s only swim-up rooms and suites, which are adults-only. They offer direct access to a shared pool, though choosing one means giving up the elevated views and greater privacy of an upper-floor balcony.

Guests at The Westerly can pay $150 per person per day for access to Terrace on 10, an adults-only rooftop club. The upgrade includes:

A breakfast buffet with made-to-order omelets.

A sunset reception with entertainment and hors d’oeuvres.

The space features modern art, outdoor cabanas and lounge chairs overlooking Palm Beach and the Caribbean Sea. During my visits, it felt like a quiet retreat from the busier resort, with attentive service and few other guests.

I ate four meals at the club. While the breakfast selections were good and a step up from what you’ll find at Laguna (the resort’s breakfast spot), lunch was by far my favorite meal and included tomato carpaccio, spring rolls with peanut sauce, tuna crudo, roast beef sliders and a shrimp cocktail.

The daily $150 per person fee is steep, but could make sense if you:

Plan to wine and dine on-property for the duration of your stay.

Want the best view I’ve found anywhere on Palm Beach.

Crave a more exclusive, white-glove resort experience.

The resort’s dining options include the renovated Sunset Grille steakhouse and Laguna breakfast restaurant, plus the new beachfront Shore Club.

Sunset Grille was my favorite dinner spot of the stay. The steakhouse offers indoor and outdoor seating overlooking the resort, and both the sea bass and scallops were excellent.

The tableside gin cart added a memorable touch: Staff brought it over and prepared our cocktails in front of us. The chocolate cake was another highlight — good enough that I returned for it a second time at the end of my stay.

Opened in 2026, Shore Club is a two-story, open-air restaurant and bar steps from Palm Beach. It’s accessible to both resort guests and the general public and serves craft tropical cocktails, sushi, ceviche and burgers.

The rooftop opens for dinner, with a DJ creating a lively setting for sunset drinks. I enjoyed the sushi and cocktails, but the staff made the strongest impression. Our server was personable and energetic, and the bartender spoke multiple times about how much he loved making cocktails.

Laguna serves breakfast à la carte and from a buffet, with homemade pastries, fruit and made-to-order eggs and omelets.

Hilton Diamond members receive complimentary continental breakfast for up to two guests sharing a room, including fruit, pastries and cereal. During my stay, an upgrade to the full buffet and unlimited mimosas cost $14.

This was the one place where the resort felt crowded during my visit, as it’s the main breakfast spot at the resort. I enjoyed the breakfast burrito and the buffet’s fresh fruit, though the options and ambience at Terrace on 10 were superior.

The Westerly offers direct access to Palm Beach, with white sand and calm water that make for great swimming. I spent each morning in the water and saw multiple turtles and lots of tropical fish. Restaurants, beach bars and water sports operators line the shore near the resort, putting plenty of activities within walking distance.

The resort offers lounge chairs beneath palapas (thatched shade structures), with food and drinks available to order from your seat. Beach seating can be reserved through The Westerly’s concierge or the resort’s online booking system (for an additional fee that varies based on demand).

Guests at The Westerly share two main pools with the rest of the Hilton Aruba Resort. Both had recently been resurfaced, and there was enough space to spread out so that neither felt overly crowded during my stay.

The Westerly’s guests receive complimentary access to amenities of the Eforea Spa throughout their stay, including the relaxation area, sauna, cold plunge pool and steam room. There are 10 treatment rooms for services including facials and individual and couples massages, a full-service salon and a spacious fitness center.

The property’s Las Vegas-style casino includes one of the island’s largest poker rooms, slot machines and table games including craps, roulette and blackjack. Guests at The Westerly receive a voucher for $25 of match play for both the tables and slot machines.

There was no shortage of on-site activities throughout my stay, including a variety of fitness classes, yoga and morning paddleboarding in the Caribbean Sea. But my favorite was hanging out with the rescue birds at the property’s bird garden and sanctuary. There were around a dozen tropical birds, including macaws, parrots and a toucan. Staff open the sanctuary for visitors each afternoon, with an opportunity to feed and take photos with the birds.

The Westerly is worth considering if you want personal service and modern accommodations with the amenities of a large beachfront resort. My oceanfront suite and the warm, attentive staff made it one of my favorite hotel stays in Aruba.

The main drawback is the price, as a room at The Westerly will cost a few hundred dollars more than one with a comparable view at the Hilton Aruba Resort. Guests in the Hilton’s other towers share many of the same facilities, so compare rates before paying more for The Westerly’s rooms and additional perks. High award prices also make it a less compelling choice for travelers trying to stretch their Hilton points.

Terrace on 10 adds excellent food, sweeping views and a quiet place to unwind, but it’s a separate splurge. Its value depends on how much of your vacation you plan to spend enjoying the club.

There are performance improvements across the board in Ghostty 1.4 that yield significant gains compared to prior Ghostty versions.

The biggest win came from a surprising discovery: on macOS, the kernel caps every read from a pty at about 1 KiB no matter how large the read buffer is. Previously, Ghostty processed each read as it arrived, so per-read overhead was paid every kilobyte, and the program writing output sat blocked while we parsed. Ghostty now drains the pty on one thread while parsing on another, raising end-to-end I/O throughput by 25% to 55% on macOS (M4 Max, plain and Unicode text) over Ghostty 1.3.x.

VT processing itself is also much faster. In parser benchmarks, plain ASCII text went from 128 MB/s to 725 MB/s (5.7x), CJK text got 3.4x faster, and a 2.6 GB dataset of real-world terminal recordings from asciinema is processed about 25% faster. Together, these changes took cat-ing a 150 MB text file on macOS from 1.5 seconds to 566 milliseconds.

The GTK app has a new rendering pipeline. It no longer uses GtkGLArea. Instead, the renderer draws with EGL on its own thread, exports each frame as a DMABUF, and hands it to GTK to display. Rendering is now triple-buffered, which should greatly reduce tearing, and no OpenGL calls run on the main thread anymore, which let us remove a number of workarounds.

The most visible result is crisp text under fractional scaling. Text and box-drawing characters are no longer blurry with fractional display scaling such as 125% or 150% (#1938 ). We fixed this once before, but it came back after upstream GTK changes. Ghostty now renders at the window's true fractional scale, so every rendered pixel maps to exactly one pixel on screen.

The new render surface needs a system EGL library (see Changes for Package Maintainers). On GPU drivers that can't export DMABUFs, Ghostty falls back to copying frames through the CPU, which is slower but works.

#674. Dead Planets Orbiting a Dead Star

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

All stars die eventually, but they don't all die the same. A star's death is determined by its mass. Only the most massive stars die in supernovae explosions, and those calamitous explosions announce their death in a burst of pent-up light, an exclamation point marking the end of their lives of fusion.

But our Sun, a more moderately-sized star, will experience a much different end. As it burns its fuel and slowly loses mass, it well eventually lose it gravitational hold on its outer layers of gas. It will expand, and the outer layers of gas will be expelled into the space around star, creating a gorgeous planetary nebula, quite possibly Nature's most beautiful creation. The nebula will last tens of thousands of years, and after that, only a cinder-like white dwarf will remain. Fusion will be a distant memory for this stellar corpse, which will radiate only remnant heat. White dwarfs spend an astronomical eternity in this state, until they eventually cool enough to become a black dwarf. But the Universe hasn't existed long enough for any of those to form yet.

There's another part of this transition to white dwarf, and it's destructive. When a star like our Sun expands on its way to becoming a white dwarf, it engulfs and destroys its inner planets. In our case, the Sun will definitely destroy Mercury and Venus, and almost certainly destroy Earth.

When astronomers gaze out into the heavens, they find many planetary nebulae (a historic name that has nothing to do with planets), lit up by the white dwarfs in their centers. Sometimes, they find something else. Sometimes, they find a debris disk made up of the planets that once orbited the star. For a long time, astronomers have speculated that planets can form out of this debris, a strange, second chance at almost-life. It's highly unlikely that life could once again spawn on one of these resurrected planets orbiting a zombie star. But it's a huge Universe, so you never know.

Astronomers recently found a white dwarf surrounded by a debris disk that's split into two components. This could signal the presence of planets.

These findings are presented in research titled "A double debris disc around a white dwarf sculpted by two planetary bodies." #2163. Alibaba and the Forty Lobbyists, and the First Amendment

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From a long opinion yesterday by Judge Eumi Lee (N.D. Cal.) in Alibaba Group Holding Ltd. v. U.S. Dep't of Defense:

On February 13, 2026, the United States Department of Defense ("Department") publicly designated Alibaba Group Holding Limited as a "Chinese military company" ("CMC"). On June 30, 2026, 10 U.S.C. § 4663 went into effect, barring the Department from contracting with any company who retained the same lobbyist as a CMC. Alibaba asserts that Section 4663 has caused lobbyists to withdraw from representing Alibaba for fear of losing other clients.

The court issued a preliminary injunction, concluding § 4663 likely violates the Free Speech Clause and the Petition Clause:

Alibaba is an e-commerce and cloud-computing company that provides products, technology, infrastructure, and services to consumers and businesses for civilian and commercial use. Alibaba provides U.S. companies and businesses with a platform to sell their products to consumers in China. Additionally, it provides commercial cloud services to companies, including U.S. companies, operating in China and other countries. Alibaba has developed open-source AI models, including Qwen, with a focus on "commercial applications that make it more efficient and secure for businesses to sell goods to consumers in China." Alibaba is publicly listed on both the New York Stock Exchange and Hong Kong Stock Exchange.

"Prior to being designated [a CMC], Alibaba had retained over two dozen lobbyists and multiple lobbying firms to represent its interests before Congress and the Executive Branch" [including] … "… the Executive Office of the President, the Federal Communication Commission [("FCC")], the Federal Trade Commission, the Consumer Products Safety Commission, the Department of the Treasury, and [other entities]" …. "They presented Alibaba's views on legislation, rules, regulations, executive orders, and other government policies of commercial interest to the company. Specifically, they advocated regarding export expansion, small business export promotion, technology policy, access to U.S. capital markets, e-commerce-related issues, consumer-protection laws, and intellectual property." …

The Court finds that First Amendment scrutiny applies because Section 4663 regulates speech. By restricting Alibaba's ability to retain lobbyists, Section 4663 functionally restricts its political speech. "Discussion of public issues" is "pure speech" that is at "the core of the protection afforded by the First Amendment[.]" The First Amendment also protects "[t]he right to petition," that is, "the right of the people to inform their representatives in government of their desires with respect to the passage or enforcement of laws[.]"Lobbying government officials is therefore an activity fully protected by the First Amendment. FTC v. Super. Ct. Trial Laws. Ass'n (1990) ("It is, of course, clear that the association's efforts to … lobby District officials to enact favorable legislation … were activities that were fully protected by the First Amendment.")….

Here, Section 4663 appears to be directed at defense contractors, but its consequences are on Alibaba's speech. "[A] government official cannot do indirectly what she is barred from doing directly" by "coerc[ing] a private party to punish or suppress disfavored speech on her behalf." NRA v. Vullo (2024)…. Section 4663's impact falls on the relationship between Alibaba and the third parties crucial to its speech rights. Thus, the Court finds that Section 4663 burdens Alibaba's speech….

Defendants observe that Alibaba is not completely banned from appearing before the federal government—it could hire more in-house lobbyists or find a firm that is willing to forgo defense contractor clients…. [But] Alibaba is unlikely to find a lobbyist willing to forgo any clients who might want to contract with the Department. {The statute applies to defense contractors who contract with "entities"—in other words, lobbying firms—who also contract with CMCs. Defense contractors would not risk their ability to enter into a contract with the federal government when there are plenty of capable lobbying firms available. The lobbying firm then must choose. One or more of its lobbyists can choose to represent Alibaba, but then the entire lobbying firm must forgo representing defense contractors. The only rational choice for a lobbying firm is to stop representing Alibaba.}

[Alibaba also] represents that it cannot hire the number of experienced in-house lobbyists needed to address the federal government for all its interests…. The reduction to Alibaba's speech is sufficient to demonstrate a colorable First Amendment claim….

The court then concluded that the law was subject to strict scrutiny:

"Content-based regulations are subject to the most exacting scrutiny because 'the government has no power to restrict expression because of its message, its ideas, its subject matter, or its content.'" In examining a statute, courts must also examine "restrictions distinguishing among different speakers, allowing speech by some but not others." "Speech restrictions based on the identity of the speaker are all too often simply a means to control content." Thus, identity-based regulations may "demand strict scrutiny when they reflect the Government's preference for the substance of what the favored speakers have to say (or aversion to what the disfavored speakers have to say)." …

{Section 4663 is not a content-based restriction…. Section 4663 operates based solely on whether a CMC is involved. What the CMC (or non-CMC) plans to say does not affect whether Section 4663 applies.} [But] Section 4663 is a speaker-based restriction on speech—it is only applicable if a CMC seeks to retain a lobbyist. As a speaker-based restriction on political speech, Section 4663 is subject to strict scrutiny.

Defendants assert that Section 4663 should be considered under intermediate scrutiny, because Alibaba's "differential treatment" is justified by its "special characteristic[ ]," namely the potential involvement of a foreign adversary. Defendants again rely on TikTok, where the Supreme Court applied intermediate scrutiny despite finding that the divestiture statute was a speaker-based restriction, because the differential treatment was due to a content-neutral reason, namely TikTok's susceptibility to foreign control.

While Section 4663 also involves a foreign adversary, TikTok is distinguishable because political speech was not at issue. Id. ("emphasiz[ing] the inherent narrowness" of its holding and observing that "[a] law targeting any other speaker would by necessity entail a distinct inquiry and separate considerations"). Here, Defendants provide no authority to suggest that differential treatment with respect to political speech may be considered under intermediate scrutiny. Citizens United v. FEC (2010) states the opposite: "Laws that burden political speech are 'subject to strict scrutiny,'" and "the First Amendment generally prohibits the suppression of political speech based on the speaker's identity." The Court thus applies strict scrutiny in determining whether Section 4663 violates the First Amendment.

{The Court recognizes that this is a close question. But for the context of political speech—specifically the ability and right to petition the government—Defendants' argument that intermediate scrutiny should apply would likely prevail, because Section 4663 is otherwise a content-neutral statute. As in TikTok, Section 4663 singles out a particular group of speakers, but not because of the content of their speech. Instead, the treatment is based on a particular characteristic of the speaker, i.e., their status as a CMC.} …

And the court held that the law failed strict scrutiny, because it was not narrowly tailored to the government interests in "(1) … prevent[ing] CMCs 'from accessing sensitive military information through their relationships with lobbyists,' and (2) … ensur[ing] that CMCs 'cannot subtly influence any of [the Department's] decisions related to national security by influencing a lobbyist'":

[T]he government cannot impose a restriction on speech based solely on "mere conjecture." Rather, it must "point to record evidence or legislative findings demonstrating the need to address a special problem."

Here, Defendants fail to meet their burden. Defendants provide only one exhibit in support of their opposition: the joint explanatory statement submitted as part of the legislative record for the Act. The statement provides an overview of Section 4663 and then "direct[s] the Comptroller General of the United States to submit a report to the congressional defense committees, not later than one year after the enactment of this Act, on the national security risks posed by consulting firms who simultaneously contract with the Department of Defense and the Chinese government or its proxies or affiliates."

Of note, the Comptroller General is directed to study consulting firms, not lobbyists. The statement does not express any findings by the Comptroller General or Congress about the risks posed by lobbyists retained by CMCs.
