Unlike window air conditioners, portable air conditioners roll easily from room to room and take only minutes to set up—no brackets, no tools, no sweating through a complicated installation before you can start sweating less. Most just require a nearby window and wall outlet to get cold air flowing. Yes, they tend to cost a bit more than window units, but the tradeoff is real: you can’t beat the convenience their portability adds, which is why many people choose them over other models. Plus your view isn’t blocked by a giant metal box.

Portable air conditioners have an internal fan that moves hot, humid air over a refrigerant-gas-filled coil to cool and expel it into a room to lower the temperature. The moisture it strips from the air collects in an internal reservoir that you’ll periodically need to drain. Higher-end units either pump it out automatically or evaporate it entirely so you never have to think about it. Because the whole unit rolls on casters, you can take the cold air with you instead of running more energy intensive central a/c or buying multiple window units.

Coverage area is one of the most important factors in choosing an air conditioner. Manufacturers rate units in square footage, but that number assumes an average ceiling height (roughly eight feet) and room insulation. If the room is especially hot in the summer (for example, it’s a finished attic or it has large, south-facing windows) we recommend bumping up to the next size rather than buying a unit that will run constantly and struggle to keep up.

An air conditioner’s cooling capacity is typically measured in British Thermal Units, or BTU, per hour. To gauge coverage, figure roughly 20 BTU per square foot of space. Most portable a/c units range from 5,000 to 20,000 BTUs, which puts their effective range roughly between 350 and 700 square feet. So when in doubt, size up—an oversized unit cycles off when it hits your target temperature, while an undersized one never stops trying.

Single-hose units essentially recirculate the room’s air to cool it and have one line that runs outdoors to expel exhaust. The catch is that expelling air creates a slight negative pressure in the room, which pulls in warm air from other parts of the house and from outside through gaps around doors and windows. It works, but it’s working against itself.

Dual-hose units solve this by pulling outside air in through one hose for cooling and pushing hot exhaust out through a second hose. The result is a more self-contained system that’s noticeably more efficient, especially in larger spaces. The tradeoff is price.

Because dual-hose models are more complex, they tend to be more expensive. If you only have a small space to cool, a portable a/c with a single hose may suit your needs just fine. For anything larger, the dual-hose design is worth seriously considering.

Hose length determines how far away from a window you can maneuver your portable air conditioner. If you set it up in a large room, or one where the nearest window is far from where most people gather, hose length becomes more important.

It also plays a key role in room setup. If the hose isn’t long enough to set the air conditioner in front of large furniture pieces, you may have to rearrange the room for the most effective cooling. Most hoses range anywhere from 50 to 90 inches fully extended. This should give you enough space to make adjustments and pull the air conditioner closer to the center of the room if needed.

Most portable air conditioners hover between 50 and 60 decibels while they’re running—that’s roughly the noise level of a normal conversation. In most cases, that’s fine during the day, but if you’re planning to run the unit in a bedroom overnight, those numbers start to matter.

Units that dip below 50 decibels in their quietest modes are on par with most ceiling fans and are easier to sleep through; a few on this list get impressively close to whisper-quiet. Pay attention to the decibel rating at the unit’s lowest fan setting, not just its average operating level.

Much like larger ductless mini-split air conditioners, some portable air conditioners include a heat pump. “If you’re shopping for an a/c unit because you have a space not currently cooled or heated, one of these models may warrant consideration,” says gear editor Bradley Ford. “Similarly, you may have a room that your HVAC system doesn’t reach effectively. Rooms like this are typically either too cold or hot, depending on the season. If this is the case, a unit with heating capabilities may be able to fill your HVAC gap.”

Every portable a/c pulls moisture out of the air, similar to home dehumidifiers. The question is what happens to it next. Budget units collect it in an internal pan that you’ll need to manually drain, sometimes daily in humid conditions.

Other units use self-evaporating technology to push moisture out through the exhaust hose with no bucket to remember. A few high-end models go even further, offering a continuous drainage port where you can attach a hose and never think about water again. If low-maintenance matters to you, the drainage method is a factor to consider.

There isn’t a clear superior option between window units and portable options. Choosing between the two largely depends on your home, windows, and personal needs. That said, window air conditioning units are generally faster at bringing temperatures down and run more efficiently than their portable counterparts.

Portable air conditioners have certainly closed the gap over time in terms of cooling ability, but still lag behind if that’s your number one priority.

Portable units stand out because of their convenience and ease of setup. If you don’t have compatible windows, get a portable unit. If you need quick cooling in a spot that generally doesn’t need air conditioning, get a portable unit. If you want just one machine for your entire apartment, get a portable unit.

Portable air conditioners are compatible with a lot more window openings than window units, so you can use them regardless of your apartment or home fixtures.

New portable a/c units are relatively energy efficient across the board. That said, it’s certainly a sliding scale. There are several ways in which energy efficiency is calculated for air conditioners, but the shorthand is if your a/c unit is Energy Star-certified, it’s worth getting.

For items that don’t have Energy Star certification, pay attention to the CEER, EER, and SEER numbers. In short, look for an air conditioner with a CEER above 10, with anything above 12 qualifying for Energy Star certification. Similarly, look out for a SEER above 14 in the north and 15 in the south as well as an EER above 10. These are general numbers, but serve as a basic guide when choosing between different options.

One of the biggest issues to solve with portable air conditioners is cooling efficiency. While most units generate a ton of cold air, leakage from the hose or exhaust port can work against that. There are two key things you can do: insulate the opening in the window and insulate the hose itself.

Many portable units provide foam pieces that fit in and around the window insert to minimize the amount of hot air that leaks in from outside. The hose itself won’t come with foam insulation pieces, but you can perform a similar trick by buying one online or by making one yourself. Some people use blankets while others have used everything from reflective sun shades to Amazon plastic packaging. The goal is to prevent heat from the tube from radiating into your living space.

#762. What Is Mirin? Uses, Types, and Best Substitutes

📅 Aug 21, 2017  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If you’ve eaten Japanese food, you’ve almost certainly tasted mirin. Alongside miso, soy sauce, dashi, and rice vinegar, the sweet rice wine is a staple of Japanese cooking. It brings richness and complexity to stocks, stews, marinades, and sauces, plus a glossy sheen to glazed dishes like teriyaki.

Mirin is often described as sweet with subtle umami, but Sonoko Sakai, author of Japanese Home Cooking, prefers the term koku. “Koku is like umami, but it gives not just flavor but aroma; you feel it with your palate, and it gives depth.”

But the type of mirin you buy matters—and the bottle your local supermarket stocks likely won’t deliver the koku Sakai describes. Hon mirin, or “true” mirin, tends to be pricey and hard to find in the US. Here’s what sets it apart, when it’s worth seeking out, and what to use when you can’t find any.

Braise tofu in mirin and soy sauce for a flavorful 20-minute dinner

Traditional hon mirin is made from just three ingredients: glutinous rice, koji, and shochu (a Japanese distilled spirit often made from rice). During production, enzymes break down the rice’s starches into “simple sugars, mostly glucose,” explains Hiroko Shimbo, author of The Japanese Kitchen. That gives mirin a distinctive sweetness without added sugar.

Shimbo describes that sweetness as more subtle and “elegant” than table sugar. She explains that glucose is about 70% as sweet as sucrose (the sugar commonly used in cooking), while Sakai notes that mirin has a lower glycemic index than many other sweeteners.

Hon mirin’s alcohol content further sets it apart. With an ABV around 14%, it’s about as alcoholic as some sakes and most grape-based wines, though it’s not generally consumed as a beverage. (One exception is o-toso, a traditional spiced mirin-based New Year’s drink.) Its alcohol content also makes it useful in cooking. “Alcohol denatures proteins,” says Shimbo. “It helps them firm up slightly—it’s also hygroscopic, so it helps to retain moisture.” The result is food that’s tender, juicy, and rich with umami sweetness.

For a bottle of the real thing, Sakai recommends Fukuraijun hon mirin, while Shimbo favors Mikawa hon mirin from Sumiya Bunjiro Brewery.

Fukuraijun Hon Mirin Artisanal Sweet Rice Seasoning, 16.9 fl oz

Of course, not everyone is using true mirin. Most American home cooks likely have aji-mirin on hand. Translated as “tastes like mirin,” and widely available and inexpensive, aji-mirin is typically made with glucose or corn syrup, water, rice, salt, and alcohol rather than through the lengthy traditional production process. Alcohol content varies by brand; Kikkoman’s aji-mirin, for example, contains about 8%.

Both Shimbo and Sakai avoid aji-mirin, citing its less nuanced flavor and more intense sweetness. Shimbo also notes that aji-mirin’s lower alcohol content can affect how it performs in cooking.

That said, if you can’t find anything other than aji-mirin when shopping, don't despair. J. Kenji López-Alt, author of The Food Lab, also admits that aji-mirin doesn't have the same flavor. “Natural fermentation creates all these byproducts that give hon mirin better aroma,” he says. But he also notes that mirin often makes up only a small portion of a dish and is generally cooked alongside other strongly flavored ingredients. While he uses naturally brewed mirin when he can, “I also cook all the time with aji-mirin—it’s not a problem. You'll notice a difference, but even the cheapest stuff is still going to work just fine.”

Both mirin and sake are rice wines used in Japanese cooking, but mirin is considerably sweeter. The difference comes down to how they’re made.

Hon mirin is made with mochi gome (sweet or glutinous rice) and shochu, a distilled spirit that creates an alcohol-rich environment while koji enzymes break rice starches into sugars. Sake, by contrast, uses sakamai (sake rice) and relies on yeast to convert sugars into alcohol, leaving it considerably less sweet.

Sake is also brewed for drinking, so you’ll find a much wider variety of styles and flavor profiles on the market, some of which would be wasted on cooking.

Though mirin and rice vinegar are both rice derivatives, they have very little in common. Mirin is sweet, syrupy, and alcoholic, with an umami undercurrent. Rice vinegar is acidic and tart, with little natural sweetness.

Their shared origins might tempt you to substitute rice vinegar mixed with sugar for mirin, but Shimbo, Sakai, and López-Alt agree the result would be far too acidic. Save the vinegar for dishes that need tang, not mirin’s rounded sweetness.

Balance the heat of gochujang chicken with a glug of mirin.

According to López-Alt, Shimbo, and Sakai, the best substitute for mirin is sake mixed with a sweetener. The two wines share enough characteristics to make this a closer match than most alternatives, though the result won’t have all the complexity of hon mirin.

Any sake you have on hand will do, but Shimbo recommends keeping a large bottle of seishu (clear sake) for cooking. Avoid anything labeled “cooking sake,” which may contain added salt that could throw off your dish’s seasoning.

López-Alt generally combines two parts sake with one part sugar by volume. Shimbo prefers brown rice syrup, which she finds more closely mimics the sweetness of hon mirin. Both Shimbo and Sakai also offer maple syrup as a worthwhile swap.

If you don't have sake or aji-mirin, you can use dry white wine or dry sherry mixed with a sweetener, though neither is an exact substitute. “Dry white wine is closer than a sherry,” says López-Alt, but it brings a distinct flavor that can substantially change the finished dish. As he points out, many recipes that call for mirin also call for sake, so you may need to replace both.

None of our experts had an alcohol-free alternative they strongly recommended. Some mirin-style seasonings contain less alcohol than hon mirin, and others are alcohol-free, but they aren’t interchangeable in every application. Check the label carefully: Aji-mirin can contain alcohol, and shio mirin (salted mirin) isn't necessarily alcohol-free.

Cooking can reduce mirin’s alcohol content, but it doesn't guarantee that all the alcohol evaporates. If you’re strictly avoiding alcohol, choose a product explicitly labeled alcohol-free rather than relying on cooking time.

Be mindful of added salt, too. Some mirin-style products are salted to make them unpalatable for drinking, so you may need to adjust other sodium sources in your recipe.

Hon mirin is remarkably shelf-stable thanks to its sugar and alcohol content. Store it tightly sealed in a cool, dark place rather than the refrigerator, where its sugars may crystallize. Storage recommendations for aji-mirin and other mirin-style seasonings vary by product, so check the label for instructions after opening.

Combined with soy sauce and sake, mirin makes a silky tare (seasoning base) for ramen or, when reduced until syrupy, a lip-smacking teriyaki sauce. Try it on salmon, steak, vegetables, and more.

Mirin is equally at home in quick-cooking dishes like stir-fried udon with pork and soboro don, as well as slow braises, soups, and marinades. Its delicate sweetness complements fish and tofu, as in brothy steamed bass, and balances the tang of this versatile miso-turmeric dressing. Mirin can also be used in many applications where Western-trained cooks might reach for table sugar. “When you use real mirin,” says Shimbo, “there are many good effects.”

As romantic as candlelight dining sounds, it’s simply not practical (way too much wax!). The best dining room lighting ideas are thoughtfully planned: They take your actual eating habits, furniture proportions, room structure, and budget into consideration. They weigh the benefits of pendant lights vs. chandeliers vs. flush mounts and wall sconces, not to mention bulb type.

Lighting, it turns out, gets pretty involved—which is why we’ve tapped interior designers from around the country for their brightest ideas. And though the considerations are complex, modern lighting can be just as romantic as the candlelight option. “The evolving landscape is opening the door to more thoughtful layers and innovative ideas for how lighting can shape a space,” says Mike Shively of Chicago-based AD PRO firm En Masse Architecture and Design. “Lighting and coziness are both things we associate with luxury today, and neither necessarily comes down to price.”

In fashion designer Marysia Reeves’s Venice Beach home, a breezy, lantern-like fixture hovers above the wood dining table.

You can find alabaster in a variety of shapes—including linear forms, mini pendants, and semi-flush-mount ceiling lights—and while it’s generally pretty pricey (ranging anywhere from $300 for a single alabaster pendant lamp to $3,000 for a row of pendants), it’s also a durable material that lasts.

All you need is an arc floor lamp; they plug right into a wall outlet but stand tall, with a long, curved neck that will position the bulb and shade directly over your table. (How’s that for simplified?) The Arco floor lamp by Flos is a classic, but more budget-conscious shoppers can spring for similar styles from retailers like Wayfair (or even Amazon!).

If you’re trying to decide between options . . .Take the shape and style of your table into considerationThe overhead fixture you choose and the table itself “don’t have to [have] a direct geometrical correlation, but there should be some proportional consideration,” says Russell Groves of New York City–based Groves & Co. A long or unusually large chandelier would obviously overpower a short table, and vice versa: A teeny-tiny bulb might look silly above a big table. Same goes for the look: A lovely old farmhouse table would do well with a lovely farmhouse fixture or something super-modern for contrast—but either way, they should be considered together.

If you’d like to jazz up a fixture you already own . . .Customize the lampshadeA reflective material inside the shade will help cast light back down toward the table, Groves points out, while perforated shades will turn any bulb into a twinkly light (if that’s your prerogative). “Textured fabrics like raw silk will add a beautiful warmth and shadow to a room,” he says, but should you require a more directional cast, “an opaque shade wouldn’t give you a lot of glow in the room but would put more on the table.” Just because a fixture has shades you’re not into doesn’t mean it’s the end of the line. Swap them out!

We anticipate seeing this influence extend across lighting, from woven metal sheaths and illustrative shades to sculptural, cast forms.”

If you’re into geometric lines . . .Get creative with the cordWhen hanging a pendant lamp with a very simple silhouette, feel free to get creative with the cord: Zigzag it from wall to wall around a corner, tack it in swags along the ceiling, or loop it over a hook on the wall.

I mean it. Don’t even finish reading this—call your electrician first.” With the simple pressing of a lever or turning of a knob, dimmable lighting allows you to control the brightness of the bulbs, which is particularly important if you’re dealing with often-harsh overhead lighting. “To me, chandeliers should mimic candlelight,” says Groves—and that warm, dim glow is possible if you just install dimmers. Plus, they’re available in both modern and traditional silhouettes that will add considerably more charm to the walls than those plastic switch plates you hate anyway.

To make the room multifunctional . . .Supplement the lighting solution you chooseIf you end up setting your table with some nice, warm candlelight, you might need to add sconces or downlights to supplement that glow when the room is used for anything other than an intimate dinner. (Dining room tables are very good for DIY projects, we’d like to note.) And to up a room’s cozy factor, lean into lamps. “The more lamps the better,” says Goldbach. “We like using smaller-scale lamps and vintage pieces in unexpected ways to create a warm, layered atmosphere. Multiple light sources can make the space feel cozier and more inviting, especially as the days get shorter.”

For lots of ambience, try vintage-inspired filament bulbs. If it’s more of a particular shape and style you’re after for an exposed-bulb fixture, try decorative bulbs. And for the ultimate in energy efficiency, invest in LED bulbs, which are up to 90 percent more efficient than traditional light bulbs.

With a few tools on hand and safety precautions in mind, cleaning a dryer vent is an easy project you can do yourself.

The first and most important step is to cut power to the clothes dryer. To maintain safety, don’t do any work on the vent while the appliance is drawing electricity. The simplest and safest way to cut power is to unplug the unit. Keep in mind that this may require pulling the dryer out from its current position. Don’t strain yourself. If the machine is too heavy to move on your own, enlist help from another person. If you have a gas dryer, close the valve on the gas line as well.

Dryer vents are typically easy to find. Look for a thick silver or white hose coming from the back of the dryer. It should be about 4 inches in diameter and can be made with flexible white vinyl, thin aluminum foil, semi-rigid aluminum, or completely rigid aluminum piping. Once you have located the vent hose at the back of the dryer, just follow it along to the place where it exits the home. It should be held in place at both ends by a circular bracket that fits over the top of the 4-inch hose and tightens around the transition vent piping.

Pull the dryer out from the wall as far as your vent hose will allow (typically, about 1 to 2 feet). Place a drop cloth on the floor to catch debris. (This is especially important for a vent that has never been cleaned, because there is likely to be a large amount of accumulated hair, lint, dust, and dirt.)

#770. Alton Brown's Brined Turkey

If you’re wondering how to fit an enormous brine-bathing bird into your fridge this holiday season, stop wondering. Alton Brown’s wet-brined turkey recipe uses an insulated cooler instead, freeing up precious refrigerator space while seasoning the bird throughout.

The brine—a simple mixture of kosher salt, sugar, and vegetable broth—helps the turkey retain moisture as it roasts. Once the bird has had its soak, pat it thoroughly dry and send it into a blazing-hot 500°F oven to get the skin hurtling toward golden and crisp. After 30–40 minutes, you’ll shield the breast with foil, lower the heat to 350°F, and let the turkey finish roasting.

That’s right: No basting. No hovering by the oven. Just a little advance planning and plenty of time to focus on sweet potatoes, apple pie, and the rest of your Thanksgiving feast.

If you'd rather skip the wet brine, try our dry-brined turkey. Short on time? Chris Morocco’s turkey in parts cooks in under two hours.

For a 14–18-lb. turkey, plan on 8–12 hours in the brine—enough time for the salt to work its way into the meat, seasoning it and helping it retain moisture. You may be tempted to leave the turkey soaking for 24 hours, but an extended brine can make it overly salty. Stick to the recommended window.

Yes, as long as you can keep the brine at or below 40°F the entire time. Use a thoroughly cleaned, food-safe insulated cooler and monitor the liquid with a probe thermometer. Add sealed ice packs as needed to maintain a safe temperature. The goal is to keep the turkey just as cold as it would be in the refrigerator.

Can you brine a turkey that’s already been brined?

It’s best not to. Many supermarket turkeys are sold prebrined or injected with a salt solution, sometimes labeled “self-basting,” “enhanced,” or “contains a solution.” Brining one again can make the meat too salty. Check the packaging before you begin and choose an unbrined turkey for this recipe.

How do you keep gravy made from brined turkey from getting too salty?

Brining seasons the turkey, but it can also make the pan drippings quite salty. Taste the gravy before adding any extra seasoning, and use low-sodium or unsalted broth for more control. If the finished gravy is too salty, add a few pats of unsalted butter or thin the gravy with salt-free broth.

Place wrapped one 14–18-lb. Frozen turkey in a 5-gal. cooler with a drain spout. Place cooler in a bathtub and pour in cold water to cover turkey. Let sit, draining and replacing water every 2–3 hours and making sure to keep water temperature below 40°, until turkey is thawed, 8–10 hours.

Remove turkey and clean cooler with soap and pat dry. Pour in 3 cups (1 lb.) Diamond Crystal or 1¾ cups Morton kosher salt, ⅔ cup sugar, and 2 qt. hot tap water. Stir to dissolve salt and sugar. Pour in 4 qt. low-sodium vegetable broth and 8 lb. ice (from 1 gallon water).

Unwrap turkey and remove giblets and any other parts (such as neck) that may be inside cavity; save for gravy if desired. Place turkey, breast side up, in brine. If turkey floats, fill a 1-gallon resealable plastic bag with ice water and place it on top of turkey. Close lid and set cooler in a cool place. Let sit, turning turkey over once if possible, 8–12 hours. Check brining liquid periodically with a probe-style thermometer ; if temperature is above 40°, add a few freezer packs enclosed in resealable plastic bags.

Place a rack in lower third of oven and remove remaining racks; preheat to 500°. Remove turkey from brine and pat dry inside and out with paper towels. Fill turkey cavity with 1 large onion, coarsely chopped, 2 large carrots, scrubbed, coarsely chopped, 2 celery stalks, coarsely chopped, and some thyme, rosemary, and/or sage sprigs; keep packing loose.

Place turkey, breast side up, on a V-shaped rack set inside a large roasting pan. Cut a piece of foil big enough to cover breast and mold over breast. Remove foil, then coat foil with nonstick vegetable oil spray and set aside.

Rub 2 Tbsp. unsalted butter between your hands until liberally coated (you may use disposable food-grade gloves if desired). Rub butter all over turkey, re-buttering your hands and repeating process until you have a nice, even coating.

Slide turkey into oven neck end first and roast until well browned, 30–40 minutes. Remove turkey from oven and place greased foil over breast. Insert probe-style thermometer through foil into thickest part of breast. Reduce oven temperature to 350° and return turkey to oven. Roast until probe inserted into breast registers 161°, and 181° for thigh, 2–2½ hours (do not open oven to baste turkey).

Transfer turkey to carving board and let rest 30 minutes. Reserve drippings for gravy.

Set roasting pan across 2 burners over medium heat. Add 1 cup low-sodium chicken broth and whisk to scrape up pan drippings. Whisk 1 Tbsp. cornstarch and 1 cup cold water in a small bowl to combine. Add cornstarch slurry to pan and cook, whisking, until gravy is glossy and thickened. Taste and season with kosher salt and freshly ground pepper if needed.

It is Friday after all, so I’ll offer this little glimpse as an example from what I do at work…

A while ago, I was working for a customer (who shall remain unnamed here, but let’s call it Intel) doing system simulation software. I worked on this project for a year or so. I ran full x86 systems completely simulated. During that time I was chasing some nasty bugs in the simulated usb-disk device that caused my Windows boot to end up in a blue screen.

I struggled to figure out why Windows 7 would write 0xABADBABE to EHCI register index 0x1C – which is a reserved register – during boot some 10 milliseconds before the blue screen appears, and I was convinced that it was due to a flaw in the EHCI simulation code and thus was the first indication of the failure. If I didn’t have any simulated usb-disk inserted that write wouldn’t occur, and similarly that write would occur even if I inserted the usb-disk much later – like even after Windows 7 had started and I was passed the login screen.

An interesting exercise is to grep for this (little-endian so twist it around!) 32 bit pattern in a freshly installed windows 7 file system – I found it on no less than 16 places in a 20GB file system. This bgrep utility was handy for this.

The iconic writer, whose life was dramatized in the 2005 biopic Capote and the 2024 miniseries Feud: Capote vs. the Swans, died in 1984 at 59. Many of his belongings were left to Gerald Clarke, a journalist and friend of Capote who released his biography of the writer in 1988. Now 89 years old, Clarke is downsizing—and putting nearly 400 lots of Capote’s personal objects up for auction.

The Cornell Auction sale is officially scheduled for November 21, with an online preview and bidding slated for October 26. The Bellport, New York, auction house has not yet shared the full catalogue, but revealed in an Instagram video that “most of the items that we have in the Truman Capote auction were photographed almost exactly 50 years ago in 1976 in an Architectural Digest spread about Truman’s house here in Sagaponack on Long Island.”

It has been confirmed by images shared in The New York Times that some of Capote’s trove of paperweights, as featured in AD, will be up for grabs. The scribe “became addicted to paperweights and collected so many of them,” Clarke told the Times. Some of the pieces, made with flowers preserved in resin, were crafted by Capote himself. One paperweight in the auction was created with rocks from the Kansas farm made famous by In Cold Blood.

Other lots include a signed copy of To Kill a Mockingbird by Harper Lee, a longtime friend of Capote; a bronze frog inkwell; and even Capote’s house keys.

Read on to revisit Rosemary Kent’s story on Capote’s custom Hamptons home—and keep your eyes out for objects that may soon be on offer.

It is virtually impossible to find his Long Island home in the Hamptons, but that’s exactly the way he wants it. Hidden behind scrub pine, privet hedges and rows of hydrangea bushes is Truman Capote’s two-story, weathered-gray beach house near Sagaponack on the South Fork.

He lives in the heart of the Hamptons—a stretch of rolling potato fields and lush farmlands married to the nearby Atlantic Ocean. A year-round farming community and a summer place for city people, it is here that antique farmhouses vie with modernistic glass houses for the dunes and fields. Mr. Capote once called Sagaponack “Kansas with a sea breeze.”

The unpretentious exterior of the flat-roofed, box-shaped studio beach house, surrounded by sun-baked trees, was weathered and timeless.

It has actually become a rural Montparnasse with the likes of James Jones, Willem de Kooning, Marya Mannes, Jean Stafford, Joseph Heller and many other artists and writers living in the area. It is also the kind of sleepy sentimental town where the local bar—Truman Capote often lunches here or has a drink with fellow writers John Knowles or Willie Morris—runs an ad in the biweekly newspaper wishing him happy birthday. He can come and go as he pleases.

The best way to find Mr. Capote’s secluded house is with him at the wheel of his deep-green Buick. Then it is a short but bumpy ride down a dirt lane first opened back in 1670, a quick turn into a gravel driveway past a natural bird sanctuary and a friend’s cottage built on his property, before he jerkily pulls up in front of his box-shaped, flat-roofed wooden house. Wildflowers are still blooming, and the smell of the potatoes, almost ready for harvest, is pungent on the salty Atlantic Ocean air.

The two-story living room communicated a mood of oceanside ease tinged with intelligence and humor. Highly personal objects, memorabilia and books—which included copies of his own works in several languages—were placed casually among the simple furnishings.

“I own these fields,” he says proudly, sweeping his left hand like a plantation owner over the six acres he has owned since 1962, when he had the house built to his specifications “by a local carpenter who’s dead now. He did it to my directions. It’s exactly the way I wanted it. I work at this untended look. It’s intentional. You can see how quiet it is here because you can barely see the top of another house. This is a place to be alone.”

Only a few friends have ever ventured up the overgrown path to the house hidden in the dune grass, and they have all been invited first.

Capote created a "witchcraft altar," as he called it, on a small set of steps in the living room. The altar displays an array of accoutrements, including an antique wood cat baring its fangs and arching its back, a bronze bell and a Cocteau-designed needlepoint mask pillow.

“I’m here to work. I come here a lot in the autumn and winter, and I see almost no one. I don’t care for the Hamptons in the summer—it’s gotten too crowded. I don’t understand the way some people come out here and run themselves ragged every night. I just don’t think some of these writers are working.” This may seem an odd remark from Mr. Capote, who knows everyone from prisoners to princesses, socialites to sailors, and who simultaneously holds the reputation for being one of America’s greatest prose writers and another for being a social butterfly.

“I spend most of my time out here reading, writing, going for walks with my dog and talking on the telephone. I see people in New York; I don’t see New York people out here. I hardly see anyone when I’m here. In the past 14 years, since I’ve had this house, I’ve gone out six times for dinner. Now that’s quite a record for someone known for being so social, isn’t it? Besides, whom would I entertain? I only know about four people I’d really have over!”

Entering his house through the back kitchen door, he removes his loafers Japanese-style and walks into the living room. The barefoot Mr. Capote picks up a rag mop as delicately as Fred Astaire tapped his dancing cane and starts to dust the marine-blue floor, still not quite dry from its recent coat of boat deck paint.

“I’ve just had it repainted, and this humidity doesn’t help it dry. You should see this room at night. The floor floats like a big blue-green lake.”

The author describes his house as a “raincoat,” referring to the method of its construction. Built with the exterior walls exposed to reveal vertical studs and horizontal bracings, the skin of the building protects the inside, just like a raincoat.

“I wanted a rustic house. I really think of it as one big room. Completely winterized, it’s been designed to look unfinished. I call it ‘run-down comfort.’ I like the effect of texture in rooms—the rawness of the wood plus the mirrors that line one end.”

A floor-to-ceiling window with divided panes lets in the glorious morning sun and allows a view of the sea and sand some 200 yards beyond the house. A spiraling steel staircase leads to a balconied second-floor study lined with red sofas, wicker chairs and hassocks and a famed René Bouché sketch of Capote. Here he works or reads.

None of the furniture or furnishings have “Don’t Touch” signs. His possessions are comfortable, attractive and appear to be used rather than looked at. A priceless Tiffany lamp peeks over the shoulder of a worn yellow velvet easy chair. Upholstery has a few sun-faded patches, but the clusters of paperweights are shiny and sparkling. The wicker chairs and tables come from inexpensive import shops, or else they are rare antiques—like the round, claw-footed table downstairs where Mr. Capote writes. Needlepoint pillows done by friends are scattered about, as are silver-framed photographs of Lee Radziwill, an elegant-looking lady sitting primly in a bumper car, and Maggie.

Although she has left pale blond hairs around, Maggie—Capote’s celebrated English bulldog—is nowhere in sight.

“She’s next door visiting,” explains the writer, who loves to talk about his “sweet, quirky and huggy” pet who once ate Joanne Carson’s pink satin slippers and Charlotte Curtis’s poinsettias and who periodically raids picnickers’ baskets along Gibson Beach.

“Oh, she’s very munchy all right,” concedes Mr. Capote, pushing his Windex-blue-tinted prescription glasses up on his nose, shrugging and letting out his trademark, high-pitched giggle.

Capote's bedroom was a spartan statement. Miniature wood chests of drawers and a gilt-framed mirror rest on wall ledges above his single iron bedstead. Beside the bed are detective magazines on a card table and a floor lamp for reading before falling asleep.

Off the enormous living room is a small screened-in porch, similar to ones in many southern homes, filled with his beloved wicker furniture. He uses it as a summer room when the breezes blow mildly from the sea. His bedroom, near the kitchen, is so stark and sparsely furnished it resembles a summer camper’s tent. Near his cot-size bed are a stack of detective magazines, a reducing machine and the usual pile of favorite books.

“I like to have my own things around me. Even when I travel to my other houses in Switzerland and in California, I take things of my own with me,” says Mr. Capote, whose permanent base is a sleek apartment in the U.N. Plaza in Manhattan.

“I’m a great houseguest. I only appear at mealtimes. However, I really don’t like people staying with me. It’s interrupting.”

His Sagaponack schedule is uncomplicatedly regular. “I get up around 7:00 a.m. and work until 11:00, stopping to read the papers. I work again until 1:00 p.m., when I have my lunch. Then I drive to the post office, do my errands, go for a walk. I fool around in the evenings, reading, walking Maggie. I have my dinner by 7:00 p.m., and I’m usually in bed around 9:30 with a book.”

Currently at work on his long-awaited novel, Answered Prayers, the author recently finished his first film role, in Murder by Death, a Neil Simon script.

The living room's expansive shelving extended to the upper-level gallery, which was reached by way of a steel spiral staircase. The drawing among the books is a pencil portrait of the author. Wicker furniture is beneath windows offering views of the Hamptons landscape.

“You’ll have to wait to see what happens, but it’s going to be a lot of fun. It has a great cast—Peter Sellers, Orson Welles, Myrna Loy, Eileen Brennan, Peter Falk, David Niven, Madeline kahn, James Coco, Nancy Walker—and I have the lead part!”

He plays Lionel Twain, an eccentric millionaire living in a gloomy Victorian mansion in New England, who is a foremost authority on detective fiction. He thinks his casting is “perfect,” given his interest in crime and his best-seller In Cold Blood.

He is a collector, and he knows it. There’s a wonderful photograph taken several years ago by society photographer Slim Aarons of a youthful Capote perched on a Victorian sofa surrounded by many of his favorite things. “I like to collect things, Victorian things, and mix everything together. I enjoy looking for and at all of them. I’m not sure I need them. I’ve got too much, and I haven’t edited myself lately!”

He designs his own interiors. “For me, it’s a bore to use a decorator. I know exactly what I want. I just don’t care to have someone come in and tell me what I need to live with. I know.”

His philosophy of good interior design? “Something that’s not immediately offensive when you walk into a room. Of course it may be, eventually.”

In a brightly painted and comfortably arranged setting in the living room, the author wrote at this round wicker table. A pack of loose yellow unruled Sphinx paper, some notebooks, a photograph of Capote and his glasses share the space with an open copy of In Cold Blood.

Born in New Orleans and raised in the South, he began Breakfast at Tiffany’s, his famous classic, with the line, “I am always drawn back to places where I have lived, the houses and the neighborhoods.” Sagaponack, in many ways, reminds him of home. “I moved around so much as a child, I never had a sense of home. Maybe I did when I was 11 and I lived in a rural town in Alabama.”

Eyeing a hat rack above his bricked fireplace, the writer capriciously points to his assortment of rumpled panama hats.

“Home is where I hang my hats,” he laughs. “However, I keep leaving them when I go visit people. I don’t have as many as I used to because I don’t recover them all. People always promise to send them back, but they don’t.”
