#821. Fall Harvest Minestrone

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Recipe by Margaret Knoebel, Milwaukee, Wisconsin

Our fall harvest minestrone trades summer’s vegetables for autumn’s produce. Break out the slow cooker!

You may be familiar with the usual cluster of summer vegetables in classic minestrone, but any Italian will tell you that real minestrone changes with the season. Italians like to prioritize whole, nutritious, in-season produce—especially those living in Italy’s blue zone, where some of the longest-living people eat minestrone every single day.

As we dive into fall, we’re taking our classic minestrone recipe with us and giving it a couple of tweaks. Our fall harvest minestrone ditches the zucchini, green beans, parsley, basil and spinach, and reflects what’s growing in the garden right now: sweet potatoes, onions, kale, sage and rosemary. We included Italian sausage, too, to enrich the broth and add a big boost of protein—enough for this fall soup to officially be considered dinner.

In a large skillet, cook the sausage over medium heat until it’s no longer pink, six to eight minutes, breaking it into crumbles with a wooden spoon. Drain the liquid from the skillet, then transfer the meat to a 6-quart slow cooker.

Stir in the beef broth, sweet potatoes, canned tomatoes, kale, onion, carrot, garlic, sage and rosemary. Cook the minestrone, covered, on low until the vegetables are tender, five to six hours.

Editor’s Tip: The sweet potatoes should be fork-tender before moving on to the next step.

Stir the pasta into the minestrone. Cook everything, covered, on low until the pasta is tender, 20 to 25 minutes longer.

Ladle the soup into bowls and serve. If desired, top the fall harvest minestrone with Parmesan cheese, red pepper flakes or fresh sage or rosemary.

Allow leftover fall harvest minestrone to cool completely to room temperature in under two hours. Transfer the soup to an airtight container and store it in the fridge for up to four days.

Yes, you can freeze fall harvest minestrone. Let the soup come to room temperature in under two hours, then ladle it into freezer-safe, airtight containers. Freeze the soup for up to two months. Thaw it overnight in the fridge before enjoying it again.

Yes, you can make fall harvest minestrone gluten free. Buy a box of good gluten-free pasta and make sure the Italian sausage is gluten free, too. I like to cook the gluten-free pasta separately when I suspect I’ll have leftovers. I add the pasta to individual bowls, then top it with the soup. Gluten-free noodles will absorb too much broth overnight, breaking down and becoming mushy.

The fall harvest minestrone has everything: vegetables, carbs and meat. It doesn’t really need anything else, but if you want to stretch the meal a bit, serve the soup with cornbread, biscuits (I love our garlic bread biscuits) or slices of crusty sourdough bread. Something like a vinegary salad will cut the richness a bit and bring in fresh flavor.

#3570. built from upcycled timber and iron, portable micro-barn wanders through japan

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

dobedo studio develops ehho!², a portable micro-pavilion that reinterprets the traditional japanese kago, hand-carried by two people.

DOBEDO STUDIO edited by christina vergopoulou October 27, 2026 3 min

Designed by DOBEDO STUDIO, EHHO!² is a portable micro-architecture engineered from materials salvaged during the deconstruction of an agricultural barn in Gunma Prefecture, Japan. The mobile timber structure draws functional and formal inspiration from the traditional Japanese kago (palanquin), carrying architectural remnants of rural infrastructure into the urban public realm. Upcycling weathered corrugated iron sheets, structural logs, reclaimed timber boards, and hand-woven ropes, the project translates rural material memory into a compact, deployable social pavilion carried by two people.

The tectonic assembly by DOBEDO STUDIO balances low-tech mechanics with site-adaptive details. The rusted corrugated metal sheets are bent to form a protective curved roof, utilizing decades of atmospheric weathering as a natural, textured exterior skin. Hinged timber side panels fold downward to function as exterior benches, exposing a contrasting crimson-painted interior inspired by the seasonal bloom of local red spider lilies (higanbana). To secure the folding benches during transport, the studio integrated small wooden branches as tactile latch pins. If misplaced during transit, these fasteners can be replaced using fallen twigs sourced directly from nearby urban trees, establishing an intuitive, maintenance-free locking mechanism.

#995. A New Chapter, a Lasting Legacy: Inside the Studio Museum in Harlem’s Annual Gala

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

On November 15, the new Studio Museum in Harlem will mark its first anniversary. The refreshed building on West 125th Street, designed by Adjaye Associates in collaboration with Cooper Robertson, is a dream realized—primed for discovery, fellowship, and ultimately, fulfilling the institution’s purpose of championing the work of Black artists in Harlem.

And so, the museum’s annual gala on Monday evening could not have come at a better time to fete such an incredible moment in its history. Museum directors, artists, friends, supporters, and Mayor Mamdani and First Lady Rama Duwaji made their way to The Glasshouse in West Chelsea for a celebration befitting the milestone—dressed to the nines in black-tie attire, no less.

After a cheery cocktail hour, gala-goers took to the ballroom for the seated portion of the evening. Over a dinner of shaved Brussels sprouts, miso-glazed cod, and vegetables, a vibrant crowd was warmly welcomed by Thelma Golden, the Ford Foundation director and chief curator.

Among the room were Carrie Mae Weems, Tyler Mitchell, Rashid Johnson, and Spike Lee. Meanwhile the man of the hour was undoubtedly Mayor Mamdani, who was joined by his artist wife in a rare joint appearance on the social calendar. The couple were spotlighted not only for their ongoing support of the reimagined Studio Museum in Harlem, but also for championing the arts across all five boroughs.

“Each October, our annual gala offers us a unique opportunity to come together to celebrate the Studio Museum,” chair of the board of trustees Kathryn C. Chenault said. “It also presents us with the chance to honor and thank all of you, the faithful supporters of our mission and program, who have made it possible for us to flourish.”

Few people embody that mission like the evening’s honoree, Raymond J. McGuire, who over the last 25 years has helped guide the museum through crucial moments of growth and transformation through his work as a long-standing chairman. Of McGuire, Golden said: “It felt appropriate that we mark this moment in the museum’s history by honoring an individual who has maintained a fierce clarity of vision for who we are, who we serve, why we exist, and how we can and should move forward.”

As he accepted his award, McGuire received a round of rapturous applause before delivering a moving speech. He extended his thanks to the museum’s staff, sponsors, philanthropic leaders, artists, directors, and supporters, and most importantly, Golden and their longtime partnership. “Thelma refused to allow anyone’s standards to define our excellence,” he praised. “She built the visual grammar, the fusion of aesthetics, modernity, justice, social clarity, and spiritual uplift. As they say in them streets, Thelma Golden, ‘We did the damn thing.’”

The evening also offered a moment to present Kevin Beasley with the Joyce Alexander Wein Artist Prize. A multimedia artist who was a 2013-2014 Artist-in-Residence at the museum, Beasley is widely known for his works combining sculpture, performance art, and sound installation. Golden emphasized his vision, power, and impact: “The objects and materials he works with aren’t arbitrary,” she said. “They’re selected through careful consideration of looking, and with time and attention paid to how he vigorously studies each item’s history and to their relationship to the issues that permeate Black existence in the United States, whether shared or personal.”

The gala raised $4 million, which will support the Studio Museum’s ongoing exhibitions, programs, scholarships, and public outreach.

#3617. Comment | An open letter to Pope Leo XIV in defence of AI-generated art

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Pope Leo XIV greets attendees following a mass in Vatican City on 15 August 2026 Photo: Marco Iacobucci

Press any key to continue is a monthly column about the ways technology is reshaping contemporary art, from non-fungible tokens and artificial intelligence to attention economies and new systems of distribution. Its author, Sarp Kerem Yavuz, is a visual artist, researcher and the artistic director of the Contemporary Istanbul Foundation.

My name is Sarp Kerem Yavuz, I am a French Turkish artist, writer and curator. Having spent my most formative years of university and graduate school in Oberlin, Ohio, and Chicago, I am writing to you in the hopes that between two Midwesterners, there may be room for dialogue regarding artificial intelligence (AI).

Earlier this month, you wrote on social media: “In this era of artificial intelligence, it is becoming urgent to distinguish human art from what machines produce. There is an ontological difference, even before an aesthetic one, between art and what a machine can generate through statistical calculation based on millions of images created by others. Algorithms lack the spark of humanity. For this reason, the Church wishes to renew an alliance with artists and cultural institutions to safeguard our humanity.”

The world is already divided on AI. Fanning the flames without fully acknowledging what creating with AI entails is as reductive as dismissing Christianity on account of Leviticus 20:13. Your positioning of the Catholic Church as anti-AI, imposing a stark distinction between human artists and artists leveraging AI to generate work, creates a false dichotomy.

Humans have used tools to create art for millennia. Reframing AI as the latest addition to a long line of inventions that began with the brush is a much more constructive way to approach digital art today. We are simply artists interested in contemporary tools. Often, we employ these algorithms to illustrate the biases, shortcomings and limitations of AI. But an AI-generated work does not have to include self-reflexive commentary to be worthy of artistic recognition. Artists are uniquely positioned to create public ledgers of accountability, to demonstrate AI’s social, historical, moral, political and conceptual capacities and limitations.

Using problematic technologies to create art is not new. I often refer to Richard Mosse’s infrared photography series, which utilises film developed by the US military in the 1940s to turn all natural greenery magenta, in an attempt to detect camouflage. Planes with special cameras built into their floors would take surveillance images using infrared film to determine enemy positions and movement. Mosse used this film to take portraits of guerrilla fighters in eastern Congo, framing the rebels in a luscious sea of pink, assigning a theatrical queerness to civil war. The work relies on a surveillance tool to reframe contemporary conflicts while showcasing the intertwined relationship between photography and espionage, and how tools of oppression can be repurposed.

Jon Rafman’s delightful repurposing of Google Street View imagery to create a body of work composed entirely of screenshots, asking questions about the nature of ownership, authorship, photography and surveillance, also comes to mind. Based on your proclamation, since the cameras were programmed and operated by engineers to efficiently map streets and venues, and did not contain any artistic intention (that we know of), Rafman’s use of Google Street View imagery might also be categorised as ontologically lacking.

The misperception that AI-generated art does not require or contain human-derived labour, intentionality or craft, and is thus inferior (or lacking in human creativity), sadly ignores the reality of the digital art landscape. To be clear, AI tools at our disposal today can function without artistic intention or care. They can generate images without purpose or craft. They can serve as shortcuts or substitutes for skill. But we can also take blurry, over-exposed photographs with a digital camera on auto-focus. We choose to what degree we control our tools, and there are plenty of humans out there who use AI thoughtfully, intentionally and even masterfully.

To propose that when we do not exercise control over how these tools generate outputs, those outputs cannot be artistic, would also be incorrect. The history of art has long embraced chance, found objects and prompts, including in the works of John Cage, Sol LeWitt and Yoko Ono. Works based on prompted or delegated labour have been considered part of the canon for decades. From vague sentences encouraging the audience to do something to choreographies mapped out on an exhibition floor in the form of instructional arrows and footprints, we have had artists nudge, with very little final control, their audience into completing, activating or becoming an artwork.

When you add to that list Marcel Duchamp’s Fountain or Andy Warhol signing US currency, you can see that we have already wrestled with the implications of accepting found objects as artistic; with the notion that context, rather than output, could make something culturally significant; and with the idea that an artist could make an artwork without actually producing the underlying object.

If we live in a society that values the work of Cage, LeWitt, Ono, Warhol and Duchamp, then your assessment dismissing AI art that relies on images created by others also creates a double standard. It limits which artists get to benefit from found materials or delegated labour.

To be clear, I am not arguing about the ethics of how AI datasets are sourced. In instances where remuneration for an individual artist’s work is warranted, I fully support all public endeavours to establish payment mechanisms.

The spark of humanity that you claim is absent, I would argue, is actually omnipresent in AI-generated art. An AI tool’s approximation algorithm is ultimately human-made, and its data sets are human-derived, meaning the middle it pursues is irrevocably human. AI-generated art made today makes for a fascinating document of our (largely Western, mostly conservative) cultural and moral landscape, and the entire body of AI-generated output from the past three years will likely serve as a treasure trove for future historians.

But, to many of us artists, AI’s limitations are much more fascinating than its abilities, which is why we are using it today.

The artist Maya Man, who was recently named to the inaugural cohort of Dataland’s artist-in-residence programme, a partnership with Google Arts and Culture, uses AI to re-imagine elements of her own competitive dancing past, leveraging the tool to ask questions about performance in the age of social media, the culture of spectacle, and individual agency and memory. The iterative nature of AI is a fundamental part of Man’s work, not a hindrance, and where AI "fails" to deliver something accurately, it mimics the real-life failures inherent in performance and the fallibility of memory. The very real shortcomings of the technology contribute to her practice in ways that an accurate tool might fail to, making this precisely the right moment for Man to use nascent AI technology in her art.

I am in constant awe of Sasha Stiles’s AI-generated poems, recently exhibited at the Museum of Modern Art in New York, which she creates by training a machine on her work, tone and nuance, provoking questions about the implications of being seen by an entity so thoroughly that it can continue your work as though it were a part of you. Stiles’s work incisively interrogates the human condition by asking AI to serve as a mirror or echo of herself. Her use of AI tools is the point, and thus the technology is vital for her profoundly human work to exist.

Edward Burtynsky and Alkan Avcioglu’s imaginary landscapes offer a new look at the world through the eyes of two masters of their crafts—one who excels at understanding topography as portraiture, and the other who skillfully leverages digital tools to collapse depth, perspective and gravity in ways that create impossible vistas. Together they have tamed their respective tools and archives to bend to their will. It is wildly, delightfully inventive, a pleasure to behold.

In my own photographic works—one of which was recently named as a promised gift for the photography collection of your hometown museum, the Art Institute of Chicago—I leverage AI’s inability to accurately imagine the Istanbul I grew up in to create a new kind of Orientalism. I love its shortcomings, comparable to those of 19th-century painters more interested in presenting the Middle East as exotic—the wonderful architectural mistakes, the misplacement of tiles and floral patterns and rugs, the incorrect framing and erroneous choreography of rituals, such as the public bath. When the AI was unwilling to generate scenes from Turkish baths because, it suggested, something as casual as a visit to a public bath could only be a sexual encounter and therefore must be censored, it was as though I was attempting to take photographs with a camera that was wrestling with me. To create a scene that is as close to my reality as possible using AI tools became not just an act of defiance, but an act of sabotaging that invisible, inoffensive centre that most (Western) AI companies continuously pursue.

The linguistic back-and-forth involved in generating visuals with AI tools has become a new form of artistic practice. Language itself has become a paintbrush, which not only democratises art-making for a global community, but also instills new life in countless practices that were until recently bound by the limitations of the physical world. There is genuine skill involved in parrying with AI by using words.

Most of us agree that AI needs greater accountability. But when you denounce all AI-generated art as lacking the spark of humanity, you denounce our very human practices as well, and we just might be your greatest champions in the pursuit of a human-driven future. So when your Holiness proposed a renewed alliance between the Church and artists, I could not help but wonder: what would a Vatican artist residency focused on artists using AI look like?

Wishing you a speedy recovery,Sarp Kerem Yavuz

#683. Massive Stellar Collisions Could Explain Early Supermassive Black Holes

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Astronomers are in an ongoing scientific wrestling match with some of the JWST's observations. The telescope can gaze back in time by sensing red-shifted light from the early Universe. One of its surprising findings is that supermassive black holes (SMBH) existed long before theory says they should. Some of the SMBHs it detected were found only 400 million and 500 million years after the Big Bang.

So far, there's no solid, widely-held explanation for this. But one contender involves stellar mergers.

New research suggests that in the dense stellar environment in the early Universe, repeated stellar mergers could've lead to eventual SMBHs. It's titled "A Cosmological Framework for Stellar Collisions at High Redshift in Proto-globular Clusters, Nuclear Star Clusters, and Little Red Dots," and it's published in The Astrophysical Journal. The lead author is Claire Williams, a doctoral student in the Department of Physics and Astronomy at UCLA.

"Observations and cosmological simulations indicate that the early Universe hosted numerous compact, high-density stellar systems, where close encounters and physical collisions between stars were likely common," the authors write. "We develop a bottom-up framework for stellar dynamics in such environments, spanning systems with and without intermediate-mass and supermassive black holes, and covering regimes where stellar collisions may or may not dominate the evolution."

The work connects stellar collisions in dense environments with different outcomes. In this work, the researchers started with initial conditions from the AREPO cosmological simulations. They used these to set the parameters for early star clusters, along with the JWST's observations of early star clusters and high-redshift galaxies.

The authors note that these clusters are very similar to Nuclear Star Clusters (NSC) like the one found near Sgr A*, the Milky Way's SMBH. "There, collisions can lead to unusual outcomes, such as the apparent regeneration of an old population with merger products that appear as young massive stars," the authors write.

The researchers point out that almost all galaxies host a SMBH and about 80% of early galaxies also host an NSC. That "suggests that collision-rich environments exist across the Universe," they explain. "As massive black holes and their host star clusters formed, collisions shaped their dynamical and physical evolution."

The team ran simulations to try to understand all of this. The simulations included a variety of different cluster environments with different parameters like velocity dispersions and mass density profiles. "We initialize 1000 stars for each test case, located within 1 pc of the center," they write. This is fewer stars than are within 1 parsec of the Milky Way's black hole. Estimates for that stellar population vary, but some sources say there are more than 6,000.

The results show that stellar collisions are prevalent in a range of star cluster and NSC environments. Not only that, but runaway sequential collisions can eventually create very massive stars (VMS) very early in the Universe. There's no universal definition for VMSs, but something above 100 solar masses is often used.

This isn't the first time that astrophysicists have wondered if stellar collisions could be behind the early SMBH. In fact, this idea precedes the JWST by several decades. But this research takes it a step further by showing how the JWST's intriguing Little Red Dots could be connected.

Little Red Dots are compact objects that appear to be black holes surrounded by ample gas. The black hole is rapidly feeding on this gas and growing very quickly, perhaps at a super-Eddington rate.

This research supports that idea, by showing that plentiful stellar collisions could be generating all the gas, which is fuel for the black hole's growth, but also masks x-rays. Not all of the stellar collisions in the simulations resulted in more massive, merged stars that were doomed to explode as supernovae, leading to black holes. In some of the collisions, both stars were destroyed, and reduced to gas. This could have created a situation where a black hole is surrounded by not only a dense stellar population, but also a dense gas environment.

"Finally, we show that high rates of destructive collisions can rapidly build up extremely dense gaseous environments around massive black holes, potentially providing an analog to the observed population of Little Red Dots," they write.

Intermediate-Mass Black Holes (IMBH), an elusive type of object predicted by the theory of hierarchical black hole growth, is also part of this. IMBH are more massive than stellar-mass black holes, but less massive than SMBH. There are some firm IMBH candidates, but none that everybody agrees are IMBH. The best example might be in the Omega Centauri globular cluster. Others have been detected via gravitational waves emitted during the mergers that created them.

As a newly RRCA-certified run coach, I had an idea of how my next training plan would look: A steady progression of mileage and intensity—one that kicks off with easy runs in the first week, followed by short intervals or strides the next, and then getting into more structured workouts. The Runna plan didn’t mimic anything I was currently doing at the time, so I was skeptical it was aligned with my fitness level.

But I didn’t want to give up on following a plan in the app, so I went through the setup process several more times, until I finally got a program I felt comfortable with following.

Finding the right training plan that aligns with your fitness level and goals isn’t always easy. If you’re a runner who’s looking for a more tailored approach, apps like Runna can fill the gap, especially if training with a coach is out of your reach. The key, though? Knowing how to navigate the app for smarter training, so you avoid taking on too much too soon, while also building consistency and progress that allows you to perform your best on race day.

I successfully used Runna to train for the Copenhagen Half Marathon, cutting more than 10 minutes off my finish time. Here’s exactly what I did to make the plan work for me and my fitness level.

Before you start training with Runna, you’re asked a series of questions about your current fitness and mileage, injury history, and race history. This helps the app generate the best training plan for you based on your answers.

It’s important you respond to these questions honestly, and not based on what you think you can do, says Stephanie Kessell, elite marathoner and head coach at Runna. You can always tweak your training plan, dialing things up or down based on how your training is going afterward.

I told the app I would like to run three days a week, and I used my second half marathon time of 2:48:54 to estimate my finish time. (I completed that race about a month before I started training with Runna.)

Based on that information and a few other answers, the app outlined my 14-week training plan and predicted I could finish my next race anywhere between 2:25 and 2:35. But I took the questionnaire several more times, each time filling in slightly different answers, hoping I could push off the speedwork.

But because I wanted to get faster, I wasn’t able to avoid the initial speed workout. However, I adjusted the training volume of my plan from “progressive” to “steady” and the difficulty level from “balanced” to “comfortable,” which dropped my speed workouts from once a week to once every two weeks. This brought my predicted race time to between 2:30 and 2:40, and I was ready to work toward that target.

Making time for a warmup helps me better settle into a running pace, so I always try to do one (even when I’m not training with an app). When you skip the warmup, you’re forcing your muscles to go from not working at all to working really hard, and that increases your risk for injury, Kessell says. Doing a targeted full-body warmup can help you transition better.

If you don’t know where to start, Kessell recommends doing dynamic stretches and walking or light jogging.

Runna actually suggests warmups, which I always follow, that include moves to target tight areas, like the calves (via a standing calf stretch in which you step one foot back and drive heel to the floor) and hips (with a move like open gates, which requires driving one knee up to hip height, then out to the side and back down).

When I do a shorter warmup or if I skip one entirely, it usually takes a couple of miles for me to get comfortable with moving. But after a 10-minute warmup, I find my pace more easily.

You might find you need more time or to focus on different areas of your body to better prepare. Regardless, making the time to warm up can help you ease into a workout and feel better as you run it—especially if you have speed on the schedule.

Tracking your runs with rate of perceived exertion (RPE)—on a scale of one to 10 with 10 being an all-out effort—helps you tap into how your body is responding to different paces. This also lets you consider other factors that influence pace, such as sleep, nutrition, and weather. The rain, wind, heat, and humidity can make running feel harder, so you might want to go slower when in these conditions, for example.

If you opt for it, Runna even tweaks your pace targets based on heat and time of day. But it’s still important to pay attention to how you feel, especially midrun.

Paying attention to how you feel in between sessions is also important, considering fatigue and soreness can affect your performance. Feeling sore after a hard workout is normal; that’s your body adapting to the work, but if you make huge jumps in mileage or increase intensity too quickly, that can lead to aches and pains, which signal you need a break from running and to see a medical professional, Kessell says.

If you’re feeling fatigued with tired legs, week after week, that can also mean a plan isn’t working for you, she adds. In this case, you may need to adjust intensity or mileage or how many days a week you run.

On the flip side, when you’re recovering well (showing up to each of your runs feeling strong and ready to go) and are consistently ahead of your pace targets a couple of weeks in a row, it’s time to consider progressing your plan.

Runna will automatically recommend training adjustments when you’re under or over your pace targets for more than three consecutive runs, but it’s up to you to accept or reject the change. Keep these signs in mind—about how you’re feeling, not just the speed you’re running—to determine if a tweak to the plan is right for you.

Runna doesn’t have an advanced training program for those who use the run/walk method as a race-day strategy, but that doesn’t mean you can’t train with it. In the past, I’ve experimented with different run/walk intervals to figure out the ratio that works for me. For example, I prefer to run for three minutes and walk for one on my easy days, but on hard days, I run for 90 seconds and walk for 30. I used these ratios to get through all of my training sessions with Runna.

I also downloaded the Simple Interval Timer app to time my run and walk intervals. This helped me better track my walk breaks, so I didn’t miss them. (Runna doesn’t have a built-in timer for you to use.)

When training with Runna, you have the option to monitor your workout targets based on time or feel. I went with a time-based approach, so the app told me whenever I needed to slow down or speed up to keep me on pace.

If you have a time goal, Kessell recommends toggling between both options, so you learn exactly what your target pace feels like. (To do this, select “manage plan” in the Runna app, then change the units of measure.)

Every runner should take some time off after a race, whether it’s a few days or a few weeks. Runna makes this easier by offering postrace training plans to help you make a smoother transition and avoid ramping back up too quickly.

The postrace recovery plan I followed allowed me to start running again without the pressure of a goal. Almost all of the runs I did were 30 minutes or less, and that was just enough to keep me motivated. Running didn’t feel like a chore; it felt like maintenance—exactly what I needed after a big race.

#583. Restoring rivers brings back wildlife, but their natural functions may not recover

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A global analysis of 281 streams found restoration helps species return, but key processes like nitrogen removal often look unchanged.

When a stream gets its curves back or loses a dam, the project is usually judged by what lives in the water afterward. More species often do come back.

The work that goes on in a healthy stream, such as algae growth and the removal of nitrogen from the water, doesn’t reliably return with them.

A new global analysis checked those processes. For most of them, restored streams looked about the same as damaged ones that were never repaired.

The finding matters in Europe, where the Nature Restoration Regulation lists those processes as a goal of river restoration.

The analysis was led by Ulrike Haase, a doctoral researcher at TU Dresden in Germany.

River ecologist Mario Brauns coordinated the study at the Helmholtz Centre for Environmental Research (UFZ), with scientists from 13 research institutions taking part.

The team looked at work that changes a stream’s shape and flow. Researchers call it hydromorphological restoration.

“Hydromorphological restoration can improve biological structure, but ecosystem functions do not automatically recover as a result,” Haase said.

“Our findings show that improvements in stream structure or biological recovery do not necessarily indicate that ecosystem functions have recovered.”

Many of Europe’s rivers and streams have been straightened, blocked by dams and weirs, and had their banks and beds reinforced.

According to Germany’s Federal Environment Agency, fewer than 10% of the country’s rivers and streams receive at least a good rating for ecological health.

The rating depends mainly on which species live there.

Reshaping and polluting rivers cuts the variety of life in them. It also weakens their natural ability to clean themselves.

Restoration projects try to undo the damage. Crews reshape channels, remove dams and other barriers, reconnect rivers to their floodplains, restore plants along the banks, and add large pieces of wood to the water.

Scientists measure a stream’s health partly through ecosystem functions – the natural processes that keep it working.

One is how fast algae and water plants grow using sunlight. Another is how fast fallen leaves and other dead material break down.

A third happens in the streambed, where microbes turn nitrate, a form of nitrogen common in polluted streams, into nitrogen gases.

That process, called denitrification, removes nitrate pollution from the water.

Haase’s team sorted through more than 5,900 publications and kept 66 studies that measured these processes in restored streams.

Together they covered 281 streams or sections of streams in North America, South America, Europe, Asia, and Oceania. The sites ranged from about 50 feet (16 meters) above sea level to nearly 9,800 feet (2,974 meters).

The team compared nine processes across three kinds of sites: damaged, restored, and near-natural. The near-natural sites showed what good health looks like.

Of the nine processes, only denitrification clearly differed between restored and damaged sites. The rates were lower at the restored ones.

“However, this does not automatically mean that the function has deteriorated,” Brauns said. “Lower denitrification rates may also indicate lower nutrient enrichment.”

The researchers also suggested more oxygen or less buildup of organic matter, such as dead leaves, as possible reasons.

But damaged and near-natural sites didn’t clearly differ on this process.

That made it difficult to determine whether lower denitrification rates reflected healthier conditions or other environmental changes.

For the other eight processes, restored and damaged sites showed no clear difference.

The type of restoration didn’t seem to matter either. The authors wrote that their four broad groups of methods may be too coarse to show a difference.

“This shows that the restoration measures commonly used to date are usually not sufficient on their own to ensure a measurable recovery of the ecosystem functions we studied,” said Brauns.

Plant and algae growth showed why a higher number isn’t always a better one. That growth was higher at damaged sites than at near-natural ones.

Extra nutrients from pollution can feed algae, so a polluted site can look more productive than a healthy one.

Restored sites also tended to have more growth than near-natural sites, but that gap was too uncertain to count.

Restored sites removed nitrate from the water more slowly than near-natural sites did, too. At older restored sites, algae growth and denitrification were lower than at damaged sites.

Even so, none of the gaps between restored and near-natural sites changed measurably as the projects aged.

“However, we can assess whether an ecosystem function is actually recovering only if we know its status under appropriate reference conditions,” Brauns said.

Some of the studies tracked both the processes and the living community, such as the number of species and how many living things there were.

In those studies, the living community generally did better at restored sites than at damaged ones. The processes changed less consistently.

The researchers wrote that this comparison rested on only three to eight studies per process. The data were too uncertain to say that life and function recover separately as a rule.

What the analysis does show is that a rising count of freshwater species isn’t enough to show that the processes have recovered.

Besides the Nature Restoration Regulation, the EU’s Biodiversity Strategy for 2030 calls for restoring natural river functions.

The Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework lists restoring ecosystem functions as a goal too.

Scientists call plant and algae growth gross primary production, and the rate at which nutrients are removed from the water is called nutrient uptake.

“Quantifying ecosystem functions such as gross primary production or nutrient uptake often requires considerable effort,” said Brauns.

Unlike biodiversity, these processes still lack common standard methods and reference values. Natural rates also differ between regions and types of rivers, so one healthy number for every site is unlikely to exist.

“The main priority is to further develop and standardize functional indicators for practical use,” Brauns added. “We must incorporate these indicators into future monitoring and assessments of restoration success.”

Until those reference values exist, researchers can measure how fast a restored stream removes nitrogen. They just can’t say whether that rate means it has recovered.

The authors also called for studies that track sites before and after restoration, alongside damaged and near-natural sites.

That approach would help researchers distinguish the effects of restoration from natural changes in weather and water flow.

The full study was published in the journal Global Change Biology.

#3982. People Are Surprisingly Good at Predicting the Future

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

To make better decisions under uncertainty, you must first accept one limitation of human psychology: On your own, you are an unreliable forecaster. Ask one person to predict the outcome of an election or a football game, and their intuition often fails. Bookmakers build empires on this exact vulnerability.

We process reality through a customized lens of cognitive biases and psychological defense mechanisms. These are adaptation strategies that build up over a lifetime for functional reasons: They protect our sense of self, manage anxiety, and help to maintain social cohesion.

But heterogeneous crowds can make predictions differently. Correcting for individual error requires a large number of predictions, people with access to different information, and some skin (or cash) in the game.

When you pool the predictions of thousands of people who assign a monetary value to their confidence, idiosyncratic errors cancel each other out.

This is how prediction markets work. If you believe a political candidate will win an election, you can purchase a contract that pays out if you are correct. You’re not locked into that contract. You can sell or trade it at whatever the market price is, as the odds shift.

The market is an ongoing financial conversation about the likelihood of something happening, and every new transaction is additional information.

The emerging empirical evidence for this collective accuracy is impressive. A study that has just been published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences shows that prediction markets pretty much get the probabilities right.

The researchers evaluated over 1 million contracts drawn from major prediction market platforms, with data spanning 12 distinct topics and 10 years of market activity. To capture genuine foresight rather than obvious hindsight, the researchers extracted probability estimates at standardized fractions of each contract's lifespan.

Think about an election that is 100 days away. If the prediction market opens 100 days before the election, the researchers examined the market's probabilities when the election was 75, 50, and 25 days away. This revealed whether the crowd held genuine early insight long before the outcome became obvious.

The results reveal an excellent ability to predict probabilities. When a market assigns an event a 25 percent probability, you can expect that event to occur between 20 and 35 times out of 100. Even when information is scarce, the crowd tends to get their predictions right.

Just to be clear, this doesn’t mean if a prediction market says that an event is likely, the result will always occur. If a prediction market estimates there is an 80 percent chance of a sports team winning their next match, they tend to win it 4 out of 5 times, not every time. A 95 percent chance of an election going one way still delivers a surprise upset about 1 time out of every 20.

The market and its participants still have some biases. Whether estimating the outcome of a football match, a national election, or the outbreak of a war, prediction markets remain largely accurate in the likelihoods they assign to events.

However, the crowd consistently underestimates political outcomes while consistently overestimating the likelihood of military and geopolitical conflicts. When a prediction market prices the probability of a war or security event at 75 percent, the historical data proves the true likelihood is closer to 63 percent.

We also predict the breakout of war better than we can predict peace. By evaluating the sentiment of geopolitical and security questions, the researchers found that forecasts concerning pessimistic or negative outcomes yield consistently higher accuracy than optimistic or neutral predictions. The collective unconscious may be more accurate as Cassandra than Pollyanna.

To navigate uncertainty, we have to abandon the myth of the perfectly rational and fully informed individual navigating a marketplace (sorry, economists). On their own, our personal forecasts are unreliable, especially about major world events.

Prediction markets also reveal a vital insight about human reasoning: Groups with identical assumptions repeat the same mistakes. Overconfidence amongst like-minded individuals makes groups worse at predicting the future. Combining information across groups that make different mistakes brings us remarkably closer to the truth.

Improving our own decision-making requires a deliberate shift in what we think we know about the future. Instead of consulting your gut or polling a room of like-minded peers, actively seek out objective ways to pool information you have with people who disagree with you.

To see the future more clearly, adapt alongside those who possess entirely different blind spots.

#4246. Four Months of Zig

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It has been about four months since I returned to Zig after being absent for a few years. In this time, I have written some software in Zig and reengaged with the community, online and in person. I decided to share some of that experience, including what I have liked about Zig so far and what I haven't. I'll include some comparisons to Rust along the way since that was my previous go-to language.

The first project I started in Zig was snurl. It is a library for interacting with the smallnet (Gemini, Finger, Gopher, Spartan, and such). My goal has been to provide an alternative to curl for the smallnet, with similar testing and fuzzing requirements but on a smaller scale. I wanted to roughly follow TigerStyle insofar as it made sense for the project.

Something I ran into early on was that the fuzzer didn't actually work. When getting excited about fuzzing in my last post, I didn't realize that the feature had not yet been completed. This isn't surprising, considering Zig's status, but it was disappointing, especially since the fuzzer is demonstrated in the generated main.zig file provided by zig init.

After this, I wrote a two programs which I have not published yet. The first is a small TUI typer racer. It was simpler than I expected to get a minimum version working so far. The second is an n-body simulator with stellar evolution using raylib.

The most exciting opportunity in the last few months was the chance to go to a local Zig Day. I learned of Zig Day Seattle occurring on Lobsters on August 20th. I was ecstatic to learn that there was a Zig Day in my area. Then I saw Andrew Kelley would be there and became more excited.

Zig Days are local meetups where people interested in Zig hang out, work on their projects together, and talk about Zig. For this Zig Day, I decided to work on snurl. A friend, Evin Yulo also decided to attend.

The event itself occurred in the Bothell library, moved there from the Bellevue library after so many people signed up that capacity was overwhelmed. Having the creator of Zig at the event will do that.

I love the format of these Zig Days and I'm glad they are a part of the community. Since they are run by local groups, it gives the whole Zig community a more interconnected feeling. It's a way to interact with other people who are interested in Zig in person. And, in this case, the creator of Zig itself.

I got the chance to talk with other programmers about their projects. Hopefully some ideological cross-pollination occurred that will make all our projects stronger. I ended up working on snurl with evin, who started work on integrating Zig's standard library TLS implementation as alternative backend.

I also briefly talked with Andrew Kelley. I hope Zig's built-in fuzzing gets fixed sometime soon!

Ryan Hendrickson wrote an excellent article outlining more of the events of that day from his perspective if you want more details.

I will definitely be attending Zig Day Seattle again in the future. I may even head down to Portland to catch one before the next local one comes around. I enjoy the opportunity to connect with other people interested in Zig and its values.

I love anyzig. I've had to use zig master and 0.16.0 for different projects, and anyzig has made that doable.

Unfortunately, it breaks outside of directories without a build.zig.zon, meaning if you have tooling that requires a zig command to work, it can only occur in project directories. This breaks autoformatting for my one-off Zig scripts. Maybe a default zig version could be provided in that case.

I know it frustrates some people, but I love the fact that you have to pass IO and allocator parameters to functions in Zig.

I'll start with allocators, since they've been around longer. For each Zig function you have to accept an explicit allocator parameter. This is different from most programming languages which either use a default allocator that may be changed globally or one changed by a local context (see Odin).

I like being able to change allocators for different parts of code. You might want an arena allocator for some things but need a general purpose allocator for others. Sometimes a FixedBufferAllocator is all you need to perform an operation. Zig's style makes this all obvious to handle.

I prefer Zig's system to Odin's implicit context system since Zig keeps things explicit. It's clear whether a function allocates memory or not, and hard to accidentally pass in the wrong allocator.

This is also superior to Rust's hidden, infallible allocations that panic when memory is exhausted. They're working on adding explicit allocations, but this will be yet another feature tacked onto the language rather than something built from the ground up.

It's much easier to reason about programs that allocate memory in Zig.

The IO parameter is even more powerful, even if it is newer. It is well worth the breaking changes required to bring it about (even if I skipped the 0.15.0 -> 0.16.0 transition). In Zig, every function that interacts with the underlying operating system (with caveats) requires an "IO" parameter. This provides the context for input, output, and everything else. Porting Zig to new operating systems is mostly just porting IO.

Practically, this is really nice for testing. When testing behavior, you just need to put in a testing IO instance that works as you need it to. You can change the input stream however you want, cause writes to fail, and anything else.

Just like with allocation, passing an IO argument makes it clear which programs are pure and which ones are not. You can tell at a glace whether or not a program is going to modify something external.

Zig has a really good track record of keeping things simple while also adding powerful features. Instead of adding 100 small features that do slightly different similar things, it tends to provide one major feature that supports all the various other use-cases. Low floor, high ceiling.

I love comptime. It's a seamless way to do metaprogramming and precomputation all in one. Other people have written more detailed accounts so I shall move on.

Zig's build system is stellar. So much so that companies like Uber have adopted it for their software written in other languages.

One thing I like about it is that I can integrate other tools with it seamlessly in ways that Cargo complains about. This is especially important for one of my projects that involves code generation.

Zig has an excellent dependency culture. The main ethos is simple: avoid. When you don't need a dependency, don't add one. However, when you do, it's often a battle-tested library that Zig can effortlessly integrate with translate-c. You can easily also vendor in external dependencies and audit their code.

Most of my Zig programs have about one or two direct Zig dependencies, with zero indirect dependencies. This makes the codebase much easier to audit and control.

This stands in stark contrast to Rust, where the smallest of my projects regularly have a few direct and dozens of indirect dependencies.

This is thanks both to Zig's dependency culture and the fact that essential features like reflection, random number generation, clean error handling, and JSON parsing are built into the language or standard library rather than maintained as separate individual libraries. No need to depend on serde or syn in every program.

Zig encourages careful consideration before adding dependencies, and I appreciate that.

Zig pushes programmers to handle errors correctly by default. The laziest way to handle errors in Zig is to use the try keyword and add ! to the function's return type.

This contrasts to Rust, which leads programmers to insert panics into their code. The issue is that it's often easier to panic when prototyping, and then leave that in the code longer than it should be. I've used production code written in Rust that panic instead of returning a proper error.

In Rust you can use ?, which is quicker than unwrap, but you also have to do something like Result. That's a mouthful! Alternatively, you can use the anyhow crate and return anyhow::Result, which also provides some nice helper methods. But then you have to add a dependency for reasonable error handling. There's also Zig-style composable errors with yet another library.

The core team members at Zig have been doing amazing work at incremental compilation. If you run zig build --watch -fincremental, the compiler will watch for changes, check exactly what you changed, and then modify only what you've changed down to linking. No effort beyond what's required, so it's faster that human perception even for large codebases. Zig was designed from the ground up to support this behavior.

This makes for a really nice workflow. I can just run the compiler in incremental mode right next to my text editor, and get updates on my code as I save. No time to get distracted with something else. With Rust projects 10+ seconds incremental compilation loops were common even in smaller projects. That's enough time to get distracted but not enough time to be productive doing something else, leaving annoying gaps that Zig eliminates.

Rust is disadvantaged here. It's a stable language that needs to maintain backwards compatibility. This means it can't afford to break things in order to improve compilation speed, at least not to any large degree. Meanwhile, if it makes Zig better, the maintainers are willing to break just about anything to ensure the end result will be perfect.

Currently, Zig only supports incremental compilation on x86_64 on Linux and macOS. If you use another operating system or architecture you won't have access to this feature yet, but in that case it's another thing to look out for!

This makes Zig just a bit more wonderful to work with. I'll take it.

Like every other user of Zig, I would like to see the standard library become more stable. I know that's one of the last tasks to do before 1.0, and there are many others before then, but it is frustrating to deal with the constant breakage in the ecosystem. My understanding is that that's why 0.17.0 took so long to be released.

Andrew Kelley said at Zig Day that he would like to see 1.0 more than anyone. I believe him, so I will have patience and trust that the final version will be excellent.

This is the one reason I still use Rust for some projects. Some cases require a stable programming language and the ecosystem that provides. But considering the impressive software that's being built in Zig these days, stability isn't everything. And there are projects for which the niceties of Zig outweigh its instability. I can't wait for 1.0.

I would like to see more assistance with lifetimes in the Zig compiler. Specifically, I would like to see returning local variables to fail in debug mode, either as a kind of built-in lint (as Andrew Kelley has mentioned wanting to implement) or as literally invalidating that memory after the function returns. That way new users quickly learn to avoid that pattern. Currently, it's easy to unintentionally break your code this way, even if you think you understand lifetimes.

Relatedly, the compiler currently accepts code that will be broken in the future. The compiler lets variables live the entire scope instead of the scope of a block. This means that if you stack-allocate a buffer and return it from a block, that buffer should be invalidated but isn't. My understanding is that this could silently break in a future Zig version.

Zig is poorly documented. It has enough to get you started, but to continue onward you have to parse intention from sparse doc comments that are sometimes attached to functions. Examples are rare. I still haven't got Zig's standard library TLS working in snurl because neither I nor a friend can figure out how to set up the buffers correctly.

Because of this lack of documentation, a lot of the knowledge for building solid Zig programs is distributed across the Web. If you're well integrated into Zig communities, having read the existing documentation, watched the presentations, and read the code, you can succeed. If you aren't that well integrated, however, it can be a challenge to work effectively.

The benefit of this is that it encourages people to engage in Zig communities (I'm an admin for the Matrix room, for example). While LLMs seek to demolish human collaboration, we need human-to-human collaboration now more than ever. However, we can still have that and nice documentation too. As we all know, people will still ask questions if you have things well-documented. And a good community knows how to point people towards good resources rather than just saying "RTFM".

I would also be curious to see what the intersection of Zig and literate programming could be. My literate programming tool is written in Rust right now, but that might change.

I would love to parse JSON at compile time. For one of my projects, there is a JSON file that is stored inside the binary. It's static and could be parsed at compile time into a collection of structs.

However, the standard library JSON parser requires an allocator. Because there are no allocators that work at compile time, this means that any JSON parsing must be done at runtime. So that's what I did with my project.

I believe there is an intention of adding a JSON parser that does not require an allocator (the data already exists in the JSON string, after all). That will solve the immediate problem, but there are more general cases that may require an allocator. I believe FixedBufferAllocator worked at compile time before and served this purpose. It could do so again.

Zig's bootstrapping story is impressive. They erased the C++ version of their compiler and replaced it with a small WASM blob and WASM interpreter. The WASM file is a stripped-down version of the compiler that compiles the full Zig compiler to C, which is then compiled by the system C compiler. It's an interesting approach and you should read the linked article if you want more details.

The one caveat to this is that it requires the compiled binary blob checked into the source code. This is better than requiring the full previous compiler (like Rust), but it's not as good as having the full source available for every step of the process at all points. A malicious actor could still sneak something into the blob. More importantly, many distributions (like Debian) cannot properly package Zig at the moment because it's impossible to do so in a way that's compliant with their guidelines.

Their original plan was to use the compiler's C backend to generate a C version of the compiler which would then be maintained separately. They would then have two different source versions. They decided this approach would be too difficult, unfortunately.

Because of this, someone will have to make another Zig compiler in another language (probably C) that will improve the bootstrap process. This is explicitly not something the Zig Software Foundation wants to develop right now. This means that at the moment there is no way to bootstrap Zig fully from source.

This issue isn't just a theoretical problem. Because Zig can't be packaged on Debian, I had to choose another language for one of my projects to ensure wider possible usage.

Fortunately, this issue is temporary. Once Zig is stable, building an alternative compiler should be straightforward. Zig is designed to be a simple language where alternative implementations are humanly achievable, unlike other languages. Currently, the instability means any alternative compiler will be playing a constant game of catch-up, which is probably why the core team doesn't want to manage it while they're rapidly iterating.

Alas, this may also require waiting for 1.0. May it come soon!

I will continue to use Zig. I'm excited about the direction it's going and I look forward to continuing to be a part of its community. So far, I've found no other programming language that I prefer more than Zig, even with its shortcomings. It's just stable enough that I don't have to make massive changes between releases. It will continue to be my go-to programming language for most projects.

One of these projects is ReTangled. It was previously written in Rust but I grew tired of the massive dependency trees and want to take full advantage of Zig's build system.

I've also been looking to contribute more to Zig directly. Between my limited time as a student, Zig's overflow of contributors already, and the poor documentation, this will be a challenge. But I want to contribute back, make an impact on the project, and become a better software engineer. Thankfully, improving contributor onboarding seems to be a priority for Andrew Kelley moving forward.

I'll also be at future Zig Days in Seattle, if you want to meet and work on Zig projects together!

Mark Kiptoo averaged 5:10 pace at the Frankfurt Marathon to reset a 35-year-old masters mark.

Mark Kiptoo has had some pretty memorable trips to Frankfurt. He won the city's marathon in 2014, returned four years later to set a then-world record for runners over 40, and on Sunday added another achievement to the list. This time, at 50 years old.

The Kenyan distance runner finished the Frankfurt Marathon on October 25 in 2:15:24, averaging roughly 5:10 per mile across 26.2 miles. He placed 13th overall, but his performance was particularly impressive for another reason: It was the fastest marathon ever recorded by a man in the 50–54 age group—and it wasn't especially close.

For 35 years, the M50 record belonged to South Africa's Titus Mamabolo, who ran 2:19:29 in Durban in July 1991. Kiptoo cut four minutes and five seconds off that mark on Sunday, a substantial improvement that now awaits official ratification by World Masters Athletics.

Kiptoo has barely had time to get used to being 50. He celebrated his birthday on June 21, less than two months after finishing 2:12:21 at the Geneva Marathon. That performance came too early to qualify for an M50 record, but it offered a pretty good preview of what he could do once he entered the age group.

Frankfurt wasn't even his first record-setting performance this month. On October 4, Kiptoo won the San Sebastián Half Marathon in Spain in 1:03:57, establishing a new M50 world-best time for the distance. Three weeks later, he was back on a starting line, this time with twice as many miles to cover.

Kiptoo didn't start competing until he was 28, entering his first race in 2004 while serving in the Kenyan Air Force. He went on to win the African 5,000-meter title in 2012, but didn't make his marathon debut until age 37. That first 26.2 was in Frankfurt, where he finished second in 2013, just one second behind the winner.

He also lost roughly a year of training to Achilles tendon trouble and the pandemic, according to the Frankfurt organizers. He returned to win the Zurich Marathon three years in a row from 2023 through 2025, clocking 2:09:16 in his last victory at age 48.

Before Sunday's race, Kiptoo told organizers he hoped his running would inspire people not to give up or stop taking care of their bodies as they got older. Running a 2:15 marathon at 50 certainly gives him a pretty persuasive example to point to.

#3991. Shame Is a Repeating 6-Act Cycle: An IFS View of Self-Attack

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

IFS is an 'experience-near therapy,' which is to say, it follows subjective experience closely. As a result, IFS practitioners understand the mind as plural. It is a community of parts (as people say) of varying ages and roles. When something bad happens, some parts become protective and other parts (the vulnerable ones) get blamed and exiled.

But that’s not all. In addition to parts, the mind produces (or channels, depending on your perspective) a different kind of consciousness, which is characterized by cognitive clarity and centered emotional states like curiosity and compassion. IFS calls this (again, as people say) Self, with a capital S to signal a special use of the word: “This is my true Self.” When the Self is overshadowed by the strong feelings of parts, we lose balance. Shame is especially unbalancing.

Here is a description of shame as a long-term experience featuring distinct acts in relationship and in the theatre of the mind.

An authority figure (caretaker, relative, teacher, older child, sibling, or peer) shames a child. The bad thing that happens is interpersonal. The shamer is not me. The shamed child is a passive recipient of the shaming with no intent in this situation. If we flash forward to therapy in the present, the bad thing that happened has become history.

Act 2. Acceptance: The Shamed Part Believes I Am Bad (Defective, Worthless, Evil, Rotten, Monstrous, Too Much, Too Little)

The most vulnerable part of the shamed child accepts the judgment and concludes I am shameful. Acceptance, like the shaming event itself, sets the stage for the problems that might lead a person to therapy.

Act 3. Critics Go Large: That’s True, You Are Bad

Proactive manager parts hold the shamed part of the child responsible for being shamed. Hoping to improve or at least suppress its despair, they start shaming it. I call this instrumental shaming because, even though shaming will eventually lead to antisocial avoidance and reactive rage, this inner shamer has a positive, prosocial intent. By inhibiting the shamed part, who it views as a liability, it aims to take control of a dangerous situation. With the advent of inner shaming, "I" am not only the recipient of shaming, “I” am also the shamer.

Anticipatory proactive "scout" protectors do a lot of warning: Do be this! Don't be that! Be quiet! Apologize! Aiming to prevent more shaming, inside or out, they anticipate the complaints of a (real or imagined) critical audience. Because of them and other proactive critics, a problem that was once external is now self-­generated. Their intent is prosocial, but their actions reinforce both the antisocial existence of exiled young parts and the antisocial behavior of reactive protectors.

Reactive protectors cover a lot of territory. Some shame other people, diminishing them by comparison and rebooting the self-­esteem of a system that is collapsing under the weight of internal shaming. This external shaming is also instrumental. This reactive protector is rebellious, disinhibited, and ready to throw the hot potato. Though its behavior is antisocial and will have bad consequences, its internal intent is still prosocial. It aims to ward off the dangerous consequences of continual shaming.

Other reactive protectors get relief from shaming and shamefulness with some preferred way of self-soothing or distracting. A wide range of behaviors involving disinhibition can fit this bill. They drink too much, use drugs excessively, smoke cigarettes, gobble soothing foods, gamble compulsively, and so on. They take risks without concern for consequences. They also mean to be helpful, and they are essentially asocial—they just don’t care about other people—but their behavior generally has a negative effect on other people.

I’ve organized the shame cycle neatly into separate acts, but neither life nor therapy is neat. Protectors can all be in action at once—and that can be overwhelmingly confusing. Here’s the thing to remember: This confusion can be sorted by the motives and actions of parts. It is the story of shame. The parts who are caught in the shame cycle need your help to slow down and take turns telling their portion of the story. They need you to listen with kindness and compassion, and ask questions with genuine curiosity. When you do, the big story will gradually change from I am bad to A bad thing happened to me.
