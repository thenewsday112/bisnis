#2242. Vietnam: Independent Trade Unions Suppressed

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

(New York) ‒ The Vietnamese government is systematically suppressing the rights of workers to organize independent unions, Human Rights Watch said in a report released today. Despite pledges to trading partners in several free trade agreements, the government has failed to put in place meaningful reforms to enable trade unions to form and operate freely, and it routinely represses independent labor organizing.

The 49-page report, “There Are No Independent Trade Unions in Vietnam: Hanoi’s Continuing Assault on Workers’ Right to Organize,” documents the Vietnamese governments’ continuing use of vague, overbroad criminal laws to suppress the activities of human rights and labor rights defenders, while maintaining an atmosphere of repression to thwart independent labor organizing. The government has issued decrees and articles in state-run media that reaffirm that all labor organizations in Vietnam are under the government’s authority, and portray independent trade unions as dangerous, anti-state entities. 

“Officials in Hanoi have repeatedly made false, misleading, and deceptive claims about labor rights, while lauding Vietnam’s supposed reforms to suggest they allow unions to form,” said John Sifton, Asia advocacy director at Human Rights Watch. “In reality, the Vietnamese government remains deeply hostile to the very idea of independent trade unions, which is why none exist.” 

Human Rights Watch interviewed more than 30 workers, human rights organizers, labor rights and legal experts, and others familiar with the labor rights situation in Vietnam. It also analyzed Vietnamese laws and regulations and reviewed articles about labor issues in Vietnamese state-run media.

Vietnamese officials have repeatedly stated publicly that legal reforms in 2021 allow workers to organize and that Vietnam has thousands of “grassroots” trade unions. These claims have primarily been directed at trading partners in the context of various economic agreements. They include the 2020 European Union-Vietnam Free Trade Agreement (EVFTA), later agreements with the United Kingdom and European Free Trade Association, and the 2019 Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP), which includes Canada, Australia, the United Kingdom, Japan, Brazil, and Mexico, among other countries. 

These agreements obligated Vietnam to undertake reforms that would allow independent trade unions to exist. In 2021, the government made some legislative changes that purportedly authorized the creation of independent “worker organizations,” but subsequently failed to create any regulatory framework that would allow workers to form them.

The only legal mechanisms that Vietnamese workers can utilize to address labor rights concerns exist within entities, or so-called “trade unions,” set up under the authority of the Vietnam General Confederation of Labor (VGCL). Under Vietnamese law, the confederation and its sub-units explicitly fall under the control of the ruling Communist Party of Vietnam. Party officials choose or approve leaders at every level, and the government provides their budget, which is funded by mandatory fees paid by employers and workers. These worker entities do not meet legal criteria under international law to be considered independent trade unions, Human Rights Watch said. 

While VGCL entities sometimes engage in activities ostensibly addressing labor issues, their efforts are limited and do not genuinely represent the workers, and they ultimately act in the interests of the Communist Party. None of these entities has ever called for a strike or led any confrontational activities against employers.

“A union has to have its own freedom, and freedom to grow,” one exiled labor organizer told Human Rights Watch. “But the party remains the leadership of all things in Vietnam.”

Even if independent worker entities could form in Vietnam, existing provisions of Vietnamese labor laws do not meet international standards and are inadequate to protect labor rights, Human Rights Watch said.

The government’s intolerance for independent trade unions has harmed other labor rights of Vietnamese workers, Human Rights Watch said. Without the freedom to form independent unions, workers in Vietnam are less able to bargain effectively or seek higher wages, better working conditions, or remedies or redress for individual and collective work-related grievances.

Workers said that even if the government adopted procedures to allow independent labor organizations to form, authorities would misuse provisions in Vietnam’s penal code to target organizers. The government has long used criminal laws to prosecute labor rights and human rights activists and suppress the creation of independent organizations. 

For decades, the authorities have imprisoned prominent independent journalists, bloggers, and activists, several of whom have advocated labor rights. At any given time in the last 10 years, Vietnam had in detention at least 150 to 180 political prisoners, either awaiting trial or imprisoned after unfair convictions. Repression has increased more recently: in 2025 and 2026 alone, the authorities have jailed over 50 dissidents.

Authorities have specifically targeted human rights defenders and labor organizers who raised rights issues in the context of the 2021 trade agreement with the EU, and even jailed some of its own officials involved in negotiations with the EU on labor rights issues.

Numerous Vietnamese-language statements and articles by officials reveal the negative views and deep hostility of the authorities toward independent trade unions. Officials cited in government-controlled media outlets portray independent trade unions as “reactionary” forces opposed to the Communist Party, engaged in “subversive” and “sinister” plots to undermine and sabotage the government,” with the “true objective” of undermining government-controlled unions, “confronting the State,” and “sowing social instability.” The heading over one such article is “Crime Warnings.”

The Vietnamese government should ratify and put into effect all relevant International Labour Organisation treaties without delay and set a clear timeline for when it will allow genuinely independent trade unions to form, Human Rights Watch said. Vietnam’s trading partners should press the government to meet these commitments and release all human rights and labor rights activists imprisoned for exercising their right to free expression. 

“Many of Vietnam’s trading partners have accepted Hanoi’s claims of reform without scrutiny, and some still contend that trade agreements have improved Vietnam’s rights record,” Sifton said. “Vietnam’s trading partners need to face reality and make clear to the government that its broken pledges on labor rights carry consequences.”

#2947. Upgrading To A Home You Don’t Need: A Three-Year Review

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Toward the end of 2023, we upgraded to a larger finished home we didn't need. Three years later, I want to share whether it was worth it, so you can make a better decision if you're tempted to do the same.

More than any other material item money can buy, a home is the most desirable. The average person spends over 14 hours a day at home, so there's also probably no investment we use more. Sure, we have to wear clothes in public, but we swap them out every day. And last I checked, my t-shirts and shoes don't appreciate in value.

Fine watches and jewelry are nice and can appreciate too. But we don't wear them every day, and we definitely don't live inside them. So hands down, a home is the best combination of enjoying your money while also potentially making a positive return.

Upgrading to a home you don't need is a slightly different animal.

If you upgrade into something other people find undesirable or unnecessary, your home probably won't give you a positive return you're hoping for. But if you upgrade in the “frenzy zone” price range, the one buyers fight over in your market, you'll almost always have strong demand when it's your turn to sell.

My wife and I talked about this on our podcast, and we've concluded the purchase was worth it.

It took a tremendous amount of convincing to get her to agree to move. She finally acquiesced after I promised this would be our last house ever, assuming we stay in San Francisco. That promise was the main thing that got her over the line.

Our children have enjoyed the extra space tremendously. They play in the enclosed front yard every day, and we don't have to worry about them getting hit by a car or snatched by a stranger. The neighborhood is quiet and safe, although there's now a lot of remodeling going on.

I've also noticed our boy needs to run around more than our daughter, which seems typical. Once he's burned off the energy, it's easier to get him to focus on homework.

If you have a boy, or any extremely active child, a home on a large, usable lot can be beneficial for childhood development. That's something we didn't fully appreciate before buying.

There's also peace in knowing we'll never move again. For 20 years, from 2003 to 2023, I was addicted to real estate and constantly climbing the property ladder. Now that we've reached the top rung we can afford, I feel settled in a way I didn't expect. No more wanting feels amazing!

If you're focused on decumulation, or at least on spending more of the hard-earned money and risky investments that have paid off, it feels good to turn that money into something you actually enjoy.

A nicer home checks the box of being something we use every single day, which is more than I can say for most things I've spent on.

From a purely financial standpoint, the toughest part of upgrading to a home you don't need is thinking about how much more you could have made had you invested elsewhere, usually in stocks.

The S&P 500 is up over 80% since we sold stocks and Treasury bonds to buy our home. The opportunity cost of not making more elsewhere must be weighed against the utility you derived from your home.

It could always be worse, though. I talked to one dad who sold his Salesforce (CRM) stock in 2009 to buy his home. Had he held on, the stock would be up about 10X. I talked to another dad, currently thinking about upgrading, who worked at Tesla and sold shares in 2015 to buy his place. That stock is up more than 20X since.

Both of their properties are also up since purchase. But their old stock positions are up dramatically more.

All in, I'm sure I've left over $1 million on the table by buying our house, net of how much the house has appreciated. The opportunity cost could have been worse, but luckily the San Francisco housing market is strong thanks to AI. It's boom loop time.

I'm not sure we could buy our house today if it came on the market. In 24 years of searching for property in San Francisco, I've never seen a lot this size at this price point. So maybe the market is adjusting higher than I think, especially as the west side of San Francisco gets more popular.

The general recommendation is to set aside 1% of your home's value each year for maintenance. It's a decent rule of thumb, especially if you own a condo with a homeowners association, which I also do.

The low cost isn't entirely luck. We were thorough during the three-month escrow and had the sellers fix as much as possible before closing. The house had also been gut remodeled and fully landscaped right before our purchase, so we bought near the top of its maintenance cycle, not the bottom.

Run through this checklist before you fall in love and experience buyer’s remorse:

1) Make sure your spouse is genuinely on board. Not “fine, whatever” on board. Actually on board and extremely excited. A reluctant partner turns a great house into a recurring argument. Make a promise you can keep, like I did, and mean it.

2) Run the 30/30/3-5 rule. Have at least 30% of the purchase price saved (20% down, 10% buffer), keep the monthly payment under 30% of your gross income, and limit the home price to no more than 3 to 5 times your annual gross income. Break this rule and you invite stress.

3) Buy just above the frenzy zone. Pick a home and neighborhood that buyers fight over, then look for a price point about 10% higher. The frenzy zone is where most people bid, which means bidding wars that can leave you with tremendous buyer’s remorse if you win. Just above it, there’s less competition and better deals.

3) Stress test your cash flow. Model the new mortgage, property taxes, insurance, and maintenance, then assume one income disappears for six months. If the math gives you a panic attack, you’re stretching too far. I went through a six-month period of living paycheck to paycheck which recreated the stress I felt in banking almost every day.

4) Decide mortgage vs. cash with your opportunity cost in mind. Before you sell investments, ask what that money would likely earn if you left it alone, because that’s the cost you’ll feel most. Paying cash removes the monthly payment but concentrates your money in one illiquid asset. A mortgage keeps more invested but costs you fees, paperwork, and a lien.

If you can afford to pay cash and the home is worth 30% or less of your net worth after purchase, the cash route makes sense with interest rates this high. You can often get a better deal, or win the bid, by closing quickly with cash.

5) Hire an inspector and use a long escrow. This is where you create leverage. Find every defect and push the seller to fix as much as possible before closing. Our low maintenance bill is a direct result of being relentless during escrow.

6) Match the home to your family’s actual life. Active kids need a lot to run around on. Aging parents with bad hips prefer a single-story or a guest suite. Buy for how you’ll really live now and in the future, not for the staged photos, and not to impress people who aren’t paying your property tax bill.

In conclusion, we would happily buy this home again. The experience has worked out, but of course, it could easily have not. I cannot get these three years back, but I can appreciate how this house has provided a nicer life for my wife and children.

The one thing I might change is taking out a mortgage so I wouldn't have had to sell so much stock. But a mortgage would have cost about $10,000 in fees upfront, plus more work, plus the bank holding a lien. So maybe not.

Money is meant to be spent and enjoyed while we're still alive. So if you want to upgrade your home, go right ahead. Just follow the 30/30/3-5 rule so you don't put yourself in financial jeopardy and end up too stressed to enjoy the place.

In addition to fall being a great time to get new plants in the ground, it can also be an ideal season to fertilize your lawn, trees, shrubs, and perennials. But you have to do it correctly: During these cooler months, plants are slipping into dormancy and not actively growing. Depending on how severe or mild your winter is, they can be anywhere between completely dormant to growing very, very slowly. If you fertilize right, you’ll be giving them the best send-off to their winter sleep.

Before you start, you may want to get in touch with your local cooperative extension. They can help you get a soil test (you can’t help your plants if you don’t know what they need). And they can tell you the first frost date for your area (for practical purposes, fertilizing should be done before the first frost). They know your climate best and can give you advice specific to your location.

Note: We don’t recommend using synthetic fertilizers because of the large environmental impacts associated with them, including water contamination from run off and decimation of soil microbes. Restoring soil health naturally should always be the first option.

Here’s what you need to know about fertilizing (naturally) in the fall.

The best and easiest way to fertilize is to do one last mow with a mulching mower and leave the clippings on the lawn. Mulching the clippings back into the lawn can provide up to 50% of the needed nutrients for the grass. To make up the rest of what your lawn needs, there are two low-cost and environmentally sustainable ways to fertilize. First, you can aerate the lawn and top dress with compost. Second, if you have fallen leaves, mulch them into the lawn as well. Just remember to rake them around so they aren’t too thick. It is a smart idea to keep the nutrients created on your property, on your property. (See Ask the Expert: Doug Tallamy Explains Why (and How to) Leave the Leaves.)

A more expensive, less eco-conscious option is to use organic lawn fertilizer. While organic fertilizers are certainly better than chemical fertilizers, there are still manufacturing and transportation costs to the environment. If you go this route, follow the directions exactly. More is not better.

A closed loop is the best type of fertilizer. Keep the leaves from the trees under them. They have everything the trees need—for free. They help on so many levels. They act as mulch and keep the moisture in the soil, which in turn helps the microbes that break down the leaves, making their nutrients available to the trees. The leaves also become winter homes for good bugs. Just be sure to keep the root flare exposed; piling the leaves up the trunk can cause can cause the bark to rot. No trees on your property? Organic compost is your next best choice.

For shrubs that were healthy over the growing season, a leaf well around the base will be enough. If they didn’t do well over the summer, they may need a bit of help. Aerate the soil and add some compost and water well.

If you want to add store-bought organic fertilizer to your tree or shrub, you may want to consider consulting with an arborist first. It’s easy to over-fertilize and cause damage. Leave it to the professionals.

Yes, leaves again. Really. And compost. Both the leaves and the compost break down slowly. Nobody is in a rush here, it’s winter and nothing is growing. The idea is that the nutrients will be ready and in a form the plant can use once it wakes up in the spring. If you’re concerned about burying your plants too deep in leaves, lightly cover what remains of your almost dormant plants, but pack the leaves thickly around them.

There are no other real options. If you use synthetic fertilizer while they are going into dormancy, they could come out of dormancy early during an extended warm spell—and then when a cold snap follows, the new growth may be killed. This can weaken the plant, causing it to fail to thrive in the spring or even die.

Our gardens are part of a larger ecosystem, so it’s important to prioritize soil health and natural methods over synthetic fertilizers. Aeration of compacted soil and addition of organic matter will have a better and a longer-lasting effect on plant health.

#4088. Visual Identity: Montreal Jazz Festival Concept

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A bold visual identity concept for Montreal Jazz Festival pairs generative TouchDesigner trumpet forms with agile jazz typography.

Designing for an institution like the Montreal Jazz Festival means grappling with decades of visual heritage. I've always loved digging through classic festival archives. When you sift through fifty years of festival posters, the visual languages shift wildly from era to era. Yet one instinct stays unbroken: raw, expressive color. That historical continuity sparked this self-initiated project. The goal wasn't to wipe the slate clean, but to channel that historic warmth into a responsive, modular system built for contemporary stages and digital channels.

Typography carries the heartbeat of the identity. A tall, condensed display face tilts at an aggressive angle. Its elongated vertical stems mimic guitar strings pulled taut across a wooden fretboard. For dense lineup schedules and stage times, a calm grotesque keeps functional information legible. But the real spark comes from loose, hand-drawn lettering. These gestural letterforms twist across the layout like an improvised trumpet solo. They scale up, duck behind photography, and break out of the margins. Structured information and loose expressive riffs play off each other continuously.

To translate live acoustics into graphics, the system relies on node-based networks in TouchDesigner. Sound waves expand through concentric rings that echo the brass bell of a trumpet. This procedural pattern ripples across large outdoor building banners, staff credentials, transit posters, and canvas deck chairs. Because the graphic engine generates infinite variations, no two festival touchpoints ever look identical. It captures the very essence of live jazz: disciplined structure giving way to spontaneous joy.

Physical collateral brings this energetic language down to earth. Printed guides use tactile folds that tuck easily into festival pockets. Silk-screened enamel keychains turn the trumpet motif into an enduring keepsake, while woven wristbands separate zones through punchy color contrast. Everything feels unified without turning repetitive. It honors the soul of live music while giving the Montreal Jazz Festival an agile toolkit for the future.

Nin herself — a woman uncommonly liberated from the common traps of convention, control, and self-consciousness — took up the spiritual mechanics of this paradox in her first published book, D. H. Lawrence: An Unprofessional Study (public library), composed when she was still in her twenties.

With an eye to D.H. Lawrence (September 11, 1885–March 2, 1930) and his “philosophy that was against division,” his “plea for whole vision,” she writes:

When the realization came to the moderns of the importance of vitality and warmth, they willed the warmth with their minds. But Lawrence, with the terrible flair of the genius, sensed that a mere mental conjuring of the elemental was a perversion… Lawrence believed that the feelings of the body, from its most extreme impulses to its smallest gesture, are the warm root for true vision, and from that warm root can we truly grow. The livingness of the body was natural; the interference of the mind had created divisions, the consciousness of wrong-doing or well-doing.

In a sentiment central to my own animating ethos, she adds:

Life is a process of becoming, a combination of states we have to go through. Where people fail is that they wish to elect a state and remain in it. This is a kind of death.

It was Lawrence’s own writing that awakened in her this awareness of ongoingness and the urgency of total aliveness — the way “livingness is the axis of his world, the light, the gravitation, and electromagnetism of his world.”

In his 1924 novel The Boy in the Bush, Lawrence makes a stunning case for the indivisibility of it all — the beauty and the sorrow, the ache and the astonishment:

All real living hurts as well as fulfils. Happiness comes when we have lived and have a respite for sheer forgetting. Happiness, in the vulgar sense, is just a holiday experience. The life-long happiness lies in being used by life; hurt by life, driven and goaded by life, replenished and overjoyed with life, fighting for life’s sake. That is real happiness. In the undergoing, a large part of it is pain.

This was the foundational philosophy of Lawrence’s worldview — the pulse-beat that makes his writing so resonant and eternally alive, the way all great spiritual texts are. He distilled this view in an especially beautiful passage from his 1923 novel Kangaroo, reckoning with the most universal reality of life — the reality we spend our lives fighting, yet the one that peeks through in all of our greatest works of art and highest triumphs of the creative spirit. Echoing Whitman’s defense of our inner multitudes, often at odds with each other, he writes in an era when every woman was a “man” purely as a matter of linguistic convention:

If a man loves life, and feels the sacredness and mystery of life, then he knows that life is full of strange and subtle and even conflicting imperatives. And a wise man learns to recognize the imperatives as they arise — or nearly so — and to obey. But most men bruise themselves to death trying to fight and overcome their own, new, life-born needs, life’s ever strange imperatives. The secret of all life is obedience: obedience to the urge that arises in the soul, the urge that is life itself, urging us to new gestures, new embraces, new emotions, new combinations, new creations.

In the same epoch when Hermann Hesse so beautifully defended the wisdom of the inner voice, Lawrence’s protagonist makes a passionate case for listening to the song of life as it reverberates through the singular cathedral of each self, yours and mine, as it did for Nin and Lawrence and every other great mind long sung out of existence:

I offer no creed. I offer myself, my heart of wisdom, strange warm cavern where the voice of the oracle steams in from the unknown; I offer my consciousness, which hears the voice; and I offer my mind and my will, for the battle against every obstacle to respond to the voice of life.

A sarcophagus in Westminster Abbey with Roman and Anglo-Saxon decorations baffled archaeologists, but now a 150-year-old mystery has finally been solved.

In the late 1800s, Westminster Abbey clergy stumbled upon a mysterious sarcophagus. The lid was decorated with an 11th-century Anglo-Saxon cross, but the base had a fourth-century Roman funeral inscription. For nearly 150 years, the tomb has baffled experts, but the abbey's consulting archaeologist may have finally figured out who was buried there, according to a new study.

Westminster's chief mason unearthed the stone sarcophagus in 1869, but when experts investigated it, they discovered that the burial had been looted earlier that century. Experts also noticed that the carved decoration on the side of the sarcophagus was stylistically Roman, with a Latin inscription that can be translated as "To the memory of Valerius Amandinus. Valerius Superventor and Marcellus made this for their father." But an enormous medieval cross had been carved into the lid of the sarcophagus, suggesting that the tomb had been reused to bury an important religious figure hundreds of years later.

The mystery of the medieval individual buried in a Roman sarcophagus remained unsolved for 150 years, as no one thought that any bones were left inside. But when the stone coffin was opened for conservation in 2016 and a full skeleton was discovered, clues to the identity of the deceased finally came to light, Warwick Rodwell, the consultant archaeologist for Westminster Abbey, wrote in a study published Wednesday (Oct. 21) in the Journal of the British Archaeological Association.

Although the skeleton had been disturbed over the years, with a hole accidentally made in the skull and arm and leg bones mixed up, it was clear that the person was male, middle-aged, and of average height, bioarchaeologist James Langthorne of Pre-Construct Archaeology wrote in a 2016 report. The man also had some evidence of osteoarthritis, two healed rib injuries and gout. The bones were carbon-dated to between 987 and 1052, confirming that the Roman-era sarcophagus was reused in the Middle Ages.

"This brings us to the final question of importance: who was he?" Rodwell wrote in the study. Despite the lack of grave goods, which may have been stolen in the early 19th century, "he was almost certainly an abbot" who was given a dignified burial in a stone sarcophagus with a large cross, Rodwell wrote.

Based on the birth and death dates of the abbots of Westminster and their tenure as clergy, Rodwell narrowed the candidates down to Wulfsige (abbot from 988 to 993), Ælfwig (abbot from 993 to 1020) and Wulfnoth (abbot from 1020 to 1049). Given that Wulfsige left Westminster to become a bishop elsewhere, he was likely not buried in the abbey.

"Consequently, we have two viable contenders for the occupant of the sarcophagus," Rodwell wrote. "The fact that Ælfwig was already a monk of Westminster might favour him."

Researchers propose a new subtype of depression linked to body-clock patterns.

Some people with depression sleep far more than average, wake up exhausted, habitually stay up late, and experience mood shifts with the seasons. A new study suggests that these symptoms may cluster in a distinct subtype of depression that's tied to the body's internal clock.

While research has long linked a scrambled circadian rhythm to a risk of mood disorders, neuroscientists are now proposing that these disruptions define a specific form of depression that they call "circadian depression." First conceptualized in 2021, the body-clock subtype may affect about 1 in 4 people with severe, recurrent or persistent depression, the researchers estimate.

In their new study, published Wednesday (Oct. 21) in The British Journal of Psychiatry, the team identified the subtype in about 2,600 people out of over 11,000 evaluated.

"Circadian depression is a biologically informed subtype," said study co-author Emiliana Tonini, a neuroscientist at the University of Sydney. "Unlike subtypes defined mainly by symptoms, the core feature here is a disturbed circadian system, which common antidepressants may not fully address," Tonini told Live Science in an email.

The researchers think treatments that target the sleep-wake system, such as the sleep hormone melatonin or bright light therapy, might be beneficial for people with this subtype. However, because the subtype is still a proposal and not formally established yet, other experts say that further research is needed before the findings can inform clinical practice.

Tonini and her team analyzed data from 11,380 adults ages 18 to 90 who took part in the Australian Genetics of Depression Study (AGDS), a nationwide study exploring how genes and life experiences influence depression and responses to treatment. They classified participants under the "circadian" umbrella if they had at least three of six features: seasonal mood swings, late sleep schedules, social jet lag (a mismatch between sleep routine during workdays and free days), night-owl habits, excessive sleep (hypersomnia), and fatigue upon waking.

A total of 2,604 people fit the proposed subtype. Compared with others in the AGDS cohort, this group was younger and developed depression earlier in life — around 19, compared with about 23 for the rest of the cohort.

The group also skewed female, and individuals reported greater psychological distress, frequent suicidal thoughts and more physical complaints than people who didn't fit the proposed subtype. Some experienced weight gain during depressive episodes and symptoms resembling hypomania, a revved-up state of elevated mood and energy that's milder than full-blown mania.

However, having this constellation of symptoms doesn't mean someone's biological clock is disrupted, said Brant Hasler, a psychologist and sleep researcher at the University of Pittsburgh who was not involved in the study. The researchers relied on the participants' self-reports of their sleep patterns; that data correlates only modestly with direct measures of circadian timing, such as measurements of melatonin in saliva, he said in an email.

"One can identify as an evening-type, and report later sleep timing, and still have physiological circadian timing that is not particularly delayed," he said.

So, in addition to checking symptoms, the team hunted for genetic clues underlying the proposed subtype. They analyzed DNA from participants' saliva samples and calculated "polygenic risk scores," which add up the influence of thousands of different genetic variants to estimate how likely someone is to develop a given condition.

The body's sleep-wake cycle is largely controlled by a hub of cells within the hypothalamus (red).

"Mental health conditions are shaped by hundreds or thousands of variants that each add a small amount of risk, so everyone carries a unique 'portfolio' of risk," Tonini explained.

People in the circadian group had a higher genetic risk for depression, anxiety, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and bipolar disorder, the analysis suggested. They also had a greater genetic risk of higher body weight, insulin resistance, inflammation, insomnia and late-night sleep habits, compared with the other study participants.

This suggests "circadian depression is more than just 'depression with bad sleep,'" Tonini said. "It involves a mix of metabolic, inflammatory, circadian, and mood-related processes."

The researchers looked at the participants' past experiences with antidepressants, finding that the circadian group reported less benefit from common medications, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs). Common examples include the SSRI Lexapro and the SNRI Cymbalta.

The circadian group also experienced more side effects from these treatments, such as drowsiness, nausea, anxiety and weight gain.

"If circadian disruption is a core biological mechanism in these individuals, treatments that fail to address those abnormalities may naturally be less effective," said Dr. Ruth Benca, a psychiatrist and sleep researcher at Wake Forest University School of Medicine who was not involved in the study. However, Benca cautioned that body-clock disruption may not be the only reason standard drugs fall short for these patients.

"People with more pronounced circadian disturbances often have more severe illness and other psychiatric conditions, which can contribute to treatment resistance," Benca told Live Science in an email.

Even so, tracking sleep, measurements of circadian rhythm, and seasonal shifts in mood could help clinicians identify at-risk patients earlier and select more effective treatments sooner in the course of illness, Benca suggested. "Potential approaches include behavioral routines, structured sleep schedules, or chronotherapy," in which the timing of medication or light exposure is strategically coordinated with internal body rhythms, she added.

Clinical trials are still needed to draw firm conclusions about potential treatments, Tonini noted. The findings also must be validated in broader populations, since the study drew on a single Australian cohort and its genetic analysis included only people of European ancestry.

For now, "the study is an important step toward a more personalized treatment approach," she said.

#1363. Where Did Ernest Hemingway Live?

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

His addresses included a Cuba estate with 9,000 books and a final Idaho retreat.

Ernest Hemingway is almost as closely associated with certain places as he is with his books: Paris and its cafés, Key West and its cats, Cuba and its fishing boats. The American novelist and journalist spent much of his life abroad, writing books including A Farewell to Arms and The Old Man and the Sea before winning the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1954.

With The Sun Also Rises turning 100 this week, we’re looking at the places he called home. His addresses ranged from a modest apartment in Paris, where he lived as a young writer with his first wife, Hadley Richardson, to a house outside Havana filled with thousands of books. Here’s where Hemingway lived, how he put together his homes, and what remains of them today.

Hemingway’s first address was decidedly more Victorian than the spare prose that would make him famous: He was born on July 21, 1899, in his maternal grandparents’ Queen Anne house at 339 North Oak Park Avenue in Oak Park, Illinois. Built in 1890, Ernest, his parents, his siblings, and his widowed grandfather all lived in the home. Today, the birthplace operates as a museum, restored to reflect the house the Hall and Hemingway families knew.

When Hemingway was six, the family moved to 600 North Kenilworth Avenue, a 1906 stucco house designed by architect Henry G. Fiddelke with close involvement from Hemingway’s mother, Grace. Its Prairie-style architecture offered a departure from the prior home’s Victorian silhouette. Hemingway lived here until he was 17.

For the Hemingway family, summer meant Windemere, a cottage on northern Michigan’s Walloon Lake where Ernest spent much of his childhood vacationing. The original building measured just 20 by 40 feet, with a brick fireplace anchoring the living room and deep window seats that doubled as beds for the children.

Northern Michigan also became enduring literary material. The region supplied settings and experiences for stories including “The End of Something,” “The Three-Day Blow,” and “Big Two-Hearted River.”

In 1917, an 18-year-old Hemingway moved to Kansas City to work as a reporter for The Kansas City Star. His approximately six-month reporting stint took him to police stations, the hospital, and Union Station, an education in both city life and economical writing. He stayed at 3733 Warwick Boulevard before moving to 3516 Agnes Avenue, where he shared a large room with a friend. He also spent time with his aunt and uncle at their nearby Victorian house on Warwick Boulevard, a building later lost to a fire.

Hemingway’s return to the Chicago area brought a succession of shared rooms and apartments, including a room on North State Street and quarters at the Belleville Hotel on East Division Street. More consequentially, Chicago was where he met Hadley Richardson in 1920.

After their September 1921 wedding and Michigan honeymoon, the newlyweds settled briefly into a top-floor apartment at 1239 North Dearborn Street. Biographer Gioia Diliberto’s account describes small rooms and worn furniture that Richardson found dispiriting, hardly the romantic setting the marriage might have suggested. They did not stay long: By that winter, the couple had left for Paris.

Hemingway and Richardson lived at 74 Rue du Cardinal Lemoine from January 1922 until August 1923. He also rented a room nearby at 39 Rue Descartes to use as a writing studio. The couple then moved to Toronto, where their son, John—later known as Jack—was born that October. In January 1924, the family returned to Paris and rented an apartment at 113 Rue Notre-Dame-des-Champs, directly above a noisy sawmill.

Hemingway worked for the Toronto Star in the 1920s, and in 1923 he and the pregnant Hadley returned from France for the birth of their first child. John Hadley Nicanor Hemingway, known as Bumby, was born there on October 10.

After a hotel stay, the family settled into apartment 19 at 1599 Bathurst Street, a residence Hemingway leased for a year but occupied only briefly before returning to Paris. The building later acquired a fitting name, The Hemingway, and a heritage plaque commemorating its former tenant. It remains a residential building today.

Hemingway first arrived in Key West with his second wife, Pauline Pfeiffer, in April 1928, and in 1931 her uncle Gus purchased a house at 907 Whitehead Street for the couple. Built in 1851 from locally quarried stone, the residence needed extensive work. Ernest and Pauline restored and remodeled it, furnishing the interiors with European antiques they had acquired during their years abroad.

Here, Hemingway had a separate writing studio above the carriage house. They eventually added the home’s most extravagant feature: a 60-foot-long swimming pool. Completed in 1938, it cost $20,000 and required workers to excavate the coral beneath the site. The house now survives as a museum, preserving the rooms, studio, and gardens associated with Hemingway’s years on the island. Its resident cats have become nearly as recognizable as the yellow shutters.

In 1939, Hemingway began living at Finca Vigía, a property outside Havana rented by the writer Martha Gellhorn, who would become his third wife. Cooking with pumpkin can help get more fiber into your day. High-fiber foods feed the beneficial bacteria in your gut, keep your digestive system moving and support regular bowel movements. #1029. This Is What Halloween Candy Looked Like in the 1960s

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We've lost some excellent Halloween candy since the '60s, yet somehow, we've kept the raisins.

Halloween in the 1960s was a little less elaborate than the theatrical holiday we know today. There was no popping into Target for a complete costume in a plastic bag. Costumes were often assembled from whatever was already in the house, and the treats waiting at the neighbors’ doors weren’t necessarily wrapped in plastic either. My mom was a teenager in the ’60s, and when I asked her what she remembered, she told me about going as a cowgirl one Halloween, likely outfitted with my grandpa’s bandana and her older brother’s hat and boots. A little ingenuity could get you through the evening, preferably with a good haul of candy to show for it.

The contents of kids’ Halloween bags at the time offer a fascinating glimpse of the era. Familiar chocolate bars shared space with forgotten confections, homemade sweets and the occasional box of raisins that probably disappointed children then just as much as it would now.

A chocolate bar called Chicken Dinner sounds like a joke, but Milwaukee’s Sperry Candy Company was entirely serious when it introduced the candy in 1923. Despite the name, it contained no poultry, just a chocolate-covered nut roll. The name borrowed from the old promise of a chicken in every pot, and Sperry took the bit well beyond the packaging.

The wrappers featured roasted chickens, and the company even sent enormous chicken-shaped promotional trucks onto the streets to advertise the candy. I can’t decide if seeing one drive past would have made me more or less inclined to try a Chicken Dinner bar, but I’d certainly have wanted a closer look.

Sadly, the chickens came home to roost in 1962, when Pearson’s acquired Sperry and discontinued the bar.

Butterfinger turned 40 years old in 1963, and its crisp, flaky peanut butter center was as distinctive then as it is now. The bar was among those advertised for Halloween that decade, and its appeal was easy to understand. Unlike the smooth fillings and chewy centers of most other chocolate bars, Butterfinger offered a delicate crunch that shattered into little peanut buttery flakes beneath its chocolate coating.

It’s a texture that still sets Butterfinger apart from nearly everything else in the candy aisle.

A Snickers bar in your Halloween bag in the ’60s was a particularly good find, especially in 1968, when the Fun Size version first appeared. Now, we almost take the handful of minis we grab from the bowl for granted!

The bar itself was familiar territory: milk chocolate, peanuts, caramel and nougat, just as it had been since its introduction in 1930. And while smaller candy bars certainly existed, finding a full-sized one would have been a welcome improvement over a handful of pennies or a stale piece of hard candy.

The Seven Up Bar is one of those discontinued candy bars that makes me question the judgment of everyone involved in its disappearance. The bar had nothing to do with the lemon-lime soda, although the two companies did eventually find themselves negotiating over the Seven Up name. Made by Pearson’s, the candy bar contained seven individually filled sections beneath a chocolate coating. Depending on the period, the fillings included flavors like caramel, coconut, fudge, buttercream, cherry cream, orange jelly and Brazil nut.

It was essentially an assorted box of chocolates in bar form, with none of the uncertainty of biting into a mystery piece. I can’t help thinking it was an excellent idea abandoned too soon.

If you found a 100 Grand in your Halloween bag in the ’60s, you’d have known it by a different name. Nestlé introduced the $100,000 Bar in 1964, filling it with chewy caramel and crisped rice beneath a milk chocolate coating. It was a rather ambitious name for a candy bar that originally cost a dime.

The original wrapper promised “A Fortune in Flavor,” and the candy quickly caught on. By the end of the decade, miniature versions had appeared too. The name eventually changed to 100 Grand, but the combination of gooey caramel and crunchy rice has stuck around. Apparently, it’s the one fortune that hasn’t suffered much from inflation.

Tootsie Rolls were practically antiques by 1960, having been introduced in 1896, but the individually wrapped chocolate chews were perfect for trick-or-treating, and miniature versions appeared in Halloween advertisements that October. Tootsie Pops were available, too, although the famous television commercial featuring the owl wouldn’t arrive until 1970.

The candy itself has changed remarkably little. It still occupies that curious territory between chocolate and caramel, requiring more chewing than its modest size would suggest. And for anyone who has ever bitten into a Tootsie Pop rather than patiently licking their way to the center, the appeal of the combination needs no explanation.

By Halloween in the 1960s, candy corn had long since traded its agricultural roots for a permanent place among the seasonal sweets. The little tricolored kernels originated in the late 1800s, when candy makers were marketing the treat as Chicken Feed. Seems a questionable way to persuade people to eat something.

Whatever your feelings about candy corn, its unusually waxy texture and honeyed flavor have proved remarkably resistant to change. Set a handful of candy corn from the ’60s next to today’s version, and you’d have a difficult time telling them apart.

Of all the things a child might find in a Halloween bag in the ’60s, candy cigarettes may be the most revealing of the time. The chalky white sugar sticks came in miniature cigarette packs, sometimes designed to resemble actual tobacco brands. Kids could even pretend to smoke them, because nothing says childhood innocence quite like a pack-a-day habit.

The 1964 Surgeon General’s report would formally establish smoking as a major health hazard, helping change public attitudes toward cigarettes. But in the early ’60s, handing a child a tiny pack of candy smokes was still considered an adorable treat. It’s hard to imagine a better illustration of how much Halloween—and the world around it—has changed.

Bazooka offered something the chocolate bars couldn’t: reading material. Each piece of bright pink bubble gum came wrapped with a Bazooka Joe comic, a feature introduced in 1953 that gave kids something to enjoy long after the gum had lost its flavor.

The gum itself was famously firm, particularly when first unwrapped, but once you managed to soften it, there was the prospect of blowing an enormous pink bubble.

Cracker Jack had a persuasive argument for its place in a trick-or-treat bag: It was two treats in one. The caramel-coated popcorn and peanuts were only half the attraction; every box also contained a surprise. By the early 1960s, the company was promoting Cracker Jack for Halloween, and I suspect the possibility of finding a miniature toy inside the box was enough to make it a welcome addition to the evening’s haul.

The prizes weren’t particularly valuable, but that was hardly the point. Half the fun was digging through the popcorn to discover what you’d gotten. Cracker Jack retired its physical prizes in 2016, replacing them with digital experiences. I can’t imagine anyone feeling quite the same anticipation about scanning a QR code.

A candy apple was a rather extravagant addition to a Halloween haul in the 1960s. Instead of a few bites of factory-made chocolate, you got an entire apple encased in a glossy red shell of hardened sugar, often with a wooden stick for a handle. It was probably inconvenient to carry around while trick-or-treating, but I doubt many children objected to receiving something that looked as though it had come straight from a carnival.

Pixie Stix made their debut in 1959, just in time to give the children of the 1960s a remarkably efficient way to eat sugar. The colorful paper straws were filled with sweet-and-sour powdered candy, which you poured directly into your mouth. No chocolate, no caramel, no pretense of nutritional value. Just sugar with an easy delivery system.

The candy actually began as a powdered drink mix until someone noticed that children were skipping the water and eating the powder instead. Rather than discourage them, the company leaned into the idea. The kids had spoken, and they wanted their sugar straight.

Before individually wrapped candy became the default Halloween handout, plenty of neighbors made their own treats, and popcorn balls were a classic. Making them was a somewhat sticky undertaking: Freshly popped corn was coated in hot sugar syrup, then shaped into balls while the mixture was still pliable. Wrapped in waxed paper or cellophane, they were ready for the trick-or-treaters who came knocking.

And then there were raisins. In October 1962, the Chicago Daily Tribune recommended miniature boxes of Sun-Maid raisins for Halloween. The writer seemed confident that trick-or-treaters would be delighted, which suggests a rather optimistic understanding of what children hope to find in their Halloween bags.

Of course, the miniature Sun-Maid boxes are still with us, and so, I suspect, is the disappointment of discovering one at the bottom of a bag full of chocolate. Some traditions survive without the benefit of popular demand.
