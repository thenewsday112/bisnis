#3580. How Long Should Your Runs Be During Marathon Race Week?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Taper nerves are real, but one last workout can do more harm than good. Here’s the right range to stick to for the week.

After months of early alarms, long runs, and workouts, logging fewer and fewer miles from the start of your taper through race week can feel uncomfortable. Short runs may leave you wondering whether you should add just a few more miles, pick up the pace, or squeeze in one last confidence-building workout before you hit the starting line.

But the final week is not about chasing fitness. At this point, training has little effect on your progress; the remaining runs primarily serve as maintenance. You want to feel fresh and ready to race—not like you just put in a tough workout. That restraint will pay off on race day, when rested legs let you tap into the fitness you’ve already built.

So what should you cap your runs at during race week? Here’s what to know to protect your fitness and show off your performance gains when you get to the starting line.

Runner’s World previously reported that, beginning Monday of marathon week, runners should keep runs to about four miles or less. Yes, just four miles.

That comes with a few exceptions. Many Runner’s World marathon training plans recommend one six- to seven-mile run about four days before race day, often involving some intervals to keep you sharp.

The point of keeping runs so short? The months before a marathon are when you build endurance. The final days before your race are when your body gets the chance to fully absorb that work.

Research backs this up, demonstrating an association between progressively reduced training in the final weeks before a marathon and better race performances.

For those thinking about pushing it, know that a tough workout during race week is unlikely to make you fitter for your race, and on top of that, it can actually hurt the performance you’ve spent months preparing to achieve.

This is why the four- to seven-mile cap on race-week runs may prove especially useful for those who feel unsettled by the light taper schedule, especially in the final days before their race. It’s a boundary that gives you enough time on your feet to stay connected to your normal running routine, while helping keep your restless pre-race brain from turning an easy run into an unnecessary training session.

Nearly all race-week running should be easy—about 90 seconds to two minutes per mile slower than goal marathon pace, Runner’s World previously reported. The few stretches that aren’t easy come in the form of familiar race-pace pickups, strides, or short intervals in an effort to keep you loose, not wear you out.

We’ve all heard of the adage, “Nothing new on race day,” but in this case it applies to race week as well. Josh Wessler, an ACSM-certified personal trainer and RRCA-certified run coach, previously cautioned runners against ditching their training plan entirely during race week, meaning you should continue practicing consistency without introducing new stressors. Keep things short, easy, and predictable.

Taper nerves can make the urge to push harder feel especially convincing; Wessler says it’s common for runners to question their fitness and readiness during race week. But a hard effort is unlikely to make you fitter now.

Race week is about “preserving energy and sharpening, not building fitness,” Maya Ellison, CPT, RRCA-certified run coach and owner of P.A.C.E.D. Coaching Services, previously told Runner’s World.

Runners should also avoid trying new shoes, paces, gels, or fuel alternatives in race week, Namrita Brooke, PhD, RDN, endurance coach and sports dietitian, previously told Runner’s World. Those variables should have been tested weeks earlier, with time to make adjustments.

The bottom line: Let your final runs before 26.2 be boring. Wear the gear you know works. Run at an effort you recognize. You’ve already built your marathon fitness; keeping race-week runs capped at about an hour helps ensure you get to that hard-earned fitness when it counts.

When it arrives, the probe will attempt to land on one of the Red Planet's twin moons and scoop up rocks to bring back to Earth. Experts tell Live Science what these "transformative" samples could teach us.

On Monday (Oct. 19), Japan's space agency launched a first-of-its-kind sample-return mission to Mars. The historic endeavor marks another major step toward humanity's shared goal of understanding — and perhaps eventually colonizing — one of our closest planetary neighbors, experts say.

The Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency's (JAXA) Martian Moons Exploration (MMX) probe is an international effort, constructed with help from NASA, the European Space Agency (ESA) and others. Over the next few years, it will closely study the Red Planet's twin moons, Phobos and Deimos. The spacecraft will snap close-up photos of the pair before attempting to land on the larger satellite, Phobos, to scoop up rocky material that will hopefully be carried back to Earth in around five years.

If the mission is successful, the returned samples will finally settle a decades-old mystery about the origins of both moons. The findings could also help paint a picture of Mars' ancient history and provide clues about what the early solar system was like.

The MMX probe will study Phobos (bottom center) and Deimos (upper right) up close. [This is a composite image made by stitching together individual photos of Mars (left) and its moons.]

"This mission marks a transformative leap in our capacity to investigate the Martian system," Sonia Fornasier, an astronomer at the Paris Observatory who has studied Phobos and Deimos extensively and is a project scientist for MMX's infrared spectrometer instrument (MIRS), told Live Science. It "ranks very high" among the missions we have sent to the Red Planet so far, she added.

JAXA launched the MMX probe on board an H3 rocket, which lifted off from Tanegashima Space Center in Kagoshima prefecture at 3:41 p.m. EDT Monday (4:41 a.m. Tuesday local time), Live Science's sister site Space.com reported. (The mission was initially scheduled to launch in 2024, but it was delayed due to initial concerns about the H3 rocket.)

The camper-van-sized spacecraft successfully separated from the rocket's fairing just over an hour later, and on Wednesday (Oct. 21), JAXA announced that the mission had passed the "initial critical operations phase," meaning the probe is now locked into its roughly 140 million-mile-long (225 million kilometers) journey to Mars, which will take around 10 months.

"MMX took its first step today," Naoki Murakami, general manager of Mitsubishi Electric’s space division, which led the design of the MMX probe, said in a postlaunch news conference early Tuesday morning (Oct. 20), according to The Japan Times. "This marks the beginning of a long, five-year journey."

Large crowds gathered at the Tanegashima Space Center to watch the launch of the H3 rocket carrying MMX in the early hours of Monday (Oct. 19).

Once the probe arrives at the Red Planet in August 2027, it will insert itself into a "quasi-satellite orbit" around Phobos. In other words, MMX will circle Mars alongside the moon but will not be gravitationally bound to it, similar to around half a dozen "quasi-moons" that currently accompany Earth around the sun.

This mission marks a transformative leap in our capacity to investigate the Martian system

After collecting data from a distance for a few months, MMX will swoop down and attempt to land on the surface of Phobos ‪—‬ not once, but twice.

During the first landing, MMX will hover just above the moon and deploy a mini rover, dubbed Idefix, onto the moon's surface. The wandering robot, built by the French and German space agencies, will have to contend with extremely weak gravity as it trundles across the surface of Phobos: The rover weighs around 55 pounds (25 kilograms) on Earth but will weigh only around half an ounce (15 grams) on Phobos, according to ESA.

The MMX probe will arrive at Mars in August 2027, following a 10-month journey from Earth.

Once Idefix has completed its initial assessment of the moon's surface, the entire MMX probe will set down on Phobos, on top of the spacecraft's four-legged lander module. Mission scientists hope this goes a lot more smoothly than the attempted landings of Japan's "Moon Sniper" and Resilience lunar landers, which crash-landed on Earth's moon in 2024 and 2025, respectively.

Two of the MMX lander's legs contain built-in sampling devices ‪—‬ one that will drill into Phobos and another that will hoover up surface material. The second device, dubbed the Pneumatic Sampler (or P-Sampler), was built by the Blue Origin-owned private company Honeybee Robotics on behalf of NASA and will collect its material in a "matter of seconds," Kris Zacny, vice president of the Exploration Technology Group at Honeybee Robotics, said at an Oct. 15 NASA news conference. "You blink, and it's all done."

In total, JAXA aims to collect around a third of an ounce (10 grams) of material from Phobos. That would be around twice as much material as the space agency managed to snag on its most recent sample-return mission, Hayabusa2, which collected samples from the "spinning-top" asteroid Ryugu in 2019, finding materials rich in surprising organic compounds.

The probe will deploy the IDEFIX rover on Phobos before it lands to collect its samples. The wandering robot will provide initial data that will help select a suitable landing site for MMX.

Once the samples have been acquired, at some point between late 2028 and early 2029, the probe will conduct a series of close flybys of Mars' smaller moon, Deimos, before beginning the long trip back to Earth in late 2030 — the first return flight ever attempted by a spacecraft sent to Mars.

When the probe returns to Earth in 2031, it will release its samples in a 20-inch-wide (60 centimeters) container that will fall back to our planet's surface, most likely somewhere in Australia, according to ESA.

Mars' twin satellites, which were first spotted by American astronomer Asaph Hall in 1877, are nothing like Earth's constant companion. For starters, they are much smaller; Phobos is around 14 miles (22 km) across, while Deimos is just 7.5 miles (12 km) wide. The duo also have lopsided shapes and are between 12 and 64 times closer to Mars than the moon is to Earth. (Phobos, meaning "fear," and Deimos, meaning "terror," are named after the sons of the Greek war god Ares, the counterpart to the Roman war god Mars.)

Phobos is dominated by the 6-mile-wide (10 km) Stickney crater, which was left by an ancient asteroid impact. The larger moon is also slowly falling toward Mars and may eventually be ripped apart before it reaches the Red Planet's surface.

The most notable feature on Phobos is Stickney crater, which is more than 5 miles wide. This photo of the crater was snapped by the HiRISE camera on board NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, and was released earlier this month.

Other Martian spacecraft, such as the Emirates Mars Mission orbiter, have flown close to the moons, and NASA's Mars rovers have frequently looked up to see the satellites eclipsing the sun in Mars' sky (sometimes both at the same time). Yet many mysteries remain about the pair.

"Mars is right in our cosmic backyard, and yet we haven't explored the planet's moons," Nancy Chabot, a space exploration expert at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory in Maryland and the deputy principal investigator of NASA's MEGANE instrument on board MMX, told Live Science in an email. "MMX will do this!"

MMX will closely analyze Mars' moons using nearly a dozen different sensors and instruments to help pin down the mysterious origin of the twin satellites.

The biggest mystery surrounding Phobos and Deimos is their origin. Some researchers suspect the satellites are asteroids, or maybe even comets, that have been captured by Mars' gravity, while others think the moons emerged from the debris of an ancient impact on the planet's surface, similar to how Earth's moon formed.

Neither theory is conclusive, meaning the moons' origins "remain enigmatic," Christopher Edwards, a planetary scientist at Northern Arizona University and one of NASA's participating scientists for MMX, told Live Science.

For example, explaining how Mars captured the moons is "quite hard from an orbital mechanics perspective," but if they did form from a debris disk from an ancient impact, they should have coalesced into better-defined spheres, Edwards said.

"MMX will solve this debate thanks to the spacecraft observations and the detailed analysis of the samples of Phobos on Earth," Fornasier said. The data the probe collects from its flybys of Deimos will also allow researchers to generate a "full characterization of this body," she added.

This photo of Deimos was snapped by the Emirates Moon Mission in 2023 and is one of the best images of either of Mars' moons to date.

Fornasier is "particularly excited" by the possibility that the moons are captured asteroids because that would mean they would likely contain "organic and hydrated materials," which would have a big "astrobiological implication" and would shed light on what the early solar system was like.

Regardless of how the twin moons formed, researchers also stand to learn a lot about Mars and how planets form and evolve.

"Phobos and Deimos are interesting, but not just in their own right," Tom Statler, an MMX program executive and scientist in NASA's Planetary Science Division, said at the Oct. 15 news conference. "Their presence is a key piece of the larger story of the Mars system. By uncovering the moons' origin, we'll gain new insights into the history of both Mars and Earth."

MMX has been more than a decade in the making and involved input from agencies and companies around the globe. This photo shows the project's manager, Yasuhiro Kawakatsu, standing in front of two parts of the probe inside a clean room at the Tanegashima Space Center in August.

If the moons did form via an impact scenario, the samples MMX collects may also contain actual pieces of Mars for researchers to study.

"If these objects are in fact pieces from the Martian surface that have coalesced moons, we'll collect the best-preserved samples of the Martian surface," Edwards said. Other fragments of Mars have already reached Earth as meteorites. However, entry into Earth's atmosphere can transform them.

If Phobos is a chunk of Mars, that would be a real boon to NASA, which canceled its Mars Sample Return program — a mission that would have retrieved samples collected by the Perseverance rover — earlier this year. The agency's researchers will receive up to 15% of the MMX samples to analyze.

MMX will also shed light on Mars' ancient history and evolution, as well as analyzing the planet's moons. It will depart the Red Planet at some point in late 2030.

During its three-year stay in the Red Planet's system, the Japanese probe will also collect lots of data on Mars that could contribute to a wide range of future findings. "MMX will conduct in-depth studies of the Martian atmosphere, with a particular emphasis on dust and water transport mechanisms and the escape processes shaping Mars' atmospheric dynamics," Fornasier said.

This atmospheric data could temporarily fill a gap left by the recent death of NASA's iconic MAVEN spacecraft, which stopped sending data back to Earth late last year.

Analysis of craters on the Martian moons could also provide clues about other events in Mars' history. For example, some researchers think the Red Planet may have once had rings like Saturn or a larger moon.

Experts have described MMX as a "transformative leap" and "paradigm-shifting" for Martian science.

Other experts are hopeful that the MMX findings could help lay the groundwork for future missions to explore ‪—‬ and perhaps colonize ‪—‬ Mars. "The samples from MMX will bring forth paradigm-shifting science on the Mars system for generations to come, especially as NASA prepares to send the first humans to Mars," Nicky Fox, associate administrator for NASA's Science Mission Directorate, said in a recent statement.

The next sample-return trip to Mars will be China's Tianwen-3 mission, which is scheduled to launch in 2028 and will aim to land on and retrieve samples directly from the Red Planet's surface, according to Space.com.

#2607. 10 Questions Worth Asking At Your Annual Physical After Age 40, According To Doctors

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

They want to talk to you about prevention, prevention, and more prevention.

Between work, kids, kids’ extracurriculars, family obligations, and everything else, it’s easy, as moms, to let our own health fall by the wayside. If nothing’s bothering you, of course your annual physical is the first thing to get canceled in a week loaded with other appointments and to-dos. But doctors agree that while moms are often the last people to complain about feeling tired, rundown, or off, they also tend to wait too long to bring their concerns to a medical provider, as Scary Mommy previously reported. So, this is your sign to keep that annual physical and use it to talk to your doctor about all the health concerns that come with aging. While you might be fine now, your doctor is eager to tell you how to keep it that way.

“Cardiovascular disease is the number one killer in the United States for both men and women. In order to live a long and happy life you need to understand your risk for developing cardiovascular disease and how to combat and lower your risk,” says Dr. Colleen Reidy, DO, is a family medicine physician at The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center.

“This is the blood work that I recommend for most patients,” says Reidy. “CMP will evaluate electrolytes, kidney function, and liver function. A CBC will look into your blood counts. Ferritin level will screen for iron deficiency, which can be the first sign of colon cancer. Lipoprotein a and LDL/lipid panel can give us insight into your risk for high cholesterol and cardiovascular disease. If you are obese or have a family history of diabetes, you might want to consider getting your A1C checked to rule out pre-diabetes or diabetes.”

Make sure you discuss any family history of cancer with your primary care provider while you’re at it. “Age-based cancer screening allows for early detection of certain types of cancers and reduces cancer mortality,” says Dr. Jacqueline Jepsen, family medicine physician at The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center.

Your family history may influence when screening should begin and how frequently it should be performed, according to Dr. Harold Jaimes, board-certified internal medicine physician and owner of Jaimes Medical Group in Chicago.

“Always tell your doctor about any family history of cancer, especially when relatives were diagnosed at a younger age. This can significantly change screening recommendations,” he says.

“Routine colon cancer screening now begins at age 45 for most people, rather than 50. Be sure to tell your doctor if anyone in your family has had colon cancer, particularly at a younger age,” Jaimes says.

You might be at higher risk of colon cancer — and therefore require screening at an earlier age — if you have a history of radiation to the abdomen, inflammatory bowel disease, or certain types of polyps, according to the American Cancer Society.

“Age 40 is the year to start doing routine yearly mammograms. If you have dense breast tissue, then you might qualify for follow-up ultrasound,” Reidy says.

Women should stay current with cervical cancer screening, including Pap smears and/or HPV testing, as appropriate for their age and medical history, Jaimes adds. New guidelines allow for women to self-swab the vagina at home to be screened for HPV. If you’re someone who struggles with annual well woman visits, talk to your OB-GYN about this option.

Keeping up with the recommended vaccine schedule is an important part of disease prevention that boosts personal immunity and herd immunity, Jepsen says.

“The average age of menopause in America is 51 years old. However, the perimenopausal phase can last a few years and can look different in everyone. It’s good to talk to your doctor about what to look out for,” says Reidy.

Women, especially after menopause, are at risk of decreased bone density, Reidy points out. As Scary Mommy previously reported, you can maintain bone density with exercise, like strength training two to three times per week or adding walks, and even dancing, into your day-today. Bringing up osteoporosis with your doctor allows them to talk to you about any potential risk factors too.

“You might want to talk to your doctor about any chronic conditions, like celiac disease, or long-term use of steroids that might put you at increased risk of osteoporosis and warrant earlier screening,” Reidy says.

Exercising isn’t just about your weight; it turns out that your muscle mass is the best indicator of your longevity and quality of life as you age.

Most doctors recommend 150 minutes of moderate intensity exercise per week, but it’s important to talk to your doctor about what that means for you, Reidy says. Make a plan with your provider that takes into account your unique medical history, risk factors, and lifestyle.

“As we age, certain hormone levels change, and this is normal,” Jepsen says. “It is important to discuss with your doctor what signs of aging you should expect and what symptoms are ‘red flag’ symptoms, meaning you should reach out to your doctor to discuss further.”

“I remind my patients that many serious medical conditions develop silently, long before symptoms appear. Annual visits give us an opportunity to identify problems early, address risk factors, and hopefully prevent more serious illness down the road,” says Jaimes. “After all, we wouldn’t think twice about maintaining our cars to keep them running for years. Shouldn’t we give our own bodies at least the same attention?”

#2984. Colorado Hiker Creates Ultimate Thru-Hike Across His Home State

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Donovan Rice got into thru-hiking almost by accident. Already outdoorsy, he graduated from long hikes to planning to climb all 53 of Colorado’s 14,000’ers (mountains above 14,000 feet, or 4,267m). While researching which peaks required overnight camping, he realized that some people’s hikes just keep going.

“I thought, there’s no way somebody can walk from Mexico to Canada. That’s just ridiculous,” Rice explained. “I fell in love with that idea.”

From there, it was a slippery slope. First came the Pacific Crest Trail (PCT), from the Mexican border in California to the Canadian border in British Columbia. Then he did the Continental Divide Trail (CDT), tracing the Rocky Mountains from New Mexico to the Montana-Canada border. Before he knew it, he had a trail name — Iceman —and was working the gig economy to free up time for wilderness adventures.

But that’s where most people’s slippery slope levels out. Hiking the 4,988km CDT is as meaty a project as most of us can imagine. But for Rice, it only opened the floodgates. He was fascinated by everything that didn’t make it into famous routes, especially the ridges and mountains he passed in his home state of Colorado that were just off trail.

“There’s all this stuff the CDT doesn’t hit because it is following the Continental Divide,” he told ExplorersWeb. “So when I got to the end of the Colorado section, about 700 miles, I was thinking, man, if there were a 2,000-mile version of that, it would be my dream thru-hike. Just that non-stop, relentless brutality that is Colorado.”

Colorado has 14,000′ peaks but also deserts. Photo: Donovan Rice

Rice started thinking about a massive thru-hike loop of the state. His idea was to stitch together existing trails and form new ones where necessary.

“I dropped a load of pins across the areas in Colorado I wanted to visit, then I pulled out a public lands layer of the map to see how I might connect the areas,” he explained. “The three core design philosophies were beauty, breadth, and epicness. I wanted to see beautiful views, I wanted to cover as much of Colorado’s diversity as possible, and I wanted to take the most iconic and epic route possible.”

Unlike places like the UK, there is no “right to roam” in Colorado, so connecting the route to avoid private land was tricky. Occasionally, he had to link bits of public land and sections of trail in the “least worst way,” with short road walks when private property appeared to halt progress.

“The crux might be between Bailey and Rocky Mountain National Park. You go over the Mount Evans Wilderness, two 14,000’ers, hop on the Front Range across several 13,000’ers, and then across the Tenmile Range, and then the absolutely brutal Gore Range.”

However, he did pack an ice ax, micro-spikes, snow gaiters, and a lightweight helmet to protect against rockfall. He also made an effort to structure the route to provide relief between the most challenging sections, giving the trail an ebb and flow, and potential hikers time to decompress.

“This is one of those routes where bailouts will have to happen,” Rice explained. “You’ll have to take a low route or an alternate. I’m hoping to revisit some of the areas in the future and establish some of these alternative routes.”

“I need to see how many people will actually give it a try. People can do segments. I’d love to see more thru-hikers do this, but the big challenge is that I think the majority of thru-hikers will look at this and think ‘that’s above my pay grade’.”

He sees a certain vulnerability in that transparency, and a responsibility, too. If climbing is his profession, he believes the work should be visible.

That mindset is revealing, because Siegrist’s career has never really been about a single breakthrough. It has been about accumulation: of routes, of experience, of increasingly refined judgment about what deserves his time and what does not.

The obvious American antithesis to Siegrist is Chris Sharma. By comparison, Sharma has climbed harder, established new grades, and helped redefine the upper limits of sport climbing. But how many routes 5.14d or harder has he climbed? Who knows.

Unsurprisingly to anyone who has followed his career, Sharma does not keep a public logbook. His résumé has always held a slightly mythological quality. Major ascents appear in interviews and old reports, while others seem to survive mostly because somebody remembers he did them. It fits the larger Sharma mythology: the barefoot, wandering savant projecting an almost preternatural sense of it’ll happen when it happens…

Siegrist is wired almost exactly the opposite way. He keeps meticulous records. He makes plans. He trains his ass off and gets things done.

There is, of course, immense natural ability underlying all of this. But Siegrist has never seemed content to simply possess that gift. He has wrung an extraordinary amount out of it.

And after nearly two decades of doing so, something is beginning to change. From the outside, Siegrist seems as committed to sport climbing as ever. But when I spoke with him at length a couple weeks ago, he admitted that his interest in it has begun to wane. For years, his friends have teased him about wanting to become a “trad dad,” only to watch him disappear onto another sport-climbing project.

These days, Siegrist talks increasingly about big walls, trad climbing, and new-route development. He seems more and more drawn to the simple novelty of arriving somewhere unfamiliar—learning a new rock type, figuring out where to camp, discovering the warm-ups.

Even Siegrist’s relationship with difficulty has apparently shifted. There was a time, he says, when a route being hard constituted reason enough to try it: Oh, is it hard? I have to do it. That impulse is fading. He is less interested in collecting another hard tick simply because it exists, and more willing to spend his time—and even fail—on the routes that genuinely matter to him.

That makes the timing of route No. 100 feel almost too perfect. Siegrist has reached one of the most pointless, yet impressive statistical milestones in modern climbing at the same moment that he is reconsidering the next phase of his career.

In the conversation below, Siegrist reflects on what 100 routes at 5.14d or harder have taught him. He also considers how his relationship with grades and projecting has changed, and what—after nearly two decades of hard climbing—still motivates him.

This interview was conducted in early September, when Siegrist had climbed 99 routes graded 5.14d or harder. We have updated portions of the conversation to reflect his 100th ascent. The conversation has also been lightly edited for concision and clarity.

Jonathan Siegrist: My dad has been a climber since the ’70s, so I was exposed to climbing when I was eight or nine years old, but it was like once a year, following 5.6 trad routes. I normally say I started climbing when I was 18, just out of high school, because now when someone says they started climbing at age 11, it often means they were in a youth program doing comps and campusing.

My own interest in climbing started when I was 18 in Boulder, Colorado, and my passion for it just grew over the years. I eventually moved out of Boulder and lived in my pickup truck for seven years to pursue climbing. At the end of 2017, I moved to Las Vegas, and I’ve lived here ever since. But I still probably travel in pursuit of climbing seven or eight months of the year.

Siegrist: It’s interesting you would ask that because, while it maybe wouldn’t seem this way from the outside, my interest in sport climbing is waning.

My close friends make fun of me because I’ve been telling them I wanted to be a “trad dad” for five or 10 years. And then I just keep sport climbing. If anything, I’ve become more focused on sport climbing over the last five years.

I used to integrate more wall and trad climbing, but I don’t think I’ve done any in the last five years. My last trad trip was to the Gunks in 2020. Then I went to the New River Gorge with every intention of just trad climbing, but I was introduced to this project [Full Metal Brisket, 5.15a], got really psyched, and it totally derailed my trip.

Siegrist: It’s not that I won’t ever sport climb again, but I have a few remaining goals. Earlier this summer, I failed on a big objective in Wyoming. Afterward, I started thinking about my favorite climbing trips and where I could go next. You know that moment when you go to a spectacular area for the first time? You’re doing the warm-ups, learning about the place, seeing that epic route you’ve heard about forever for the first time, figuring out where to get groceries, where to eat, where to camp.

Those moments are so invigorating, and I don’t get to have that experience very often anymore. I really look forward to doing more wall climbing and trad climbing because I like the novelty of going somewhere new, learning a new style, being challenged by a new rock type. That and new-route development are where most of my interest is now.

Siegrist: The reality of climbing this many hard routes is that you can’t do them all in your preferred style. You’re forced onto different angles, hold types, rock types, environments, conditions. Eventually it becomes more about certain areas, conditions and styles feeling a certain way, rather than a grade strictly feeling a certain way.

Especially with first ascents, I have a really difficult time grading things now. If something suits me really well, does that mean it feels easy for me, but it’s hard for someone else? Or maybe it actually doesn’t suit me, but I like the climbing, so I think it does. I have no idea.

I don’t necessarily always feel my strongest, but experience really helps with problem-solving, strategy, and the logistics around projecting. I see younger climbers who are much stronger than me making so many mistakes when it comes to giving themselves the best opportunity to send something quickly. But then I see how insanely strong they are, and I’m like, “Holy shit, that’s crazy.”

Siegrist: Interestingly, I actually think my willingness to fail is greater now.

One thing that has waned over the last couple years is that a route being hard used to be enough for me. It used to be, “Oh, is it hard? I have to do it.” That’s starting to fade. I don’t feel like I need to do every linkup or every one-star route. I’m much more drawn to really motivating routes and willing to fail on them, rather than feeling like I need another tick just because it’s hard.

Siegrist: The main thing I had in my late 20s was that I recovered so quickly. If something at your absolute limit requires 15 or 20 good climbing days, back then I could get those days in a single season.

Now, if I want to rest as much as I need to, that can be really difficult. If I climb day on, day off, 20 climbing days takes 40 days, assuming the weather is perfect. Sometimes the key window for an area is only four weeks. Then you’re like, “Fuck, you don’t have enough time.”

The other difference is that I wasn’t married. I didn’t own a house. I lived in my car. Climbing was literally everything, and I was completely happy with it being that way. I was pursuing climbing every single day.

It was an awesome time, and I miss it sometimes. But I also love having other focuses in my life and other ways to get fulfillment. It’s nice to feel like an adult sometimes, too, and have a house and have enough money to buy blueberries.

Siegrist: Absolutely. It happened a couple of times, but the most remarkable was climbing Biographie, my first 5.15a, in 2014.

I first tried it in 2012 on my first climbing trip to Europe. I went to Céüse, tried Biographie, and was like, “Holy shit, this is so hard.” On paper, I had the ability to try it, but when I actually got on it, I remember thinking, “This is insane. This is so over my head.”

When I had the opportunity to go back to Europe in 2014, I realized that whatever I had been doing naturally to get to that point wasn’t going to be enough. I had basically never trained. I’d never worked with a coach or done anything other than go to the gym and flog myself.

Around then, Mike and Mark Anderson released The Rock Climber’s Training Manual. If you were climbing at that time, it was a fucking sensation. It was like the climbing textbook. It really spoke to me because I’m very much an engineer, mathematic type. I reached out to Mark and said, “I want to go to Céüse and climb this route. I have to get better. Would you train me?”

I spent a winter training in Boulder at the Boulder Rock Club, and it changed my climbing forever. I’d spent years climbing à muerte outside, so I had learned how to move my body and use my feet. Then I added the strength and finger-strength elements on top of that, and it was like I took rocket fuel.

Then I went to Céüse with this singular goal. Biographie is really mentally difficult. It’s slippery, the crux is at the top, it’s hard to get there, and the rest before it sucks. It tested my mental capabilities in a big way. Trying a truly limit route while traveling, rather than in my backyard, was scary to me, too.

There were so many elements that came together. When I sent that route, my climbing changed forever. There was before that route and after that route.

Siegrist: It’s really different. First, there’s just so much more work involved.

If I show up at a cliff and start trying a 9a that already has chalk, quickdraws hanging, and beta I can share with somebody, I’m probably starting two or three weeks ahead of where I would be with a route I bolted. With a new route, first you bolt it, then clean it, then climb on it and realize you need to move a bolt. You’re discovering all the beta yourself.

But there’s also something fundamentally different about knowing a route has been climbed and can be climbed. With a first ascent, especially at 9a+, 9b, or even 9a sometimes, you’re genuinely unsure whether it even goes until you do it.

That’s very different emotionally and mentally from knowing five people have done something before you. Maybe one is your size, you know what they’re good and bad at, and you can see their beta.

It’s like when you’re trying a boulder problem and you can’t figure it out. You’re like, “This is fucking impossible.” Then your buddy does it, you try again, and suddenly you do it. Something changes inside of you just from knowing it’s possible.

So first ascents are a much more drawn-out, introspective, and intense process than repeating something. And I really enjoy it. Earlier in my career, developing was partly, “Well, I need stuff to climb on, so I should go check out that cave some guy said was cool.” Now I enjoy the process itself even more.

Siegrist: One that’s really important to me that basically no one has ever heard of is L’Étrange Ivresse des Lenteurs at Céüse. It’s on the Biographie sector, right there where people walk past it every day. Adam Ondra did the first ascent, then Megos climbed it, then me.

It’s one of the best routes—top three of my whole life. It was a long process because of wetness; it’s like the only route at Céüse that seeps. I also tore my LCL on it on the first day of my second trip trying to finish it.

It has everything. It’s hard, it’s beautiful, the rock is insane. It’s also violently run-out—borderline dangerous. I fell at the anchor, and so did Megos. If the last bolt failed, you could potentially hit the ground from like 100 feet. It’s scary, bold and somehow obscure despite being in the middle of one of the most famous hard-climbing zones in the world. That route is really special to me.

Siegrist: I’m sure I’ll get dogged for this: La Rambla. It’s historically important, and it was an important route for me, but I can’t say I loved it.

The rock in the crux is great, but a lot of the other rock is kind of meh. I have really dry skin, and El Pati is always windy, so I always felt really slippery on it. If La Rambla wasn’t as hard as it was, I think it would be a good route, but there are so many better routes at the grade. To put it in the same category as Biographie or even Papichulo, in my opinion, is wrong.

That being said, La Rambla is like a rite of passage. You’re interacting with history in a way that’s pretty interesting.

Siegrist: Oh my God, there are so many. I have a running list.

My mega-project at the Fins is one. I haven’t been back for seven or eight years. I tried it on a couple different trips and got close, but it’s this crazy style that’s really hard to be good at. And it’s a hard route, period. I’d love to go back.

The one at the front of my mind right now is a route I bolted in Wyoming and tried this summer. I fell on the very last move probably 10 times in the final week. Then the heat of summer came, and I wasn’t able to finish it.

That one means a lot to me. It’s really, really good and very hard. I cannot wait to go back, but sadly I have to wait until spring.

Siegrist: It’s so important to me because it’s my body of work. I’ve dedicated so much effort and time over such a long period to traveling, exploring, doing first ascents, and visiting areas that interested or inspired me.

I’m proud of what I’ve done, and I want people to be able to feel inspired by it or get some kind of motivation from it. I haven’t logged every 5.13 I’ve ever done, but all the hard stuff is super accurate.

At the end of the day, it’s my job to work with my brands, but also to share inspiration and stories with the community, my friends and people who follow along. At a minimum, I’d like them to be able to know what I’m up to and what I’ve done in the past.

If you were any other type of professional athlete, your downfalls, shortcomings and successes would be available to other people. I’m not afraid of being vulnerable in that way.

TENERIFE, CANARY ISLANDS — NASA's Artemis II astronauts were just minutes away from a critical engine burn that would send them toward the moon when an unexpected cabin leak warning threatened to change the course of their historic mission.

"If it had happened five minutes later, we may not have been able to have that burn," Artemis II pilot Victor Glover revealed during an Oct. 18 discussion with Canadian former astronaut Chris Hadfield at the Starmus VIII festival here in Tenerife.

The incident unfolded on April 2, the second day of the Artemis II mission, as the astronauts' Orion spacecraft swooped back toward Earth after traveling some 38,000 miles (61,000 kilometers) away. Orion had been completing a highly elliptical orbit around Earth, giving the crew and mission control an opportunity to test the capsule's systems before committing to the journey to the moon. Orion was now approaching its closest point to our planet, roughly 100 miles (160 km) above the surface, ahead of the crucial translunar injection (TLI) burn that would send the astronauts moonward.

"Jeremy looks out the window and says, 'I think we're going to run smack dab into the middle of Earth,'" Glover recalled, referring to Artemis II mission specialist Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency. "Because we were getting so low."

The close approach brought additional challenges, as Orion passed through a region populated by orbital debris and satellites.

"The density of things is going up from where we are, and also an increase in radiation," Glover explained.

It was at this particularly demanding point in the mission that Orion issued a warning that indicated a possible cabin leak, around 15 minutes before the critical TLI burn.

"So, determining whether it was real or not was a big deal," Glover said.

The crew and mission control faced a crucial question: Was it safe to continue heading to the moon, or would they have to abandon the planned maneuver and cut the mission very short? "Are we going to do a TLI and burn to go to the moon, or are we putting on our spacesuits to prepare to come back to Earth right now?" Glover recalled.

The timing was particularly critical. The TLI burn was designed to propel Orion out of Earth orbit and onto its trajectory around the moon, marking the first time astronauts had headed toward Earth's nearest neighbor since the Apollo era.

Luckily, as NASA flight director Judd Frieling told us during an April 2 media briefing, the warning turned out to be a false alarm.

So Artemis II received the go-ahead. Orion fired its main engine for approximately five minutes and 50 seconds, successfully setting the four astronauts on their journey around the moon.

"We're watching the timer count down, velocities go up, and the magenta and green numbers coming together," Glover recalled.

"Everything is tracking. It's looking good."

Before long, the crew had broken free of Earth's gravitational hold and were hurtling toward the moon at around 24,500 mph (39,430 kph).

Despite the tense moments before the burn, Artemis II went on to make history. NASA astronauts Reid Wiseman (the mission's commander), Glover (pilot) and Christina Koch (mission specialist), along with Hansen, traveled farther from Earth than any humans before them, reaching a maximum distance of 252,756 miles (406,771 km) during their journey around the moon.

The four astronauts returned safely to Earth on April 10, splashing down in the Pacific Ocean off the coast of San Diego after nearly 10 days in space.

About 251 million years ago, volcanic eruptions wiped out most life on Earth. For about a million years, the Siberian Traps in Russia spewed huge clouds of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere, leading to the most severe extinction event in our planet's history.

However, in a corner of what is now China, a refuge of pollen-producing plants avoided that fate and created an oasis for surviving animals. Research suggests that this was not the only place where plants were able to weather catastrophic climate change during the Great Dying, and that plants may have been better able to withstand the extinction event than other organisms.

"Plants do indeed sometimes have a lot of resilience because they can't move individually, and even as a population, they're not very good at moving," Chris Thomas, an ecologist and evolutionary biologist at the University of York in the U.K., told Live Science. As a result, many plants have evolved mechanisms to survive in place despite difficult conditions, he said.

As climate change raises global temperatures and throws ecosystems into upheaval, plants will need to draw once more upon this resilience. Many plant species have evolved unique adaptations that help them weather harsh conditions. Experts told Live Science that these adaptations — along with a hefty dose of luck — will now decide the difference between survival and extinction.

Unlike most animals, many plants have the ability to regenerate limbs — and those limbs can make them better able to adapt to new environment conditions. Plants in general have higher developmental plasticity than animals, which means that they have greater flexibility to change how their cells and tissues grow and develop.

"Many plants have what we call indeterminate growth and modular body plans," Amy Angert, a botanist at the University of British Columbia in Canada, told Live Science. Indeterminate growth means that an organism continues to grow and develop indefinitely throughout its lifespan.

"If we lost an arm, it would be gone forever. But on plants, if you cut off a branch, they can sprout new ones and those new ones can then receive signals from the current environment instead of being stuck with sort of developmental programs from the past environment."

For example, researchers found that resprouts on holm oak trees (Quercus ilex) are better able to cope with drought than those that had not resprouted.

And because buds can go dormant, many plant species can resprout after catastrophic events, such as fire. Cape Floristic Region in South Africa, for example, is the smallest of the world's six recognized floral kingdoms, and most of its plant species depend on fire — its plants resprout after fire and many of their seeds require fire to germinate.

Some plants, such as the Snow Gum (​​Eucalyptus pauciflora), have a thick layer of insulating bark to protect their buds, while others like stinging nettles (Urtica dioica) bury them underground in a "bud bank". The soil insulates the seed's buds from extreme heat and cold and protects most of the plant from life-threatening conditions. This widespread adaptation allows herbaceous plants to endure through the winter and plants with bulbs to hide their tissues from the ravages of fire.

Snow gums (Eucalyptus pauciflora) under snow at Mount Franklin, Namadgi National Park, Australia.

Plants can also store seeds underground when times are difficult. Almost 350,000 plant species produce seeds. For most of these species, the seeds contain dormant tiny versions of the young plant, with all the structures that will turn into leaves and roots.

"Seeds can live essentially in stasis in the soil," Daniel Anstett, an evolutionary ecologist at Cornell University, told Live Science. "You can have the seed bank where certain seeds might come back, and there's access to the backward library of genetic information that many plants can ultimately draw upon."

Seed banks are a vital tool for resilience in many plant species. They create the opportunity for metabolically inactive individuals to reanimate when environmental conditions improve, according to a 2021 paper published in the journal Nature Communications. "When lucky, at least some of these individuals will awaken in the future when the environment is more conducive for growth and reproduction," the authors wrote.

Seedbanks can also be reservoirs of genetic diversity, which is integral to being able to adapt to new environments.

"The raw material for adaptation comes from genetic variation," said Angert. "Genetic variation is larger in large populations, and genetic variation can be shared between populations as the environment changes — if individuals can disperse successfully between them."

In a paper published in journal Science earlier this year, researchers, including Angert and Anstett, showed rapid evolution saved scarlet monkey flower (Erythranthe cardinalis) populations from prolonged drought in the U.S. and Mexico between 2010 and 2018.

"They can either grow really fast, flower and put out seeds before the fall drought takes effect, or they can grow much more slowly and bunker down,” Anstett explained.

Scarlet Monkeyflower (Erythranthe Cardinalis) in a greenhouse drought experiment.

The researchers sequenced the genomes of 55 scarlet monkeyflower populations to find areas within their genomes linked to coping with stress and drought. They tracked the populations and how they changed over the period. Some populations experienced rapid evolution to cope with the changing environment.

They found that, based on populations’ genetic diversity, they could predict whether a population would be able to survive the drought — even before it started.

While this is only one plant species, Anstett believes it could be true of other organisms too.

Some plants are able to transform how they produce energy to endure a changing environment. This is what Zhen Xu, a paleobotanist at the University of Leeds in the U.K, thinks allowed some plant species to survive the Permian-Triassic mass extinction and might become increasingly important for plants in today's changing climate.

Plants use photosynthesis to convert carbon dioxide and water into sugars and oxygen, and use stomata, or small pores on their leaf surface, to regulate the amount of carbon dioxide entering the plant.

Reproductive cone (strobilus) of a Triassic lycophyte from South China. It is approximately 2.4 inches (6 centimeters long).

Many plants open their stomata during the day to let in CO2 and release oxygen. Others use CAM photosynthesis, which stands for "Crassulacean acid metabolism," to close their stomata during the day and protect themselves from the heat. At night, they absorb CO2 and transform it into an acid, which they store in their tissues to be used to make sugars during the day.

"CAM photosynthesis is very common in present day desert plants, like the cactus and succulents," Xu told Live Science. "It's a very smart way to escape from the high temperature or aridity during the daytime."

In a study published in journal Nature Ecology & Evolution earlier this year, Xu and colleagues showed that plants that used CAM photosynthesis had an advantage during the Permian-Triassic mass extinction.

They found that Lycophytes, or are ancient vascular plants, switched to CAM photosynthesis around the time of the mass extinction. "We think CAM photosynthesis may be the special strategy to survive" and allowed these plants to dominate after the mass extinction, Xu said.

Some plants go to even more extreme lengths. During cataclysmic events, plants have turned into "hopeful monsters" to survive environmental upheaval, past research shows.

In nine separate events over the past 150 million years, many flowering plants duplicated their whole genome to give themselves a better chance of survival, according to research published in journal Cell earlier this year. The duplicate genome gives them more opportunities to evolve and adapt to challenging environmental conditions.

"We see clusterings of whole genome duplications in time, and every time it corresponds with a described, important geological event, whether it's a global cooling period, whether it's a global warming period, or whether there's an extinction event," study co-author Yves Van de Peer, a genome biologist at Ghent University in Belgium, told Live Science at the time.

Still, the survival of specific plant populations or species often boils down to luck, experts told Live Science.

Plants "essentially have this broad toolkit" but "some of the plants have some pathways, and other plants have others, and some have some traits, and some have others", Anstett said. Whether the particular tools they have will help depends on the conditions they face.

During the Triassic-Permian mass extinction, the plants in refugia in China (and elsewhere) were in the right place at the right time, with the right suite of potential adaptations at their disposal and enough genetic variety to tap into them.

The same holds true for modern-day species facing a changing climate and potential extinction. Angert said there are ways to make plant species "luckier" in the years ahead, such as connecting fragmented populations to increase genetic diversity, which increases the suite of potential tools at a plant species' disposal.

"The best thing we could do is to leave larger and better connected populations on the landscape," she said.

Anstett agreed. "If populations become really disconnected, then potentially we're eroding away whatever toolkit that these plants have to be able to adapt."
