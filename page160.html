#3897. STG Design unveils upcoming “unconventional, sinuous” Houston skyscraper

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Texas architecture studio STG Design has released renderings of O27, an office skyscraper covered with triangular panels that is planned for Houston’s River Oaks area.

The 32-storey building will contain flexible office floors, as well as luxury amenities and a rooftop terrace on top of its nine-storey base.

It will have a parking garage block at its podium and a tower that rises in an undulating form.

The design is the result of an “intensive computational design process” by STG Design that sought to prioritise “highly efficient, repeatable floorplates” within the building’s curved geometry.

Renders show two, curving glass facades folded around either side of the building. They are divided at the middle, where a spine of terraces travel up its length.

“Its unconventional, sinuous form resists the rigid verticality that has come to dominate contemporary office buildings, resulting in a structure that feels fluid, expressive and perpetually in motion,” said the team.

Its facade will be made up of triangular glass panels to create a “shimmering skin” that will appear to shift in the changing light of the day.

Other elements include an entrance drive covered in a sculpted ceiling embedded with lights to “evoke a night sky” and a double-height, glass-clad lobby, both designed by international design practice Design ALT + TAB.

Interior renderings show spaces covered in a metallic bronze, with black-and-white marble surfaces and curved seating.

According to developers Broadview Capital, the project was informed by the admiration its founders, brothers Anish and Ashay Bavishi, have for Houston’s Williams Tower, designed by architects Philip Johnson and John Burgee.

The skyscraper symbolises “ambition, possibility and global sophistication” for Houston, said the team, an element the designers and developers hope to bring to the city’s “prestigious” River Oaks area, known for its historic estates and luxury shopping.

“O27 serves as both a geographic marker and a tribute to a neighbourhood whose century-long legacy has been defined by thoughtful planning, architectural distinction and enduring relevance,” said the team.

The building is expected to be completed in 2028.

STG Design is based in Austin, Texas and was founded in 1976.

Other projects recently announced for Houston include a coffee plant set to be converted into a music school by OMA and a performance space created by Theaster Gates and Studio Zewde.

    #3678. Morning vs. Evening Runs: Which Is Better for Run Performance?

    📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

    Experts help us unpack all the pros and cons of morning and evening runs.

    We earn a commission for products purchased through some links in this article. Why Trust Us?

    Most races want you at the starting line early in the morning, making an a.m. run routine an obvious choice for mimicking race day conditions. But if you’re a night owl or have a jam-packed schedule, evenings might better suit your schedule.

    Runners need to juggle sleep, hydration, nutrition, and training, all while making time for work, family, and a social life. So your unique lifestyle likely determines the time of day you run.

    But could running when the sun comes up offer more benefits than heading out after work or vice versa?

    To help you optimize your schedule, we spoke with exercise physiologists and run coaches to weigh the benefits and drawbacks of morning versus evening runs and help you choose which works best for your goals.

    As mentioned, most runners race in the morning, says Greg McMillan, exercise physiologist and USATF-certified coach, so if you run in the morning consistently, you’ll be in the routine you need for race day. This allows you to practice how long it takes you to warm up, fuel properly, and head out the door on time.

    Many coaches agree with McMillan, stressing the importance of simulating race day to eliminate any unknown variables. If you only ever run in the evenings but your race is in the morning, you just won’t be as prepared, says Janet Hamilton, CSCS, exercise physiologist and running coach with Running Strong in Atlanta.

    She explains that morning runs can help you prevent any issues on race day by getting you used to eating an earlier breakfast, facing colder temperatures, and adjusting your sleep schedule so that you have enough energy when you wake up.

    Kickstarting your day with just a 30-minute run may be all you need to feel more productive, according to research. A study published in 2019 in the British Journal of Sports Medicine examined a group of 67 sedentary, older men and women. The researchers had them perform 30 minutes of aerobic exercise on a treadmill in the morning along with three-minute walks every half an hour throughout the rest of the day.

    Their findings suggest that starting your day with 30 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise may improve cognitive function related to memory and decision-making skills.

    Exercising earlier in the day means you’re more likely to feel focused and productive right away, adds Kate Baird, CSCS, an exercise physiologist at the Hospital for Special Surgery in New York City. “This includes mood-boosting endorphins and neurotransmitters that help with focus and mental clarity,” she says.

    Baird explains that you may also feel a sense of accomplishment from getting something done first thing in the morning, which can motivate you to keep going.

    Running in the morning ensures that you start your workout with fresh legs, which is especially helpful if you know you’re going to spend all day on your feet, Hamilton says.

    Hamilton explains this is especially helpful for runners who work long shifts at physically demanding jobs, as running in the morning ensures they get the most out of their muscles. Plus, walking can boost recovery after high-intensity exercise, so you can spend the rest of your day recovering after your morning workout and preparing for the next.

    Your gut may not wake up when you do, says Hamilton. She explains that some people don’t empty out when they first get out of bed, and that generally, you won’t expect a bowel movement until about 10 a.m., which is probably later than an early morning run.

    Meanwhile, research suggests it’s probably best to get things moving before your run so that you have more energy: A study published in 2023 in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition tested the exercise performances of 13 triathletes with two cycling time trials: one before defecating and one after.

    The researchers found that pooping before their exercise led to enhanced performance, mainly because the brain received more oxygen and that decreased fatigue.

    So, if you’re a person who needs time to move around, have breakfast, and use the bathroom first, then you might want to schedule your runs during afternoons and evenings.

    In the mornings, your body needs time to properly warm up and acclimate to the temperatures outside. If you wake up feeling groggy and you’re pressed for time, it may be harder to give your body the time it needs to prepare for impact.

    “The quality of the runs can be slightly lower in the morning because you’re still trying to wake up,” McMillan explains. “That can be a challenge for older runners. It can be challenging for runners that have injury issues, who need to warm up a little bit more,” he says.

    If you’re somebody who spends time commuting into work early in the morning, or going to bed later at night, working out in the morning might require you to cut into your sleeping schedule, making it harder to feel refreshed in the morning.

    The National Sleep Foundation recommends seven to nine hours of sleep per night for adults, and for runners, sleep is one of the most important factors for recovery.

    Practically, if you work a 9-to-5 job, need time to get ready, commute, take kids to school, or walk your dog, you might have to start your day early in order to get in a morning run. This could look like getting out of bed around 5 a.m. and going to bed around 8 p.m., which might not be possible for some busy runners.

    If you wait until the end of the day to work out, you might tap into some added benefits that can support your run performance. Research suggests that you’re more likely to run longer in the evening than in the morning.

    A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2023 in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research examined 31 studies on the effects of exercise on cardiorespiratory responses and endurance performance, and found a longer time to exhaustion when working out in the afternoon and evenings versus the morning.

    Evening runs can help you unwind and relieve stress after a long day of work, McMillan says. You get a chance to let go of whatever happened during your day.

    Research backs the benefits of exercise for stress relief: A 2023 survey published in Sports Physiology and Physical Performance examined the association between exercise frequency and stress among 3,440 Korean adults and found that higher-frequency exercise is linked to a lower perceived level of psychological stress.

    This might be because running stimulates a part of your brain that regulates cognitive functions that help you deal with stress. A study published in 2018 in Scientific Reports, which examined 26 healthy runners, suggests 10 minutes of running can improve executive function, including memory, attention, self-control, and emotional response.

    Running is a great way to care for your overall health, but it turns out the time of day you run can affect your metabolic health in particular.

    A study published in Obesity examined 186 men and women who were overweight or had obesity and were diagnosed with at least one metabolic impairment, like high blood pressure or high cholesterol.

    The researchers tracked their physical activity, what time of day they exercised, and their glucose levels over the course of two weeks. They found that those who did more than 50 percent of their exercise in the evening had significantly lower glucose levels compared to those who were sedentary, and better glucose regulation compared to those who mainly exercised in the mornings.

    Additionally, a study published in 2022 supports these findings. Researchers gathered 775 active participants and assessed their blood sugar levels based on when they exercised. They found that those who performed moderate-to-vigorous activity in the evening had 25 percent lower insulin resistance than those who exercised in the morning.

    If you run in the evening, you might not have enough energy left to devote to your training, which can leave you feeling unmotivated or struggling to run. Your job can affect your energy levels and leave you feeling fatigued at the end of the day, Hamilton explains.

    This includes mental and physical exhaustion, McMillan points out. “Sometimes you’re not as fresh mentally or physically at the end of the day, particularly if you had a challenging day, and that can compromise your run,” he says.

    Research supports McMillan’s point. A study published in 2023 in the International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance suggests that too many cognitively demanding tasks can have a significant effect on endurance.

    Hamilton agrees, pointing out that whether you’re tired because you’re on your feet all day or because you’re under a lot of mental stress can affect how well you exercise after a day of work.

    Hamilton explains that if you eat lunch around noon, and then start your run six or seven hours later, you need to have had a snack at some point so you have the energy to complete your miles.

    “It might be a half a peanut butter and jelly sandwich, or a bagel, or some oatmeal—something to keep you from having hunger pangs while you’re out on your run,” she says. (For runs that are shorter than 90 minutes, though, runners can usually get away with just sipping on a sports drink or something light before taking off, Hamilton notes.)

    This can get challenging for people that work, adds McMillan. He explains that if you have a busy day, or you’re working on a project and lose track of time, you might accidentally skip lunch, forget to hydrate properly, or might be eating later than you’d like. That certainly won’t lead to the best run in the evening.

    If you wait to run in the evening, you might give yourself more time to talk yourself out of a workout. McMillan explains that your workouts, especially if you’re training for an upcoming race, should take priority in your schedule, and if you push them to the evenings, you may feel less motivated to get it done.

    The most important thing is that you exercise at a time that works best for you. No matter if you’re waking up with the sun to run, or squeezing in a workout before you eat dinner, consistency is always king, says McMillan.

    He adds that a consistent running schedule ensures you have an organized “stress-rest cycle,” where your body has enough time to recover between workouts, which can help prevent injury. To accomplish that, he suggests picking one time of day to run—mornings or evenings—and giving yourself 24 hours to recover between each workout.

    It’s also crucial to note that no matter what time of day you run, you have to feel safe. You’re less likely to stay consistent with your training plan if you’re scared to run outdoors, so use your best judgment on where and when you run.

    #688. The ‘Tavern-Style’ Pizza Takeover

    📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

    It’s the year of the tavern — and that means pizza style, too

    In the suburbs of Chicago where they both grew up, it was just pizza, according to Brittany and Brennan Grosvenor, the couple behind Proof in Portland, Oregon. Ordering came down to the question of “thin-crust, deep-dish, or double-dough,” Brittany recalls of her family’s Friday night tradition. The Grosvernors sell the former at Proof, though the language of “thin crust” has given way to the more nationally familiar phrasing of “tavern-style,” the pizza trend that has swept from coast to coast in recent years.

    Outsiders might associate Chicago with its famous, tourist-beckoning deep-dish pizza, but Brittany says that’s just not reflective of reality, citing deep dish as a much less frequent treat than the Midwestern, thin-crust style that’s gaining popularity. “The pizza you get every week, or if you go to a kid’s birthday party, it’s always tavern-style,” she says.

    “Tavern-style” pizza — which is prevalent across the Midwest, with Chicago, Milwaukee, Minnesota, and St. Louis touting similar pies — is known for its thin, cracker-like crust, cut into a lattice instead of triangular slices. Depending on the region and the pizza shop where one’s nostalgia lies, the sauce might lean sweet or spicy. Lightness and crispness, though, is the style’s unique sell. Writing for Esquire in 2023, Omar Mamoon called the variant, which was then still on the rise, “a foil” to famously thick Detroit-style pizza, which had its big moment in the late 2010s.

    The beauty of the tavern-style pie is its shareability. A pizza yields more slices when cut into a grid than into triangles, explains Bradley Shorten, who’s behind both Kim’s Uncle Pizza in his hometown Chicago suburb of Westmont, Illinois, and the recently opened La Merna in Washington, D.C. Pizza cut into little squares makes for great drinking food, especially since it fits on a cocktail napkin. What makes a good pie, according to Shorten, is that every square has a little of every ingredient; the toppings go to the edge. And while the four, small corner slices might not be the “best” ones, Shorten says, they’re the ones “everybody goes for,” especially if they’re from Chicago.

    Over the past few years, as “taverns” have become growing fixations for a cadre of restaurateurs, the “party cut” pizza and tavern-style pizza spots have also become ubiquitous, with openings like Keepers in New York city; One Trick Pony in Forth Worth, Texas; Tillie’s Tavern Pizza in Wheat Ridge, Colorado; and Zarella and Dimmi Dimmi Corner Italian in Chicago, where the style has always been abundant. For some Chicagoans, these openings across the country are an opportunity to correct the record about their city’s pizza culture, and for other Midwesterners to claim their stake in the region’s pizza, too.

    When Mike Fadem opened the second location of his popular NYC pizzeria Ops — known for its wood-fired Neapolitan-esque pies with leopard-spotted crusts and pliant dough — he wanted to offer something new. Fadem is from St. Louis, where the city’s namesake pizza is super-thin. Despite his love for it, “it’s maybe not the best version of tavern pizza,” Fadem admits, noting the iteration’s many critics, some of whom take issue with its use of the gooey processed cheese called provel. The trajectory of tavern-style pizza has been like a game of telephone, he explains: St. Louis pizza is derived from thin-crust Chicago pizza, which itself came from Neapolitan pizza. Divergence has accompanied each jump.

    One of Fadem’s employees at Ops, meanwhile, is from New Jersey, which claims its own thin-crust, tavern- or bar-style pizza tradition. (The bar pies of the Boston area also have their own devoted following.) Accordingly, “I had this interest in ‘can we make an Ops version of this?’” Fadem says. With Ops known for sourdough, Fadem also wanted to highlight the rich flavor of whole grains. And he wanted it to be really crispy, a quality he says draws in New Yorkers. Fadem’s take is on the menu as the regionally agnostic “tavernetta.” Though St. Louis and New Jersey served as initial inspiration, the best pizza from his research was actually in Milwaukee.     The magnitude 7.7 quake hit on March 28, 2025, near the city of Mandalay and shook buildings as far away as Bangkok, 830 miles (1,330 kilometers) away. The death toll was at least 3,700 people.     The Sagaing Fault in Myanmar that triggered the quake is analogous to many strike-slip faults worldwide that threaten population centers, said Alice-Agnes Gabriel, a geophysicist at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography at the University of California, San Diego and co-author of the new research, which was published Oct. 14 in the journal Nature Communications. This includes California's San Andreas Fault, which is similarly tilted and likely capable of generating a 7.8 magnitude earthquake. Studying what happened as the ground ruptured near Mandalay can reveal what might occur in future earthquakes on similar faults, Gabriel told Live Science.

    "The idea is, we cannot predict when and where an earthquake might happen" along a particular fault, she said, "but we may be able to predict certain aspects of it."

    Gabriel uses powerful supercomputers to model earthquake scenarios, but these scenarios are only as good as the data that goes into them. In the case of the Myanmar quake, the researchers had a unique piece of data to ground-truth their model on: a security video of the ground cracking as the fault ruptured. This phenomenon had not been captured on video before.

    The researchers' models revealed that the fault is not a vertical plane under the ground. Instead, the surface dips west on the northern end of the fault, becomes vertical in the middle, and then dips eastward at the southern end, they found.

    In general, more shaking occurs in the direction in which a fault line dips, Gabriel said. In the case of the San Andreas Fault, the dip is inland, toward California's high desert — an area that's less populated than coastal regions on the other side of the fault. This means that, if the Big One acts similarly to the Myanmar quake, the high desert side of the fault will experience more intense shaking than the coastal side. The San Andreas Fault has also built up strain for over a century, so the researchers believe it could act in a similar way to the Sagaing Fault.

    #2252. Corporate Alignment with the Human Rights Campaign: Legal, Financial, and Reputational Risks for Shareholders

    📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

    In 2024, Bowyer Research conducted two coordinated shareholder engagement campaigns targeting more than 400 publicly traded U.S. companies, focusing on potential risks related to corporate alignment with the Human Rights Campaign (HRC)—particularly through participation in the HRC’s Corporate Equality Index (CEI)—and from extensive Pride Month messaging. This Backgrounder summarizes the substance and scale of those engagements and documents the legal, financial, reputational, and moral concerns raised by shareholders, with particular attention to policies and communications involving children.

    The Corporate Equality Index has served as the primary mechanism through which the HRC exerts its influence on corporate governance, employment policy, and public messaging. Participation is nominally voluntary, but companies that achieve a perfect score of 100 are required to satisfy a set of criteria that, because it is ever-evolving and regularly expanded, creates more and more compliance obligations with no defined endpoint. These criteria now include coverage of sex-rejecting procedures for minors as employee health benefits, mandatory identity-based training programs, and public corporate endorsement of contested social positions.[REF]

    Shareholders represented by Bowyer Research, most notably The Heritage Foundation, engaged these companies to identify legal vulnerabilities, financial risks, and the structural incompatibility of CEI participation with sound, long-term fiduciary governance.

    The Corporate Equality Index: Structure and Escalating Demands

    The Human Rights Campaign’s Corporate Equality Index is a benchmarking tool that scores corporations on their compliance with a defined set of employment, benefits, and public-messaging criteria. First introduced in 2002, the CEI has expanded steadily in scope, and its criteria are revised fairly frequently.

    Bowyer Research’s engagement database, compiled in collaboration with the 1792 Exchange, focused on 160 companies that publicly promote perfect CEI scores of 100. These companies span a variety of sectors, including technology, finance, health care, retail, energy, and consumer goods, and reflect the broad reach of the HRC’s influence across America’s capital markets.[REF]

    Companies frequently present perfect CEI scores as evidence of commendable corporate citizenship, but shareholders represented by Bowyer Research take a contrary view: A score of 100 is evidence not of sound governance, but rather of deep structural entanglement with a controversial external activist organization’s ideological compliance demands. Because the HRC revises its criteria frequently, companies committed to maintaining a perfect score face escalating requirements with no defined endpoint. This process is incompatible with sensible risk management and long-term shareholder value creation.

    More to the point, current CEI requirements extend well beyond foundational anti-discrimination policies. They include or have included, among other criteria, employee health benefit coverage for sex-rejecting transition procedures (including for minor dependents); mandatory participation in identity-based employee training programs (prior to this year); and a demonstrated pattern of “outreach” to the LGBTQ community, often involving support for contested social and political positions. Each of these creates distinct legal, financial, and reputational risks.

    Legal and Regulatory Exposure

    CEI participation creates legal vulnerability across several distinct areas of law. The 1792 Exchange has documented these risks in its Corporate Bias Ratings analysis, noting that the CEI “promotes policies that may violate existing civil rights laws” and that participation exposes companies to “unnecessary legal risks.”[REF]

    • Employment Discrimination. Policies that prioritize gender identity over biological sex in employment have generated litigation under the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and similar state statutes. The case of Activision illustrates how these policies can generate claims from multiple directions simultaneously—from allegations of discriminatory treatment on the basis of gender identity to allegations of discrimination arising from the company’s CEI policies that infringe on the well-grounded rights of others.[REF]
    • Religious Discrimination. Mandatory participation in gender-affirming or identity-based training programs has produced religious discrimination claims in federal courts. A lawsuit involving Alaska Airlines, in which employees alleged that mandatory diversity training conflicted with sincerely held religious beliefs, illustrates the risk companies face when compliance with external advocacy frameworks meets statutory protections for religious belief.[REF]
    • State-Level Legislative Risk. Benefits policies that include coverage for puberty blockers, cross-sex hormones, and irreversible sex-rejecting surgical procedures for minor dependents are increasingly subject to state restrictions. As more states limit or prohibit procedures for minors—including treating such procedures as child abuse[REF]—companies that include them as standard employee health benefits may find themselves in conflict with state law, creating complex compliance obligations and potential liability exposure.[REF]

    Financial and Brand Risk: Lessons from High-Profile Cases

    The financial consequences of activist-aligned corporate messaging are notably well documented. Several high-profile cases provide clear evidence of material harm to shareholders.

    • Bud Light. Anheuser-Busch InBev's 2023 promotional partnership with transgender social media influencer Dylan Mulvaney resulted in a consumer boycott that produced an estimated $1 billion or more in lost sales and a sustained, multi-quarter decline in Bud Light’s market share, dropping from the number-one brand overall to number three.[REF]
    • Target. Target Corporation’s introduction of a controversial Pride merchandise collection that included “tuck-friendly” swimsuits in 2023 generated significant consumer backlash that had a measurable adverse impact on the company’s revenues and share price. Target scaled back its 2024 Pride Month campaign significantly—a widely noted signal that even companies with historically strong activist-alignment track records need to be open to reassessing risks.[REF]
    • Disney. Disney’s public positioning against Florida’s Parental Rights in Education legislation resulted in the loss of the company’s Reedy Creek special tax district, significant negative media coverage, and a period of brand and financial turbulence.[REF] The episode demonstrated that corporate activism on politically contested issues can result in unwanted regulatory and legislative attention.

    These cases illustrate a consistent pattern: Activist-aligned corporate messaging that extends beyond non-discrimination into overt participation in polarized cultural debates creates consumer division, brand damage, and measurable financial losses. The 1792 Exchange’s analysis concludes that such alignment “divides employees, alienates customers, and distracts businesses from making good products and services.”[REF]

    Child-Related Concerns: Medical, Moral, and Reputational Dimensions

    A central and especially sensitive element of both engagement campaigns involved corporate policies and messaging that affect or appear to target children. Shareholders characterized this domain as a moral and reputational red line—one with consequences that extend well beyond standard brand-risk frameworks.

    • Health Benefits for Minors. The CEI’s rating criteria encourage or require companies to include coverage for puberty blockers, sterilizing cross-sex hormones, and sex-rejecting surgeries for minor dependents as part of standard employee benefits packages.[REF] State-level restrictions create growing compliance complexity. Additionally, studies on the long-term medical effects of puberty suppression and cross-sex hormone administration on minors have shown that the risks and harms substantially outweigh any known benefits. In fact, several American and European health authorities have recently restricted such interventions pending further evidence.[REF] Offering benefits that facilitate irreversible medical procedures for minors creates significant exposure with a substantial segment of the consumer population.
    • Marketing and Messaging Involving Children. Pride Month engagement placed particular emphasis on the intersection of sexualized content and children in corporate marketing, in-store displays, and digital communications. Consumer research consistently demonstrates that parents are highly protective of their children, as a result of which they desire to prevent their exposure to sexualized content, and that brands perceived as targeting children with such messaging suffer long-term reputational harm.

    Coerced Speech, Workplace Risk, and Employee Relations

    Beyond benefits and public-facing messaging, the Bowyer/Heritage engagement campaigns addressed the internal workplace implications of HRC-driven compliance frameworks. Three categories of concern were identified.

    • Compelled Expression of Ideological Positions. Requiring employees to display ideological symbols such as pride flags or related insignia on uniforms or company-affiliated digital profiles constitutes compelled speech. This practice raises both moral and potential legal concerns and risks alienating employees who have sincere scientific, ethical, and religious beliefs that differ from the positions they are required to endorse.
    • Mandatory Identity-Based Training (prior to 2026). Unconscious bias and privilege-acknowledgment training programs that require employees to segregate or make affirmations based on race, sex, or gender identity create both discrimination exposure and workforce morale risks. As documented above, mandatory participation in such programs has already produced federal litigation.
    • Selective Inclusion Policies. Genuine inclusion requires protection for all employees, including those whose views differ from those of activist organizations. Companies that apply inclusion principles selectively risk discrimination claims and undermine the internal cohesion that drives long-term organizational success. The CEI’s “Responsible Citizenship” rating criterion penalizes companies up to 25 points for irresponsible citizenship, including donating to “anti-LGBT” non-religious groups.[REF]

    The Scale and Scope of Shareholder Concern

    The scope of the Bowyer Research/Heritage Foundation 2024 engagement efforts reflects the breadth of shareholder concern about these issues.[REF]

    The CEI-focused engagement letter was distributed to 160 corporate contacts spanning technology, finance, health care, retail, energy, and consumer goods.[REF] Recipients included Amazon, Disney, Coca-Cola, PepsiCo, Johnson & Johnson, McDonald’s, Bank of America, BlackRock, Visa, Mastercard, Microsoft, and many others, demonstrating that shareholder concern about HRC influence is far-reaching and distributed broadly across the U.S. economy.

    Pride Month messaging engagement was distributed to nearly 400 companies.[REF] In both engagement campaigns, shareholders communicated that they hold sufficient share positions to qualify to submit a proxy resolution at the next annual meeting but also expressed a preference for constructive direct dialogue before resorting to formal shareholder mechanisms.

    A Gallup survey cited in the engagement materials found that a majority of U.S. adults do not want companies to speak publicly on political and social issues—confirming that the concerns raised by these shareholders reflect a significant segment of the consumer and investor base.[REF]

    Recommendations for Action

    Based on the evidence developed in the Bowyer Research/Heritage Foundation engagement campaigns as outlined in this analysis, it is recommended that corporate boards, investors, and policymakers seriously consider the following specific actions.

    • End or limit CEI participation. Participation in the CEI by Fortune 500 companies has declined by 65 percent in 2026. This serves as evidence that efforts to eliminate overt politicization of corporations can have a significant impact. The boards of corporations that maintain their participation in the HRC’s Corporate Equality Index should evaluate whether ongoing participation serves the long-term interests of shareholders, employees, and customers. Given the continually escalating nature of CEI requirements and their attendant, documented legal and reputational risks, companies should conduct a structured risk review before seeking renewal of participation or actions that would result in a “perfect score.”
    • Review benefits policies affecting minors. Companies should conduct a thorough legal and risk review of health benefit policies that include coverage for sex-rejecting procedures or treatments for minor dependents, accounting for evolving state law, current medical evidence, and potential litigation exposure. It should be noted in this connection that on October 22, 2025, the United States District Court for the Southern District of Mississippi issued an order vacating provisions of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services’ “Nondiscrimination in Health Programs and Activities” final rule relating to gender-identity discrimination. Consequently, the provision of health coverage for sex-rejecting procedures is no longer required under Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act.[REF]
    • End sponsorship of the Human Rights Campaign and the HRC Foundation.[REF] Companies should limit sponsorships and partnerships to organizations that advance the interests of shareholders. Sponsorships and partnerships that are not business-related draw unnecessary scrutiny to the company and waste shareholder resources.
    • Limit public communications to core business. Companies should resist external pressure to engage in overt cultural advocacy and focus public communications instead on products, services, and business performance. In other words, businesses should focus on business.
    • Adopt viewpoint-neutral inclusion frameworks. Rather than pursuing scores on advocacy-affiliated benchmarks, companies should adopt more viewpoint-neutral inclusion frameworks—such as the Alliance Defending Freedom’s Viewpoint Diversity Score—that protect all employees regardless of their political, cultural, or religious perspectives.[REF]
    • Establish explicit protections for content that involves children. Companies should adopt clear policies prohibiting sexualized imagery or messaging in connection with children in marketing, in-store displays, or digital communications.
    • Ensure voluntary participation in training programs. Companies should review mandatory training programs for potential legal exposure under civil rights and religious freedom statutes and ensure that no employee is segregated or required to make affirmations that conflict with his or her sincerely held beliefs.

    Conclusion

    Corporate alignment with the Human Rights Campaign—particularly through participation in the Corporate Equality Index—creates a set of escalating legal, financial, reputational, and moral risks that fiduciary-minded companies can no longer treat as cost-free. The evidence from Bud Light, Target, and Disney demonstrates that activist-aligned messaging often leads to real financial harm. The structure of the CEI ensures that today’s compliance threshold becomes tomorrow’s minimum with no natural endpoint.

    The concerns raised in the Bowyer Research/Heritage Foundation 2024 engagement campaigns—spanning more than 400 publicly traded companies—are backed by logic and evidence. They reflect the views of a broad and growing segment of the investor community and are supported by legal precedent, consumer research, and cautionary lessons from companies that have already paid a significant price for over-alignment with external activist agendas.

    Corporate boards have both the authority and the fiduciary obligation to evaluate these risks clearly. Genuine inclusion—of all employees, customers, and shareholders—requires resistance to selective ideological capture, not capitulation to it. Companies that refocus on core business performance, protect employees of all viewpoints, and resist activist pressure will be better positioned to deliver long-term value to the shareholders they serve.

    Left unchecked, a small flame can turn into a roaring fire. Groups may follow the same principle, with a few rule breakers triggering widespread chaos.

    On the long drive to my kid’s sporting event this weekend, most drivers, myself included, cruised a few miles over the speed limit. A few drivers, though, blazed past in the left lane. Mindful of safety — and police cruisers — I didn’t follow suit.

    Left unchecked though, a few bad actors can trigger social breakdown, researchers report October 21 in Proceedings of the Royal Society B. The truly deviant among us “basically take off the guardrails of the group,” says Lucas Molleman, a psychologist at the University of Amsterdam.

    Molleman and his colleagues designed a thought experiment to show what it takes to sway people to bend or break the rules. They picked a contentious time: A week before the 2024 U.S. presidential election. The team recruited 750 U.S. participants, with roughly half identifying as Democrats and half as Republicans. The online participants watched an animated shooting star streak across a row of 100 squares and then selected which square shone the brightest.

    Though the brightest square always appeared around the row’s middle, participants received more money for selecting squares farther to the right (the farthest-right square netted participants roughly the equivalent of $1.30). Dedicated rule followers, or those who always followed the rules, and rule breakers, existed in roughly equal numbers, with each comprising about a fifth of the larger group, initial tests showed.

    The researchers then stirred the pot. Participants repeated the simple experiment several times while receiving information about a previous respondent, including their political affiliation and level of adherence to the rule. Rule followers rarely begot more rule followers, even when respondents belonged to the same political party, the team found.

    The reverse held true for rule breakers, though. Receiving information that someone with the same political leanings had just disregarded the rules shifted selections rightward on average. Absent social cues, participants selected the farthest-right square roughly 23 percent of the time. That increased to almost 40 percent of the time after participants learned that a political comrade had made the most lucrative choice. Bad behavior, in other words, was contagious.

    Most individuals’ rule-breaking levels mirrored or stayed below that of others. But just over 4 percent of individuals escalated deviant behavior beyond that of the previous respondent.

    The researchers then created a computer simulation to see how those rule-breaking behaviors might play out across time. They designed a digital twin for each of the 750 participants and had the doppelgängers interact in groups of 100. In some scenarios, those few escalation-prone individuals could push everyone in the group toward wild rule breaking. But if the 100-twin selection had the same proportion of dedicated rule followers as the human experiment, overall deviance settled at a more intermediate level.

    This study provides some keen insights into how humans might behave absent consequences, says behavioral economist Eugen Dimant of the University of Pennsylvania. “But that’s not how the world works.” His work suggests that to truly understand social contagion and the emergence of mobs, researchers must consider societal forces, such as punishments and social norms, that tend to keep behavior in check.

    To Molleman, though, the study’s insights into individual behaviors are key. This pared-down experiment shows how just a few bad actors can cause entire groups to spiral out of control. Such findings could underpin everything from collective speeding to peaceful protests that turn violent, he says. “This is fundamental science … Obviously reality is way more complex.”
