#1415. The 4 Best E-Readers That Fit in Your Pocket

📅 Mar 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every product was carefully curated by an Esquire editor. We may earn a commission from these links. Here’s how we test products and why you should trust us.

Get off your phone without having to lug around a big ol’ hardback.

I love my slightly chunky Kobo e-readers and the Kindles that you can also use as a notebook are pretty remarkable (no pun intended). But these aren’t the easiest to pack up and go—they don’t fit in your pocket like a phone. So, like cell phones before them, digital readers are now getting smaller. Some of them are getting much smaller. Amazon just made it official by embracing the trend and releasing the new Kindles with a six-inch screen—back pocket-sized if I’ve ever seen it.

Okay, cool, but at that point why not just read on my phone? Because that screen is terrible for your eyes. The special e-ink displays these devices use are meant to be stared at for hours on end without risk of eye strain. It looks more like reading paper too, which I think does some Pavlovian thing to your brain if you grew up on paper books but maybe that's just me. Above all else, all the notifications and distractions that prevent you from actually reading are absent here.

If you're trying to read more, and do it on a device smaller than your phone, you've come to the right place. If you want an e-reader that fits in your pocket, these are the four serious options to choose from.

The newest Kindle is the first to be truly considered “pocket-sized.” Dimensions-wise, it falls somewhere between a pro max iPhone and an iPad Mini, but it fits easily in a back pocket or jacket pocket—just not necessarily the front of those skinny jeans. This 32-gigabyte aluminum unit comes in two colors, Seaglass Green and Alpine Sky, while the slightly more affordable 16 gig comes in Ube and Graphite for only $170 (or $150 if you don’t mind ads on the lock screen) and is made from recycled plastics.

Functionally it’s fast and easy to use. Compared to the last generation of Kindles, it takes 30 percent less time to open up a book from your library. Page turning is responsive via the touch screen, and the power button has been moved from the bottom to the upper right side for fewer accidents. And of course, easy access to a huge library of titles to purchase. If you don’t want to go through the chore of finding files online, the Kindle is the easiest way to get into e-readers.

The Boox Palma 2 was the e-reader that opened my eyes to how crucial a pocket-sized form factor is. This new and improved edition has it all. It has the right size, a color screen, and stylus support (stylus sold separately, of course). The refresh rate is smooth by e-reader standards and navigation is simple using intuitive touch controls or one of the buttons on the side. And it's fully compatible with all the apps readers love—from the Kindle and Libby apps to Calibre and their favorite RSS readers.

The Palma 2 Pro has one fully customizable "smart button" that you can program to do whatever you want—like pull up your notepad or make a bookmark. On top of that, it sports a headphone jack and built-in speakers for playback from audiobooks or Spotify. Anything that's on the Google Play store works here.

There's a ton of other features that don't really matter for our purposes. For example, you can put a SIM card in it, but that doesn't fully make it a cell phone, which just ends up confusing the matter. Here's all you need to know. This is the handheld, pocket-sized e-reader that comes with a color screen and speakers. It's the best of the best and that comes at a slightly higher price point. Accept it or move on to something that does a little less.

This is the most inventive e-reader I've seen. Not only is it tiny—a minuscule 3.7-inch screen compared to the 6.1 inch screens of the other two on here—it also is a freaking magnet. The tiny device is fully capable of magnetizing to the back of your iPhone like any other MagSafe accessory. In fact, its size is designed with this in mind. Instead of needing to carry two separate devices, why not smash them together? Then, when it's time to read, just flip your phone over and enjoy the world's smallest e-reader.

Once you get into the actual art of reading on it, the thing is pretty barebones. There are no adjustable front lights (e-reader screens have front lights, not backlights like LCDs), it's black-and-white only, and it only supports the bare minimum in terms of document and image formats. But for only $69, it's a steal.

An AI e-reader is a bit of an oxymoron. But really what this is is an e-reader for the Android heads out there who care about flexibility and freedom. That just happens to go hand in hand with modern AI features, which this device is flush with. If you don't care much for proliferating Sam Altman's idea that intelligence is a utility to be gobbled up and sold back to us piecemeal, you may shy away from the e-reader with the AI button and "personal knowledge bank."

I hate to report that some of this stuff is genuinely useful. While I don't need a button dedicated to AI, I see how having a searchable library of every question you've ever asked it is a useful thing. And beyond what's built in, you can also install the Gemini app on here, which allows for even more expansive use of AI tools.

If you choose to ignore all that, you're still getting what you pay for. Truthfully most readers don't need color (unless they are comic-book fans, but that's more fun in the physical form) and this is the most high-fidelity black and white screen you can get, with a refresh rate rivaling the Palma 2 Pro. You can load your Kindle library, your saved New York Times articles, or even a web browser on here to read the news. The only thing it's really missing is compatibility with Calibre, an exceedingly popular program a lot of readers I know use to store their digital library.

The singer called the track “one of my favorite things I’ve ever done.”

Harry Styles’s “Coming Up Roses” is a love song, the singer confirmed, but not a typical one. Originally conceived as a Christmas song (which Styles abandoned after two lines), the Kiss All the Time. Disco, Occasionally. track captures the uncertainty of new love, the fear of that relationship ending (“And everything seems to be comin’ up roses / But I’m scared if we’re both right / Does that mean we’re not aligned?”), and Styles’s decision to focus on the moment regardless of what the future holds (“There’s only me and you”).

In October, Styles revisited the song and released a music video for it, featuring him in a dance class with strangers. He briefly dances with different people, sharing a brief moment of closeness with each. The video ends with him on his own at the end of the class, watching people leave. They then all do a group shot together, celebrating community in the final frames:

In March, the singer spoke to Apple Music’s Zane Lowe about how “Coming Up Roses” came together—and how it reflects what he’s learned about love from his relationships that didn’t work out.

He called the song “one of my favorite things I’ve ever done. The kind of writing of it was really something that just happened. Like that was one of those that felt like it just fell out, and then you’re like reading the lyrics back, and I’m like learning something as I’m doing it. I’m like, oh, magic.”

He wrote the song in the month of December: “It started because I was trying to write a Christmas song, so that lasted two lines,” Styles told Lowe, citing his first verse’s “Tell me your fears / I’ve turned back the clocks, it’s that time of year.”

But then the lyrics transformed into something different: “I started it and then it kind of just wrote itself,” he said. “And I was like, ‘Oh, this has nothing to do with Christmas.’ For me, it is a love song about how special something can be—and not everything has to last forever in order for it to be special. I think some of the greatest relationships in your life that teach you something don’t last forever, you know?”

He added that defining successful relationships as only those with “longevity and lasting fraternity” misses the point.

“It just negates all of the beauty and so much positivity that can come from a relationship where you learn something about yourself,” he said. “And I think that’s a gift that someone else gives you even when it’s difficult.”

He added, “It’s something I’m really grateful for because I kind of had to learn a lot about myself at that point.”
