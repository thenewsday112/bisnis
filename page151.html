#1432. How to Use Custom Window Treatments to Pull a Room Together

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Expert advice from the designers of the 2026 Whole Home.

Window treatments are like an accessory or piece of jewelry for the home—the finishing touch that brings a look together and makes it feel complete. Without them, a room can feel underdressed, like it’s missing something.

“They help set the mood, soften the architecture, and make a space feel finished in a way that’s hard to replicate with anything else,” says interior designer Maci McGee of Ellerslie Interiors, who put this philosophy into practice at the 2026 Whole Home in Charleston, South Carolina.

Here, McGee and Kistna Conely of House Seven Design share how they approached window treatments in the Whole Home, and offer expert advice for bringing their approach into your own space.

In the bedroom designed by Ellerslie Interiors, a velvet upholstered bed holds court below a cornflower blue ceiling, and antique furniture sits against a backdrop of mustard-hued wallpaper with a large check pattern. “We had a lot of richness happening,” says McGee, “so the window treatments needed to support that without making the room feel too heavy.”

She introduced an organic element by pairing the handwoven TWOPAGES Rae Natural Bamboo Woven Shades - Series S with patterned drapes. “The drapery brings softness and character, while the woven shade is much quieter,” McGee says. “It filters the light beautifully and adds texture without competing with everything else in the room.”

Layering window treatments solves practical challenges while adding depth to a room. For the bedroom by House Seven Design, privacy was a priority because the window faces a busy urban street. The designers also wanted to maintain flexibility regarding how much daylight entered throughout the day.

Conely’s solution was to pair TWOPAGES Liz Custom Relaxed Roman Shade in sand beige and the Rae Natural Pure Bamboo Woven Shades - Series Z in reddish brown with linen drapery. “That combination brought a beautiful layer of texture to the space and helped establish the soft, organic feeling we wanted,” says Conely.

Utilizing two shades plus drapes also offers options for managing light and privacy while making a window feel more finished. “You can let daylight filter through one layer and close the other when you want the room to feel more private,” she says.

In a home with multiple distinct spaces, window treatments help establish a visual connection without making every room look alike. The trick, McGee says, is to focus on the overall aesthetic rather than repeating the same treatment throughout.

“For us, cohesion is more about sensibility than repetition,” she says. “The fabrics can change, the treatment style can change, but there’s still a common thread. It feels collected rather than matched.”

Conely suggests carrying a color or texture from one room into another to connect adjoining spaces while preserving each room’s personality. These subtle relationships help a whole-home design feel cohesive without becoming overly coordinated. With TWOPAGES’ range of colors, fabrics, and finishes, homeowners can carry a consistent design thread from room to room while varying the treatments to suit each space.

One of the most common mistakes Conely sees is choosing window treatments that are too small for the space. “Drapery that is too short or doesn’t have enough width can make the whole room feel a little off,” she says.

Before selecting a fabric, consider where the drapery will be mounted, how far the panels should extend, and how much fullness they need. McGee also recommends thinking about how the treatments relate to the window and the room as a whole. And don't overlook function: Privacy, blackout needs, and filtering light should all factor into the decision.

If you’re aiming to achieve a more custom, high-end look with window treatments, it’s the small decisions that will have the biggest impact.

“Hanging drapery higher, giving it enough fullness, choosing a fabric with the right weight and hand, and making sure the proportions feel right all make a huge difference,” says McGee. Custom trim also offers another opportunity to add personality. “None of those choices are necessarily loud,” she says, “but together they’re what make a space feel elevated.”

#1345. On the Couch with Silka Rittson-Thomas

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The creative mind behind Le Monde Béryl’s New York flagship shares a collection of personal favorites.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE Decor editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Curator and collector Silka Rittson-Thomas has run her own independent art advisory for almost two decades. The Phillips alum has worked with scores of galleries and auction houses and established an art residency program at famed Positano hotel Le Sirenuse in 2015. Now, she’s turned her attention to a more unexpected space: a footwear flagship in Manhattan.

In collaboration with architecture studio Tutto Bene, Rittson-Thomas designed the new Upper East Side store for Le Monde Béryl, a luxury shoe brand founded in 2016 by Lily Atherton Hanbury and Katya Shyfrin. It’s the brand’s first permanent location, and the neutral-toned space is filled with design details like 20th-century Murano twin-branched wall sconces, aluminum easy chairs by Mats Theselius, and a Ransom & Dunn “Adler” sofa with custom upholstery. “On the one hand I was thinking about the principles of the Renaissance, specifically around lighting, reflections and proportions, while the idea was to create a space which feels theatre-like, a place of discovery,” Rittson-Thomas tells ELLE Decor.

Now open on 67th and Madison, Le Monde Béryl is Rittson-Thomas’s most recent project, but she has plenty in the pipeline. She’s renovating a private apartment, also with Tutto Bene, in London, and, she says, “I’m also passionate about a seed project that my friend Lena Evstafieva—the proprietor and mind behind Villa Lena in Tuscany—and I are scheming about.”

Below, we had the art advisor take our On the Couch survey, sharing her own go-to galleries, favorite artists, and destination museums.

Define your studio in three words: Unexpected, poetic, collaborative.

Greatest influences: The natural world, artists’ minds, my eyes, and light.

Favorite eras of design: Neoclassicism; the 1930s and 1940s; the 1980s; Brutalism; weathered design and architecture.

Favorite sofa: Cy Twombly’s elongated Roman sofa is, indefinitely, my favorite sofa. I have a tendency to consider sofas as objects rather than objects of comfort. I love a heavily patinated Kaare Klint sofa, the delightful yellow velvet Armand-Albert Rateau sofa for Lanvin, and Regency sofas.

Most beloved buildings: My parents’ modernist bungalow, a folly built in the 1970s. I like to think of gardens and landscapes as buildings, and among my favorites are geometric and spiritual gardens, such as those designed by the late Spanish landscape architect Fernando Caruncho, or the “musical garden” by the Danish landscape architect Carl Theodor Sørensen.

Other favorites include the Ibn Tulun Mosque in Cairo; Hagia Sophia in Istanbul; the Querini Stampalia in Venice; and the Summer Palace in Bukhara, built in the late nineteenth century by the last Emir of Bukhara as a summer residence for his beloved princess. I would also love to work on a commission with Milena Muzquiz and her vibrant ceramics and installation work.

Vintage piece of your dreams: A large eighteenth-century Sicilian Baroque giltwood mirror with sinuously carved foliage and its unusual original turquoise paint, from Tarquin Bilgen on the Pimlico Road. A pair of Spanish-colonial Viceregal Peruvian mother-of-pearl-inlaid bureau-cabinets sold by Zebregs & Röell.

Favorite getaway: Always Paris via Eurostar; lake swimming in the Austrian and Swiss Alps; and Genoa.

Hotels to check into: My favorite new Parisian hotel is Hôtel Masse in Pigalle. I would like to check into Hope by Wildland, set within the vast coastal landscapes of Sutherland; Reschio in Umbria; The Tuba Club. Le Sirenuse always, of course.

Favorite jeweler: The artist jeweler du jour, Julia Muggenburg of Belmacz; antique Jules Wièse pieces; and Art Deco Jean Després.

Fashion designers: 1990s and 2000 vintage Prada, 1970s and 1980s YSL. Contemporary: Róhe, Attersee, and RIER.

Best host/hostess gift: Beautiful napkins or seeds with a story—Gayle Warwick linens and seeds from historic gardens like Great Dixter are my go-tos.

More than 100 million voters are expected to cast ballots in the 2026 U.S. midterm elections.

In recent election cycles, political reporting based on voter polling has drawn criticism, prompted scrutiny, and found defenders. As the debate over the value of polling continues, The Wall Street Journal is exploring new ways to cover public opinion and break out of traditional horse race political coverage.

In late September, the Journal published its first story using Doppler News AI, a custom tool built to help expand the publication’s midterms coverage. So far, Doppler has ingested more than 25 million TikTok videos, roughly 500,000 podcast episodes, and thousands of Instagram videos and cable news segments. The Journal uses the tool to monitor trends across the internet and to identify voter priorities, concerns, and sentiments that traditional polling might miss.

Here are the questions they’re asking. Here are the places where those conversations are happening.'”

Other newsrooms have leaned on AI “visibility tools” in the past to support their reporting. That includes using large language models (LLMs) to transcribe and summarize “manosphere” podcasts, school board meetings, and local talk radio shows. But Doppler goes further.

Rather than using AI-generated summaries to generate leads or help beat reporters monitor online content more efficiently, the Journal uses Doppler to conduct large-scale AI analyses quickly — and publishes those insights for readers.

So far, analyses produced by Doppler have led to four major midterms stories in the Journal, with more expected to run before Election Day. For now, each story requires a custom treatment, with engineers and data journalists working alongside the D.C. politics team to refine the methodology, toggle on and off different features, and audit the results.

“The tool is only as good as the questions that you ask it,” said Whitaker-Moore. The Journal holds a weekly editorial meeting leading up to Election Day to workshop story angles and find new threads to explore.

A story published on October 8 analyzed over 30,000 hours of podcasts and documented how, in recent weeks, right-leaning podcasters had started discussing “affordability” as often as left-leaning podcasters, despite President Trump calling the affordability crisis a “hoax.”

Another story published on October 1 traced anti-AI sentiment, including opposition to Flock surveillance cameras, by analyzing more than four million TikTok videos and thousands of hours of podcasts. The Journal found that posters who shared anti-Flock sentiments disagreed on other political positions about 12% of the time, three times the average rate across other political topics tracked — putting the issue “at the top of the list of positions that unite creators who are otherwise at odds.”

For a story published on October 15, the Journal pulled content about 67 Democratic politicians from its Doppler database. The analysis showed that the Democratic politician currently receiving the most favorable commentary is Angie Nixon, the progressive Senate nominee in Florida. About 70% of TikTok videos posted about her in recent weeks were favorable. She was followed by Georgia Senator Jon Ossoff at 54%. Meanwhile, Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer ranked the lowest in the analysis; just 3% of videos posted about him were favorable.

Whitaker-Moore said the idea for Doppler was inspired by conversations with Washington bureau chief Damian Paletta and politics editor Christopher Matthews. They thought the coverage of the 2024 U.S. election missed important undercurrents in public opinion that never surfaced in polling. And though reporters acknowledged the power of podcasts in that election cycle, there weren’t many standardized ways to capture what was being said across those channels.

Coming out of those conversations, Whitaker-Moore posed a question to Rob Barry, who leads the Journal’s data journalism team: “Can you build something that can eat the internet and then feed it back to us?”

The Journal had already built its own podcast transcription and summarization tool, Orca. (The name refers to the tight-knit pods in which orcas travel.) Orca had collected countless hours of audio and YouTube videos, not just of political podcasts but from genres like health and wellness.

It’s nearly impossible to create a comprehensive database of TikTok videos, or even a truly random sample. Instead, Barry’s team used a monitoring tool that relies on TikTok’s own recommendation algorithms to identify the accounts it should track.

“Every day we’re adding tons of data across all the different platforms that we’re looking at,” Barry told me. “The database is growing in the background constantly.”

To make sense of that data, Doppler has a tech stack that includes different types of AI models, including LLMs. The model used depends on the specific reporting question.

Embedding models have been central to some of stories published so far. These AI models convert text or images into vectors — lists of numbers that capture the meaning of each piece of content. By vectorizing the content, Doppler can map out the ideas in a dataset and see where they naturally cluster together.

The Journal’s data journalism team has used vectorization in the past, including in its Pulitzer Prize–winning story that mapped out Elon Musk’s entire history of tweets. In Doppler, vectorization facilitates a kind of internet radar, helping reporters identify where patterns are forming across its social media and podcasting database.

For now, the Journal’s political reporters can’t just start using Doppler on their own. The exact methodology has been tailored to each story published, with the help of engineers.

Despite manual spot checks of data classifications and other safeguards to make sure the tool’s outputs are accurate, Barry said, “there’s almost always going to be some level of error and uncertainty and blur around the edges.” The disclosures are one way to “give readers a sense for what that level of issue is.”

Looking ahead, the Journal is considering bringing Doppler to other desks in the newsroom. The data being collected is not inherently political, and it could be used to help answer a host of other reporting questions, drawing on insights scattered across the internet.

“We definitely think that [Doppler News AI] can live beyond the midterms and live beyond elections,” Whitaker-Moore said.

And these weren't the obviously artificial wood-look tiles you might remember from years past. With realistic knots, nuanced color variations, and finishes designed to replicate the texture of actual timber, some were convincing enough to warrant a second look—or touch.

Of course, wood-look tile isn't new. It's been around for decades, offering homeowners the appearance of hardwood without some of its maintenance demands. But as tile trends continue to favor texture and material experimentation, manufacturers are making a compelling case for giving it another look, introducing increasingly sophisticated collections that blur the line between natural and manufactured materials.

The question is: Just because tile can look like an authentic hardwood floor, does that mean designers actually want it to?

If there's one thing the latest collections have in common, it's an obsession with realism. Manufacturers are going beyond printed wood grain, developing surfaces that attempt to replicate the way natural timber looks and feels.

At Cersaie, Italian manufacturer Atlas Concorde debuted Log Anthology, an expansion of its wood-effect porcelain offering that introduces six new wood species, including Douglas fir, ash, elm, walnut, teak, and zebrano. The collection moves beyond the familiar oak aesthetic, offering everything from understated, Nordic-inspired finishes to richly grained exotic woods.

"Realism today is as much about touch as it is about sight," says Viola Pagani of Atlas Concorde. The collection incorporates the company's Sensitech surface technology, which combines slip resistance with a softer feel underfoot. High-definition decoration also allows the brand to reproduce the natural irregularities of different wood species.

Meanwhile, Marca Corona introduced Elisir Pura, a porcelain collection inspired by Slavonian oak. Available in two variations, one with minimal knots and subtle veining and another with more pronounced natural markings, the collection features a soft-touch, wax-effect finish designed to mimic the tactile qualities of wood.

According to Francesco Giusti of Marca Corona, the collection's distinctive texture comes from a custom-blended glaze that creates subtle depressions along the grain, similar to the way natural wood feels after sanding.

And it's not just luxury Italian manufacturers investing in the category. Floor & Decor has introduced its NatureMatch line, which emphasizes more realistic wood visuals and textures.

"Wood-look tile has matured since its original debut in the market," says Ashley Biscan, design and trend expert at Floor & Decor, who reports growing demand for the material as its appearance improves.

The most popular styles? Think light wood tones, minimal color variation, and understated grain patterns. "Fabricated, exaggerated knots and grains are a thing of the past," Biscan says.

Beyond appearances, wood-look porcelain has a few practical advantages over the real thing. Unlike natural hardwood, porcelain is highly resistant to moisture, scratches, and everyday wear, making it particularly appealing for bathrooms, kitchens, mudrooms, and other hardworking spaces.

Climate also plays a significant role. Biscan notes that wood-look tile is especially appealing in coastal and desert regions, where moisture, humidity, and environmental conditions can make traditional hardwood more challenging to maintain.

Britney Stein, designer at Florida-based Sketch Design Studio, has seen those benefits firsthand. For an oceanfront residence, her team specified wood-look porcelain rather than hardwood to better withstand the home's exposure to humidity, salt air, and moisture.

"As designers, we always want to find that balance between creating something beautiful and ensuring the materials will perform well in their environment," Stein says.

The material can also create visual continuity between spaces where real wood might not be practical. Stein is currently working on a project featuring wood-look chevron porcelain tile in an outdoor loggia, carefully color-matched to the natural hardwood floors inside. When the home's large sliding glass doors open, the two spaces will appear to flow together.

Atlas Concorde is taking a similar approach with Log Anthology, offering coordinating indoor planks, outdoor pavers, herringbone formats, and large-format slabs that can extend the wood aesthetic onto walls and even furniture.

Still, wood-look tile isn't without drawbacks. Porcelain is harder and cooler underfoot than hardwood, and while it can be a more affordable alternative, that isn't always the case. Stein points out that higher-end porcelain products, particularly when installation is factored in, can cost just as much as natural hardwood.

Here's where things get interesting. While manufacturers are pushing the boundaries of what wood-look porcelain can achieve, not every interior designer is convinced it deserves a place in their projects.

"I tend to gravitate towards more timeless materials and finishes rather than those that feel trend driven," says Lauren Weisner, founder and principal designer of Shoppe Weis. "While I can certainly appreciate the appeal and practicality of wood-like tile in certain spaces, it's not something I would reach for."

For Weisner, the issue isn't necessarily how convincing the imitation looks. It's what gets lost in translation.

"Natural wood is irreplaceable. Its warmth, texture, and tactile quality bring a depth that man-made materials simply can't replicate," she explains.

Designer Evan Millárd shares that sentiment, pointing to repetitive grain patterns, visible grout lines, and textures that don't quite match the wood they're imitating as common giveaways. He also worries that wood-look tile could date a space more quickly than natural materials, particularly as preferences for plank widths, finishes, and installation patterns change.

"There's something to be said for letting materials be what they are," Millard says.

But that doesn't mean wood-look tile is entirely off the table. Even Weisner acknowledges that it could make sense in a basement, mudroom, or bathroom if executed exceptionally well.

Stein, who has incorporated the material into several projects, takes a more flexible approach. She believes the latest iterations can come remarkably close to natural hardwood, especially when manufacturers prioritize realistic grain variation, quality printing, and textured finishes.

"The size and proportion of the planks, grout selection, and installation also make a significant difference in the final result," she says.

In other words, a convincing wood-look tile floor requires more than simply picking the prettiest plank.

That depends on what we're expecting it to do.

If the goal is to replace natural hardwood entirely, designers aren't exactly lining up to make the switch. Even those who appreciate the practical benefits acknowledge that there's something about the warmth, character, and authenticity of real wood that porcelain can't fully reproduce.

But as an alternative for spaces where hardwood isn't necessarily the smartest choice? That's a different story.

"I do believe wood-look tile has staying power, particularly in projects where durability and practicality are just as important as aesthetics," Stein says. Rather than viewing it as a replacement for hardwood, she considers it another valuable option in a designer's material palette.

And perhaps that's the most compelling argument for the material's future. Wood-look tile doesn't need to fool everyone into thinking it's hardwood to earn its place in a home. It simply needs to look good, perform well, and make sense for the space.

With manufacturers continuing to refine its appearance and texture, the gap between the two materials may keep narrowing. But whether wood-look tile becomes the next big flooring trend or remains a practical alternative, one thing is clear: It's come a long way from the faux wood floors of the past.

And while I wouldn't necessarily trade beautiful hardwood floors for porcelain, after seeing the latest collections at Cersaie, I'm certainly more open to the idea.

If you’re a seasoned marathoner, a 12- to 16-week training cycle may be enough to get you to the starting line. But if you’re tackling your first marathon, it’s better to give yourself at least 18 to 24 weeks for formal training, says Katie Cyrana-Hillery, PT, DPT, CSCS, a physical therapist, RRCA-certified run coach, and strength and conditioning specialist at Evolve Physical Therapy and Performance. “This longer timeline allows your bones, muscles, tendons, and joints to adapt to the increased workload, along with your cardiovascular system,” she says.

“The objective can either be to gradually increase your running intervals until you’re able to run continuously, or to train to complete the marathon using a run/walk strategy,” she says. “Both are valid approaches to covering 26.2 miles.” The secret is finding a rhythm you can sustain over the long haul.

Stronger muscles also support running mechanics, improve stability, and combat fatigue as your weekly mileage increases, she adds.

To set yourself up for success, Dr. Weber recommends two to three strength-training sessions per week, with a special emphasis on building strength in your glutes, quads, hamstrings, calves, and core. If you’re new to resistance training, just be sure to start with lighter weights and focus on good form.

More running doesn’t always mean better results. Rest days give your body time to repair muscle tissue and adapt to the demands of training, ultimately helping you build both strength and endurance. “You absolutely do not need to run every day as a first-time marathoner,” says Cyrana-Hillery. “I recommend starting with three days of running per week to allow enough time for recovery and strength training.”

Just as important as scheduling rest days is recognizing when your body needs extra recovery. Disrupted sleep, lingering soreness, an elevated resting heart rate, or declining running performance can all signal that you’re pushing too hard, says Cyrana-Hillery. Prioritizing gentle mobility exercises, dynamic stretching, adequate sleep, and proper fueling can also help maintain range of motion, support muscle repair, improve running efficiency, and reduce injury risk, adds Dr. Weber. Think of it this way: Your body gets stronger not just from the work you do, but from how well you recover afterward.

“It’s normal for your body to feel challenged as you increase your training load, but persistent or worsening pain is different from normal training fatigue and shouldn’t be ignored,” says Dr. Weber.

If something doesn’t feel right, don’t be afraid to shorten a run, slow your pace, take an extra rest day, or switch to a run/walk approach. If pain affects how you move, check in with a health professional rather than trying to push through. As Dr. Weber puts it, “A training plan is a guide, not a contract. Your job is to adapt the plan to your body, not force your body to conform to the plan.”

Training for a marathon can be hard, intimidating, and nerve-wracking, but it should also be fun, exciting, and rewarding. So instead of only focusing on the end goal, celebrate the small wins along the way, whether that’s running a little farther than you did last week, feeling stronger on your runs, or sticking to your training schedule when things feel good.

“Training for your first marathon is a big accomplishment in itself, so don’t lose sight of the joy and excitement of the experience,” says Dr. Weber. “The ultimate goal is to arrive at the starting line healthy, enjoy the journey, and discover what you’re capable of.”

#3902. Frank Lloyd Wright’s Taliesin West closes after visitor killed by wind-blown debris

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Frank Lloyd Wright’s Taliesin West, Scottsdale, Arizona Photo: Andrew Horne, via Wikimedia Commons

Taliesin West in Scottsdale, Arizona—Frank Lloyd Wright’s former home in the Sonoran Desert—closed to the public last week following the death of a visitor, who was struck by part of a roof that had dislodged from a building on the property.

The Scottsdale Fire Department revealed that a Brazilian national, Rogério Renato Mateucci, had been struck by an acrylic fragment during a walking tour on the afternoon of 16 October, during which wind gusts had reached 32mph. Mateucci, who was pronounced dead at the scene, was the rector of the Pontifical Catholic University of Paraná in Brazil. He had travelled to the US to participate in an event at Arizona State University.

One of Mateucci’s colleagues, Vidal Martins—who was not present on the tour but later saw Mateucci’s body at the scene—provided a photo to The Arizona Republic that showed a curved, translucent roof panel lying on the rocks of the property. He believes this was the object that struck Mateucci. Martins also questioned how the roofing had been attached and urged for security measures to be taken.

A former Taliesin West employee, who spoke to the local news on condition of anonymity, said he had witnessed two other objects breaking loose from the roof. Over the summer, the anonymous source had recorded a video of a similar panel coming partially loose during a monsoon. “I don’t think that there is money being funnelled into the proper channels to ensure that the building is safe,” he said.

Police are investigating the death as a weather-related accident. The Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (FLWF), which manages the site, says it will not release further details out of respect for the deceased’s privacy and to allow authorities to complete their investigation. The site will remain closed until further notice.

“The foundation extends its deepest condolences to the family and loved ones of the individual who died following an accident at Taliesin West,” Joe Spector, the chief executive of the FLWF, said in a statement. The foundation did not comment when asked about reopening the site or any safety measures it plans to take before it does.

Taliesin West, a Unesco World Heritage Site and National Historic Landmark, was established in 1937 as Wright’s winter home and studio. It also served as an architecture school. The sprawling 491-acre site in the foothills of the McDowell Mountains features a series of structures built with local stone, desert sand and concrete. The complex is known as an emblematic example of Wright’s concept of “organic architecture”, the harmonisation of architecture with the landscape. Shortly after Wright’s death in 1959, Taliesin West became the headquarters of the FLWF and began offering guided tours.

The original roofs at Taliesin West were made from canvas stretched across wooden beams. The translucent and lightweight material could be opened or closed to regulate airflow and temperature, and was removed and stored when Wright and his students left the site during the summer. In the late 1950s, when Taliesin West was gradually adapted for year-round use, the canvas roofs were replaced with fibreglass panels, and later with insulated acrylic panels.

Earlier this year, the director of preservation of the FLWF, Rebecca Barron, said in an interview that an energy audit in 2011 had found that the acrylic roofs were not energy efficient. Over the past decade, the foundation has been developing a plan to replace the acrylic panels—which were “nearing the end of their serviceable life”, Barron said—with modern fabric roofs that could both withstand the desert climate and honour Wright’s original vision.

#2824. Justin Salas Becomes the First Visually Impaired Athlete to Climb 5.13c

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Justin Salas reaches for a far hold on 'America's Most Wanted' (5.13c) (Photo: April Davidson)

Justin Salas is on fire. On Friday, October 9, the 33-year-old sport climber and boulderer made the hardest known ascent by a visually impaired athlete, ticking America’s Most Wanted (5.13c) at Mount Charleston near Las Vegas, Nevada. The 35-foot cave climb starts with a tough V7, proceeds up through a series of power endurance moves to a second V7 crux, then continues onward to the chains at the top of the Robbery Cave roof.

“In really light or bright-colored limestone cliffs, where everything’s gray or tan, I can’t see any of the holds,” Salas told Climbing after his ascent. “So I feel completely blind.”

Ever since he started climbing at 21, Salas has been pushing the limits for visually impaired climbers. He ticked his first V11, Worm Turns in Joe’s Valley, in 2018 after just three years of climbing. Since then, he’s knocked down more than 20 double-digit boulders, accrued six national titles, and won a paraclimbing world championship.

But America’s Most Wanted represented more than simply another personal challenge. It was also his answer to being excluded from participation in the 2028 Paralympics. Just four months prior, Salas found out, along with the rest of the world, that when it comes to visually impaired climbers, the Olympic debut of paraclimbing will only include the men’s B1 (most blind) category and the women’s B2 (second-most blind) category.

Salas, who developed bilateral optic atrophy at age 14, can only compete in the B2 category. He retains his peripheral vision, but has no central vision in either eye. Like other paraclimbers have said, Salas says he would have preferred if the International Paralympic Committee combined all categories into one category, rather than exclude whole groups of athletes.

“I got pushed out of the Olympics,” he said, “and it hit me really, really hard. I’m not going to lie. It’s still hard to think about.”

America’s Most Wanted came about as a new project when Salas and his spouse, April Davidson, moved back to Vegas. Salas started to shift his focus from bouldering to sport climbing. “The movement’s crazy rad,” said Salas, adding that climbing in the cave felt particularly disorienting.

When I called Salas to get the play by play, I wasn’t surprised to hear that his movement relied on extensive beta memorization. What did surprise me, however, was all of the additional expertise he had to command in order to climb at the 5.13c level. Not only did Salas have to hang onto each hold for longer than a sighted climber, but he also had to anticipate which direction every single quickdraw was facing. On America’s Most Wanted, in fact, he had to stop relying on his innate sense of body positioning that had served him so well in bouldering and on other routes. “My biggest strength became my greatest weakness,” he said.

Read on to learn about Salas’s journey on America’s Most Wanted, why the redpoint crux still feels like a blackout in his mental map, and what it’s like to navigate a professional athlete career with a disability that’s not immediately obvious to others.

The following interview has been lightly edited for concision and clarity.

Justin Salas: When I was really hitting the ground running with professional rock climbing, I was primarily bouldering and I was only training sport for competition. I’ve always been relatively gifted with being strong—not necessarily in finger strength, but just physically gifted. I grew up lifting weights and doing powerlifting with my younger brother. I think because of losing my sight at the age that I did, 14, I was still struggling with the idea of being blind—and I still do right now. I have a really hard time coming to grips with it. It’s been almost 20 years now, and I can’t flow with it. I haven’t accepted it still.

I want quick rewards. I want to be strong. I want to feel like I did. I want success, and I want to win. That’s what bouldering was giving me for a long time. Then [V11] happened in three years. That was a pretty quick journey into being a double-digit climber. And it didn’t stop. As soon as I found out I could climb at that level, I continued. I’ve climbed more than 20 double-digit boulders now. Last year, I flashed my first V8, which was crazy for me because as a blind guy, I’m not very good at flashing.

Salas: Over the last two and a half years, I’ve started working full-time. I’m helping the founder of a company I work for, Perspective Tester, build a digital accessibility technology company. It’s been a ton of work because it’s a startup. In the beginning, it was anywhere between 40 to 80 hours per week. When I would go climb, bouldering wasn’t satisfying. Not that it’s not fun. When I did get that chance to go climb, I wanted to go get on a rope, whether that was doing easy trad or sport climbing or following people up multi-pitches. Not only was it the physical side, it was the mental, the physiological, the overcoming fear. There was a spiritual component to being back out in nature and being connected with creation again.

Salas: I have to memorize everything. It has to be really simple for me to do well with onsighting, especially longer sport climbs or trad routes, because I can’t see any of the holds. I have no idea where the feet are. Yes, I have peripheral vision still, but if you could imagine staring up at the wall and using your peripheral vision to be able to see your feet, that’s what I’m working with. It’s really challenging to try to onsight things.  I might as well be completely blind, especially on limestone. Where everything’s gray or tan, I can’t see any of the holds, so I feel completely blind. I can orientate myself in space, but I don’t know where the holds are at all.

Salas: [In February 2024, April and I] ended up back in Vegas. That’s when I was like, “I just want to go climb the things that are exciting to me that look rad, regardless of how hard they are.” [America’s Most Wanted] was the first one. I just thought it looked the sickest.  I don’t remember when it was bolted, but it’s one of the easiest things in the cave because everything else in the cave is freaking nails. The next easiest thing is 5.14b, so it’s an entry-level hard climb for that cave—for the elite climbers that come to Vegas and live here in the climbing community. It’s like a rite of passage for hard sport climbing.

Salas: Last October 2025, I tried it for two sessions. I don’t know why; it just did not go well. I’ll be perfectly honest: It felt too hard. And I was out of shape. I was still climbing V10 at Red Rock, but being able to execute short sprints of hard climbing, versus having the fitness to climb America’s Most Wanted. It’s not the longest thing in the world, but I didn’t even have the fitness, so I decided not to try it again until this year. I finally sunk my teeth into it sometime between late June and mid-July.

Salas: The movement’s crazy rad. The position’s outrageous. Climbing in that cave is really sick. The cave is not just a flat piece of ground with a big dome over you. It’s like a diagonal slot in the mountain, and the cave rises up with the landing. So you’re not really climbing linearly. It’s like you’re traversing at the same time. It’s disorienting. It feels weird when you first get in there to figure out where you are in space.

Being in a cave was way more exciting for me because I use heel hooks, toe hooks, bikes, and a lot of cave technique to stay on the wall longer, as someone with blindness. I can use some trickery and increase my time on the wall before I pump out and fall. I’m naturally more geared toward going to those types of climbs.

America’s Most Wanted has a really sick hold set. All the grips are not really bad. They’re mostly full-pad edges and jugs, but they’re not traditional jugs. They’re really unique shapes and cool pods. There is a lot of trickery with doing the toe hooks and biking and body tension, and a lot of core tension involved.

Salas: Clipping the bolts are moves by themselves, because you have to figure out, “How the hell am I going to clip this bolt in this position?” There is one clip in particular where you have a left toe jam and a right toe hook, and you’re biking off this toe jam and right hook. It doesn’t feel like it’s going to work until you get into the position. It’s really unique, pretty novel.

Salas: In the very beginning of trying anything, it definitely looks like that—working pretty closely with a sight guide to find the holds. In the [send] video, it won’t look like I’m super blind because it’s so dialed by now. This is like 20 sessions deep by this time.

You’ll notice I do this hilarious little tapping thing with my feet. It looks like I have Elvis leg, but each tap, if you can imagine, is like a drop in a puddle. There are ripples that come out from that drop. The ripple is the understanding of what I’m trying to put my foot on. So I’m looking at the hold with my foot while you watch my foot tap quickly on things. But if you were to have watched me at the beginning of trying this route, someone is belaying me and someone else is shouting to me where the holds are, where feet are.

Salas: I’m like, “If this hold is here, I want a foot here. Is there anything over here?” Because I know how to climb; I just don’t know where the feet are. It’s like, if I were a routesetter, I would put a foot here. But I don’t know if there’s a foot over there. [Someone will say] “Put your hand out,” and they’ll guide my hand to where they think something’s going to be. Then I’ll feel it, and I’ll be like, “That’s too small. I need something else. Is there another option you can see?” We do that process from the first bolt to the last.

Then there’s a full three or four sessions of layering. So the first session is memory, the second session is better memory, the third session is even better memory. The fifth session I’m starting to do links and connecting areas together. At some point, I don’t need a sight guide because it’s so dialed. Or maybe my belayer just needs to cue me here and there: “Yeah, that’s the foot, you’re on it, good. Get your hips up.”

Salas: This is the best part of the whole shit. The first move off the ground of America’s Most Wanted is objectively the hardest. It’s a single V7 move just to start the climb. There’s a really uncomfortable, left-hand side pull that’s slightly undercut, a right-hand undercling, and good feet, but you’re diving backward over your head into this really stupid crimp that’s got a hood on it—protected, so to say. It took me a long time to even figure that move out. But because it’s the first move, you do it fresh, so you have the best chance of executing the hardest single move on the climb right away.

Then there are probably eight more moves to get to the redpoint crux: another single V7 move. You get this big double undercling. It’s a fat jug, mad incut and super positive. You build your feet up really high and launch super far—full wingspan for me—to a three-finger drag. It’s slightly incut, full pad, really good hold. I fell there many, many, many dozens of times.

Salas: Yeah. It’s a super accuracy-dependent move. I’m not a stranger to doing accuracy-dependent moves. I’ve done quite a few really hard dynos to small grips. That’s just a part of bouldering. You have to be accurate because you’re thrashing to small grips and in hard positions. This was no exception to that. If anything, it suited my style well, being already really accustomed to climbing quite a few double-digit boulders in limestone roofs.

But once I got the first half dialed, I would climb to this redpoint crux and fall, pull back on, do the move, and go to the chains. This was probably for 15 sessions, maybe more. I was living in this hilarious purgatory of, “I know what’s going to happen this session.” And what was happening was I would fall, sit in the dog, pull back on, and do the move immediately.

That’s the crux for everybody because by the time you get there, you’re tired, but I had one of the biggest realizations of my climbing on this route because of that move.

Salas: I couldn’t feel how my body needed to be in the space to execute the move. Even now, I couldn’t tell you what that grip looked like. But what was happening was that by the time I would get there from the bottom, I didn’t have the sensitivity in my body to understand where my body needed to be in space to do that move.

So my biggest strength became my greatest weakness for this route. I wasn’t pumped, but I was tired enough. All of my proprioception and the sensitivity I had was gone.

Salas: I had to go away from intuitive climbing to like, logic-brain climbing: “This knee has to do this thing.” My right knee has to tag the wall when I’m at the top of the swing, but I still need to execute the swing in full. There was so much about what my body was doing that I needed to break down to the nth degree because it wasn’t going to work with just intuitive climbing.

I had to understand the move logically to be able to do it. Otherwise, I had no sensitivity to how the move was going to work.

Salas: I was talking with my best friend, Matt Frederick. He’s been my sight guide in competition for the last 11 years. He was like, “Break that context window up. Show up to the crag with someone else.” Just throwing out random ideas, right? “Wear different clothes, eat something different, start the day off differently. Break the cycle, because you’re in a bad loop right now.”

I have a tendency, as someone that relies so heavily on the way that I feel while I climb in muscle memory. If I get in a bad loop, I’ll continue to fail habitually because I’m stuck. So I was trying everything I could to change everything about it.

Salas: I started going up there with Joe Kinder and Cam Hörst more. I was being mentored by these dudes that are so much better at climbing than I am. I’ve looked up to Joe basically for my entire life of climbing, and Cam and I have a long history of going out to Red Rock together in 2020 with our friends Drew Ruana and Brooke Raboutou. So reconnecting with him, going back to the Roost, sessioning with him again, and going out there with Joe were more ways for me to change the context. It wasn’t just going with friends; it was going with mentors.

Salas: I’ve described these tapping sensations I do with my feet—if you can, imagine Sonar, where each tap gives me an idea of what I’m about to put my foot on. I can visualize the grips with my mind because I’ve touched them, because I know how they feel in my hands or on my feet.

[But] now that I’ve done it and have thought about it, I still don’t know what that crux hold is like. I legit have a blackout moment right there. The mental map is smooth and the redpoint crux comes and there’s nothing. Then I remember the rest of it after that.

Salas: Everything about it was total blindness, total hope and prayer that I would eventually stick the move. It would eventually start fucking with my head space. It was like Russian roulette: I’m not climbing anymore, just gambling. It was frustrating for a moment. It was like, “Am I going to do this route because I’m a capable enough climber to do this route? Or am I going to do this route just because I get lucky?” I don’t like the latter option. I want to be able to actually be a capable rock climber.

Salas: There was a climb in Arkansas—It was my first V8 outside, called Kung Fu, [in 2016]. It was a really long V7, and then you get into the final dyno. Back then, this was the first year of me climbing, so I was numbing out every time I would get to the end of Kung Fu. It was the same thing. I tried Kung Fu for probably 20-odd sessions. I hadn’t had that level of shutdown on an accuracy-based move like that [until] doing America’s Most Wanted. But we’re talking about a full decade later. Sometimes you think you grow, and you get better, but at some point, you’re reintroduced to the same bullshit.

I was numbing out on Kung Fu because I was a newer climber and I was struggling with being fit enough. But for this one, I wasn’t numbing out because I was too pumped or too cold. It was like the communication between my body and my brain was fizzling out. By the time I got to that redpoint crux, I didn’t have the sensitivity in my body to understand how to put my hips into the box to stick the move.

Salas: Cam started coming and staying with us more frequently because he was trying to clean up some—I think he’s got one more 5.14c in the cave to do, and then he’s basically done everything.

When I first started getting out and traveling by myself—this might sound hilarious and not that big a deal, but when you lose your vision, you lose a lot of confidence. So I was always going on trips with friends or with people that were guiding me on my travels. And when I started going to Vegas with Drew Ruana and Cam Hörst, it was me going out on my own. I would travel through the airport on my own; I would Uber around on my own. Everything was just completely on my own.

So that’s the context I had with Cam. Actually, my first time going out [to America’s Most Wanted] with him, I stuck the [redpoint] move from the ground. Salas: In 2021, when I first heard about it, I thought it could be, but then in between that time period, Nicolas Moineau had climbed 8a/5.13b on a rope.
