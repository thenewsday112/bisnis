#3554. Abu Dhabi reveals design for youth concert hall by the late Frank Gehry

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Abu Dhabi‘s Department of Culture and Tourism has unveiled plans for a concert hall dedicated to young musicians, designed by Canadian-American architect Frank Gehry before his death.

Expected to open in January 2027 in the Mina Zayed port district, the Youth Orchestra Abu Dhabi will contain a 600-seat concert hall and 18 rehearsal rooms.

The orchestra building, designed by Gehry before he died in December last year, will have a more pared-back appearance compared to the fluid, organic forms for which the architect is famed.

Renders show a two-tiered building with long, straight rooflines and tall glass facades broken up by white panels.

Inside, wood will line the staircases and balcony walkways, and the top floor will open onto rooftop terraces.

Youth Orchestra Abu Dhabi will provide free access to musical education programmes for children aged six to nine, with the first cohort enrolling 100 students.

“The Youth Orchestra Abu Dhabi is an important investment in our future and in our commitment to making culture and creativity accessible to all,” said Department of Culture and Tourism Abu Dhabi chairman Mohamed Khalifa Al Mubarak.

“Through its free access to world-class music education programmes, we are creating opportunities for young people to discover their talent, develop their potential and experience the lifelong benefits that music can bring,” he continued.

“As Abu Dhabi continues to strengthen its position as a global centre for culture and creativity, and as a UNESCO Creative City of Music, institutions such as this ensure the next generation has every opportunity to participate in, contribute to and shape our cultural future, while preserving the rich musical traditions that form an important part of our heritage.”

Elsewhere in Abu Dhabi, construction is underway on Gehry’s Dar al Funoon Abu Dhabi, a performing arts venue cloaked in a rippling, fabric-like exterior.

The architect’s long-awaited Guggenheim Abu Dhabi is expected to open to the public on Saadiyat Island on 11 December.

As fall edges forward, strawberry patches can often look like they have finished their seasonal duties. The berries are long gone, creeping runners have tangled across walkways, and older foliage is beginning to bronze. It is tempting to simply walk away and leave the beds until spring. However, neglecting your patch in late autumn is one of the most common reasons for more disappointing crops the following year.

What happens to your strawberry plants in October has a massive impact on next year's crop. A few simple fall tasks now can help them get through winter in good shape, which directly leads to more berries that taste yummy next summer. Anyone who grows strawberries knows how much rides on the crown – the thick stem at the center of each plant, where new leaves and flowers start. It sits at the soil line, which makes it easy to damage in winter. Most of these fall jobs come back to protecting it.

Whether you cultivate June-bearers like Honeoye or Jewel, everbearing varieties like Ozark Beauty, or continuous day-neutral types like Albion, this next week is a critical window to protect strawbs. Time is short before the ground freezes solid. By taking a few quick steps now, you protect those delicate crowns and lay the foundation for a riot of sweet, juicy fruit next year. So don't skip these fall care essentials…

These strawberry tasks bridge the gap between active growing and total winter dormancy. Because strawberries have shallow root systems that rarely penetrate deeper than 6 inches (15cm) into soil, it’s good to check in with conditions both on and in the ground. Checking your patch's moisture, ambient temperature, and foliage health this month ensures plants enter dormancy with maximum energy reserves.

A steady cold at this time of year isn't always the biggest problem for strawberries. A sunny thaw followed by a hard freeze can heave plants right out of the soil and tear their roots. So where you live changes the following strawberry game plan a little bit. In colder USDA hardiness zones (zones 3-6), October is mostly about getting ready for winter and priming beds and containers before freezes strike. In warmer southern regions (zones 8-10) it can be a great time to plant new runners.

Strawberry care this month also varies according to whether you are growing in the ground or in containers. In-ground matted rows benefit from the relative continued warmth of surrounding earth, while strawberries grown in raised beds, terracotta jars, or vertical planting towers may face a tougher time now. Performing targeted checks on both border beds and container plants ensures that every strawberry crown remains healthy and protected through the cold months ahead.

Before you start these strawberry jobs, quickly check the soil with a good diagnostic tool like the budget-friendly Raintrip 4-in-1 Soil Meter from Amazon. This will help you to get a good sense of current root moisture and temperature levels ahead of any extra soil treatments or mulching extras. With that in hand, follow these simple steps…

One of the simplest ways to prime your strawberry plants is to quickly check for weathered, damaged or diseased foliage. This will eliminate overwintering pests and fungal pathogens before cold weather sets in. Walk your rows with sharp snips and trim away foliage that looks brown, decayed, or has reddish-purple spots (the telltale sign of strawberry leaf spot). Use spring-action snips like Fiskars Micro-Tip Pruning Snips, available from Amazon, or Fiskars SoftGrip Handle Bypass Floral Hand Pruning Shears, from The Home Depot, to make any smaller targeted cuts.

Don’t leave the clippings on the ground, as pests can still overwinter in the old growth. Rake up the trimmings and get them off the bed, placing them in the trash rather than your compost heap. However, avoid aggressive cutbacks. The healthy green leaves are still feeding the crown, and June-bearing plants set next year's flower buds as the days get shorter. Save hard shearing for post-harvest renovation.

Another part of tidying up strawberry beds involves timely weeding. Weeds that get settled in during fall are usually harder to get rid of in spring, and they will actively compete with strawberries for water and nutrients. Remember, your strawbs have shallow roots, so avoid deep hoeing or aggressive tilling. After a spell of rain, pull the weeds by hand. Damp soil lets go of roots more easily. Work methodically around each crown, taking care not to accidentally loosen or heave plants from the soil.

Strawberry plants continue producing runners right up until the first hard frost. This can be exciting in terms of the potential for new plants, However, left unchecked, excessive runners can drain energy away from the mother plant. In established rows of June-bearers, you can keep a few runners and direct select healthy daughter plants into bare gaps. Pin them down using landscape staples or small soil mounds. Once the daughter roots, snip the connection with the mother.

Keep an eye on plant density, though. Thin any excess plants so that the crowns sit roughly 6-8 inches (15-20cm) apart. For everbearing and day-neutral varieties, it’s best to snip off all fall runners completely. Repeat-blooming cultivars produce higher spring yields when all that late-season energy stays focused inside the crowns. Day-neutral types make far fewer runners, and in cold regions a lot of them won't survive winter, though mulching them might give them a chance.

In warmer zones 8-10, October can be a good time to plant bare-root crowns and plugs. Work a balanced feed into well-drained, slightly acidic soil. A good organic option is Espoma Organic Berry-tone Fertilizer, available in budget-friendly packs from The Home Depot to condition your beds. Set new plants 12 inches (30cm) apart. Make sure the crown is at soil level, as a buried crown can rot. Keep a frost cloth handy for vicious cold snaps. In colder zones, it’s best to plant in spring.

Fall weather often brings more rainfall, prompting many of us to abandon our watering cans. However, rain is rarely sufficient for shallow-rooted strawberry patches. Because strawberry roots are shallow, they can dry out rapidly during crisp, windy afternoons. Keeping strawberry crowns in dehydrated soil as they enter winter can stress plants and invite winter kill. New plants are especially vulnerable, since their roots haven’t spread far yet. This task is so easy to keep tabs on. Your plants just need one deep weekly soaking, roughly an inch (2.5cm) of water per week.

Water your strawberries early in the day so the foliage can dry before nighttime chills. Use a soaker hose or watering wand if you can, and aim for the base of the plants. Moist soil holds thermal heat better than bone-dry earth. Keep watering until the ground freezes. For a light nutrient boost, add a diluted seaweed drench every 2 weeks until the ground is frozen solid. A splash of Neptune’s Harvest Fish & Seaweed Organic 2-3-1 Fertilizer, available from Walmart, is a great root tonic to help see your strawbs through winter.

Winter mulching is vital for strawberry survival, but timing is everything. Mulching strawberries too early in October, while daytime temperatures remain mild, can trap warmth and moisture against the crowns. This seeming act of kindness can create more problems, preventing plants from hardening off and inviting crown rots. So wait until the plants have been through several hard frosts and the leaves start to look gray or red, which usually lines up with a few nights around 20-25°F (-4 to -7°C). This signals that plants are dormant.

Once the ground begins freezing, spread a loose, airy 3 to 4-inch (8-10cm) blanket of clean, weed-free straw (such as oat, wheat, or barley) or clean pine needles over the entire planting bed. Straw bales cover plenty of ground. You can grab some Bell Nursery Straw Bales from The Home Depot for an airy yet cozy blanket over wintering strawberry beds. Skip fallen leaves, which can mat down into a soggy blanket and trap moisture. Unprotected crowns can die at around 15°F (-9C). Just wait for a real cold snap before tucking everything under straw.

Container-grown strawberries face higher winter risks than those in beds. Pots don't give the roots much protection, and in a hard freeze the soil inside can get nearly as cold as the air. This can expose roots to brutal freeze-thaw cycles that can crack containers and kill sleeping strawberry crowns. So once your container plants enter full dormancy in late October, extra protection is necessary in zones 7 and colder.

The easiest fix is to carry the pots into an unheated garage or shed for the winter. As long as temperatures remain around 25-40°F (-4 to 4°C), they will be fine. If indoor shelter is unavailable, push the pots together against a sheltered, south-facing wall. Surround the outside pots with some burlap or insulating bubble wrap, and mound loose straw over the crowns.

Before tucking pots away for winter, water and refresh the upper layer of potting soil with some organic compost. For a simple soil refresher, add a sprinkle of Espoma Natural Mushroom Blend Compost, available from Walmart, to your containers. Top-dressing winterized containers will help to prime your strawbs for spring growth.

October strawberry care is all about thoughtful, timely preservation. By cutting away the worst of the leaves, managing trailing runners, keeping roots well watered, adding a timely straw mulch, and protecting container-based strawbs, you can shield your plants from the harshest winter weather. Tackle these simple tasks today, and you look forward to an abundance of homegrown berries next year.

#336. Over 75% Of European Pilots Surveyed Say They Fell Asleep In The Cockpit, And Many Fear Speaking Up

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

More than 3/4 European pilots responding to a new survey reported experiencing at least one “microsleep” in the cockpit during the preceding four weeks. That is alarming enough, but the concerns about reporting fatigue and refusing longer duties may even more troubling.

A new report by aviation consultancy Baines Simmons, commissioned by the European Cockpit Association (ECA), examines responses from 7,198 pilots across 32 countries. The survey was conducted in August 2026 and asked pilots about their experiences with fatigue, work schedules, and reporting systems.

A microsleep is a brief, involuntary episode of sleep. This is different from a planned rest period under an approved procedure. Of course the survey does not establish that both pilots were asleep simultaneously or that an aircraft was left without an alert pilot, like this case on ITA I wrote about.

We also need to describe the sample accurately. These are the experiences reported by the pilots who answered the survey, not proof that three-quarters of every European airline’s pilots are falling asleep at work. Nevertheless, nearly 7,200 responses warrant considerably more than a shrug.

> Read More: Both ITA Airways Pilots Fall Asleep On New York – Rome A330 Flight

The ECA’s summary highlights another concern: 24.2% of respondents said they usually do not report themselves unfit when fatigued and fly anyway.

Then there is “Commander’s Discretion,” which permits flight-duty extensions in exceptional circumstances. Some 38.3% reported using it at least once during the previous four weeks, while 69.1% expressed concern about negative consequences if they refused an extension.

ECA board director Captain Coen George put it this way:

“When pilots fear career consequences, fatigue goes unreported and the risk returns to the cockpit.”

That strikes me as the heart of the issue and potentially a much bigger problem than I imagined.

I want pilots to exercise good judgment, including recognizing when they should not fly. But an airline cannot reasonably expect honest reporting if employees believe that exercising that judgment will damage their careers. A reporting system can look excellent in a presentation and accomplish very little if the people expected to use it do not trust it…

I hope this was an honest service, but even a low number of fatigue reports would not necessarily reassure me. If pilots are reluctant to submit them, management may be measuring silence rather than safety.

I understand the operational consequences. A pilot declaring himself unfit can mean finding a replacement, delaying a flight, or cancelling it. Passengers miss connections, crews are displaced, and the airline incurs costs. Those are real problems, but problems an airline needs to be prepared to handle. The alternative cannot be relying on someone who believes he is too tired to operate safely.

I complain about delays frequently, particularly rolling delays that leave passengers unable to make sensible decisions about their day. In fact, I have a prime example I’ll share tomorrow that directly highlights this tension.

But as much as I hate delays and cancellations, I would never want a pilot to continue simply because passengers are impatient or management wants the aircraft moving. Pilots must use their rest opportunities responsibly and be honest about their fitness for duty. Airlines must build schedules that allow adequate recovery, which is a statutory requirement, but also make it possible to report a problem without intimidation.

A duty being legal should be the starting point for assessing it, rather than the end of the discussion.

One more thing. I would not use this survey to declare a particular airline (or flying in general) unsafe. It is based on self-reported experiences and was commissioned by an organization representing pilots. That context belongs in the story. It does not make the findings irrelevant, but neither does the report establish the circumstances of every episode or allow us to predict an accident.

What I take from it is a serious warning about the gap between fatigue policies and how pilots experience them in practice. I’d like to think that the minimum rest periods already enshrined in law are enough, but I’d welcome commentary from pilots as to whether you actually do feel well-rested before work, especially if you use your off-duty period as intended.

A survey of nearly 7,200 European pilots found widespread reports of cockpit microsleeps, inadequate recovery, and concern about the consequences of refusing extended duties.

The headline number is striking, but the lack of trust in fatigue reporting deserves just as much attention.

I want my flight to depart on time and I want the pilots to be fit to operate it considerably more. An airline that takes safety seriously should make it easy for a tired pilot to say so…and have a plan for what happens next.

Photographer ‘Almost Died’ After Police Hit Him With Grenade While Covering Paris Protests

A photographer, who was covering the Paris protests, reportedly almost bled to death after he was struck by a police grenade.

A 25-year-old photographer identified as Ahmed B was gravely injured by police during the demonstration in Paris, France, on October 17, according to a report by AFP. Over the last month, more than 400,000 people have been protesting across France over school funding and soaring living costs.

Ahmed had come to the French capital to document the demonstration when he was allegedly struck by a grenade shot by a police officer while taking photos at Place de la République. According to a complaint filed by lawyers on behalf of Ahmed, the police grenade struck the photographer in the femoral artery of his thigh.

The lawsuit alleges that Ahmed nearly bled to death at Place de la République as a result of the grenade and that police did not come to his aid.

“He almost died,” the photographer’s lawyer Me Arié Alimi tells AFP. “The police did not help him, letting him bleed out on the sidewalk.”

On Thursday, Alimi filed a civil lawsuit on Ahmed’s behalf alleging “attempted voluntary homicide” against the photographer. Ahmed was reportedly left seriously injured in the incident. The lawsuit accuses the police of deliberately causing serious physical harm to Ahmed, which has potentially left him permanently disabled or disfigured.

Following the lawsuit, the Paris public prosecutor’s office announced on Saturday that it was opening of an investigation entrusted to the General Inspectorate of the National Police (IGPN) into the law enforcement methods used against the photographer during the October 17 demonstrations.

The prosecutor’s office says it wanted to “prevent any loss of evidence,” and it opened the investigation for “violence by a person holding public authority, followed by mutilation or permanent disability, during a demonstration in a public place.”

The French government has deployed 70,000 security officials, including elite anti-terror forces, against protesters whose core demands have centered on providing more money for schools and help with the cost of living. Demonstrations have drawn more than half a million people, with some protests descending into violence and looting.

#3561. Photos reveal walkable Oxford Street as first phase of pedestrianisation begins

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Almost a kilometre of Oxford Street in central London has been permanently closed off to vehicle traffic as pedestrianisation of the major shopping street and thoroughfare began yesterday.

Nearly a kilometre of the two-kilometre-long street – the stretch between Orchard Street and Great Portland Street – is now fully pedestrianised.

Traffic, including cars, buses, taxis and private hire vehicles, cycles, scooters and pedicabs, will not be allowed on this part of Oxford Street and will instead be routed via side streets.

Photographs from the unveiling yesterday show the road decorated with a large sign reading “Oxford St” and green bollards keeping out traffic.

The design for the pedestrianisation was made by local architecture studio East, which is working with landscape architecture studio JL Gibbons and urban design studios Gehl and Publica on the project.

Meanwhile, architecture studio Hawkins\Brown has been developing a long-term vision for the entirety of the street, which stretches from Tottenham Court Road to Marble Arch.

This first pedestrianised section is now undergoing a transition period, during which the East team will add new signage, seating and planters to make the area more visitor-friendly. The full transformation is expected to begin in 2027 and be completed by 2028.

“As we have seen from Borough High Street to Eltham, from Croydon to Green Lanes, London’s high streets are the public face of the city in all its diversity, its greenery and its energy,” East director Julian Lewis said.

“We design to preserve and support this energy, maintaining the ebbs and flows of these places which make our neighbourhoods unique.”

The transformed section of Oxford Street will feature two new squares, one close to Holles Street and the other close to Duke Street. These will be marked with a new surface dressing that will make the ground level across the carriageway.

Trees will also be added in “sculptural pots”, East said, before being planted into the ground for the permanent scheme.

London mayor Sadiq Khan had originally announced plans to make Oxford Street traffic-free almost a decade ago, in 2017, but they were blocked by Westminster Council.

East revealed updated renders of its vision for the final scheme earlier this month.

#472. James Webb discovers a scorching lava planet with a “giant sunscreen”

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Astronomers have discovered surprising evidence that a scorching planet covered in molten rock may be protected by a reflective layer of clouds that acts like a giant sunscreen. Scientists previously believed that the planet's extreme conditions would make it nearly impossible for an atmosphere to survive, but new observations suggest otherwise.

An international team of researchers, including Dr. Anjali Piette, an astrophysicist at the University of Birmingham, reported the findings in The Astronomical Journal. Using the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), the scientists monitored a rocky exoplanet called TOI-561 b, located approximately 280 light-years from Earth. Over 37 hours, they observed nearly four complete orbits around its host star.

The observations indicate that TOI-561 b may be surrounded by a substantial atmosphere, although its conditions would be far too hostile for life as we know it. This atmosphere appears to help the planet reflect much of the radiation coming from its star. One possible explanation is a layer of highly reflective silicate clouds that prevents the surface from reaching the extreme temperatures expected on an exposed rocky planet. The atmosphere also appears to move heat from the permanently illuminated dayside toward the nightside, further reducing temperatures on the star-facing side.

A Lava Planet With an Atmosphere That Should Have Disappeared

Scientists still do not know exactly which gases make up the atmosphere of TOI-561 b. One possibility is a thick layer of carbon dioxide, resembling the atmosphere of Venus but under much hotter conditions. Another is an atmosphere dominated by sulfur dioxide, a gas released by volcanic activity on Jupiter's moon Io.

Dr. Anjali Piette, Assistant Professor in Astronomy at the University of Birmingham, said: "Our discovery challenges current theories. We would expect a heavily irradiated rocky planet like TOI-561 b to have lost its atmosphere billions of years ago.

"Instead, the evidence suggests it still has a significant atmosphere today. This raises major questions such as whether the planet started with an unusually large supply of gases or if the ocean of molten rock covering its surface, acts as a planet-wide volcano that is constantly feeding its atmosphere."

James Webb Reveals Why the Planet Is So Reflective

Earlier measurements revealed something puzzling about TOI-561 b. Its dayside was significantly cooler than researchers predicted for a dark, rocky planet orbiting so close to its star. That unexpected temperature suggested that much of the incoming energy was either being transferred to the nightside or reflected back into space before it could heat the surface.

To investigate, the team observed the planet throughout its orbit, allowing them to examine its nightside for the first time. Because the same side of TOI-561 b always faces its star, the nightside receives no direct starlight. However, the measurements showed that this darker hemisphere was not warm enough to explain all the missing heat.

The findings instead indicate that TOI-561 b reflects approximately 75% of the starlight reaching it, a remarkably high proportion comparable to Venus.

To determine what could produce such strong reflectivity, the researchers compared the telescope measurements with computer models of the planet's climate. Their simulations suggest that clouds containing silicate minerals, including silicon dioxide (SiO2) and magnesium silicate (MgSiO3), may form over the dayside. If present, these clouds could reflect incoming radiation before it reaches the surface, functioning as a natural sunscreen.

Professor Lisa Dang, who led the project at the University of Waterloo in Ontario, Canada, said: "We measured how the planet's brightness and heat changed throughout its orbit. This technique, known as a phase curve, revealed that the planet reflects a significant amount of light from its star, and that heat is being redistributed around the planet instead of only on the sunlit side. Together, these point to the presence of an atmosphere."

Could a Global Ocean of Lava Keep the Atmosphere Alive?

The new findings strengthen earlier evidence gathered by Professor Lisa Dang, Dr. Anjali Piette, and their collaborators that TOI-561 b has a thick atmosphere that may be continually replenished by gases escaping from its molten interior.

That possibility could help explain how the planet has maintained an atmosphere despite enduring intense radiation from its star for billions of years. Rather than simply losing its gases to space, the planet's vast reservoir of molten rock may be constantly releasing new material into the atmosphere.

Researchers now hope to determine whether TOI-561 b is more like an extremely hot Venus or a much more intense version of the volcanic moon Io. Additional observations with JWST at longer wavelengths could reveal important details about the atmosphere's chemical composition and help resolve the mystery of its long-term survival.

Professor Dang added: "The next big question we will seek to answer is whether TOI-561 b's atmosphere resembles Venus or an extreme version of lo. Observing the planet at longer wavelengths with JWST could give us insights and help explain how a planet this hot has held on to an atmosphere for billions of years."

#610. Scientists Discover Mysterious 150,000-Year-Old Bone From an Extinct Human Relative in Chinese Cave

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A search through more than 60,000 bone fragments uncovered the first confirmed Denisovan radius, a forearm bone offering rare clues to how these ancient human relatives were built.

A forearm fragment from a cave in southwest China combines features resembling those of Neanderthals and modern humans, yet it belonged to neither. Proteins preserved in the bone identify its owner as Denisovan, part of an ancient human group recognized through genetic evidence.

The fragment is the first, and so far only, confirmed Denisovan radius, one of the two long bones in the forearm. Denisovans are thought to have lived across much of Asia, but their fossil record is sparse, particularly for bones below the skull. That scarcity has left researchers with little evidence of the physical traits that helped them adapt to different environments.

The radius is among five remains from Bianfu Cave in Yunnan Province that a new study identified as Denisovan. Dating to the late Middle Pleistocene, the fossils fill a longstanding gap in the group’s known distribution in southwest China. Professor Qiaomei Fu of the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology (IVPP) of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) led the research in collaboration with multiple institutions. The findings were published in Nature on September 9.

Cataloged as BFD771, the radius fragment preserves the end nearest the elbow. Its large dimensions at that end resemble Neanderthal radii, as does the likely inward orientation of its radial tuberosity, the bony projection where the biceps muscle attaches. Its overall external shape, however, is closer to that of modern humans. So is the geometry of a cross section through the middle of its neck, the narrowed region near the elbow end. The researchers interpret this combination as evidence of distinctive mechanical demands and behavioral adaptations in Denisovans, differing from those inferred for Neanderthals and modern humans.

Two skull fragments, BFD767 and BFD769, provided another anatomical comparison. Both are pieces of parietal bones, which form part of the top and sides of the skull. Their skull vault thickness patterns most closely resemble those of Homo heidelbergensis and late Middle Pleistocene archaic Homo from East Asia.

Finding these bones required narrowing a collection of more than 60,000 fragments. Researchers increasingly use Zooarchaeology by Mass Spectrometry, or ZooMS, to screen large fossil collections by examining protein signatures. The technique requires minimally invasive sampling and is simple, fast, and relatively inexpensive. Even so, hominin fossil recovery rates at sites such as Denisova Cave and Baishiya Karst Cave have been just 0.1%. Applying ZooMS to every fragment from Bianfu Cave would have been costly and inefficient.

The team instead developed a “morphological pre-screening + ZooMS identification” strategy, first examining bone shapes to select 22 potential hominin remains. ZooMS then confirmed that the two parietal fragments and the radius fragment belonged to hominins, the group that includes humans and our extinct close relatives. The combined approach substantially improved screening efficiency and provides a repeatable method for searching other Paleolithic sites with abundant bone fragments.

BFD767 and BFD771 came from Layer 7, dated to approximately 148,000 to 134,000 years ago. That layer also contained four hominin teeth, two of which the researchers included in the study: a lower fourth premolar, YHB3518, and a lower second molar, YHB3075. The other skull fragment, BFD769, came from the older Layer 9, dated to approximately 167,000 to 150,000 years ago.

Identifying the bones as hominin remains did not reveal which ancient human group they belonged to. To find out, researchers analyzed proteins preserved in three bones and two teeth, recovering 6 to 16 proteins from each specimen. Protein fragments in the tooth enamel also indicated that both teeth belonged to males.

All five fossils carried the Denisovan-specific protein variant COL1A2 R996K. Additional variants in the teeth supported this identification and helped distinguish the fossils from other ancient human groups, including Homo erectus.

The researchers also constructed evolutionary trees using the recovered protein sequences. All five specimens clustered with the Denisovan specimen Denisova 3, with 100% posterior probability, providing strong support for their identification.

These findings make Bianfu Cave the richest known source of Denisovan fossils outside Denisova Cave. Dating to a glacial period known as Marine Isotope Stage 6, the remains suggest that the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau’s relatively warm climate and abundant food resources may have provided a favorable habitat for Denisovans.
