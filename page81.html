Buy now, pay later (BNPL) plans are loans, and when they’re spread across several apps, no one app shows everything you owe. Before you split another purchase, open every BNPL app you use, total what’s due in the next month, and budget that number like any other debt payment.

In the same week, split a $120 pair of sneakers, $280 headphones, and a $400 mattress into four payments each, and $200 leaves your checking account every two weeks, in three payments on dates set by three different apps.

In Consumer Financial Protection Bureau research on six major lenders, about 63% of BNPL borrowers in 2021 and 2022 held overlapping loans at some point each year, and a third had them at more than one company.

Pay-in-four usually charges no interest, so it’s easy to forget it’s borrowing, even if you’d never carry a credit card balance. Each plan is still a loan. On one credit card, those three purchases would share one statement and one due date. As BNPL plans, they’re three loans at three apps, and no app shows the other two.

Credit reporting is uneven. Affirm reports every new plan, late and missed payments included, to Experian and TransUnion, though that history doesn’t count in traditional credit scores yet. Klarna doesn’t report pay-in-four plans, and Afterpay doesn’t report to U.S. bureaus. FICO has built scores that include these loans, but FICO Score 8, the version lenders use most, isn’t one of them.

So keep the count yourself. List each remaining payment and its due date on one calendar, since every app collects on its own schedule. Then check that the account each app draws from will hold enough on every one of those dates.

Most BNPL lenders charge late fees, and an autopay that overdraws your checking account can add an overdraft fee from your bank. A return doesn’t pause the payments by itself. Afterpay keeps collecting until the store processes the refund, and Klarna pauses only once you report the return.

Your dispute rights are also weaker than a credit card’s. A card gives you a federal right to withhold payment on a disputed charge while the issuer investigates. A BNPL dispute follows the app’s own policy.

Use one rule at every checkout. If you couldn’t buy it outright today, splitting it into four doesn’t make it affordable.

Identifying these differences could make it possible to provide treatments tailored to the specific cause of each patient's illness.

In a study published in the New England Journal of Medicine, researchers examined more than 4,900 people with IBD and identified two important findings. First, some patients produce antibodies that interfere with interleukin-10 (IL-10), an essential protein that helps prevent excessive inflammation. Second, this autoimmune reaction explains how one of the strongest known genetic risk factors for IBD contributes to the disease.

Under normal circumstances, IL-10 acts as a chemical messenger between cells, helping the immune system regulate inflammation. However, certain antibodies can block its activity, effectively disabling one of the body's built-in safeguards against excessive immune responses. Without this protection, inflammation can persist and become difficult to control.

Hidden Antibodies Reveal a Cause of Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Inflammatory bowel disease includes Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, two chronic conditions that affect approximately 500,000 people in the UK and millions more around the world.

Symptoms often first appear during adolescence or early adulthood, and patients may face lifelong medical challenges. Managing the disease can involve repeated hospital visits, medications that suppress immune activity, and sometimes surgery. Although treatments have improved, many people must try several different therapies without achieving lasting relief. This places a substantial burden on patients and creates significant costs for healthcare systems.

The researchers discovered that approximately 3.5% of patients with IBD had elevated levels of antibodies that neutralize IL-10. These antibodies were detected in people with both Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, but not in healthy individuals.

Based on the prevalence of IBD in the UK, the researchers estimate that 15,000 to 20,000 patients nationwide could carry these harmful antibodies.

Scientists Solve a 30-Year Genetic Mystery

The investigation also revealed a strong association between the IL-10-blocking antibodies and a genetic variant called HLA-DRB1*01:03.

Scientists at Oxford first connected this genetic variant to a severe form of inflammatory bowel disease 30 years ago. However, the biological process linking the gene to the condition had remained unclear.

The new results help resolve that longstanding mystery. People who carry HLA-DRB1*01:03 are substantially more likely to develop antibodies that interfere with IL-10, providing an explanation for how this genetic risk factor can contribute to severe intestinal inflammation.

Professor Holm Uhlig, a pediatric gastroenterologist and Director of the Centre for Human Genetics at the University of Oxford's Nuffield Department of Medicine, was a senior author of the study.

"We've suspected an important role of interleukin 10 in patients with inflammatory bowel disease for decades. The study now provides clear evidence and contributes the missing link between a well-known genetic variant that had been linked to severe inflammatory bowel disease in the past and the very recently discovered autoimmunity to interleukin 10."

"Understanding what drives the inflammation, provides a clear explanation for disease in this group of people and opens the door to new treatments that target the autoantibodies themselves or cells that produce those autoantibodies."

How Earlier Discoveries Helped Explain Severe IBD

The findings build on more than a decade of research into rare inherited disorders that disrupt the body's ability to control inflammation.

Sophie Hambleton, Professor of Pediatrics and Immunology at Newcastle University, explained that studying these uncommon conditions provided important clues about the mechanisms involved in more widespread forms of IBD.

"This discovery shows how the study of rare, inherited disorders can shed new light on common conditions. The groundwork was laid over 10 years ago when genetic defects in IL-10 or its receptor were found in young children with severe IBD. Later, we realized that neutralizing autoantibodies against IL-10 were mimicking this effect in 2 little girls with colitis."

These earlier observations helped researchers recognize that antibodies blocking IL-10 could produce effects similar to inherited defects involving the same protein or its receptor. The latest study now establishes the importance of this mechanism in a larger group of patients with inflammatory bowel disease.

A Blood Test Could Lead to More Targeted IBD Treatments

Beyond explaining a previously mysterious cause of intestinal inflammation, the discovery could have important implications for patient care.

Researchers believe the results provide a foundation for developing a blood test that identifies people whose disease is associated with antibodies against IL-10. Such a test could help doctors recognize these patients earlier and select treatments that address the underlying immune malfunction.

Dr. Rainer Doffinger, a clinical immunologist at Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust, highlighted the potential benefits for both patients and healthcare systems.

"By identifying patients early and giving them targeted treatment, we could reduce reliance on expensive ongoing therapy and prevent complications. Even modest reductions in biologic treatment, admissions and surgery could translate into savings of many millions of pounds annually for the NHS, alongside the major benefits for patients through fewer interventions, improved quality of life or even durable remission."

Targeting the antibodies themselves, or the immune cells responsible for producing them, could offer a new treatment strategy for this particular group of patients. The approach could potentially reduce the need for repeated therapies, hospital admissions, and surgery while improving the chances of sustained disease control.

Professor Simon Travis, Professor of Clinical Gastroenterology at the University of Oxford and the Kennedy Institute of Rheumatology, emphasized the significance of identifying a specific biological cause of IBD.

"This is the most exciting discovery in a lifetime specialising in IBD. It means that we can now identify a group where we know what is causing the disease and that creates a real opportunity to change how we manage this disease."

The research marks an important advance toward personalized treatment for inflammatory bowel disease. Rather than relying primarily on symptoms to guide medical decisions, doctors may eventually be able to use biological testing to determine what is driving inflammation in individual patients and choose more appropriate therapies.

The study involved researchers from the Nuffield Department of Medicine at the University of Oxford, Newcastle University's Translational and Clinical Research Institute, and the Department of Immunology at Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust.

#4533. Humanity can't afford not to spend $800B on AI, Anthropic insists

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It's more important than chips, 'leccy and the net, after all

Anthropic's IPO filing dropped on Monday morning, and the prospectus reads like a real estate agent desperately trying to sell a house of cards as an investment property:

The upsides are enormous. There are just a few disclosures you should be aware of. Sure, the foundation is cracked; it's in a flood plain, and you can only really afford it if you land that promotion, but there's a non-zero chance it'll work out eventually, and just look at the views.

Every S1's risk factors read a bit like a doomsday prophecy. Anthropic, like any other company that's going to trade on public markets, has to cover its butt. It doesn't change the fact that the company's success is built on a gamble that its revenues will grow fast enough to keep up with its rampant compute spend, for which its obligations now exceed half a trillion dollars over the next decade or so.

Going off the S1 filing's numbers, things don't look that bad for the Claudefather at first blush. Revenues in the first half of 2026 outpaced compute and infrastructure costs by nearly 2:1 and operating losses were a relatively paltry $693 million, compared with $2.14 billion in the first half of last year. The company is still burning money, but things are finally headed in the right direction.

Or, they would be if the company's compute spend were forecast to plateau over the next few years. That's very much not the case.

"Our ability to develop, train, improve, and deploy frontier models depends on our access to enormous and increasing quantities of specialized compute," the prospectus reads.

The continued development of Anthropic's models hinges on ever larger quantities of compute. Anthropic hasn't put a firm number on its compute commitments involving large cloud providers, neoclouds, and equipment providers, but the company's obligations over the next few years could be north of $800 billion, depending on how you wrangle the numbers.

According to the S-1, Anthropic entered into new or amended compute and infrastructure agreements after December 31, 2025, carrying roughly $417 billion in future minimum payment obligations. Since June 30, 2026, the company has entered into further new or amended agreements carrying approximately $245 billion in future minimum payment obligations, some of which are subject to contractual conditions.

But if you add up all the contracts listed, the total is even higher.

TPUs from Google and other providers: "approximately $125B to $150B"— 2028-2031

AMD: "to exceed" $20B — initial tranche starting in 2027

Nscale: "up to approximately $44.6B" — through 2033

Lambda: "approximately $41.3B" — through 2034

Broadcom: "approximately $161.2B" — starting in 2026

That's a total obligation between $782 billion and $807 billion, though some aren't as airtight as others. According to the S1, Anthropic's deals with Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Broadcom are non-cancellable. In fact, the contracts with Microsoft, Google, and Amazon dictate payment regardless of usage.

Similarly, Anthropic's only way out of its contracts with Nscale and Lambda, which extend through 2033 and 2034 respectively, is if they fail to deliver on their commitments on time or breach them.

Anthropic does have some flexibility with regard to its widely reported collab with Elon Musk's SpaceXAI to lease capacity of its Colossus supercomputer in Memphis. The deal, worth roughly $120 billion through 2029, can be canceled with just 90 days' notice by either party beginning in early 2027. Meanwhile, Anthropic's agreement with AMD is expected to cost more than $20 billion for the initial tranche of compute capacity, provided it procures the full amount, but this can be satisfied by leasing capacity from the likes of Microsoft.

And while the timetable for Anthropic to meet those obligations remains unclear — several fields relevant to this remain blank at this time — it's safe to say the company's growth trajectory will need to be astronomical to keep up with its rampant spending, let alone ever turn a profit.

This spend is all predicated on the idea that more compute equals better, more desirable models that will grow the customer base and support higher-margin products, which is why the company is telling investors now that it's going to keep signing and amending compute contracts. At this rate, we wouldn't be surprised if the model dev's obligations through 2040 exceeded a trillion dollars before year's end.

Whether Anthropic will ever actually make good on those commitments is another matter entirely. "Our ability to meet the substantial and growing financial obligations of these requirements to secure compute depends on our ability to generate significant revenue growth," the filing reads.

If Anthropic's revenues can't keep up with its compute commitments, it could end up defaulting, which it notes could result in a cascade that takes the entire house of cards down with it.

Anthropic does have some backstops should it run into trouble paying its bills on its own. For example, "we have the ability to issue, if needed, up to $42.0 billion in aggregate principal amount of convertible promissory notes to Broadcom," the company's S1 notes. However, this only applies to Anthropic's commitments to Broadcom.

The company could also turn to equity or debt financing to pay its other debts. But if it can't make revenue targets, the company warns that doing so risks diluting shares and/or taking on additional debt on unfavorable terms.

Worse, Anthropic's biggest compute providers are not only some of its biggest customers, but also its biggest competitors. We're talking major cloud providers like Google, Amazon, and Microsoft, which are far larger and actually profitable.

This poses a different problem for Anthropic. Cloud providers are convenient because Anthropic doesn't have to pay for the compute up front. But if the company can't get adequate compute from Google, Amazon, or Microsoft because they're using it for training or running their own or someone else's models, they may need to look at alternative options, including building out their own infrastructure.

"We may in the future finance a greater portion of our compute on an up-front basis, including through capital leases, prepayments, debt-financed purchases of equipment, or similar arrangements," the company explained.

In fact, to some degree Anthropic is already doing this through its lease deal with Broadcom. This appears to be tied to Google and Broadcom's collaboration with Anthropic to provide TPU-based compute earlier this year. Meta was also reportedly in talks to deploy Google TPUs in its own datacenters so the arrangement wouldn't be that unusual.

And then of course there are external threats, like conflict in the South China Sea severing supply chains for the vast majority of AI accelerators.

The only way to justify this kind of spend and risk is if you believe the upside is enormous. Like, the biggest economic transformation in the history of humankind.

As Anthropic puts it: "We believe this abundance of AI will rapidly transform every sector of the global economy in ways greater than the impact of industrialization, electricity, semiconductors, and the internet." ®
