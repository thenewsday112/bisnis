📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Why do we "fall back" in November? We answer your questions.

Daylight saving time ends this weekend, with the clocks officially changing at 2 a.m. on Sunday. While it’ll still be light enough for trick-or-treating on Halloween, the time change will hit the day after, when most of the US adjusts back one hour.

The time shift is notorious for disrupting sleep patterns and schedules. Some politicians are advocating for the abolition of time changes and the permanent adoption of daylight saving time, or DST. The House passed the Sunshine Protection Act in July, but it still needs Senate approval and the president’s signature to become law — meaning that, for now, we still have dark winter hours coming.

If you, too, dislike how early it gets dark each day through winter, just remember that brighter evenings will return in mid-March.

Daylight saving time in the US ends at 2 a.m. local time on Sunday. It will return at 2 a.m. local time on March 14, 2027. The end of DST is when we “fall back,” whereas in March, we “spring forward.”

The US isn’t alone in observing daylight saving time. Check out this list of other countries that use DST, along with their start and end dates.

The official US time website shows where time zone boundaries are located.

The exact start dates for daylight saving time and standard time float a little. DST kicks off on the second Sunday of March and ends on the first Sunday of November with the return of standard time.

We spend about eight months of the year in DST. We can thank the Uniform Time Act of 1966 for bringing some order to what had been a complicated history of time changes in the US.

“Motivated by transportation improvements, this act mandated standard time within the existing time zones and established a permanent system of uniform DST, including the dates and times for twice-yearly transitions,” the Bureau of Transportation Statistics states in a history of time zones.

The country’s time zones date to the railroad boom of the late 1800s. DST formally entered the chat in 1918, but its application was inconsistent until 1966. The country tried year-round daylight saving time in January 1974 under President Richard Nixon to address the energy crisis. It didn’t go over well. Congress and President Gerald Ford restored standard time in October 1974.

If you truly hate time changes, then consider moving to Arizona (except for the Navajo Nation) or Hawaii. Those states are on standard time all year long, and their residents don’t have to deal with the physical and mental health impacts of the change. Puerto Rico, Guam, the Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands also skip DST.

Read more: Why Is NASA Working on a Time Zone for the Moon?

Organizations such as the National Sleep Foundation, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Society for Research on Biological Rhythms are united in calling for permanent standard time, saying it’s better for human biology.

“There’s a mismatch between the outside world and our internal clocks during daylight saving time that can result in some negative health consequences,” says Joseph Dzierzewski, senior vice president of research and scientific affairs at the National Sleep Foundation.

Dzierzewski cites mental health concerns and upticks in cardiovascular events and drowsy driving. He also calls out safety issues with young children walking to school or sitting at bus stops in the dark. Those safety issues were a big reason why permanent DST didn’t last in 1974.

Time changes aren’t popular. An October 2024 YouGov poll (PDF) of more than 1,100 US adults found that 63% would like to see the changing of the clocks eliminated. Only 17% wanted to keep time changes, and 20% weren’t sure.

Efforts to end the time changes have so far failed to pass into law. The bipartisan Sunshine Protection Act, which would have made daylight saving time permanent, passed the Senate in 2022 — the new version of the bill passed the House earlier this year and was sent on to the Senate to review in July.

While many people agree on eliminating time changes, the schism between daylight saving time and standard time remains. Standard time may have a bit of an image issue compared to DST.

“Part of the issue is that people associate daylight saving time with summer. People love summer, right?” Dzierzewski said. “But the simple fact of the matter is, it would still be summer if we were on permanent standard time.”

President Donald Trump criticized time changes in a 2024 Truth Social post: “The Republican Party will use its best efforts to eliminate Daylight Saving Time, which has a small but strong constituency, but shouldn’t! Daylight Saving Time is inconvenient and very costly to our Nation.”

Bills and resolutions at the state level haven’t had real-world impacts yet. Politicians are still split on whether they want permanent daylight saving or standard time. Dzierzewski is hopeful that permanent standard time will win out.

“This is the first time in several years where I’ve seen more legislation introduced at a state level in support of permanent standard time as the solution,” he said.

If you’d like to see time changes end and want to advocate for either daylight saving or standard time, contact your state and national representatives and express your opinion.

Daylight saving time is happening, so now it’s a matter of figuring out how to deal with it. It’s not just about the day of the time change.

“You’re in a better situation to cope with the effects of the clock change if you have good sleep health generally,” Dzierzewski said. He recommends exposure to bright light in the morning, physical activity during the day, eating meals at regular times and practicing a relaxing wind-down routine at night.

The majority of Americans struggle to get the National Sleep Foundation’s recommended 7 to 9 hours of sleep for most adults. A CNET survey found that more than half of US adults use some method to cope with sleep-related challenges. Check out some expert-backed tips for improving your sleep quality.

“If you’re doing these healthy sleep behaviors, you’re probably more resilient to some of these changes,” Dzierzewski said. “If you have poor sleep to begin with, you’re probably going to feel it a little bit more.”

Establishing a better sleep routine can take time. There are two things you can do in direct response to the time change. Dzierzewski suggests adjusting your bedtime and wake-up time gradually ahead of the change. For example, you could shift your schedule by 15 minutes per day and ease yourself into DST. He also recommends getting a good dose of bright morning light on the day of the time change to help set your internal body clock.

Think of DST as another kind of wake-up call, one that can motivate you to evaluate your sleep routine and quality and make positive changes. Start with these six simple habits. And don’t forget to change your clocks. Do it the night before, and you won’t have to worry about it later.

#3577. "Things start in really simple gestures" says Es Devlin of her Design Museum retrospective

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

British artist and designer Es Devlin explains how ambitious creative projects begin with simple ideas in this exclusive video interview with Dezeen, filmed at Es Devlin: Other Worlds, her first European retrospective at London’s Design Museum.

“This is literally my favourite project. A book which took me seven years to make and it contains everything I’ve ever made,” Devlin told Dezeen, referring to a companion book created to coincide with the exhibition.

The book, as well as the exhibition, brings together three decades of Devlin’s work across theatre, opera, concert stage design and large-scale public installations.

The exhibition explores how Devlin’s projects develop, from early drawings and models to monumental installations experienced by audiences around the world.

In the interview, she explains how she hopes the presentation will demystify the creative process and encourage visitors to pursue their own ideas.

“I really hope that when people leave this exhibition, they’ll see objects and traces of things which make them say, ‘Oh, I could do that’,” she said.

“It’s not a mystery. It doesn’t come by magic. It comes step by step, with attention, curiosity, work and collaboration.”

The exhibition begins with a replica of Devlin’s studio in Peckham, complete with her books, objects, models, drawings, tools and paints.

“I love visiting other artists and designers’ studios,” she said. “I think it’ll make people feel something about what these rooms feel like, where creativity is allowed to happen at all times.”

From the studio, visitors encounter Come Home Again, Devlin’s installation originally presented outside Tate Modern in 2022.

The original work featured illuminated cut-outs of endangered wildlife arranged within a cross-section of the dome of St Paul’s Cathedral.

Reconfigured beneath a mirrored ceiling for the Design Museum exhibition, it is followed by A 30-Year Desk, a series of concentric circular desks displaying archival objects, drawings and models from across her career.

The archive includes material relating to her designs for the 2018 theatre production The Lehman Trilogy, the Forest of Us installation in Miami and the closing ceremony of the London 2012 Olympic Games, alongside more than 30 models of projects that have previously been exhibited in Britain.

The exhibition culminates in An Infinite Library, an eight-metre-wide rotating sculpture containing more than 2,000 books that have shaped Devlin’s worldview.

The artwork was originally installed on the beachfront of the Faena Beach hotel in Miami Beach and has been reimagined for the Design Museum retrospective.

Arranged in bands of colour above a circular mirror, the books are illuminated by projections and accompanied by recordings of Devlin reading selected passages.

Devlin began her career designing sets for small-scale theatre productions in 1996 before moving into opera, dance and concert stage design. Over the past decade, she has increasingly focused on large-scale public artworks and installations.

For Es Devlin: Other Worlds, she brought together material spanning her career, including childhood experiments and paintings made shortly before the exhibition opened, to reveal the ideas and processes behind her finished projects.

“I began to develop an attitude on how to take things that are amassed in piles and are somewhat overwhelming, to spread them out along a timeline and make them digestible and legible to an audience,” she said.

“Actually, these things that seem huge and overwhelming and complicated, they start in really simple gestures.”

Es Devlin: Other Worlds runs at the Design Museum in London until 4 April 2027.

InfoQ Homepage News Cloudflare and VoidZero Introduce tsrs: An AI-Generated Rust Port of TypeScript

Oct 27, 2026 2 min read

tsrs, a Rust port of the TypeScript compiler, has been announced by Cloudflare, which is developing it with VoidZero and Max Schwenk of Owner.com, incubating the project inside the Oxc toolchain and working in close collaboration with the TypeScript team. Cloudflare is emphatic that this is not a fork, with the TypeScript team retaining full control over the language's design.

Cloudflare's benchmark comparing tsrs against TypeScript 7.0 at the same number of checker workers

The headline numbers are a claimed up to 8x faster type checking and around 70% less memory than TypeScript 7.0 at the same number of checker workers. Rust also unlocks the use case of compiling to WebAssembly through @tsrs/wasm so checks can run in a browser or on Workers, without dragging a garbage collector into the bundle the way a Go binary does.

To try, developers simply point it at an existing tsconfig and run:

In a detailed write-up, Schwenk explains he wrote "zero" code, instead directing agents led by Claude Opus 5.5 to produce a faithful port, using TypeScript's own 15,197 conformance cases as the spec. On Owner's 37,000-file monolith, a check that needed 19 GiB and 80 seconds on TypeScript 7.0 dropped to 7 seconds.

One developer benchmarked both npm packages and measured 5.3x faster checking and 72% less memory, with identical diagnostics and byte-identical emit, but spotted that tsrs exits with code 2 on type errors where tsc exits 1, enough to quietly break a CI script. Measuring against the newer TypeScript 7.1 beta rather than 7.0, ImportStatic found a smaller 2.7x gain, a reminder that the 8x headline is measured against the older baseline.

TypeScript's Daniel Rosenwasser responded that much of the speed "does not just come down to language choice," and that some optimisations discovered in tsrs are feeding into a leaner TypeScript 7.1. Peter Steinberger reported on X:

Moved to tsrs over the weekend. Drop-in replacement. Around 5x faster for OC.

Kind of amazing that it took Microsoft a team and two years to rewrite the TS compiler in go, and now you can just let an agent rip and get sth significantly better within weeks.

Others were wary, questioning whether the arena-based, never-freed memory model is sound.

tsrs sits alongside Microsoft's own Go-based compiler, known as tsgo, and Theo Browne's rival Rust port, tsc-rs. Rather than compete, Cloudflare is merging tsc-rs and tsrs into a single package, adding language server and content mapper support.

The release remains experimental. There is no watch mode or language server yet, --init and --showConfig are unsupported, and diagnostic order can differ, the compatibility notes provide more details. Because tsrs tracks TypeScript 7, teams still on TypeScript 5 or 6 should first adopt Microsoft's native Go port before swapping in tsrs, after which migrating is as simple as reusing the existing tsconfig.

tsrs is open source under Apache-2.0 and available on GitHub.
