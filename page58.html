#4186. What Should a Dog’s Name Sound Like?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Perhaps the most important sound that a dog can learn is its name. Our domestic dogs live in a sea of spoken human language from which they must pick out the sounds that are relevant to them. Thus, if you happen to say to your spouse or housemate, “Come over here and sit down,” how is your pet dog able to know that you haven’t just issued three commands aimed at them, namely: “Come!”; “Sit!”; and “Down!”? The answer is that, if a dog has been correctly trained, they understand that sounds that are relevant to them are usually preceded by their name. Thus, the specific canine syntax that the dog is looking for is “Lassie come!”; “Lassie sit!”; or “Lassie down!”

Some new research from Eötvös Loránd University in Hungary suggests that all dog names are not equal. Certain linguistic sounds seem to have the ability to capture canine attention better and allow them to pick out their name from the flow of language more easily.

Human speech is a continuous stream. For example, say:

out loud. Notice that there are no gaps between the written words, but as you speak these sounds, your brain adds the breaks between the words and syllables. This is a process known as speech segmentation, which is used to identify the boundaries between words or phonemes in continuous spoken language. In normal written text, we help the process by using spaces to separate words more easily and clearly.

Similar to that written example, most often human spoken speech is a continuous audio signal without specific pauses between the words. You can see this from the speech spectrogram below (where the sound frequencies are on the vertical axis and time is measured on the horizontal axis). This particular recording represents a measure of the sounds produced by a person speaking the phrase “I owe you.” Notice that although we would hear this as three distinct words, there are no explicit physical markers or gaps in the actual audio signal. The psychological question is “Which aspects of the stream of sound allow us to pick out the specific words?”

An important clue comes from the fact that words are made up of two major types of speech sounds, namely vowels and consonants. These are treated differently by the brain. Despite the fact that vowels are louder and more noticeable, you can say that consonants generally form the skeleton of words. So, if we have an uninterrupted stream of speech sounds, it is easier to detect specific words when we concentrate our attention on consonants. This strategy, called the “consonant bias,” is what human brains have adopted, and this mechanism is apparent even in infancy.

A recent report by a team of researchers led by Boglárka Morvai (a biologist) and Kinga Tóth (a psychologist), with the cognitive neuroscientist Attila Andics as the corresponding author, attempted to determine if dogs have a similar consonant bias when listening to human speech sounds. This study used 20 adults and their companion dogs. The participants (both human and dog) separately listened to a stream of speech made up of nonsense words while they were connected to an EEG monitor that measured the activity of their brains.

The signal the participants were listening to was a continuous flow of contrived three-syllable sounds. There were no pauses to indicate where one meaningless “word” ended and the next began. Three types of language streams were used. In one, consonant patterns formed the repeating three-syllable words while the vowels changed; in another, the vowels repeated and the consonants changed, while in the third there was no repetitive pattern.

The researchers were looking for a phenomenon called “neural synchronization,” which occurs when brain activity takes on the rhythm that we detect in the sounds we hear. The more clearly we detect the cadence or tempo of the words, the more distinctly that pattern appears in the electrical brain response.

The differences in responses to the speech signals were quite significant. In humans, the response to the consonant-patterned words was around 25 percent higher than to the vowel-based rhythm. Dogs showed a similar consonant bias, with their neural responses around 42 percent stronger for consonant-structured words compared to vowel-structured words.

An interesting additional finding was that human brains could also track words built from vowel patterns, but considerably less strongly than the consonant-based words. However, dog brains did not show a similar response to vowels. This was interpreted by the researchers as indicating that people may simply be better at detecting unexpected or atypical patterns of speech.

All of this brings us back to the matter of which sounds might be best for a dog’s name. It is important for the dog to be able to detect its name from whatever flow of speech sounds are going on around him. Taking the results of this new research at face value, it would seem to suggest that it would be more efficient and effective if you gave your dog a name with more consonant sounds in it than one with more vowels. Therefore, names like Ruby, Wendy, Merlin, or Barrett would appear to be preferable to names like Ava, Iris, Arlo, or Owen. At least such consonant-rich names would be easier for a dog to distinguish or detect when they are embedded in the ongoing stream of human speech. Of course, whether your dog actually responds to the commands that they hear associated with their name is not just a matter of whether they can hear and recognize what is said, but it is also a matter of how well you trained them.

#1709. Consumers Hold the Mic: Fanta Gives Them Something to Talk About

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For decades, marketers could afford to build a brand around one big piece of communication. Today, consumers move across retail, social, entertainment, search, physical experiences and, increasingly, AI-powered environments.

The challenge now is knowing what about the brand should stay fixed, and what needs to change with the culture around it.

In this episode of The Speed of Culture, Ibrahim Khan, global VP of marketing at Fanta, joins Matt Britton to unpack this dichotomy. From adapting occasions, and packaging across markets to building Fanta’s Haunted Universe for Halloween, Ibrahim explains why strong global brands protect their fundamentals while constantly reinventing how people experience them.

He also makes the case that marketers need to get over the “hangover of the TV era” and start treating the product, retail experience, live experience, and consumer conversation as part of one connected brand system.

Ibrahim Khan leads strategy for Fanta, a global beverage brand with $18 billion in retail value. His career spans nearly two decades across major consumer brands, including Coca-Cola, Sprite, and Fanta, with experience across Pakistan, Thailand, Southeast Asia, and global markets. His approach to marketing is grounded in one fundamental truth: Marketing still has to drive the business.

[00:54] Brand Truth Needs New Expression — Ibrahim argues that the fundamental reason people choose a product rarely changes dramatically from market to market. What does change is how people experience it: language, channels, daily routines, cultural habits, and consumption occasions. For a global marketer, consistency does not mean executing the same way everywhere. It means knowing which parts of the brand are non-negotiable, and giving local markets room to express them differently.

[03:54] Assumptions Are the Enemy of Global Marketing — Cultural relevance can materially change business strategy. Ibrahim points to beverage consumption with food as an example: that occasion is less developed in parts of Asia than in Western markets, leading Fanta to lean more heavily into leisure occasions there. Even visual conventions differ. An in-store environment that initially felt overwhelming to him turned out to match what consumers in that market expected. The marketer’s instinct to “simplify” is not automatically universal.

[08:07] Not Every Cultural Moment Needs Your Brand — Ibrahim is disciplined about cultural participation: “not every festivity needs a brand, and not every brand needs a festivity.” Fanta leans into Halloween because indulgence, fun, and soft-drink consumption already belong naturally within the occasion. His warning to marketers is equally clear. Chasing passion points or trends where the product has no authentic role can make the work feel forced instead of relevant.

[09:39] Build a Platform, Not Just Another Annual Campaign — Fanta has spent years building around Halloween, but Ibrahim says the ambition has now moved beyond year-to-year collaborations. Haunted Universe is designed as an expandable world of characters, stories, lore, partnerships, and experiences that can develop over many years. Fanta researched horror archetypes across cultures and fandoms before entering the space, treating entertainment IP as something to build deliberately rather than a seasonal advertising stunt.

[15:05] AI Can Scale the World Without Replacing Human Taste — Ibrahim sees technology as an enabler of bigger creative worlds, much as advances in animation and graphics changed entertainment before it. But he draws a firm distinction between capability and authorship. Marketing contains art, taste, ethics, and judgment that he still sees as inherently human. For Fanta’s Halloween work, AI supports an effort shaped by a large group of people rather than replacing the people doing the creative thinking.

[25:06] Marketing Needs to Get Over the TV Hangover — Twenty or thirty years ago, Ibrahim says, marketers could shape most brand perception around a 30-second TV commercial. He believes that success has made the industry lazy. Today, the product itself needs to become a marketing channel, and marketers need to think across the full offer rather than obsessing over communication alone. His sharpest point: consumers now hold the microphone. If they are not talking about what the brand is doing, the old tools cannot manufacture the same relevance they once could.

#4400. CISA confirms PHP2Shell under attack as researchers clock successful exploits

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Both critical PHP flaws land on the agency’s exploited list as infosec pros confirm hackers are getting working shells

The US government's cybersecurity watchdog has confirmed attackers are exploiting both critical PHP2Shell vulnerabilities, giving federal agencies three days to patch the bugs that have wreaked havoc across the web over the past week.

CISA added CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619 to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog on Monday, setting an October 29 deadline for federal civilian agencies to address the flaws. The move marks a shift from last week, when the agency warned of the danger posed by PHP2Shell but said it had not yet observed successful exploitation in the wild.

Both bugs are rated 9.2 on the CVSS scale and can be exploited without authentication to run code on vulnerable PHP servers. That's bad news given how much of the web still relies on PHP, and just how many sites could be left exposed.

Patches landed on October 21, but attackers had already beaten the maintainers to it. AWS spotted attempts to exploit the flaws as early as October 17, four days before the bugs were publicly disclosed.

CISA's confirmation comes amid growing evidence that attackers are doing more than simply knocking on the door.

On October 24, Perplexity security researcher Adel Karimi recorded 35 successful shell executions across three honeypots running PHP 8.4.26 under Apache mod_php. The attacks came from two IP addresses and used memory exhaustion and disclosure to achieve remote code execution.

The attackers then tried to contact external servers using PHP, curl, and wget, although no additional malware was successfully downloaded. Karimi's findings were consistent with exploitation of CVE-2026-109618, but he found no evidence that the second PHP2Shell flaw was involved. He also warned that the honeypots used a favorable single-worker configuration, so the results don't necessarily reflect how reliably the exploit works against production servers.

Still, attackers appear to be giving it a good go. Delft University's Threat Intelligence Lab has tracked more than 50,000 PHP2Shell exploitation requests from over 600 IP addresses across 70 countries. Of those, 171 addresses went beyond probing for vulnerable servers and started sending exploit code or malware droppers.

CISA's confirmation also comes after some of the earlier reports of successful PHP2Shell attacks turned out to be rather premature. Sophos walked back its initial assessment after determining that the activity it observed amounted to scanning and failed attempts, while Palo Alto Networks withdrew claims linking the vulnerabilities to an intrusion at a European government agency.

CISA has not disclosed which organizations have been compromised or provided details of the attacks behind its decision to add the flaws to KEV. The listing confirms that the agency considers both vulnerabilities actively exploited, but it does not confirm how many production systems have been successfully breached.

For federal agencies, the clock is now ticking. CISA is also urging organizations outside the federal government to prioritize the fixes, although the deadline does not apply to them.

Admins should update to PHP 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36, or 8.2.35, depending on their release branch. Those running older, unsupported versions face the added headache of upgrading or obtaining vendor-supported fixes, while systems that cannot be secured should be taken offline until the risk is addressed.

And with CISA now officially acknowledging exploitation, anyone still waiting for more proof before patching may be waiting a little too long. ®

#3801. Google May Use Your Google Photos Data to Target Ads

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google May Use Your Google Photos Data to Target Ads

Google says it may use data from Google Photos to target ads to users who have certain personalized AI features enabled.

The change introduces an exception to Google’s existing policy on personalized advertising. Previously, the company said it would not use content from Google Drive, Gmail, or Google Photos to personalize ads. However, an update that took effect on October 16 adds an exception for users who choose to make data from these services available to other Google services that support personalized advertising.

Google’s policy does not mean that the company automatically uses everyone’s private emails and photos to serve ads. The default restriction remains in place, but users who explicitly connect their personal data to certain Google services may be affected by the change.

Specifically, people who connect Google Drive, Gmail, or Google Photos to AI Mode and other Search tools through Google’s Personal Intelligence feature may see ads informed by that content. Personal Intelligence allows Google’s AI tools to provide more relevant responses and recommendations by drawing on information about individual users.

Users who want to prevent their personal data from being used for targeted ads can adjust their Google Account settings. According to Android Authority, they can do this by navigating to “Data & privacy”, selecting “Personalization settings” under “Personalization & activity,” and choosing “Manage Personalized Recommendations in Search Services.” Under “Additional Personal Intelligence,” they can then find “Connected Content apps.” They can then disconnect Google Workspace and Google Photos to prevent Google Search from accessing content from those services.

Google says advertisers do not receive access to users’ Drive, Gmail, or Google Photos content, or to personally identifying information such as their names and email addresses.

#652. Scientists discover the Arctic has three distinct climate zones

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

New research reveals three distinct Arctic atmospheric zones, each responding differently to warming, with major implications for future climate projections.

A vast new aerosol dataset covering two decades and eight monitoring stations has revealed that the Arctic is not a single climate system but three distinct zones, each responding to warming in its own way.

The Arctic is warming roughly four times faster than the global average.

What almost no one has worked out until now is that “the Arctic” is doing at least three different things at once.

A recent study draws on over 20 years of aerosol measurements from eight monitoring stations scattered across the Arctic.

The measurements were combined with satellite data and AI-assisted mapping, to divide the Arctic into three zones with distinct atmospheric “personalities.”

In one, warming could increase the number of particles that help clouds form. In another, those particles could become less abundant.

For the third zone, the answer depends almost entirely on what happens at factories and in forests thousands of kilometers away.

The research was led by Samuel De Xun Chua at the University of Helsinki. The team’s measurements were collected between 2003 and 2024.

Clouds need seeds. The seeds are aerosol particles of just the right size and chemistry – cloud condensation nuclei, or CCN – around which water vapor condenses into droplets.

Under suitable conditions, more CCN can produce more numerous, smaller cloud droplets, making clouds more reflective.

In the tropics, CCN are usually abundant enough that adding or removing a few makes little difference. In the Arctic, concentrations can be so low that cloud formation is limited by particle scarcity.

A small change in the aerosol budget can swing the energy balance of a region-sized stretch of ocean or ice.

One important source of these particles is new particle formation. That means the spontaneous assembly of aerosol from gas-phase precursors.

The precursors include iodine compounds from sea-ice melt, sulfur vapors from biological activity, and oxidation products from organic sources.

The rate at which this happens varies with temperature, sunlight, humidity, and the availability of precursor gases.

This means it varies across the Arctic in ways that prior research, limited by patchy station data and short time windows, could not fully map.

To get around the patchiness, the Helsinki team built a satellite-derived metric they call NPF potential.

It is a monthly index derived from UV radiation, water vapor, vegetation cover, sea-ice concentration, atmospheric aerosol loading, and surface temperature.

The metric is then calibrated against what the ground stations actually observed.

Run across the Arctic from 2006 to 2021, it produced a fifteen-year map of where particle formation was high, low, or simply irrelevant to the local CCN budget. Three zones emerged.

The High Arctic – permanent ice, snow-covered land, Greenland, the Canadian Archipelago – is where NPF and CCN move together most cleanly.

Local processes rule. Sea-ice retreat at the margins is already releasing biological and chemical precursors, nudging particle formation upward.

By 2090–2100, under an intermediate warming scenario, CCN concentrations in this zone are estimated to increase by 3 to 16 percent.

The Maritime Arctic is the open water of the Barents and Norwegian seas – seasonally ice-free, biologically productive, and rich in iodine and sulfur precursors. It has the highest NPF potential of the three regimes.

But NPF potential and actual CCN are heading in opposite directions.

More water vapor raises what atmospheric chemists call the condensation sink. Freshly born particles get absorbed by existing aerosol before they grow large enough to seed clouds.

By the end of the century, CCN concentrations here are estimated to fall 15 to 31 percent. This decline could affect cloud formation across a significant stretch of the Arctic.

Then there is the Continental Arctic, the zone that does not fit the pattern at all. It sweeps from Lapland east across Siberia and into parts of northern Canada.

Here, NPF potential and CCN concentrations are essentially uncorrelated. Local particle formation is not what fills the CCN budget.

What fills it is transport: sulfate aerosol and organic particles carried north from European industry, Siberian wildfires, or North American agriculture.

The atmosphere above this belt is downstream of human decisions made far away. Its future cloudiness will be determined less by Arctic temperatures than by the trajectory of global emissions.

“The question is not just about whether the Arctic will get cloudier,” Chua said. “You also have to ask where and how much?”

Clouds are the largest single source of uncertainty in climate projections. An Arctic that brightens in one sector while darkening in another is not a wash.

The feedbacks are coupled to sea-ice loss, ocean heat uptake, and the atmospheric circulation patterns that export weather into the mid-latitudes.

Changes in Arctic clouds could affect how much sunlight reaches the surface and how much heat remains trapped in the atmosphere, potentially influencing sea-ice loss and regional warming.

These interconnected processes are difficult for climate models to capture, adding uncertainty to projections of future Arctic warming.

The long measurement records made the difference. Arctic aerosol physics is intensely seasonal.

A station that runs for three years can tell you what a particular summer looked like. One that runs for twenty tells you whether that summer was typical, and in which direction things are moving.

The Continental Arctic is the finding that should unsettle modelers most. Its CCN budget cannot be projected from local conditions.

It depends on what drifts north from the industrialized world. And as European emissions have fallen over the past decades, CCN concentrations in this zone have likely shifted already.

Future reductions would thin them further. Meanwhile, rising wildfire frequency across boreal forests could push them back up.

For a region that the world has spent 30 years treating as a single, rapidly warming entity, the study’s central message is a corrective: the Arctic is a mosaic.

Accounting for these regional differences could help scientists make more accurate projections of the Arctic’s changing climate.

The study is published in the journal Nature Geoscience.

In a small human study, unsweetened pure cranberry juice was linked to slower plaque regrowth and lower acid production, hinting at a possible oral health benefit.

Cranberry juice has a reputation for being tart, acidic, and often sugary, qualities that might seem poorly suited to protecting teeth. A small human study from the University of Illinois Chicago, however, found that unsweetened pure cranberry juice was associated with slower dental plaque regrowth and lower acid production.

UIC College of Dentistry researcher Christine Wu examined whether compounds naturally found in cranberries could affect dental plaque bacteria, which feed on sugars and produce acids that contribute to enamel decay. Cranberries contain proanthocyanidins, anti-inflammatory compounds known for their ability to interfere with the attachment of bacteria such as E. coli to cells.

“Just like sugar damage is linked to diet, I’m interested in how the food and beverages we consume can help combat the harmful bacteria and benefit our oral health,” Wu said.

Rather than testing isolated cranberry compounds in the laboratory, Wu and her colleagues used commercially available beverages. Fourteen children and eight adults were randomly assigned to drink either unsweetened pure 100% cranberry juice, a cranberry juice cocktail containing 27% juice with added sugar, or water.

“It was important to us to conduct these tests with human participants and use products available on the market. Most cranberry juice studies have been conducted using laboratory-prepared cranberry juice extract. Moreover, limited studies have been performed in children,” said Wu, who is a professor of pediatric dentistry.

Participants arrived on the morning of the study with plaque that had accumulated overnight. Researchers first used cotton swabs to collect plaque samples from one side of each participant’s mouth. After rinsing with water, participants drank three small portions of their assigned beverage, swishing each several times before swallowing.

Thirty minutes later, researchers collected plaque from teeth that had not been swabbed earlier. This allowed them to compare plaque collected before and after exposure to the beverages.

Plaque exposed to pure cranberry juice appeared to regrow more slowly than the samples collected before the experiment. Swishing with pure cranberry juice was also associated with lower acid production in both children and adults. That matters because acids produced by plaque bacteria can contribute to the breakdown of tooth enamel.

The findings raised the possibility that cranberry juice could eventually be considered a “functional beverage” with potential oral health benefits. Wu noted that other commonly consumed juices, including orange and apple juice, had not shown the same promise in her work.

“Most people think of cranberry juice as very sour, very acidic and sugary, and therefore may not contribute positively to oral health. This study found the opposite: that cranberry juice affected dental plaque regrowth and acid production, which may counteract some of the damage done by sugar,” she said.

The results are preliminary. The study included only 22 participants and measured short-term effects, so additional experiments will be needed to determine whether the findings hold up in larger groups and over longer periods.

For now, Wu said she would not rule out occasionally using pure cranberry juice as a mouth cleanser.

“But maybe by adding an appropriate sugar substitute,” she said, “to make it a bit more palatable.”

#4304. Compile-Time Dependent Types

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It seems inevitable that compile-time dependent types will come to systems programming.C++ has templates and constexpr. Rust has const generics. Zig has comptime. Even C has _Generic. However, these features tend to be highly restricted, lack a proper type system, or both.

I’m working on Syl, a language which aims to encode compile-time computation in a modal type system. Syl is primarily inspired by András Kovács’ work on two-level type theory, as well as plans for OxCaml.

In Syl, all values have a mode along with a type. Modes describe properties of values independent of their types. For example, OxCaml uses modes to indicate whether a value is uniquely owned, may be written to, may be sent to another thread, etc.1

Syl has two mode axes: staticity and erasure. On the staticity axis, a value may be either static or dynamic. On erasure, either erased or unerased.

For example, literals are static unerased, since they are clearly knowable at both compile time and runtime. You can check by writing a mode annotation:

Each mode axis has a sub-moding relation. If you have a static value, it’s safe to forget that it is knowable at compile time and hence treat it as dynamic. Similarly, an unerased value may be treated as erased. Mode annotations may “weaken” the mode, but not “strengthen” it:

As you might expect, branching on a dynamic value causes the result to be dynamic.

As long as they don’t capture or branch on dynamic values, functions preserve staticity.2

Functions that return erased values are themselves erased, so cannot be applied at runtime.

Now, what can we do with this information? When a value is knowable at compile time, surrounding code can be monomorphized. Functions of static parameters take advantage of this fact:

Whenever we call f x, the compiler requires that both f and x have mode static. If they do, it selects a particular implementation of f specialized to the value of x. Concretely, f true would call a function that hard-codes print_bool true, and likewise for f false.

In this example, we’re printing the value of x at runtime, so it needs to be unerased. That’s often unnecessary: if x only determines how to compile the body of f, it can be erased.

Here, erased also implies static, so f will be specialized on x. The if erased expression accepts a static erased condition and is guaranteed to resolve at compile time. Therefore, f true refers to a thunk with the body print_int 0, and likewise for f false.

On their own, the static and erased modes let us explicitly request specialization-based optimizations, but they don’t give us dependent types. Functions of static arguments do.

Here, if erased allows us to return an integer if x is true and a boolean otherwise. Hence, f has a dependent function type, which encodes how the result type depends on the value of the input.

Note ty as name gives the argument a name for use in the result.

Applications can then determine the correct result type by evaluating f’s type.

There is no distinct type grammar: type formers like -> and type annotations are normal expressions. int and bool are just erased values of type type, and abstracting over them gives us polymorphism.

As we saw above, functions of static arguments are monomorphized. At runtime, id int refers to an identity function that directly operates on integers, and likewise for id bool.

That is, Syl is dependently typed at compile time and simply typed at runtime.3

Constructs that determine the shape of the runtime program require static information.

Types are always erased, so the runtime program cannot inspect types.

Syl implements dependent typechecking similarly to existing languages, so we won’t discuss it in detail. However, the relationship between types and propositions in Syl differs from that of languages like Lean, Rocq, and Agda.

In particular, Syl supports general recursion in compile time computations and gives type type type. Allowing either of these features implies that constructing an expression of type t does not prove the proposition corresponding to t.

Although this type system would not be suitable for a theorem proving language, it does not rule out type safety or even useful proofs. It has two practical consequences:

Functions used in type-level computations may diverge, so typechecking is not decidable.

Functions don’t discharge universally quantified claims, so the compiler must evaluate all applications that produce proofs before relying on their results.45

In return, we don’t need a totality checker (all static expressions may be used in types) or universe machinery (type-level programming is intuitive).

Because Syl is a programming language, the point of compilation is to produce a runtime artifact satisfying the invariants enforced by the type system. Therefore, we only care about invocations of dependent functions that actually occur during compilation—it’s fine for others to diverge.

Still, typechecking getting stuck during development is a poor user experience, so Syl also implements fuel limits. Given competent compile-time debugging tools, I think this is reasonable.

Syl’s design is far from complete. In particular, it needs stories for mode polymorphism, lifetimes, linearity, implicit parameters, and proofs. The compiler needs incremental compilation, useful error traces, and an LLVM backend. I hope to share more thoughts on these areas in the future.
