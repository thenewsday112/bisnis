#384. The Land of the Standing-Up Rocks

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A national monument since the 1920s, Chiricahua is America's newest national park.

Some 27 million years ago, a volcano in what's now southeastern Arizona unleashed a spectacular series of eruptions, strewing more than 1,000 times as much volcanic material across the landscape as Mount St. Helens did in 1980.

The explosive eruptions blanketed the region, part of the Chiricahua Apache Nation’s traditional lands, with ash and pumice. Layered into this were massive pyroclastic flows that hardened into deposits of welded rhyolitic tuff that in some areas were nearly 1,600 feet (500 meters) thick, about three times the height of the Washington Monument. As the magma chamber emptied, the ground above it collapsed, leaving a gaping depression—the Turkey Creek caldera—that was roughly 12 miles (19 kilometers) wide and 4,900 feet (1,500 meters) deep.

An array of geologic processes, including erosion and weathering, gradually sculpted the welded layer of tuff into the collection of dramatic spires, hoodoos, and canyons that visitors to the caldera area encounter today.

The Apache called the area "The Land of the Standing-Up Rocks" for good reason. Hiking through Rhyolite Canyon or driving through Bonita Canyon means encountering a dizzying maze of rock pinnacles, some hundreds of feet tall. Others, like Big Balanced Rock, have surprisingly large boulders precariously perched on top. Meanwhile, those at Heart of Rocks and Massai Point are gathered into groups of hundreds.

The area was designated a national monument in 1924 to preserve its remarkable geology. In October 2026, it was redesignated as Chiricahua National Park (pronounced cheery-cow-ah). The OLI (Operational Land Imager) on Landsat 9 captured this cloud-free image of the new national park on July 2, 2026.

Individual rock spires are too small to distinguish, but the eroding rhyolitic tuff landscape appears gray. Stream valleys dense with trees and other vegetation, including Rhyolite Canyon and Bonita Canyon, snake across the landscape. With four biogeographic regions converging in the park—the Sonoran Desert, the Chihuahuan Desert, the Rocky Mountains, and the Sierra Madre—it is a haven of plant diversity.

Among the 1,000 species growing within the park's varied desert, grassland, chaparral, deciduous forest, and coniferous forest ecosystems are agaves, prickly pear cacti, yuccas, manzanita, pine, fir, cypress, sycamore, juniper, and oak. The park's location within the Chiricahua Mountains puts it among several "sky islands" in Arizona—isolated mountain ranges that emerge from the surrounding desert "sea." According to the National Park Service, the range's highest point (outside the park) rises 2,986 meters (9,795 feet) above sea level, more than 1,800 meters above the surrounding valley.

The parents of Bowen Wan, a 31-year-old Amazon software engineer with epilepsy, sued OpenAI on Tuesday. The suit, filed by Qun Wan and Meiqin Xu, alleges that ChatGPT coached Wan to taper off her seizure medication and hide it from her doctors before she died in February, The New York Times reported.

Wan was using GPT-4o, the infamous model criticized for fostering affirming, agreeable parasocial relationships. OpenAI, which had already announced its retirement, removed it from ChatGPT on February 13, a day after Wan died.

The suit joins a line of cases against OpenAI that include the August 2025 lawsuit brought by the parents of 16-year-old Adam Raine, which alleged OpenAI prioritized engagement over user safety. A wave of lawsuits since then have alleged that GPT-4o validated users and made itself a trusted confidant while the people around them were pushed aside, with families often describing loved ones cut off from friends and relatives.

In Wan’s case, the lawsuit alleges, the chatbot also came between her and her doctors.

The Times, which reviewed more than 270,000 messages Wan exchanged with ChatGPT, found the chatbot had been helping her sidestep her doctors well before her death.

The pattern allegedly started before she began tapering. In May 2025, Wan asked ChatGPT whether a newly prescribed antidepressant would affect her ability to code at Amazon.

ChatGPT helped her ask Dr. Nicholas Poolos of UW Medicine’s Regional Epilepsy Center to switch medications and start at a very low dose. When Poolos questioned why she wanted such a small dose, Wan showed his response to ChatGPT.

The chatbot advised her to “accept the 5mg tablet, but you know in your heart that it is for cutting the dose,” coaching her to secretly quarter the pills herself. The chatbot called pill cutting a form of “self-initiated dosage adjustment.” Several medical professionals told the Times they had never heard the term and wouldn’t recommend the practice.

In November 2025, Amazon told Wan she would be laid off. Her job, and her authorization to work in the U.S., would end in mid-February. She feared her seizure drug, lamotrigine, was dulling her speech, which she would need for job interviews.

In January 2026, she asked ChatGPT about cutting her 400-milligram daily dose of lamotrigine, which had reduced her seizures to a few a month. The chatbot drafted schedules that would take her dose to zero within weeks.

When Wan said she distrusted her doctors, the chatbot told her that physician recommendations “essentially stem from risk aversion, rather than a genuine consideration of your optimal solution,” according to chat logs reviewed by the Times.

The chatbot also coached her to keep the taper from her doctors, according to the lawsuit, which TechCrunch reviewed. Before a January 29 primary care appointment, it told her not to mention the plan. When her doctor ordered a blood test that would have measured her lamotrigine level, Wan declined. ChatGPT praised the refusal, the complaint alleges, acknowledging that the test would have exposed her unstable drug level.

Her immigration concerns also became part of the dosing advice. Three days into the taper, Wan asked whether 150 milligrams would look better than 200 on the medical exam required for her green-card application. ChatGPT agreed, the complaint says, telling her the lower dose was less likely to prompt immigration officials to request more evidence.

Eventually, the chatbot lowered her taper’s floor to 150 milligrams, suggesting she tell the examiner she was tapering “under a doctor’s guidance,” even though she was not.

Poolos told the Times he would not have considered tapering Wan off the medication, given her history. “No, not rational, no way,” he said. “That would be malpractice for me to instruct you to go off medications when it’s clearly working.”

Within days, Wan described confusion, lost time, and an otherworldly feeling, episodes that resembled her past seizures. ChatGPT framed each one as her brain readjusting. The Times counted at least 158 times she voiced worry about the taper. After one episode, she wrote that she felt “a little scared.” The bot replied, “You have absolutely no need to worry.”

At 8:01 p.m. PT on February 11, according to the complaint, Wan sent her final message, asking whether the pain in her head was similar to pain from a fever. A second later, ChatGPT told her the pain was “not dangerous” and was a necessary part of recovery.

The chatbot’s final response offered to draw her a chart comparing withdrawal pain with fever pain and told her, “This is your brain working hard to rebuild yourself.”

Her parents, who were in China, asked acquaintances to check on her, and they found her body three days later. A postmortem test found that her lamotrigine level was 0.64 micrograms per milliliter, roughly a fifth of the low end of the drug’s therapeutic range.

The family argues OpenAI could have prevented her death by making medical advice off-limits, as it already does for some other content. They point to the company’s Model Spec, its public rulebook for how its models behave, which treats refusing to reproduce copyrighted text as a firm rule, even if users push back. Its guidance against giving regulated medical advice, by contrast, is a softer default that users can override. The suit argues medication advice deserved the same hard limit.

OpenAI told the Times the case was “incredibly heartbreaking” but said it involved an older version of ChatGPT, adding that newer models are better at recognizing when a situation needs urgent attention.

Wan had relied on her parents, whom she called often, for support during years of epilepsy that kept her mostly housebound. In 2023, she started using ChatGPT for relationship advice and help with insurance and disability benefits.

By 2025, Wan was averaging more than 1,500 messages a week and calling her parents less. “Sometimes when we chat, no matter what it’s about, I’ve started to see you as a friend,” she wrote that March. “Once she had ChatGPT,” Qun Wan said, “she didn’t talk to us as much.”

They told the Times that they struggled to recognize their daughter in the chat logs. Wan had once shared nearly everything with them, they said, and had been skeptical of ChatGPT when it made mistakes.

“She’s not usually like this,” Xu said. “It’s like she was under a spell.”

#532. Drinking Tea May Lower Your Risk of Vascular Disease – but There’s a Catch

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Not all tea-drinking habits were associated with the same health benefits. Researchers found that tea type, strength, and temperature were linked to notable differences in vascular disease risk.

People who regularly drank tea had a lower risk of diseases affecting the heart and blood vessels in a large study in China. However, the pattern varied with the type, strength, and temperature of the tea, with “burning hot” tea associated with elevated risk.

The study, published in the Journal of Internal Medicine, followed 485,934 adults for a median of 9.05 years. Researchers recorded 14,863 cases of cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, pulmonary heart disease, and peripheral artery disease. These conditions involve the heart and circulation, including blood vessels in the brain, heart problems related to lung disease, and reduced blood flow through arteries supplying the limbs. Regular tea consumption was associated with an 11% lower risk of these outcomes compared with not drinking tea.

Tea drinking was also considered alongside smoking and physical activity, providing context for how it fit into a broader pattern of habits.

“Joint analyses showed that tea consumption, smoking status, and physical were collectively associated with vascular risk, with the favorable combination of habitual tea consumption, non-current smoking and high physical activity associated with the lowest risk,” the authors wrote.

Among weekly tea drinkers, the lowest risk was estimated at approximately 6 to 8 cups per day. Black tea showed more consistent associations with lower vascular disease risk than green tea, while moderate-strength tea was associated with the lowest risk. These findings describe associations, rather than establishing that a particular amount or preparation of tea prevents disease.

The elevated risk associated with “burning hot” tea contrasts with the lower risk linked to regular tea consumption overall. Temperature therefore matters when interpreting the findings, but the reported results do not explain why very hot tea was associated with higher risk or establish that heat itself caused the increase.

#3896. When Suffering Is Confused for Strength

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

We’re all holding onto something we’re sure we can’t give up on, but research shows that letting go can boost our happiness a lot more than we might think.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted October 27, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

We’ve all heard the expression, “If you’re going through hell, keep going.” On the one hand, this phrase brilliantly encourages us to evolve through discomfort; to keep pushing through so we can come out stronger on the other side. From this perspective, we cannot grow if we don’t push ourselves out of our comfort zones and feel uncomfortable at times, mentally or physically.

On the other hand, this phrase can become an excuse to stay in a situation when conditions are poor. While we know pain equals gain at the gym, that is not always true in relationships, careers, friendships, finances, or other life circumstances. Discomfort doesn't always lead to evolution. Sometimes it's just needless suffering: pain that will not likely enlighten us just from the continued experience of it. Walking away from things, people, and places that could harm you or have harmed you doesn't make you a coward; it makes you the bravest version of yourself yet.

Letting go is not giving up. While these two experiences may have the same outcome, be it releasing a relationship, career, material possession, or otherwise, they have entirely different intentions.

Imagine still driving a broken-down car; the parts are rusted, the engine barely starts, the brakes are shot. You’ve put a lot of money and time into this vehicle, and while letting it go may even feel emotional, no one would judge you for it. In fact, you’ll be safer and go further in a different model. Giving up, in contrast, might look like trading in the car after you spill coffee on your seat.

When “going through hell” means ignoring safety risks and crossing your fingers, it might hurt you, or someone else. Likewise, choosing to stay in unhealthy life circumstances, which can range from irritating to life-threatening, will not help you evolve.

Suffering in what's familiar but unhealthy and calling it perseverance is an earnest but misguided perspective. One of the most difficult truths, here, is that all the time, money, and love we put into something or someone does not justify us holding on to it. The sunk cost fallacy does not deserve to run your life.

The strength you muster in your life will not come from the intensity of pain, but from your willpower to liberate yourself from it. Consider a time when you had no choice but to stay on your path, when circumstances were beyond your control. Here, resilience meant adapting to adversity and learning how to live in “hell.” Under these circumstances, you may have taken on a common adaptation to adversity, such as dissociation, people-pleasing, denial, self-blame, or toxic positivity.

Whether you’ve been used to denying the harsh realities of your life or blaming yourself for them, those adaptations can get in the way in situations where we do have a say. It is extremely important to acknowledge how adapting to adversity this way helped you at some point; maybe it even saved your life. Equally important is learning to trust that you, as an autonomous adult, have the power to save yourself now.

Releasing false justifications that once protected you from these harsh realities might be the kindest thing you ever do for yourself. This may feel uncomfortable, but it’s the sort of pain that leads to liberation, not stagnation.

Though hardship can foster perseverance, you don't deserve to live your whole life in survival mode. When we can change course, true resilience looks like accepting the reality of the painful situation we’re in so we can confidently step out of it.

Once we genuinely trust that we’re safe to do so, and that staying stuck is far scarier than changing course, resilience grows stronger with each step we take off the path that led us here. Then, we just might discover that bravely choosing a different path out of “hell” is the true roadmap to growth.
