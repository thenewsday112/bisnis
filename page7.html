#1974. Weil, Gotshal &amp; Manges creates AI-centric 1L summer program

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

“I don’t believe that to be a great lawyer that is AI-enabled someone needs to have deep technical history,” Andrew Simon, the chief AI and innovation officer at Weil, Gotshal & Manges, told Law.com. (Photo from Shutterstock)

Weil, Gotshal & Manges is launching an artificial intelligence-based program for 2027 1L summer associates that will pair law students with the firm’s AI and innovation team, the law firm announced Wednesday.

The 10-week program, now accepting applications, includes coding lessons in Python, developing skills to build a neural network, and working on active legal projects. Two members of the cohort will work directly with a member of the Lawyer Augmented Solutions Engineering and Research team, and the students will get offers to return for the following year’s 2L program.

“I don’t believe that to be a great lawyer that is AI-enabled someone needs to have deep technical history,” Andrew Simon, the chief AI and innovation officer at Weil, Gotshal & Manges, told Law.com. “Anyone who wants to learn this stuff—sufficient enough to be an advanced AI builder as a lawyer—can do so.”

The focus on learning the Python programming language and the PyTorch machine learning framework aims to help students develop usable tools.

#1964. Did ‘corporate speak’ and PowerPoint lead to complacency at GM?

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

General Motors headquarters in Detroit. Jonathan Weiss Shutterstock.com

General Motors employees were taught to be careful when writing about safety issues.

A 2008 Power Point presentation had some helpful suggestions, according to a report by Jenner & Block chairman Anton Valukas on the automaker’s failure to respond to defective ignition switches.

A “problem” should be described as an “issue, condition, matter.” The word “safety” should be replaced with “potential safety implications.” The word “defect” should be nixed in favor of “does not perform to design.”

A defective ignition switch in Chevy Cobalts that could switch to the accessory or off position? GM personnel didn’t make the connection between the low-torque switch, which required less force to turn, and airbags that wouldn’t deploy when the switch changed out of the run position. They saw stalls caused by the switch as a “customer convenience” problem, rather than a safety problem, according to the report (PDF).

The Washington Post notes the proliferation of “corporate speak” at GM. “Idioms and corporate cliches are more than just buzzwords that threaten our linguistic sanity,” the story says. “At GM they stood to mask real meaning, obscured leaders’ responsibilities, and created labels that hindered the critical thinking needed to keep real problems from getting worse.”

The story cites another instance where words got in the way. A top GM lawyer asked a vice president of global quality to be a “champion” in the probe of the switch “nondeployment” problem. The executive believed he was supposed to be a person who helps the team remove roadblocks and obtain resources. After that executive’s retirement, a new executive stepped in who also did not understand the urgency of the safety issue.

A Wall Street Journal article says PowerPoint presentations were a substitute for meaningful communications at GM. In one instance in 2009, then GM CEO Rick Wagoner “may have viewed” a PowerPoint presentation that included a “back-up” slide about a change in design to the Cobalt’s key. The change was described as a move to reduce warranty costs as a result of customer complaints about the ignition switching out of the run position because of a heavy key chain swinging in a wide slot.

“What if someone had simply stood up,” the Wall Street Journal asks, “without a visual prop, and said: ‘People are dying’?”

#1957. Disbarred lawyer allegedly collected client fees, faked performing work

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Jared Thomas, 41, of Evansville, Indiana, was booked into the Vanderburgh County Jail in Evansville, Indiana, on Oct. 25. (Photo from the Vanderburgh County Sheriff’s Office)

A disbarred Indiana attorney has been accused of collecting legal fees and pretending to perform the work that he was hired to do.

Jared Thomas, 41, of Evansville, Indiana, was recently charged with forgery, fraud and practice of law by a nonlawyer after a client hired him to expunge her criminal record. Police questioned Thomas about allegedly taking $950 from a client and lying about filing the paperwork.

Thomas claimed that a paralegal with access to his computers had been filing fake documents, according to coverage by WFIE.

Thomas was arrested in 2021 on a check deception charge and sentenced to probation. Later, the Indiana Supreme Court found that Thomas had mismanaged a trust account, forged a judge’s signature and falsified a document.

He was barred from practicing law in Indiana in 2022.

Nature volume 658, pages 883–896 (2026) Cite this article

Recent breakthroughs in pig-to-human xenotransplantation have successfully prevented hyperacute rejection of kidney, heart, liver and lung xenografts, marking a milestone long considered a critical barrier. However, these major advances also brought new challenges into focus, as immune-mediated graft injuries including antibody-mediated rejection, T cell-mediated rejection and innate immune activation have been observed in most xenotransplantation procedures to date. Detection and management of these injuries remain a critical bottleneck, as conventional histopathology and standard clinical monitoring lack the sensitivity required to identify subclinical injury early enough to guide timely intervention. This diagnostic gap underscores an urgent need for the development of advanced, multimodal precision diagnostic frameworks to enhance immune surveillance, which will be essential for standardizing clinical protocols and enabling pathogenesis-driven therapeutic intervention. This approach will inform both immunosuppressive regimens and future gene editing strategies aimed at engineering rejection-resistant porcine organs, ultimately prolonging graft survival. Broad adoption of such an integrative strategy will be essential to transition xenotransplantation from an experimental intervention into a safe, scalable and patient-centred clinical therapy. In this Review, we synthesize recent progress in xenotransplantation and position multimodal precision diagnostics as a pivotal strategy to address the remaining immunological and clinical barriers to widespread clinical implementation.

Some of the richest people in history are using charitable donations to explore fresh approaches to funding scientific research. Their ranks could soon grow: in the next year or so, initial public offerings by some of the biggest artificial-intelligence companies, OpenAI and Anthropic, both in San Francisco, California, could see the companies valued at more than US$1 trillion apiece, making hundreds of their employees millionaires in the process.

How tech billionaires are disrupting biomedical science

How tech billionaires are disrupting biomedical science

Many of the new philanthropists are adopting an engineering mindset, focusing their donations on technological approaches to solving societal problems. As Nature reports in a News feature, they are also bringing many of their own ideas about how this research should be prioritized, funded and executed.

These donors speak of high-risk, long-term and interdisciplinary research. Their goals include finding ways to make research faster and more efficient by encouraging data sharing and speeding up grant approvals, with solutions that include incorporating AI into the processes.

But there’s a disparity when you compare how these funders and those in the public sector operate. Like much of the research that takes place in companies, the results of projects funded by philanthropy won’t necessarily be published. Private organizations are not always obliged to collect and report data on how well their projects have worked — by publishing in peer-reviewed journals, for example — or to make other details about their grants public. At Nature, we think they should be more transparent. Transparency in science funding is a component of transparency in science itself: a non-negotiable principle. Research that is not transparent cannot be verified or built on by others, who might then improve it or find new applications.

An increasing number of examples of science philanthropy can be found around the world. In China, tech entrepreneurs have launched awards such as the Xplorer Prize for young researchers, which is funded mainly by the company Tencent in Shenzhen. New philanthropic foundations are emerging, including the Beijing-based Xiaomi Foundation, which supports universities and provides grants to teachers and researchers. And in Brazil, the Serrapilheira Institute in Rio de Janeiro was established in 2017 by private donors to fund fundamental research, which is usually supported by governments.

When the best decision is no decision: the rise of randomization in grant funding

When the best decision is no decision: the rise of randomization in grant funding

But it is in the United States that philanthropists have one of the longest histories of shaping the direction and structure of the scientific enterprise, from the industrialists of the early twentieth century to today’s tech entrepreneurs.

In 2015, Facebook founder Mark Zuckerberg and his wife, paediatrician Priscilla Chan, pledged to donate 99% of Facebook shares, then valued at US$45 billion, to the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative. One of the initiative’s ambitions is to build interdisciplinary research infrastructure to allow the study of complex biology, to take research beyond the study of individual diseases. By 2025, Bill Gates and Melinda French Gates had donated nearly $64 billion of their wealth to the Gates Foundation (formerly the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation) in Seattle, Washington, which is focused on projects to improve public health and alleviate poverty in some 140 countries.

These and newer donors are keen to fill some of the perceived gaps in publicly funded research. Some, such as those financing the Arc Institute in Palo Alto, California, have created funding schemes to allow investigators to rapidly change direction in response to research results — something that is difficult, if not impossible, to do with conventional grants. Others, including former Google chief executive Eric Schmidt, are investing in technologies to speed up research. Philanthropic foundations do not have the same reporting requirements as government funding agencies, which must publicly document their investments and the outcomes of their spending choices. It can be argued that freedom from such requirements is liberating. Without the fear of being called before lawmakers to explain the value of specific research projects, philanthropists are free to be more adventurous with their money. They can invest in long-term and high-risk projects. And with fewer bureaucratic obligations, charities can be better positioned to pivot rapidly when new technologies emerge, and to bridge disciplinary divisions to assemble teams with diverse expertise.

That said, science supported by philanthropy still relies on a foundation of public funding. Privately funded science and technology builds on published findings, many of which have been achieved through research funded by the public purse (M. Mazzucato and G. Semieniuk Oxford Rev. Econ. Policy 33, 24–48; 2017). If privately funded science is to achieve its ambition to benefit the world, philanthropists need to coordinate with and support the public sector. There are pragmatic reasons for doing so. Any new technologies that tech philanthropists want to roll out to the public will need to be compatible with existing ethical and safety frameworks.

Philanthropists should also bear in mind that in science, the line between an idea and a result is rarely straight or predictable. Those still relatively new to philanthropy would be wise to study the experiences of the Gates Foundation. In 2003, the organization, working with academic researchers, announced that it would fund up to 14 Grand Challenges in Global Health (H. Varmus et al. Science 302, 398–399; 2003). This was a set of ambitious goals to harness science to improve public health, ranging from developing vaccines that require no refrigeration to creating new treatments for infectious diseases. Some of the work, such as the use of gene editing in mosquitoes to control malaria, is paying off. But it has taken time. A decade after the launch of the challenges, Gates acknowledged that he had been “pretty naive” about how quickly the objectives could be achieved — a salutary lesson for his successors.

In countries around the world, public funding for scientific research is being cut or is at risk of being cut. That makes philanthropy an even more important source of finance. Without greater transparency about the results of philanthropic grant-making, however, there is no way of knowing what approaches have been successful or which might be instructive for guiding future science funding.

The engineer’s drive for efficiency and constant improvement, combined with the philanthropist’s freedom from constraint promise to bring fresh perspectives to science and innovation. Open science offers the best bet for ensuring that the resulting research delivers on its lofty goals.

It’s boom time for billionaires. In the next few months, the world could see a fresh crew of technology entrepreneurs join that club, and the numbers of multi-millionaires would swell even more.

Two giants of artificial intelligence, OpenAI and Anthropic, plan to go public in the next year or so, floating stocks that are expected to rocket some of their employees into the ranks of the ultra-wealthy. Some analysts have projected that Anthropic’s initial public offering (IPO) might bring in US$2 trillion, the largest in history. OpenAI is reportedly targeting $1.4 trillion for its IPO, although the company delayed going public until 2027 amid renewed concerns about AI safety.

If these IPOs go as planned, the effects might extend well beyond sales of superyachts, mansions and private jets. The windfall would probably also add to the flurry of private investments and philanthropic donations from tech-savvy entrepreneurs who are eager to jump-start their favoured areas of biomedical research, such as cancer biology, longevity and reproductive technologies. “It’s a very exciting time,” says Andrew Lo, who studies entrepreneurship at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in Cambridge. “We’re going to see the creative energy of the tech sector unlocked.”

Wealthy technology entrepreneurs have long put their cash and creative energy into medical research. And as their fortunes have grown, so has the scale of their investments, and the variety of ways in which their funds are doled out. One major player is Altos Labs, a longevity-focused company in Redwood City, California, which launched in 2022 with $3 billion in funds raised from tech titans Yuri Milner and Jeff Bezos, among others — an unprecedented sum for a fledgling biotechnology company with no drug candidates. The Chan Zuckerberg Initiative (CZI) , founded by Facebook co-founder Mark Zuckerberg and his wife, physician Priscilla Chan, has spent about $4 billion on basic scientific research and other causes, and plans to spend up to $10 billion more over the next decade.

Ultra-wealthy research investors follow a different strategy than do government funding agencies such as the US National Institutes of Health (NIH). Organizations such as the CZI and Altos Labs boast a commitment to high-risk, long-term research. Tech entrepreneurs are also often keen to invest in new technology, and to harness the power of AI for biomedicine. In April, for example, the CZI announced that it would spend $500 million on funding predictive AI models of human cells. Last year, OpenAI Foundation, the non-profit arm of OpenAI in San Francisco, California, pledged to spend $25 billion on health, disease cures and AI resilience. The foundation has announced more than $100 million for Alzheimer’s disease research and another $125 million for life-sciences data projects this year.

But this investment comes at a time when the public is suspicious of the tech elite and its political influence. It is difficult to assess the success of philanthropic programmes, and many are geared towards encouraging the use of the same technology the philanthropists profited from developing. Meanwhile, high-profile private investment in firms that study longevity or controversial reproductive technologies, such as gene editing in human embryos, raises questions about whether tech entrepreneurs will skew research towards applications that benefit the wealthy, rather than helping a broader swathe of society. “In philanthropy, no one gets elected,” says Rob Reich, a political scientist at Stanford University in California. “There’s no way of holding them accountable.”

US philanthropists have a long history of influencing what research gets done, and how. In the early twentieth century, grants from industrialists such as Andrew Carnegie and John D. Rockefeller helped to transform higher education and solidify the role of US universities in scientific research. Their giving focused on ensuring that the institutions they supported succeeded; today’s philanthropists are more likely to seek a particular outcome rather than support the long-term health of a university, says Eric John Abrahamson, a historian at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore, Maryland. In 2024, non-profit organizations, including philanthropies, supported an estimated $25 billion in research and development — about 11% of total research funding in the United States, according to the Science Philanthropy Alliance (see ‘Support sources’).

It is difficult to track all donations from technology magnates comprehensively, but individual examples show that their influence has been sizeable. In 2000, Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates established the Gates Foundation (formerly called the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation), which made massive investments in vaccine development and the study of infectious disease, among other fields. It remains an influential behemoth: last year, Gates announced that he would donate about US$200 billion over the next two decades.

Other large tech donors include Sergey Brin, a co-founder of Google, who has donated more than $1 billion to Parkinson’s disease research. Sean Parker, an early executive at Facebook, has given hundreds of millions to cancer-immunotherapy research. In 2021, several philanthropists, including tech entrepreneurs, donated $650 million to found the Arc Institute in Palo Alto, California, in the heart of Silicon Valley, to understand the root causes of complex diseases. And US software magnate Larry Ellison announced that he will spend billions of pounds over ten years on a new campus near the University of Oxford, UK. The campus will focus on health care, as well as agriculture, climate change and AI.

In addition to charitable donations, tech entrepreneurs have been fuelling biomedical innovation through private-sector investments, such as funding Altos Labs. Another company, Calico Life Sciences, was founded in 2013 with backing from Google. With a focus on combating ageing and curing disease, Calico, in South San Francisco, California, was conceived as a long-term ‘moonshot’ programme. Sam Altman, a co-founder and chief executive of OpenAI, has invested about $180 million in Retro Biosciences, another longevity-focused company, in Redwood City, California.

In some cases, the line between for-profit investments and philanthropic donations are becoming more blurred, says Louis Shekhtman, a network scientist at Bar-Ilan University in Ramat Gan, Israel. The CZI, for example, was set up as a limited liability company (LLC), rather than as a tax-exempt foundation. Doing so comes at a cost: depending on how they are constructed, LLCs might not be eligible for the tax breaks that more-conventional charities enjoy. But an LLC can be more flexible in how it spends its money, and has fewer requirements to publicly disclose its activities, says Joan Heminway, a business-law scholar at the University of Tennessee in Knoxville.

In a 2015 statement shortly after the launch of the CZI, Zuckerberg said the LLC structure would give the initiative “the flexibility to give to the organizations that do the best work — regardless of how they’re structured”. He also said that any net profits from CZI investments would be put towards furthering its philanthropic mission.

Favourable US tax policies and a high concentration of billionaires — many of them in the technology industry — mean that the United States is unmatched among wealthy nations for charitable giving, says Shekhtman. It is also a major hub for biomedical research, and home to nearly half of the world’s biopharmaceutical companies.

And US tech entrepreneurs have a penchant for experimentation in how they structure their investments, says Keith Yamamoto, vice-chancellor of science policy and strategy at the University of California, San Francisco. When it comes to philanthropy, private donors are more able to take risks with their investments than are government funding agencies, which are beholden to taxpayers to ensure that their money is spent wisely. “Public money limits your activities,” says Harold Varmus, a former director of the NIH, who has also been on advisory boards for the Gates Foundation and the CZI. “There is a freedom of motion that is associated with private money.”

Some tech titans have experimented with various ways of structuring their grants. The Arc Institute, for example, boasts a “no-strings-attached” funding system that gives its investigators more freedom than do standard government grants awarded to biomedical researchers.

Philanthropists are also able to fund long-term, interdisciplinary projects that can be difficult for government grants to take on. Convergent Research, a non-profit organization funded in part by Eric Schmidt, the former chief executive of Google, is forming ‘focused research organizations’ that bring teams together to solve important scientific or engineering challenges that could benefit a wide swathe of research. “We’re looking for the pieces of critical, fundamental infrastructure missing from a particular field,” says Anastasia Gamick, a co-founder and president of Convergent Research, which is headquartered in Cambridge, Massachusetts.

Projects include developing cheap ways to map the neurons in mammalian brains, and large-scale screening to catalogue the off-target effects of small-molecule drugs.

Meanwhile, Yamamoto points to the CZI’s Institute for Advanced Biological Imaging as another example of a long-term, high-risk project that aims to develop pivotal technology. He hopes that the team there — which has brought engineers, physicists and computer scientists together with biologists — will succeed in refining a difficult technique called cryo-electron tomography so that it can image processes in a cell at the level of individual proteins.

Like Calico Life Sciences, the company warned investors from the start: this will take time.

Arcadia has also taken an unusual step for a for-profit company and embraced open science to a degree not typically found in industry, says Yamamoto. Its lab protocols, computer code and data sets are published directly online.

Silicon Valley investors are also sometimes willing to wade into controversial territory. Last year, Brian Armstrong, chief executive of the cryptocurrency exchange Coinbase, helped to fund a company dedicated to developing methods to edit the genome in human embryos to prevent disease. The company, named Preventive and based in San Francisco, will first have to grapple with the fundamental science of heritable gene editing to learn whether and how it can be done safely, says Lucas Harrington, a co-founder of Preventive. Private investment will enable the company to do that research, which US legal restrictions prohibit government grants from funding.

Artist’s impression of the heavy-set facial features of a Denisovan.Credit: Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology/Chinese Academy of Sciences

A low, sloping forehead, a massive brow ridge that curves backwards, rectangle-like eye sockets, flat cheeks and a moderately elevated bridge of the nose. Taken together, these features are the hallmarks of the ancient humans called the Denisovans, say researchers who studied a trove of fossilized skull fragments, teeth and jaws found in northern China.

“We are finally starting to put a face to the Denisovan,” says Sally Wasef, a palaeogeneticist at the Queensland University of Technology in Brisbane, Australia.

Denisovans were first described in 2010, after researchers sequenced genetic material from a mysterious finger bone discovered in a cave in Siberia and found that it did not resemble that of Homo sapiens or Neanderthals (Homo neanderthalensis). Ancient proteins have since been used to identify remains from other parts of Asia as Denisovan, including the first near-complete Denisovan skull found near Harbin, which helped to put a face to the ancient humans.

Researchers report in Nature1 today that they have an even better idea of what Denisovans looked like after analysing the physical features of remains that belong to a collection of 20 fossils found in Xujiayao.

The Xujiayao remains are at least 130,000 years old and were found in the 1970s, alongside animal remains and tools. At the time, it was unclear what type of human these fossils belonged to, and they were classified as a species called Homo juluensis. By analysing ancient proteins from some of the fossils, Qiaomei Fu, a geneticist at the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing, and her team identified the remains as Denisovan.

Wasef says that ancient-protein analysis is a valuable tool to complement DNA studies, particularly because proteins can survive longer without fully degrading than can DNA. Although DNA provides more-detailed information about an individual, protein analysis can still yield useful information about who an individual is related to.

Fabrice Demeter, a palaeoanthropologist at the University of Copenhagen, says that the findings add to growing evidence that Denisovans were a widespread and potentially diverse group of humans. “The study represents a major step forward” in our understanding of what these humans could have looked like and where they lived.

Fu’s team analysed small fragments of proteins, called peptides, from two Xujiayao bones and compared them with peptides found in humans and Neanderthals. They found a single amino-acid change that was present in multiple peptides from the Xujiayao bones and other East Asian fossils and is a known genetic marker for identifying Denisovans. The amino-acid change was not seen in human or Neanderthal samples.

A third region, at the front of the insula, ‘talks’ to other brain areas involved in cognition and emotion, suggesting that it influences not only sensations, but also how the brain interprets them.

Contrary to previous thinking, the insula is “not just reading the body. It is partly influencing the story about how the body should be felt,” says study co-author Josef Parvizi, a physician-scientist at Stanford University in California.

The study is “quite a feat”, says Lucina Uddin, a cognitive neuroscientist at the University of California, Los Angeles. It’s also one of the most comprehensive studies on the insula and how it produces bodily sensations, she adds.

The findings were published today in Nature.

The insula has been notoriously difficult to study in detail because it’s located deep in the brain. Previous studies have suggested that this hard-to-reach region is involved in receiving signals — such as pain or pressure — from various parts of the body, then processing them into sensations that we experience. But most research on the human insula has relied on brain-imaging techniques to capture what happens in the insula when various sensations arise, Parvizi says.

To build a more detailed picture, Parvizi and his colleagues needed to find a way to send electrical signals to various parts of the insula in people and assess the bodily sensations that occur as a result. But implanting electrodes deep in the brains of healthy people would be unethical. So, the researchers turned to 87 people who’d had electrodes implanted in their brains for medical reasons.

In experiments that spanned more than a decade, Parvizi and his colleagues sent electrical currents through the electrodes into a variety of sites in the participants’ insulas.

Stimulating the rear of the insula produced pain, heat and other sensations — including tingling and numbness — in specific areas of the participants’ bodies, such as the arm or leg. Meanwhile, participants experienced more ‘visceral’ sensations, including nausea, pressure and feelings of fullness in their chest, when the researchers sent electrical currents to the middle of the insula.

“The world can no longer avoid overshooting the 1.5 °C limit, and the consequences are already devastating,” said outgoing UN secretary-general António Guterres in a press briefing announcing the report — an annual accounting of progress towards tackling climate change published by the UN Environment Programme (UNEP) on 21 October. Guterres pointed to the extended heatwaves, massive wildfires and deadly floods that have disrupted much of the world in 2026. He called for faster action from governments to cut emissions by transitioning away from fossil fuels, expanding clean electricity and reversing deforestation.

Under current policies, scientists estimate that warming will peak at 2.6 °C to 2.8 °C above pre-industrial levels this century — substantially exceeding the 2 °C upper limit of the 2015 Paris Agreement targets to avoid the most dangerous consequences of climate change. There is also a smaller, 10% chance of warming exceeding 3.4 °C on the current trajectory, which researchers say would be catastrophic. Even under a scenario in which countries achieve all existing emissions pledges, the report projects 2.1 °C of warming.

Although the 1.5 °C goal to limit the worst effects of climate change is out of reach — 2026 is projected to be the second year in which temperatures have surpassed that level of warming — the report maintains that it might be possible to ‘overshoot’ that target, then bring temperatures back down through rapid decarbonization and a massive expansion of carbon dioxide removal.

UNEP’s assessment comes ahead of November’s COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, at which governments will take stock of efforts to mitigate and adapt to climate change. “The emissions gap is now a leadership gap. And the world is running out of time and excuses to close it,” Guterres said.

There is some good news in this year’s report. Initial estimates show that global greenhouse-gas emissions in 2025 were about the same or slightly lower than they were in 2024. That was mainly due to a decrease in emissions from land-use activities, particularly as a result of a quiet year for wildfires after unprecedented blazes in 2024.

That would be the first decline seen since the COVID-19 pandemic, and it could represent the start of a plateau or even a peak in emissions (see ‘Have emissions peaked?’). But researchers caution that a single year is not enough to determine whether this is a lasting shift.

“Global emissions will have to fall several per cent before there can be any certainty that they have peaked,” says Lauri Myllyvirta, a researcher at the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air, a research organization in Helsinki. “Until then, one particularly hot summer or other cyclical development can cause a jump above the preceding peak, even if the structural trend is downward.”

Another encouraging trend is the rapid expansion of renewable sources of energy. Clean-energy capacity has increased around three times faster than was anticipated in 2015, and solar energy has expanded seven times faster than expected as costs of solar panels have fallen.

Myllyvirta points out that even if this year doesn’t represent an emissions peak, the report projects a 5–8% drop by 2030, relative to 2025 levels. “The door to a much larger reduction than this over the next five years is open,” he says, as clean energy accelerates and fossil fuels become costlier, and because of the potential for China and India to overachieve their promised emissions cuts.
