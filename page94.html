#856. I Put KFC &amp; Popeyes’ Chicken Sandwiches Head-To-Head—Here’s The Winner

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Few fast food rivalries are as serious as KFC versus Popeyes. Both chains built their reputations on fried chicken, and both offer a classic chicken sandwich that keeps things simple: a crispy chicken fillet, pickles, mayo, and a toasted bun. On paper, they're practically identical. But after trying both, I found that the differences are much bigger than you'd expect.

I've always believed that a great fried chicken sandwich comes down to more than just the chicken. The breading needs to be crispy, the meat juicy and well-seasoned, and the bun and toppings should complement everything without getting in the way. And considering these are two of the biggest names in fried chicken, I had pretty high expectations.

So I put their classic chicken sandwiches head-to-head to see which one actually deserves your order.

I'll give KFC credit where it's due: This sandwich absolutely nailed the texture.

The Classic Chicken Sandwich features a breaded chicken fillet, pickles, mayo, and a toasted bun. From the first bite, I was impressed by how juicy the chicken was. The meat was incredibly tender, and the breading provided a satisfying crunch that worked beautifully with the soft bun.

What I appreciated most was how well-proportioned everything felt. The chicken fillet was substantial without being overwhelming, the mayo added just enough creaminess, and the bun didn't overpower the rest of the sandwich.

Unfortunately, the flavor didn't quite match the texture.

For a chain that's practically synonymous with seasoned fried chicken, I expected a little more from the breading. It wasn't bland, but it lacked the bold, savory flavor that makes me want another bite. The chicken was certainly enjoyable, but the seasoning felt a little too restrained for my liking.

Popeyes' chicken sandwich made a huge splash when it debuted in 2019, and after revisiting it, I completely understand why.

The Classic Chicken Sandwich features a breaded chicken fillet, pickles, mayo, and a toasted brioche bun. It's a simple combination, but each component pulls its weight.

The biggest difference between this and KFC's sandwich was the seasoning. Popeyes' chicken had a much more pronounced savory flavor, with a crisp coating that added depth to every bite.

The mayo added a creamy tang that balanced the fried chicken's richness. Meanwhile, the pickles brought a bright, crisp bite that cut through the heavier elements and kept the sandwich from feeling too rich.

But I also have to give some serious credit to the brioche bun. It was soft, slightly rich, and perfectly proportioned to the chicken. There wasn't an overwhelming amount of bread, and the fillet, mayo, and pickles all came together in just the right ratio.

Popeyes didn't deliver the juiciest chicken of the two, but it absolutely delivered the better flavor. And when you're eating a fried chicken sandwich, that's a pretty important distinction.

This was actually a closer competition than I expected.

KFC had the edge when it came to juiciness, and I genuinely loved the texture of its chicken. If I were judging these sandwiches on texture alone, KFC might have taken the win.

But flavor is ultimately what keeps me coming back, and that's where Popeyes pulled ahead.

Its well-seasoned breading gave the chicken a much more satisfying savory punch, while the tangy mayo, crisp pickles, and soft brioche bun brought everything together.

KFC made a very good chicken sandwich, but Popeyes made the one I'd actually go out of my way to order again.

SpaceX is shooting for new heights with its upcoming Starship test flight, and the booster tapped for the mission is in its final stretch of preparations before the mission.

The Super Heavy booster for Flight Test 15, Booster 22, underwent a static test fire on Sunday (Oct. 25), igniting all of its Raptor 3 engines for about 15 seconds. "Full duration 33-engine static fire for the Super Heavy booster preparing for Flight 15," SpaceX said in a post on X.

The test is a beginning of final checks on the first-stage vehicle, which will be mated with the Flight 15 upper stage, Ship 42, ahead of launch, but not before the spacecraft completes some tests of its own.

Ship 42 will also complete a series of tests as the next Starship launch approaches, including a static engine fire and final systems checks. SpaceX CEO Elon Musk has stated that SpaceX will attempt the first launch tower catch of a ship upper stage on Flight 15, which, if successful, would be a significant step forward for the company's goal to mature Starship to full reusability.

SpaceX's confidence in its ability to catch both the Super Heavy and Ship stages has been building this year, following three launches so far in 2026. Its most recent (Sept. 28) mission, Flight 14, reached orbit for the first time and performed the successful deployment of 26 next-generation Starlink V3 satellites, marking the first operational payloads delivered to orbit by the spacecraft. Starlink V3 satellites are too big to fit aboard the company's workhorse Falcon 9 rocket.

Flight 14 wasn't without setbacks, however. An upper-stage Raptor engine shut down prematurely during ascent, prompting SpaceX to cut the planned 10-hour mission short. Ship, nevertheless, completed a controlled reentry and splashed down in the Pacific Ocean about three hours after liftoff.

Flight 15 could carry another batch of V3 Starlinks, though SpaceX has not officially confirmed a payload. Beginning to upgrade its global network with the fast V3 satellites, however, is a major reason SpaceX is pushing to bring Starship into regular service.

Full duration 33-engine static fire for the Super Heavy booster preparing for Flight 15 https://t.co/7eqbC4h0FhOctober 25, 2026

Starship's development also has implications for NASA's Artemis program, which has contracted SpaceX to develop a modified Ship upper stage capable of landing astronauts on the surface of the moon.

NASA recently updated the timeline of milestones SpaceX needs to accomplish with Starship before a lunar lander version will qualify to carry crews, with a significant demonstration mission coming up next summer on Artemis III.

The next Artemis mission will launch astronauts to Earth orbit aboard NASA's Orion spacecraft, which will dock with both lunar lander vehicles over the course of about two weeks (in addition to Starship, NASA contracted Blue Origin's Blue Moon spacecraft as a another lunar lander for the program).

SpaceX is also working toward an uncrewed lunar landing demonstration ahead of NASA's planned return of astronauts to the moon on Artemis IV, targeted for 2028. Which lander will be chosen for that mission is still undetermined, and SpaceX and Blue Origin are both still vying for the first crewed Artemis landing.

Beyond Flight 15, SpaceX hopes to demonstrate additional milestones before the end of 2026, including Starship's first launch from Florida and potentially the first re-flight of an upgraded V3 Super Heavy booster.

Booster 22's engine test marks the beginning in a series of tests leading up Flight 15, which, if recent Starship campaigns are any example, could lift off sometime in mid-to-late November.

#352. Aeromexico Adds Three U.S. Routes

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Aeromexico will launch three new nonstop routes to the U.S. next year as part of its long-running collaboration with Delta.

The Mexican flag carrier will connect Mexico City with Orange County, California, Fort Lauderdale, Florida, and Portland, Oregon. All three connections will operate daily, with Orange County starting on Jan. 11, 2027, Fort Lauderdale on Jan. 14, and Portland on Jan. 21.

Aeromexico will be the only airline in the world offering nonstop service between Mexico City and each of the three U.S. destinations.

The carrier will use Boeing 737 MAX 8 aircraft with 166 passenger seats – 16 in Premier Class, 18 in AM Plus, and 132 in Economy.

Aeromexico and Delta said the new connections will strengthen their Joint Cooperation Agreement, which allows for coordination on scheduling, pricing, and capacity, as well as revenue sharing on routes between the U.S. and Mexico.

If you’ve been wondering for a while whether you should be working with a financial advisor, here’s a thought to consider: Go meet with one. It could bring clarity to your finances, even if you decide you don’t want to take the next step to hire someone.

That’s the path my San Francisco-based colleague Lauren Schwahn took. It’s been a while since she wrote about her first visit with a financial advisor. I spoke with her recently to hear what still stands out about that experience.

Her story may sound familiar: She felt unsure of her family’s financial progress or their next steps, because they were caught up in daily life, trying to make good choices for today and for their future. What did she learn? Even just one conversation with a professional — and the process of preparing for that conversation — can help you organize your thoughts and figure out what matters most to you.

I appreciate that Lauren kept an open mind when meeting with an advisor and didn’t feel obligated to take it any further. The point of that first meeting is to get to know the advisor and what they could help you with. If you discover it’s not a good fit or your needs have changed, the meeting has served its purpose.

My conversation with Lauren has been edited for length and clarity.

Taryn Phaneuf: What were you initially hoping that an advisor would help you with?

Lauren Schwahn: I think part of it was reassurance. We wanted to see whether or not we were on the right track or if there was something glaringly obvious that we were missing when it came to handling our finances. Part of it was curiosity. I had talked to so many financial advisors for work, but I never really knew what that experience looked like on the other side. So I wanted to explore that further.

Back then we were also toying with the idea of buying a home. That was one thing we wanted to talk about with somebody who was in our area. We're in San Francisco, which is like one of the worst housing markets in the country. Things have changed since then, but that's where we were at the time.

LS: I think at the time it was more just can we make that happen and if we did, what would that look like? Would we be close to being in a position to actually save enough for a down payment or do we have some steps that we need to take to get there?

#4241. What Makes AI-Written Stories Feel Off?

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Publish AI, ML & data-science insights to a global community of data professionals.

And how we can turn that feeling into insights

We live in an interesting technological era, with so many questions about the morality and legality of using AI for creative content. As such, many researchers have taken it upon themselves to find patterns in what AI generates. They tried to put into numbers what we feel when we read or see something AI-generated.

Basically, they provided an answer to the question, “can something be too neat? Too clean? Too to the point?”

To answer this, let’s take a step back and imagine reading a short story about a woman returning to her childhood home after twenty years.

"She finds a letter hidden behind a loose floorboard. The letter reveals a family secret. She confronts her mother, finally understands why her childhood was so complicated, and learns an important lesson about forgiveness"

At first sight, that sounds like a perfectly reasonable storyline! The plot makes sense. The characters seem to have logical motivations, and the ending ties everything together.

Great, but what if we propose another version of that same story? What if the story opens at the mother’s funeral? We jump backward twenty years (because who doesn’t love a time jump?!), then forward three days, then sideways into a memory that initially seems irrelevant. The letter contradicts something we learned earlier. The daughter makes a decision that might be brave or selfish, depending on whom you ask. And the ending refuses to explain what any of it means.

Now, can we say for sure which one sounds more human?

That question is subjective, of course. Many human writers love tidy endings, and many AI-generated stories are delightfully strange. But what if these intuitions reflect measurable patterns?

In this article, I want to walk you through a fascinating paper I read recently that answers that question. The paper is titled StoryScope: Investigating Idiosyncrasies in AI Fiction. Instead of looking for suspicious vocabulary, repetitive sentence structures, or everyone’s favorite AI punctuation mark (and mine!), the em dash, the authors investigated the narrative decisions that shape a story.

And what they found was quite interesting! Without further ado, I'll walk you through the paper.

A lot of AI detection work focuses on how text is written. They look for certain words, sentence predictability, and whether the prose follows recognizable statistical patterns.

These signals can be useful. The problem is that they can also change. A language model can learn to avoid common AI clichés, a human editor can rewrite a suspicious paragraph, and a good prompt can make the machine-generated prose sound much more natural.

We can represent a piece of writing using a feature vector:

x=[x1,x2,…,xn]\mathbf{x} = [x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n]x=[x1​,x2​,…,xn​]

Where each component might represent something measurable about the text, such as word frequency, sentence length, punctuation, or syntactic structure, but if a new model changes its vocabulary and sentence structure, the feature distributions shift, and our previously useful decision boundary may no longer work as expected.

StoryScope asks whether we can look beyond these surface-level features by asking questions like:

How predictable is the sequence of events?

How much does the narrator explain the meaning of events?

How morally straightforward are the characters’ decisions?

These features describe a story's architecture, and changing it is a much bigger job than replacing a few words.

Here is where the research becomes interesting from a data science perspective, because how do we quantize “creativity”? The StoryScope researchers built a pipeline to do exactly that.

They used a dataset that contains 10,272 writing prompts. For each prompt, they paired a human-written story with five AI-generated versions from Claude, DeepSeek, Gemini, GPT, and Kimi. So, 61,632 stories!

The stories averaged roughly 4,753 words, long enough to study narrative structure at a level shorter texts might not support. They then developed a 3-step process to extract 304 narrative features from each story.

Step 1: Turn prose into structured representations.

An AI model extracts information such as characters, motivations, events, settings, plot structure, and temporal organization. Instead of comparing two stories solely through their original wording, the system creates structured representations of their content.

Stage 2: Compare stories written from the same prompt.

Because each human story has five AI-generated counterparts, the researchers can compare stories that start from the same premise. This helps control for contextual differences.

Stage 3: Convert narrative observations into measurable features.

The researchers identify recurring differences and formulate them as questions with defined answer options. For example, a feature might ask whether the protagonist’s choices or external events drive a story's resolution, or how often the narrative jumps across time.

The result is a feature vector describing a story’s narrative decisions, which the researchers then used XGBoost classifiers and feature-importance analysis to identify the decisions that best distinguish human and AI writing.

The researchers trained a classifier using narrative features while deliberately excluding stylistic features, which achieved a 93.2% macro-F1 score on the human-versus-AI classification task.

The model also achieved 68.4% macro-F1 when distinguishing among all six sources (human authors and the five AI models), which is harder to do! Because detecting whether something is AI-generated is not the same as identifying which AI model generated it.

You might be wondering now: what did they find? They distinguished three patterns AI uses when writing fiction.

They found that AI-generated stories explicitly stated their themes 77% of the time, compared with 52% for human-written stories. Think about the difference between these two endings:

She finally understood that forgiveness was not about forgetting the past, but about finding the courage to move forward.

She folded the letter, placed it back beneath the floorboard, and left the house before sunrise.

The first explicitly spells out the moral of the story, and the other leaves it open to the reader’s interpretation. Sometimes a story benefits from spelling out its message, but when a model repeatedly chooses explicit explanations, that tendency becomes a measurable pattern.

They also found that AI stories were more likely to have straightforward causal chains and fewer subplots. About 79% of AI stories had no subplots, compared with 57% of human stories. AI-generated stories also more frequently resolved conflicts through the protagonist’s choices: 69% versus 46% for human-written stories.

By comparison, human stories were more likely to embrace morally ambiguous characters, nonlinear timelines, and unresolved tensions. For example, consider two mystery novels: In the first, every clue points to the same conclusion. Each revelation follows logically from the previous one, and the detective explains the solution at the end.

In the second, the first chapter describes a funeral. The next takes us fifteen years back. A seemingly unimportant conversation becomes crucial halfway through the novel, and the final revelation makes an earlier scene mean something entirely different.

The second structure is harder to summarize as a chain of cause and effect, which human writing tends to follow. This makes AI writing have an interesting narrative shape, a very uniform shape.

One of the more amusing findings was about sensory descriptions! AI-generated stories more frequently conveyed emotion through bodily sensations and physical metaphors: 81% versus 38% for human stories.

Think of the familiar literary combination of a tightening chest, cold sweat, trembling hands, and the room suddenly seeming darker. In their dataset, human writers often used explicit emotion labels rather than physical descriptions.

Many people generate AI-written text and then edit it to sound more human. They can remove clichés, vary sentence lengths, replace generic transitions, and delete paragraphs that repeat what the previous paragraph already said.

But what happens if you improve the text without changing the story underneath?

The researchers answered this using a rewriting method called LAMP (an editing approach proposed by different researchers in May 2025). They applied surface-level edits that remove common AI tells to 278 Gemini-generated stories. This achieved 95.5% macro-F1 on the original stories and 93.9% on the edited versions! Not a very significant difference because it only changed the writing style, not the underlying story structure.

One important qualification, though: the editing experiment covered 278 stories from one model, Gemini. It is evidence that narrative features can survive a particular kind of stylistic editing, not proof that every AI-generated story will remain detectable after every kind of revision.

As a researcher, I state a study's limitations first. No study is perfect or capable of encompassing the whole picture. So the most important thing here is that StoryScope studied long-form fiction. Its results don't automatically apply to emails, essays, documentation, social media posts, or short articles.

Second, the dataset is constructed in a specific way: it pairs stories from human writers with AI-generated stories based on reconstructed prompts. This is a controlled comparison, but it doesn't reflect every way people use AI in the real world.

Third, the researchers assign narrative features using language models! Finally, as always, a high classification score doesn't prove that a particular person used AI.

The study’s 93.2% macro-F1 result applies to its evaluation setup. It does not mean a detector can examine any novel text and reliably confirm its authorship and originality.

The paper also reports stronger results from supervised text-based baselines, including near-perfect classification scores. That matters because the contribution of StoryScope is not that narrative analysis beats every other detector. Instead, interpretable narrative features retain substantial predictive power without relying on surface style.

It is tempting to turn research like this into an oversimplified conclusion: “AI-generated text is always bad.” I don’t think the data supports that. AI-generated fiction can be enjoyable; a linear plot can be exactly what a reader wants. Explicit themes aren't a literary flaw, and ambiguity isn't automatically a sign of genius.

Language models can produce convincing sentences while still showing systematic preferences in how they organize stories. We see that all the time when we deal with ML models! A model can perform well on a task while exhibiting biases in the patterns it uses to get there. Looking only at the final output score can hide those patterns.

The authors argue that evaluating generative AI requires more than asking whether its output sounds plausible; they also encourage us to think about how to make a story more interesting by going beyond replacing predictable words with unusual ones, and instead question the structure itself.

Perhaps the next step in generative AI development isn't just producing more convincing words; it is learning when not to take the obvious narrative path. Which, I would argue, is a much more interesting problem than counting em dashes. So writers (I mainly mean me) can go back to using em dashes without being accused of using AI.

Human Rights Watch wrote to the Houthis on October 21 about attacks on the road but did not receive a response.

Control over Taizz has been divided since 2015, when Houthi forces entered the governorate and surrounded its capital, Taizz city, Yemen’s third largest city. The city has remained under Yemeni government control, but the Houthis have cut off vital roads connecting it to the rest of the country, notably the port city of Aden, the Yemeni government’s current capital. 

While the laws of war do not prohibit sieges directed at military objectives, besieging forces are prohibited from using starvation of civilians as a method of warfare and must allow and facilitate humanitarian relief. Human Rights Watch has found that the Houthis’ blocking of roads into Taizz has severely restricted civilians’ ability to move in and out of the city and impeded the flow of essential goods, including water, and humanitarian access to the civilian population.

At about 4:00 a.m. on October 18, Houthi forces detonated explosives on the Hayjat al-Abd road in the town of al-Turba, significantly damaging a portion of the road. Residents in the area as well as two other informed sources said that the Houthis had been in control of al-Turba, about 75 kilometers south of Taizz city, since October 4. Since then, there had been no ground fighting in the area, including on the night of the attack. Nor had there been any indications that government forces or other armed groups were trying to re-enter the area, indicating that there was not another group that could have carried out the attack. 

Another source said his relative told him: “It was a massive and terrifying explosion—everyone woke up. Rocks reached all the way to my house.” 

Human Rights Watch verified and geolocated four photographs and a video from sources showing a damaged culvert on the switchbacks of the Hayjat al-Abd road. The damage is consistent with a subsurface detonation of explosives. 

Two weeks earlier, apparent Houthi forces had attacked the Hayjat al-Abd road about 85 kilometers southeast of Taizz city. A civil society worker in Taizz who spoke with several people knowledgeable about the attack said that on the night and early morning of September 30-October 1, Houthi forces and Yemeni government-aligned forces fought on several fronts around Taizz. The sources said that Houthi forces infiltrated and reached the Hayjat al-Abd road, placing and detonating explosives in a culvert under the road just past Souq al-Wahati, a local market. The Houthi forces then withdrew. 

Two other sources gave similar accounts based on communications with local residents. Human Rights Watch was unable to conclusively determine that Houthi forces carried out the attack. 

Human Rights Watch verified and geolocated two additional photographs posted on social media on October 1 showing a damaged culvert on the Hayjat al-Abd road at the village of al-Muharraqah, where the road starts ascending to Taizz city. The damage to the culvert in the images is consistent with a subsurface detonation of explosives. Satellite imagery captured at 6:57 a.m. on October 1 shows damage to the culvert across the full width of the road. 

Government forces quickly retook the area after the first attack and later filled the destroyed portion of the road with dirt to allow vehicles to cross. Two sources said that government authorities had largely closed down the road to avoid further attacks on vehicles, though some vehicles continued to pass through. 

Since the October 1 attack, all roads into and out of Taizz have largely been blocked by Yemeni government-affiliated and Houthi forces due to hostilities. However, before the second attack, some supplies, including flour, had still been taken to Taizz city on the Hayjat al-Abd road, said two civil society workers in the area.

However, the United Nations World Food Programme (WFP) said on October 14 that they had not been able to access the “more than 600,000 people in Taiz who depend on humanitarian assistance,” which was “especially alarming” since most of Taizz’s districts are considered to have emergency levels of hunger. The WFP also said that “wheat flour stocks are nearly exhausted” in the city and that “remaining food supplies in local markets may last only a short time if access constraints continue.”

Under international humanitarian law, roads are presumptively civilian objects that become military objectives if by their nature, location, purpose, or use, they make an effective contribution to military action and their destruction, capture, or neutralization provides a definite military advantage at the time. Even if the Hayjat al-Abd road qualifies as a military objective, an attack would be unlawfully disproportionate if the expected incidental civilian harm would be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated. Given the road’s critical role in Taizz city civilians’ access to food, water, medicine, and other basic needs, the assessment should account for the reasonably foreseeable consequences of damaging or closing it. 

The Houthis should have known, particularly given the UN’s repeated warnings, that supplies in Taizz were running out and that further closures after October 1 from attacks on the road could have grave effects for besieged Taizz civilians relying on the road for necessities.

International humanitarian law also prohibits attacking, destroying, or rendering useless objects indispensable to the survival of a civilian population. The October 18 attack disrupted the remaining major commercial supply route to Taizz city. Whether it violated this prohibition depends on whether it was carried out for the purpose of denying civilians access to objects indispensable to their survival. 

Serious violations of the laws of war ordered or committed with criminal intent—that is, deliberately or recklessly—are war crimes.

“The fighting in Taizz could have dire consequences for the hundreds of thousands of civilians in need of aid,” Jafarnia said. “All warring parties should take care to protect civilians in Yemen who have already experienced considerable suffering over 12 years of conflict.”
