#2885. Walking and Canoeing from Kansas to the Gulf of Mexico

In April, Robert Cowden, 52, set off on foot from New Haven, Kansas, on a two-stage journey across the United States. First, he planned to walk nearly 2,000km to Pittsburgh, then canoe 3,200km downriver to the Gulf of Mexico. With him is his dog, Enzo, a pointer-mix.

Robert Cowden and Enzo. Photo: Enzo The Great: An American Expedition

When he left New Haven with Enzo, Cowden was at a low point. His battle with alcohol addiction had cost him his job, car, and home. Entering a residential treatment program would leave Enzo with no place to go. Now sober and with housing waiting for him after the end of his journey, Cowden sees his expedition as part of the larger effort to rebuild his life.

During the walking stage, Cowden towed his camping gear and supplies in a cart, which Enzo could ride in to take a break from walking. Setting out with very little, Cowden has relied on the kindness of strangers, both online and on the trail, to continue his expedition.

“The number one warning you’re going to get on a walk across America is, ‘Be careful, there’s creepy people and bad people,'” Cowden recalled, speaking to a local KOMU 8 reporter while passing through Missouri. Instead, he’s been overwhelmingly met with generosity and helpfulness.

Cowden shows the wear on his trail shoes, as Enzo looks on. Photo: Enzo The Great: An American Expedition

Cowden completed the walking stage in September. Now, he’s on the Ohio River and hopes to reach the Gulf by the end of 2026. While the second stage lets him and Enzo get off their feet, it has its own challenges. They launched the canoe into the Allegheny River at Pittsburgh. It soon joined the Ohio, and lengthy stretches of slow water pushed Cowden to paddle continuously.

It’s 1,579km down the Ohio to its confluence with the Mississippi in Illinois, which Cowden and Enzo aim to complete in under 40 days. They’re currently on pace, averaging about 20km a day.

Cowden posts regular updates on Facebook and on the expedition’s webpage.

The Supreme Court on Tuesday declined to restore a Biden-era regulation that allows immigration judges to suspend some deportation proceedings. In a brief, unsigned order, the court turned down a request from three cities and a group that provides legal services to immigrants to pause an agreement between Texas and the U.S. Department of Justice that threw out the rule.

The regulation at the center of the dispute, which provides what is known as “administrative closure,” was adopted by the Executive Office for Immigration Review, part of the Department of Justice, in 2024. Under it, immigration judges may suspend some deportation proceedings – for example, to allow a crime victim or witness to seek relief, such as asylum, from the U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, which is a separate agency.

In June of this year, Texas went to federal court in Wichita Falls, where its case was assigned to Chief U.S. District Judge Reed O’Connor – the only federal judge in that area. Calling the administrative-closure rule “one part of the Biden-Harris Administration’s campaign to undermine our immigration laws,” it alleged that the EOIR does not have the authority to adopt the administrative-closure rule. Instead, it wrote, federal law requires an immigration judge, at the end of a proceeding, to “decide whether an alien is removable from the United States.”

Just a few hours after the case was filed, the DOJ and Texas asked O’Connor to enter a final judgment for the state. The DOJ also asked him both to throw out the rule altogether and to issue an order making clear that the EOIR could not adopt a similar rule in the future. O’Connor granted those requests on the same day.

On Aug. 7, three cities with large immigrant populations – Baltimore, Maryland; New Haven, Connecticut; and Columbus, Ohio – and Centro Legal de la Raza, which provides legal services to immigrants, filed an emergency motion to join the case so that they could defend the administrative-closure rule on appeal, when neither Texas nor the federal government intended to do so. O’Connor denied their request on the ground that they lacked a legal right to sue, known as standing. In particular, he rejected the cities’ contention that the elimination of the rule would cost them money because their taxpayers are likely to be deported.

The challengers then went to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit, which rejected their request to block the consent judgment between Texas and the Department of Justice and thereby reinstate the administrative-closure rule while their appeal continued.

On Oct. 9, the challengers came to the Supreme Court, calling the consent agreement the product of “egregious collusion” and asking the justices to step in. Their attorney, Joshua Salzman of Democracy Forward, argued that permitting the consent agreement to stand could have widespread implications. “If a judgment procured through such open collusion is allowed to remain in effect,” he wrote, “any agency can avoid the strictures of the” federal law governing administrative agencies “by advertising its unwillingness to defend a disfavored regulation and by immediately acquiescing to nationwide relief.”

Urging the Supreme Court to leave the agreement in place, U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer contended that the challengers’ request to pause O’Connor’s ruling “attempts to improperly leverage a clear-cut dispute over” the challengers’ efforts to join the case “into a far-reaching attack on the merits of” the court’s approval of the agreement. For its part, Texas told the justices that putting O’Connor’s ruling on hold “would revive a rule the district court declared unlawful, renew the fiscal and sovereign injuries Texas alleged, and unsettle a final judgment accepted by the actual parties.”

Four days after the case was fully briefed, the court denied the challengers’ request without comment.

In this blogpost we discuss why this new approach is both better and worse than pre-existing approaches at finding panics.

Before discussing the Call Graph Analyzer, we should first consider why panics are useful, and in what cases it might be beneficial to minimize or perhaps even completely eliminate any possible panic. This topic has been explored extensively by others [1][2][3][4], but it is useful to summarize here. Lets start with a brief description:

These unrecoverable error conditions may either be caused by a bug in the implementation of a function, or constitute violated entry conditions for a function (so a bug in the function itself or a mistake by the caller of that function).

An example is calling 1.strict_add(u32::MAX), which would result in a panic in response to the caller passing invalid arguments to the operation. Similarly a normal addition 1 + u32::MAX in Rust will panic if overflow-checks are enabled.

However striving for zero panics is very hard and quite foolish: code is rarely void of assumptions. Returning Result everywhere just pushes the burden up the call stack, until you will abort anyway at the root. This essentially yields the same behaviour as if you were to panic.

It is useful to make panics explicit though. It is better to know about your panic paths and to reason about them, than to just accidentally include panics and ignore that they exist. Having regression analysis on your panic paths is also very useful, notifying you of any new panic paths introduced in your code.

Striving for no unrecoverable error conditions is extremely hard in practice. Generally it is better to implement top-level recovery mechanisms. In embedded this would entail using a Watchdog timer which resets the device if it is stuck, and a recovery image to restore the device to a working state. On a host machine this could mean catching unwinding panics using catch_unwind. Note that the latter is not fool-proof and comes with a few caveats. Externally accessible services should be redundant and fast to recover.

In conclusion, it is useful to find unintentional panics.

Currently, ways to extract callgraphs or detect panics include:

You can download any of these projects, and with the exception of Charon (which is not explicitly meant for this usecase) it is very easy to get started.

Linker-based panic detection methods work by triggering an error in the linker when it tries to link in a special panic handler. This detection approach only works if not a single panic is included in your binary. It also only works on binaries and not on libraries.

ELF debug info analysis looks at the DWARF debug info that is included in ELF artifacts like binaries and Rust libraries compiled to object files. It is limited in what callgraph information it can provide: the panic machinery can be completely inlined or disappear during the linker phase.

And finally we have HIR, MIR and rust-analyzer based approaches, which use the various Intermediate Representations (IRs) of your code generated by the compiler (and rust-analyzer) to extract the callgraph from. In a sense all these approaches take your original Rust code, have the compiler process it, and spit out what it thinks is happening. Theoretically these approaches work on any Rust crate.

The HIR-based analysis can only analyze the code in your specific crate. This analysis can check if you explicitly write panic! in your own crate. However, it is unaware of any code in your dependencies, such as the standard library. Hence it can not trigger on panics that occur within dependencies. The compiler can also insert assertions like bound checks, which are not even a call to panic until very late in the code generation pass depending on your compiler flags.

The MIR-based analysis is ideal, as it can theoretically see all code from dependencies, before they are optimized away, including panics originating in the standard library. Hence Call Graph Analyzer uses this approach. The only downside is that this analysis is done before LLVM is able to remove unreachable code, and hence we get some false positives.

All these previous approaches take what the compiler is already doing when compiling your crate, and interrupt the compilation process to look at a snapshot of the compilation state to find out whether you are calling a panic.

The rust-analyzer approach is slightly different, as it does not depend on the compiler at all. Rust-analyzer has duplicated the Rust-HIR-MIR pipeline from the compiler, which they adapted for their process. This separate pipeline for rust-analyzer is pretty fast, can be stored in memory and supports updating the state while you are actively writing and changing your Rust code. However, it can not always analyze code that rustc is happy to compile. Most notably it often struggles with complex proc_macros like we use a ton of in Rust Embedded. As an end-user you notice this when your code editor stops giving any helpful suggestions. (The team behind rust-analyzer is working hard to prevent this!) The upside of rust-analyzer is that it does not depend on the Rust compiler.

We decided on a MIR-based callgraph exporter, which became the Call Graph Analyzer (CGA). Next up, we will discuss the design and functionality of CGA.

CGA extracts the callgraph directly from MIR as a rustc-driver. Clippy similarly is a rustc-driver. Like Clippy, CGA therefore depends on a specific version of the rust compiler, which gets us to the first real downside of CGA:

A note of caution: CGA compiles your crate with a specific (probably different) version of the rustc compiler, standard library, and potentially different compiler flags. Hence you will not get the exact callgraph as included in your binary.

Having a specific (nightly) version of rustc as a dependency using rustc-private also means that CGA can not be installed as a crate from crates.io. Instead the repository needs to be checked out locally, after which cargo install --path . needs to be run (which uses a specific version of the Rust toolchain automatically).

This user experience might be improved when rustc_public contains everything that CGA needs. Though, that scenario isn't really likely. Instead, if CGA becomes popular enough, it might be maintained alongside rustc, just like Clippy currently has this special status. Any breaking change in rustc is also applied to Clippy, and you can install Clippy for your specific toolchain using Rustup. For more information on this, you can watch the excellent talk on "Out of tree access to the compiler state" by Alona Enraght-Moony.

As a rustc-driver, the CGA binary has the same API as rustc itself. You can use cargo to run CGA using cargo cga check. Internally rustc will run as if you were to run cargo check. However CGA does some additional work to gather the call graph, which for a typical crate means that compilation with CGA is roughly 2x-3x as slow as running cargo check.

From these roots outward we perform a reachability analysis by walking through the MIR tree. Every time a function is called, that function is also explored. Thus all unused functions are not visited. In Rust, like in C++, a function with Generic arguments is monomorphized when it is called using the concrete types used to call it.

Thus we do not only gather the called functions, but the concrete instantiation of each function, as the implementations can differ wildly depending on the type arguments passed.

Having detailed what CGA can and can not do, it is useful to know there are some caveats and downsides to the approach CGA uses.

Traversing monomorphized MIR is not trivial. Resolving vtables is quite finnicky; the compiler inserts shims sometimes and actual calls are inserted later; coroutines need to be unpacked to find the async paths; and there are implicit assertions which eventually also end up being a panic. All of which is to say: the analysis is hard and it is inevitable that some parts of it are not completely correct. When the toolchain is updated, the resulting MIR can vary dramatically, which also has an impact on the call graph. It might be that down the line some part of the analysis breaks if rustc works differently.

This is to be expected: Rust MIR is unstable and will remain to be so for the foreseable future. Consequently CGA will be a bit fragile.

Depending on your compiler flags but in particular your opt-level, the MIR CGA gathers contains panics that are unreachable.

For example, the expression 1 + 1 has an implicit overflow bounds check included. If you were to const evaluate the expression, it is exceedingly obvious that the expression can never panic. With opt-level=2 (optimize for speed) this expression is optimized to 2 by an earlier compiler pass before CGA gets the MIR. However with opt-level="z" (optimize for size) this panic path is included in the MIR. Instead, the optimization is left to LLVM to perform (which it does).

Your compiler and cargo options really matter with regards to the callgraph being emitted, similarly to how they matter to the final binary generated by rustc and the linker.

I advise people to use opt-level=2 for CGA, even if the shipped binary uses something different, as generally no reachable panic will be missing with opt-level=2 (thus fewer false positives and no false negatives).

CGA has been tested on a few real-world binaries, and typically it works out of the gate. Sometimes however it does not. This is either because the project does not compile with build-std yet (which CGA requires), or because the build.rs expects the application to be built with stock rustc and not with a rustc-driver like CGA (example).

If CGA does not work for your project, or if the callgraph does not seem to be correct, please open an issue! The tooling can only evolve by testing it against real software.

Structure. Debian already understands how to have packages for multiple architectures (ABIs; Debian "ports") installed at once. For example, dpkg --add-architecture i386; apt update; apt install bash:i386 installs a 32-bit version of bash, replacing the usual 64-bit version; you can do apt install bash:amd64 to revert to the 64-bit version. Meanwhile the 32-bit libraries and 64-bit libraries are installed in separate locations, basically /lib/i386-linux-gnu or /usr/lib/i386-linux-gnu vs. /lib/x86_64-linux-gnu or /usr/lib/x86_64-linux-gnu. (On Debian 11 and newer, and on Ubuntu 22.04 and newer, /lib is symlinked to /usr/lib.)

I'm following this model for plugging Fil-C into Debian: the goal is for apt install bash:amd64fil0 to install a Fil-C-compiled (amd64fil0) version of bash, replacing the usual (amd64) version of bash, while the amd64 and amd64fil0 libraries are installed in separate locations.

[Filian uses amd64filc0, with libraries in /usr/lib/x86_64-linux-gnufilc0.]

The include-file complication. Debian expects library packages compiled for multiple ABIs to all provide the same include files: for example, /usr/include/ncurses.h is provided by libncurses-dev:i386, libncurses-dev:amd64, etc. This is safe because Debian forces libncurses-dev:i386 and libncurses-dev:amd64 and so on to all have the same version. An occasional package with ABI-dependent include files can still use /usr/include/x86_64-linux-gnu etc.

Fil-C instead omits /usr/include in favor of a Fil-C-specific directory (which will typically be different from /usr/include: even if Fil-C is compiled with glibc, probably the glibc version won't be the same as in /usr/include). This difference is the top source of messiness below. I'm planning to tweak the Fil-C driver to use /usr/include on Debian. [This is done in Filian.]

#619. Brain stimulation changes how people perceive women’s bodies

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers found that gentle brain stimulation changed how participants recognized sexualized images of women during controlled lab tests.

Turn a photograph upside down, and it usually becomes harder to recognize the person in it. But that change often causes less trouble when the photo shows a woman in a sexualized pose.

Psychologists interpret this smaller drop in recognition as a possible sign that viewers process these women more like objects.

A new experiment suggests that a weak electrical current across the forehead can change this pattern.

Ciro Civile of the University of Exeter led the study with Arwen Stephens, testing 76 university students.

Students who received the full stimulation showed no reliable gap between images of women and men, while those given a fake version still showed the usual difference.

“Sexual objectification means treating a person as a body to be looked at or used, rather than as a whole person. It’s dehumanizing and most often experienced by women,” Civile said.

Sexualized images are common in digital media. Viewing women mainly as sexual objects, and women judging themselves by their sexual appeal, has been linked to poorer mental and physical health.

When people recognize faces and bodies, they partly rely on how the features fit together, such as the distance between the eyes and mouth.

Turning a photo upside down makes those relationships harder to use, causing a drop in recognition called the inversion effect.

Flipping an object’s photo usually makes less difference.

In the past, researchers found that both male and female viewers showed a clear inversion effect for sexualized male bodies, but not female ones.

A 2015 study couldn’t repeat that finding after matching the photos for symmetry.

However, a 2022 analysis of more than 40 studies found overall evidence that sexualized female bodies show a smaller inversion effect than male bodies.

“Until now, studies had reduced this bias by changing the images or the viewer’s mindset – for example, by pixelating sexual body parts, giving humanizing information, or making people feel less powerful,” Civile said.

“We acted directly on the brain of the person doing the looking instead.”

The volunteers included 54 women and 22 men, split evenly into two groups.

Each student studied 32 photos of male and female models in black underwear for three seconds each, with half the photos upside down.

The models faced the camera in open poses intended to suggest sexual intimacy.

Students then saw the same photos mixed with 32 new ones and pressed a key to say whether each looked familiar.

The researchers placed two saltwater-soaked sponge electrodes on each student’s head, one on the left forehead and another above the right eyebrow. One group received a current of 1.5 milliamps for 10 minutes.

The other group received just 30 seconds, enough to mimic the tingling felt when stimulation starts. Neither the students nor the experimenters knew who received which treatment, and nobody reported side effects.

The researchers scored recognition by accounting for both correctly remembered photos and new photos that students mistakenly thought they had seen.

In the fake-treatment group, flipping men’s photos reduced the score by 0.69 points, compared with 0.27 for women’s photos.

That meant the upside-down penalty for women was less than half as large.

These students also recognized upright men better than upright women, matching the pattern earlier studies had used as a measure of objectification.

Students who received 10 minutes of stimulation correctly recognized about 74% of the upright women’s photos they had seen, compared with 67% in the other group.

They also mistook fewer new photos for familiar ones: 16%, compared with 24%.

The groups performed about equally well on upside-down photos of women and showed similar inversion effects for men.

But because the full-stimulation group remembered upright women better, its inversion effect for women reached 0.87 points, close to the 0.75 for men.

A second statistical check supported the finding of no gap, although it could not prove that the effects were equal.

The team tested students during stimulation or just afterward, so it did not establish how long the change lasted.

“Our findings are initial evidence that the way our visual system processes sexualized images of women can be changed, at least temporarily, by mildly stimulating the brain,” Civile said.

Civile’s lab previously used the same brain stimulation to study biases toward faces of one’s own race or age group.

In those experiments, stimulation made people worse at recognizing upright faces from their own group, whereas here it improved recognition of upright women.

A face illusion named after Margaret Thatcher may offer a clue: researchers flip the eyes and mouth within a face photo.

The altered face looks grotesque when upright, but almost normal when the whole picture is upside down.

These altered faces also show a smaller inversion effect. In a 2020 study, the same stimulation restored that effect by helping people recognize the upright versions.

Civile and Stephens suggest sexualized images may draw attention toward features many women share, such as sexualized body parts, leaving less attention for details that distinguish individuals.

This remains a hypothesis because the study measured recognition, not attention, and changes in memory or decision-making could also explain the results.

The team did not ask students which treatment they thought they received, so expectations may have differed between groups.

In an earlier study by Civile, however, people given the short dose performed like those given no stimulation.

The upside-down test is an indirect measure, and the researchers did not measure objectifying attitudes or behavior.

They also used only sexualized photos, so they cannot pin the gap on sexualization alone, especially because the male and female models may differ in body shape and pose.

Stimulation covered both learning and recognizing the photos, leaving it unclear which stage changed. The current also spreads across the head, so the team cannot identify the brain region responsible.

The researchers have shared their participant data and analysis code online.

Future studies need non-sexualized photos of women and men, plus direct measures of objectification, to find out whether stimulation changes objectification itself or just performance on this test.

The study is published in the journal Social Neuroscience.

#1045. The Cast of <i>The Gilded Age</i> Compared to the Real-Life Historical Figures

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The soapy, lavish drama The Gilded Age returns to HBO every Sunday for season four on November 1. The show welcomes back its star-studded cast, including Carrie Coon, Christine Baranski, Cynthia Nixon, and Denée Benton, among many others.

Amid fraud, new money, and rapid industrialization, The Gilded Age follows a not-so-fictional group of socialites as they become titans of American industry in the 1880s. It is a classic old-money versus new-money conflict, with bona fide, centuries-old drama while also weaving in the history of women’s rights, Reconstruction, and industrialization. To tell these stories, the series threads in real historical figures to add authenticity to this riveting historical fiction.

While not all characters share the names of their historical inspirations, they certainly share a likeness. Here are the real historical figures and their television counterparts featured in The Gilded Age.

The Gilded Age’s Bertha Russell (right) is based on Alva Vanderbilt (left), the ultra-rich and social climbing wife of railroad tycoon William Kissam Vanderbilt. At first, Alva and her family were looked down upon as gaudy and tasteless new-money folk. Over time, their lavish spending on social functions and dedication to fraternizing only with the most elite aristocrats paid off. Even the most exclusive society group, Mrs. Astor’s 400, opened to the family when Mrs. Astor realized that her family would sink on the social ladder if they did not attend the Vanderbilts’ grandiose parties. Eventually, so many industries and fortunes relied on the benevolence of the family that they became not only accepted, but revered by the oldest money in New York City. Similarly, Bertha is the wife of new-money railroad tycoon George Russell. She has an outwardly civil but internally vicious rivalry with the very same Mrs. Astor that Alva reckoned with. Carrie Coon embodies Alva in every step she takes up the social ladder, and in their matching white shirts and updos, the resemblance is indisputable.

While George Russell may appear to be based on Alva’s husband, William Kissam Vanderbilt, director Julian Fellowes revealed that he is actually based on Jay Gould, another new railroad tycoon of the 1880s. Not only did Gould have a marvelous beard that could rival George’s, he and his family had a similarly difficult time being accepted by Mrs. Astor and her old money circle, though eventually their exorbitant wealth became impossible to look down upon. The show also included a historical tidbit from Gould’s life. In season two, George had trouble with his workers unionizing, similarly to when Gould's workers went on strike during the Great Southwest railroad strike of 1886. The parallels in George and Gould’s history, social life, and yes, facial hair, cannot go unnoticed.

The real Consuelo Vanderbilt (left) and Gladys Russell, played by Taissa Farmiga (right), share a likeness and meek constitution, but the similarities don’t stop there. Consuelo was the daughter of Alva and William Kissam Vanderbilt, born into a wealthy family dominating the railroad industry, much like Gladys. Her romantic hopes were disrupted by her mother planning an advantageous betrothal. Consuelo was forced to marry the ninth Duke of Marlborough, who in turn, required her family to pay a $2.5 million dowry. While the wedding was a flawless affair of the richest decorations and the highest society, it became a loveless marriage, and the pair divorced in 1921 after having two children together. Gladys seems to be following the same character arc, with her mother dismissing her humble romantic interest and encouraging her to get to know the aristocratic Duke of Buckingham. Hopefully an equally opulent wedding will ensue on the show, though it would be preferable for Gladys to avoid the multi-million dollar dowry and turbulent marriage.

The Duke of Buckingham (right) from The Gilded Age appears to be inspired by Charles Spencer-Churchill, 9th Duke of Marlborough (left), who inherited his position in 1892 and immediately set off to marry into money and save himself from financial destitution. Alva Vanderbilt took the opportunity to have a Duchess in the family, and paid a dowry of $2.5 million to the Duke of Marlborough for him to wed her daughter, Consuelo. He was only in it for the money (shocker), and their marriage was annulled in 1921 after the pair had two sons. While the Duke of Buckingham and Gladys have been introduced in the show, the betrothal and doomed marriage have not yet come to pass.

Caroline Schermerhorn Astor (left) was a real person, and like her television counterpart (right), was the ultimate queen of New York society, terrorizing and looking down upon new money families. Her friction with Bertha Russell onscreen was pulled directly from her real-life rivalry with Alva Vanderbilt. She and her good friend, Ward McAlister, developed a real list called Mrs. Astor’s 400, which declared the 400 socially acceptable New York families. This number was not chosen at random; it was the precise number of people who could fit in Mrs. Astor’s ballroom. The list was even published by the New York Times in 1982. The names “Mr. Gould” and “Mrs. William K. Vanderbilt” did not appear on this infamous list, to no one's surprise. Neither did the name “Russell” in the fictional series. Donna Murphy’s shrewd portrayal of Mrs. Astor would send a shiver down the spine of even the loftiest New York aristocrat.

Ward McAllister (left) was a good friend of Mrs. Astor and an important voice in New York society in both the show and in real life. If it were not for his self-serving acceptance of the new-money families, the Vanderbilts and Morgans may have never been truly included with the old-money crowd. He was new money himself, and learned the culture of high society in Europe after earning his wealth in California. He faked it until he made it, and upon arrival in New York he appointed himself the authority on high society, without raising a hair of suspicion. He was the original “gossip girl,” writing about all of his rich friends’ happenings in the book Society as I Have Found It. Nathan Lane (right) mimicked McAllister’s southern accent, handlebar mustache, and dramatic flair to perfection.

Mamie Fish (played by Ashlie Atkinson)Don’t let the frown and frills fool you—Marian “Mamie” Fish (left) was the life of the party in New York society. She was known for her wit, fun-loving nature, and Gatsby-esque parties. She wasn’t the most wealthy of the New York elite, but after Mrs. Astor’s death, she and Alva Vanderbilt took her throne and served as the queens of New York high society. She is played by Ashlie Atkinson (right), who is the very embodiment of the festive socialite.

Timothy Thomas Fortune (left) was a radical journalist, publisher, and civil rights leader in both The Gilded Age and in real life. Born into slavery in Florida, he studied law and journalism at Howard University and found a career working at some of the country’s leading Black newspapers, including The People’s Advocate, The New York Age, and The New York Globe. He wrote books and spoke out against racial injustice his entire life. He worked with the NAACP, co-founded the Afro-American League in 1890, and mentored Booker T. Washington, who also appears in The Gilded Age. In the show, he is the editor of the fictional character Peggy, a fellow Black journalist. He is played by Sullivan Jones (right).

Clara Barton (left) was the heroic founder and first president of the American Red Cross. She worked as a nurse to provide aid to thousands of soldiers in the Civil War. President Abraham Lincoln helped her open the Missing Soldiers Office, which reconnected over 20,000 troops with their families. She was introduced early in season one while fundraising for the American Red Cross with some of the old-money women. She is played by Linda Emond (right), whose twisted updo, flower adorned hat, and lacy collar perfectly match the real Clara Barton.

The scandalous widow Arabella Huntington (left) had many parallels to Sylvia Chamberlain (right), a character played by Jeanne Tripplehorn in season one. While Sylvia and Arabella are not identical upon first glance, their stories are similar enough to make you do a double take. Huntington was the second wife to an extremely wealthy American industrialist, Collis Huntington, whose first wife died of cancer nine months before. Sylvia was in the same situation, having married Chamberlain suspiciously quickly after his first wife’s passing. Both women were their husbands' mistresses before the death of their first wives, and both had a son, widely known to be born out of wedlock. Even after laying low, both women’s reputations were controversial enough that the Astors barred them from society. After Huntington’s death, Arabella married her late husband’s nephew, Henry. Sylvia has yet to add that to her list of scandals.

John Pierpont Morgan is one of the newest characters in The Gilded Age, and was briefly introduced in the first episode of season three. His name is one of the most recognizable today, as the famous investment banker and financier who shaped the American economy during the Industrial Revolution. Bill Camp (right) mimics the pose and steely stare of J.P. Morgan’s real portrait (left).

Oscar Wilde is another easily recognizable name from The Gilded Age. At the time of his appearance, he would not have written The Picture of Dorian Gray or The Importance of Being Earnest quite yet, but he was already a celebrated Irish playwright. Jordan Sebastian Waller (right) portrays Wilde with the same whimsical smirk as the author and poet himself. His character appears in episode three of season two to celebrate the premiere of his first play, Vera; Or, The Nihilists.

Emily Warren Roebling (left), played by Liz Wisan (right), was instrumental in building the world’s longest suspension bridge at the time, the Brooklyn Bridge, which was referred to as the “eighth wonder of the world” both on the show and in real life. Emily’s husband, Washington A. Roebling was the original chief engineer of the project. He became ill and bedridden, so Mrs. Roebling took over, managing the crew and making technical decisions in his place. She later got her law degree when she was 56 and subsequently worked on many women’s rights cases.

Booker T. Washington was a civil rights pioneer and author during the Reconstruction era. He was born into slavery in 1856 and gained his freedom after the Emancipation Proclamation in 1863. His Atlanta Compromise speech of 1895 and autobiography Up From Slavery garnered national attention to the racial justice movement. In season two of The Gilded Age, Peggy and T. Thomas Fortune visited Washington at the Tuskegee Institute in Alabama, where he taught and led Black students of higher education. The episode delves into the difference between racial relations in the North versus the South, and includes scenes of violence perpetrated by the Ku Klux Klan.

Swedish soprano Christine Nilsson may have only appeared briefly on the show, but her impact on culture was significant. Edith Wharton’s 1920 novel The Age of Innocence opens with Nilsson performing Faust, and Nilsson is also believed to be the inspiration for Christine Daaé in The Phantom of the Opera. In season two of The Gilded Age, Bertha Russell hired Christine to sing as a guest star at her glamorous ball, in a successful attempt to one-up Mrs. Astor. Though there are no official images of her scene, Sarah Joy Miller sang the part beautifully.

#2732. A Free Soloist Got Stuck 500 Feet up a Cliff. New Hampshire Doesn’t Plan to Bill Him.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Volunteer climbers rescued a man from New Hampshire’s Whitehorse Ledge on October 25, after he climbed roughly 500 feet without a rope (Photo: New Hampshire Fish and Game/Courtesy)

A 30-year-old climber named William Carron was free soloing a route called Sliding Board on New Hampshire’s Whitehorse Ledge on October 25 when he had to stop. Nearly 500 feet above the ground, he no longer felt comfortable climbing up or down.

The incident sparked a dramatic rescue. Nine volunteer climbers helped Carron get back to the ground, according to a Facebook post from New Hampshire Fish and Game’s Law Enforcement Division.

“The climber had already done the hardest part of the route, but the rock is smooth granite with very little to hold on to,” Sgt. Alex Lopashanski of the New Hampshire Fish and Game office told Outside. “That can make it hard to figure out where to put your hands and feet next.”

Lopashanski coordinated the rescue from the ground.

“I honestly believe it was within his ability,” Lopashanski said. “It just wasn’t the day for it.”

Sliding Board, in Hale’s Location near North Conway in the White Mountains, is a multipitch route that climbers typically tackle with ropes and protective gear. Mountain Project’s description of the route lists fixed bolts, belay anchors, and placements for removable protection.

“This particular person didn’t have any gear with him because he was planning on going to the top. Obviously, this turned out to be a mistake,” Lopashanski said.

Whitehorse Ledge is known for its smooth, glacier-polished granite slabs, where obvious handholds can be hard to come by. Much of the climbing comes down to careful footwork and trusting your shoes to stick.

That slick stone has caught seasoned climbers before. According to an American Alpine Club accident report in October 2021, an experienced guide slipped while climbing Sliding Board and broke an arm and a finger before his partner helped him descend.

Recognizing his limits on the unfamiliar rock, Carron climbed back down to a small, relatively level spot where he could stand and wait, Lopashanski said. Fish and Game learned of his situation shortly after 8 A.M, according to its October 26 release.

When Lopashanski called to assess the situation, Carron told him he could wait there for hours.

“He had no equipment with him, so wasn’t clipped in or anything. But he was totally calm about it,” Lopashanski said.

Help was close by. Lopashanski called Mountain Rescue Service, a volunteer team of climbers and guides that handles technical mountain rescues. The cliff is near a golf course parking lot, and the team was based nearby, he said.

A total of nine team members climbed to Carron, secured him, and fixed ropes back to the cliff’s base, according to Fish and Game. North Conway Fire and Rescue also responded to the call.

Lopashanski described Carron’s position as relatively easy for the skilled rescuers to reach. “They make it look easy because for them it kind of is,” he said.

Carron and the entire rescue party were safely on the ground shortly before 10 A.M.

Getting safely to the ground is one thing, but in New Hampshire, the ride down can also come with an invoice. Under state law, Fish and Game can recover its reasonable response costs from people whose negligence made a rescue necessary.

Outside has previously reported on how officials assess preparedness when recommending those bills.

Carron will not be on the hook, however. A Fish and Game representative confirmed to Outside that the department does not plan to seek reimbursement for  the October 25 rescue. The agency did not specify the reasoning behind the decision.

#2189. W. Va. Law Restricting Disclosing Home Addresses and Phone Numbers of Judges and Police Struck Down, but …

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

the court suggests that a narrower law, which only requires removal and nondisclosure after the plaintiff makes a specific demand, may be constitutional.

From today's opinion in Jackson v. Thomson Reuters Am. Corp., by Fourth Circuit Judge Paul Niemeyer, joined by Judges Steven Agee and Pamela Harris; the law covers :

West Virginia's Daniel's Law …, which is designed "to enhance the safety and security" of judicial and law enforcement officials, prohibits the disclosure by any person or entity of the home addresses or unpublished telephone numbers of … {"any active, formerly active, or retired judicial officer, prosecutor, … public defender, … or law-enforcement officer"} if a reasonable person would believe that such a disclosure would create a risk of harassment or harm. The law authorizes protected persons to bring a civil action for damages….

As distinct from the Daniel's Laws in other States, … West Virginia's version does not require that the Covered Person, upon discovering an improper disclosure, notify the person or entity that disseminated the information and request that the person or entity delete the information before liability arises. In addition, for liability to arise under West Virginia's version, the person or entity need not know that they have disclosed a Covered Person's information. The only limiting principle of § 5A-8-24(e) liability is that the information must have been disclosed "under circumstances in which a reasonable person would believe that providing such information would expose another to harassment or risk of harm to life or property." …

For generations now, home addresses and telephone numbers have been disseminated to the public, initially through Whitepages and Yellow Pages and now through "people search" services on the Internet. The public has thus long had the facility to look up the name of a person and obtain that person's address and telephone number. Sharing that information has been the gateway to communication and the exchange of ideas or messages that have long been protected by the First Amendment. In the digital age, such information may now be shared with greater efficiency, but the tradition continues with the understanding that it is protected speech.

In a similar vein, it has long been custom and practice to record for public examination the names and addresses of persons buying, selling, and financing real property. And it has been the tradition to share publicly information about taxes, appraisals, liens, and voting.

To be sure, while the dissemination of such information is protected speech necessary to the exchange of ideas, it can also contribute to abuse and the pursuit of harmful and, indeed, malicious ends, as exemplified by the murder of Daniel Anderl. To address such abuse, governments have enacted laws like Daniel's Law in an effort to restrict the dissemination of home addresses and unpublished telephone numbers of specified public officials who serve in roles important to society. And the parties here agree that those governments have a compelling interest in enhancing the safety and security of such officials. The task, therefore, is to draw an appropriate line between protecting the dissemination of such information while, at the same time, excepting from dissemination the same information when it creates a potential for harm of specified public officials.

Because of the high value that our Constitution and longstanding practices place on the free dissemination of information and the exercise of free speech, we draw the line with the greatest caution, requiring that governments regulate speech about the home addresses and telephone numbers of designated public officials in a manner that is the least restrictive of speech.

In this case, West Virginia was fully justified in attempting to regulate such speech in light of the compelling government interest in enhancing the safety and security of certain public officials, but its efforts reached more broadly then necessary to accomplish the task; it did not employ the least restrictive means of doing so. For that reason and only that reason, we agree with the district court that West Virginia could have regulated more narrowly to accomplish its purpose and thereby be less restrictive of what is otherwise protected speech. Thus, we agree that the provision of West Virginia's Daniel's Law at issue here … violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments.

The court concluded that the law is a speech restriction, not just a restriction on "data":

[Jackson argues] that speech must be communicative such that it "convey a particularized message" with "the likelihood … that the message would be understood by those who" received it. Since Daniel's Law regulates disclosures of "pure facts" that lack a particularized message, he argues, the law is not subject to any First Amendment analysis….

[But] the Supreme Court has clearly held that the scope of "speech" regulated by the First Amendment is broader and includes data, reasoning that facts are "the beginning point for much of the speech that is most essential to advance human knowledge and to conduct human affairs." Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc (2011). Indeed, the Court has noted more specifically that statutes like West Virginia's Daniel's Law, which address only the disclosure of information, involve the regulation of speech protected by the First Amendment, observing, "If the acts of 'disclosing' and 'publishing' information do not constitute speech, it is hard to imagine what does fall within that category …." Bartnicki v. Vopper (2001).

The court then concluded that the law was content-based:

West Virginia's Daniel's Law …, on its face, distinguish[es] between different disclosures based on communicative content alone. It restricts disclosure of certain kinds of information (home addresses and unlisted telephone numbers), and it does so for certain individuals (judicial and law enforcement officers and their families)….

This means, for example, that a disclosure that a grocery store clerk lives at 100 Main Street is permitted, while a disclosure that a police officer lives at 100 Main Street is prohibited. The only difference between the two disclosures is the communicative content, or what the disclosures mean. In the hypothetical, the latter disclosure is prohibited because it identifies 100 Main Street as being the home address of the police officer, while the earlier disclosure is permitted because it identifies 100 Main Street as belonging to the grocery store clerk.

The court held that the law wasn't limited to "commercial speech" (which generally refers to commercial advertising), a category of speech that is entitled to lower protection:

While a speaker's economic motivation for speech may be a relevant factor in determining whether speech is commercial, economic motivation alone is insufficient to justify a categorization of that speech as commercial. Furthermore, because, even under Jackson's argument, only some of the regulated speech would be commercial, that fact does not render the law subject to the intermediate scrutiny of commercial speech. When a statute "applies with equal force to both commercial and noncommercial messages" with "no exception for noncommercial speech," the fact that some of the regulated speech is commercial does not justify an application of intermediate scrutiny….

And the court held that the law, as a content-based speech restriction, had to pass strict scrutiny, which is to say that (among other things) it had to be the "least restrictive means" of serving a "compelling government interest"; and the court held that the law failed such scrutiny:

[T]he parties agree, as do we, that the law's purpose of enhancing the safety of judicial and law enforcement officials is a compelling government interest …. [But] West Virginia's Daniel's Law does not employ the least restrictive means of serving the law's purpose.

Section 5A-8-24(e) imposes liability on a data broker immediately upon its disclosure of addresses and telephone numbers, regardless of whether the data broker had knowledge that the information related to Covered Persons. Whenever an entity such as a data broker collects names and addresses of all persons in a particular town using public land records and then publishes that information in some form of a directory, it undoubtedly violates the law because that directory includes, albeit unknowingly, information about, for instance, a police officer or public defender…. When a restriction on speech creates liability without a knowledge requirement, speakers are understandably incentivized to engage in self-censorship to avoid inadvertently incurring liability. See Counterman v. Colorado (2023) (noting that "prohibitions on speech have the potential to chill, or deter, speech outside their boundaries")….

Jackson argues, however, that the law is much more limited because it imposes liability only if "a reasonable person would believe that providing such information would expose another to harassment or risk of harm to life or property."

That provision, however, is hardly limiting, … because every disclosure of a person's name and address on the Internet exposes that person to harassment or a risk of harm…. [Indeed, in his complaints, Jackson] alleged that the mere disclosure of home addresses and unpublished telephone numbers "exposed [him] and his peers to harassment and/or risk of harm to life or property." And to support that unremarkable proposition, he quoted the FTC's report on "Data Brokers," which highlights the "potential risks to consumers from data brokers' collection and use of consumer data," including the fact that "people search products can be used to facilitate harassment, or even stalking, and may expose domestic violence victims, law enforcement officers, prosecutors, public officials, or other individuals to retaliation or other harm." Indeed, Jackson added that the FTC's report explains why the defendants' disclosures are "so dangerous." Thus, the argued "limitation" in § 5A-8-24(e) is actually not limiting but universal, as it is inevitable that every disclosure of personal information on the Internet poses an additional risk of harassment or harm to the person whose information is disclosed.

Additionally, States other than West Virginia that have enacted Daniel's Laws have uniformly required that, before liability attaches to a disclosure of home addresses and unpublished telephone numbers, Covered Persons give notice of their request that the data broker (or any other disclosing party) remove the information. Only on the failure to comply does liability attach. But West Virginia's Daniel's Law alone does not require notification as a precondition of liability. Thus, this aspect of the law fails to provide the least restrictive means of furthering the law's compelling interest.

While we hasten to note that requiring notice prior to imposing liability is one method for narrowing the law, it is not the only way. But it is the way that Congress and virtually every other State has chosen to narrow their laws and render them less speech-restrictive.

At bottom, we agree with the district court's conclusion:

[D]ue to its lack of a notice requirement, [§ 5A-8-24(e)] of West Virginia's Daniel's Law is far more restrictive then its comparators [in other States] and is definitively not the least restrictive means of achieving its compelling interest. Section [5A-8-24(e)] of West Virginia's Daniel's Law therefore fails strict scrutiny even as other more narrowly tailored federal and state statutes aiming at the same compelling interest pass constitutional muster….

The paragraph endorsing the district court's conclusion strongly suggests that the panel endorsed laws limited to situations where a covered person first demands that covered material be removed. But it's not clear whether the panel would endorse broad versions of such laws, which apply even when the address or phone number is relevant to a matter of public concern (see Kratovil v. City of New Brunswick (N.J. 2025), which upheld such a broad law), or whether it would insist that there be an exception for such public-concern situations (as there is in the federal version of Daniel's Law).

The speed with which these damage reports began to appear — some within a few hours — suggests this is a manufacturing or assembly issue, not wear. It’s a complicated hinge made up of more than 100 components, so there’s a lot of scope for things to go wrong.

There was a flurry of activity over the weekend related to what is, rather predictably, being called “Crackgate.” The teardown experts at iFixit have been busy — they completely disassembled a Duo, built a 3D model of the hinge, and identified several ways the hinge mechanism could be damaging the inner display.

It could be that the flexible display is coming into contact with a support bar that doesn’t retract properly, or that a rail that moves during folding is brushing against the screen, causing a pressure hotspot. Finally, the damage appears to start where a lot of edges, components, and moving parts converge, and that convergence could be stressing the display more than expected.
