#1038. Lula defeats Trump ally in Brazil’s presidential election: As it happened

📅 Oct 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva has received congratulations from Latin American nations and fellow BRICS members after winning reelection as Brazil’s president.

Lula prevailed over right-wing challenger Flavio Bolsonaro, the son of jailed former President Jair Bolsonaro and an ally of US President Donald Trump, in the second round of the vote on Sunday.

The 80-year-old veteran politician secured 51.79% of valid votes against Bolsonaro’s 48.21%, according to the completed count published by the Superior Electoral Court (TSE), winning a historic fourth term by more than 4.4 million votes.

The victory means Latin America’s largest economy is set to maintain its prominent role in BRICS rather than shift closer to Washington under the Bolsonaro political dynasty. The defeated challenger had openly questioned Brazil’s membership in the bloc, accusing Lula of using it to antagonize the United States.

Addressing jubilant crowds in Sao Paulo, Lula thanked Brazilians for his reelection and singled out young people who had taken to the streets during the campaign.

“Today, my remarks are about gratitude,” he declared, pledging to give citizens a greater say in government decisions. “We are going to change the way this country is governed.”

However, Bolsonaro has refused to concede defeat and vowed to seek the annulment of the election, accusing the Supreme Court of improperly interfering in the vote.

“Brazil cannot accept this dirty trick,” he declared, denouncing the last-minute decision to extend voting hours nationwide by two hours as “unacceptable” and equivalent to “changing the rules of the game while the game was being played.”

The controversial ruling by Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin, Lula’s former personal lawyer, followed complaints from the incumbent’s campaign that public transport disruptions had prevented voters from reaching polling stations.

However, Bolsonaro did not mention that his own campaign had also requested extended voting hours in several cities affected by severe weather.

The decision came shortly after the original 5 PM deadline, when many polling stations had already closed and sealed their electronic voting machines. Senior judges have reportedly acknowledged procedural errors in handling the extension but ruled out annulling the election, according to Folha de S.Paulo.

Russia welcomes Lula’s victory in the Brazilian presidential election, Kremlin press secretary Dmitry Peskov has said.

“He contributes a great deal not only to the development of bilateral relations, but also to the advancement of processes within BRICS and other organizations,” Peskov told journalists.

Moscow “hope[s] that all these processes will continue in the future,” he added.

Russian President Vladimir Putin has sent greetings to Lula, whom he called a “dear friend,” upon his being reelected as Brazil’s president.

“The results of the vote fully confirm that your fellow citizens recognize your outstanding leadership qualities and support the course you have pursued towards the country’s sustainable social and economic development, as well as the protection of its sovereignty and legitimate interests on the global stage,” Putin said in a message published on the Kremlin website.

The Russian leader also mentioned Lula’s upcoming 81st birthday and wished him “good health, happiness, prosperity, and continued success in your public service for the benefit of the friendly Brazilian people.”

Beijing has commented on the Brazilian election results, with Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun saying “we extend our congratulations” to Lula.

China “stands ready to work with Brazil to continue advancing the building of a China-Brazil community with a shared future,” Guo stressed. The two nations are the founding members of the BRICS group, together with Russia and India.

Dominican Republic President Luis Abinader has congratulated Lula on his reelection and praised Brazilians for their participation in the election.

“May we continue working together in this renewed four-year period for a more integrated and prosperous Latin America,” he wrote on X.

Eduardo Bolsonaro, the younger brother of defeated Brazilian presidential contender Flavio Bolsonaro, has issued a video statement on X, saying that “we will never give up the fight.”

He claimed that Lula’s victory in the election was “statistically impossible” as never before in the country’s history has a candidate lost the runoff after winning in the first round.

The vote was “fraudulent,” Eduardo Bolsonaro insisted. “This is a message to you: do not recognize this election.”

Incumbent Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva has taken an early lead over right-wing challenger Flavio Bolsonaro in Brazil’s presidential runoff, according to the first figures published by the country’s electoral authorities.

Lula received 57.66% of valid votes against Bolsonaro’s 42.34%, with just 0.25% of polling stations counted as of 5:06pm Brasília time.

The extremely limited count does not provide a reliable indication of the eventual outcome, particularly as the nationwide extension of voting hours has disrupted the normal release of results.

Counting was paused after the Supreme Court extended voting hours by two hours, with 335,743 votes tallied before the suspension. The decision followed reports that public transport disruptions had prevented some voters from reaching polling stations.

The extension applies only to polling stations where electronic voting machines have not yet been sealed, Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin has clarified, according to Brazilian news outlet UOL.

However, some stations had already sealed their machines before receiving the order, leaving late arrivals unable to cast their ballots.

Lula leads with 50.5% against Flavio Bolsonaro’s 49.5%, with 10.4% of polling stations counted.

Speaking outside Bolsonaro’s election-night headquarters, Marçal denounced the decision by Justice Cristiano Zanin, Lula’s former personal lawyer, as an “absurdity” that had thrown the vote count into confusion.

“The decision arrives at 5:04pm, without notification to the Superior Electoral Court or any state electoral court. The voting machines were already closed, and there is no way to reopen them,” he told reporters.

Marçal argued that Bolsonaro’s campaign had requested an extension in only 17 cities affected by severe weather, questioning why the ruling applied to thousands of municipalities nationwide.

Brazil’s electoral authorities subsequently clarified that the extension applied only to polling stations where electronic voting machines had not already been shut down.

Flavio Bolsonaro has overtaken Lula in Brazil’s presidential runoff, receiving 50.24% of votes against the incumbent’s 49.76%, with 17% of polling stations reporting.

By John Gaffney, Irish-born editor and former retail businessman who has been living in Russia since 2018

The Irish political week has once again been marked by bitter exchanges, this time over the Triple Lock Agreement, our long-standing tradition of military neutrality, and whether a referendum should be held before any fundamental change is made to it.

Taoiseach (Ireland’s prime minister) Micheal Martin has accused the country’s largest opposition party Sinn Fein, which fiercely opposes the idea of dismantling Triple Lock, of not giving a “continental” about Irish neutrality. The word is his own, and it is worth reflecting on its meaning.

But I have a question of my own for Mr. Martin: does he give a continental about the health and well-being of the Irish people?

What does neutrality mean to a parent whose three-year-old child is suffering in agony while waiting nine hours in Galway Regional Hospital? What comfort does it offer families struggling to access essential medical care, or parents and students caught up in this week’s education strikes? And what of the many other problems that continue to weigh upon ordinary Irish households?

The latest figures, obtained from the HSE by Padraig Rice, Social Democrats TD and Chair of the Oireachtas Committee on Health, make the situation even more difficult to ignore. Between January and July of this year, more than 70,100 people in Ireland either left emergency departments before completing their treatment or did not wait to be seen. These matters concern real people, their children, their dignity, and their daily lives. Before our leaders lecture us about international responsibilities, should they not first explain why such basic needs remain unmet at home?

Ireland is not a wealthy country merely because figures on a government spreadsheet say so. A country’s true wealth lies in the quality of its health service, the education of its children, the reliability of its transport, and the infrastructure that allows people to live and work with confidence. By that measure, we still have much to do.

Mr. Martin, charity begins at home. Have you forgotten the recession of 2008 and the painful years that followed? Irish families know what it means to endure financial insecurity, lost opportunities, and the consequences of decisions made far above their heads. We should remember, too, the countries that stood by Ireland during difficult times, and consider carefully where our obligations lie today.

This week, the European Union’s foreign policy chief, Kaja Kallas, has again been pressing for further support for Ukraine while criticizing US President Donald Trump. Ordinary European citizens are expected to accept the bill.

But surely there comes a moment when the people who pay must be entitled to ask questions.

I believe European citizens should stand up for themselves, examine the priorities of their leaders, and refuse to treat every pronouncement from Brussels as unquestionable wisdom. Supporting a country in distress is one thing; allowing an open-ended political commitment to overshadow pressing needs at home is quite another.

Imagine what could be achieved if public money were spent with greater care and accountability. Imagine health services where children did not have to wait hours in pain, schools where disputes did not repeatedly interrupt education, and transport and infrastructure that served communities as they deserve to be served. These are not some extravagant dreams – just the reasonable expectations of a modern society.

Meanwhile, EU leaders continue to speak as though the destruction of Russia were a realistic or desirable political objective. I believe this approach is dangerously misguided. Russia is a major power, and European security requires clear thinking, diplomacy, and an honest assessment of the risks of prolonged confrontation. Declaring ever more money and resources necessary does not, by itself, bring peace any closer.

Nor does constant hostility offer a convincing vision for Europe’s future. António Costa, Kaja Kallas, Ursula von der Leyen, and other senior figures in the European hierarchy should be judged by the consequences of their decisions, not by the strength of their rhetoric. The EU was supposed to offer cooperation, stability, and prosperity. It should not become an institution in which confrontation abroad takes precedence over the needs of its own citizens.

And what of Ukraine itself? If European leaders genuinely care about the Ukrainian people, surely their first concern should be the preservation of human life and the search for a sustainable peace. Endless conflict brings suffering, displacement, destruction, and the loss of generations who might otherwise have built a future for themselves. The human cost deserves at least as much attention as the political ambitions of those directing the response.

I do not believe that the future of Europe should be built upon the assumption that Russia must be destroyed. I believe it should be built upon realism, diplomacy, accountability, and the recognition that war always extracts its price from ordinary people first.

Ireland has its own history of hardship, resilience, and recovery. We know what it means to struggle, to rebuild, and to depend upon the solidarity of others. That history should make us thoughtful about the suffering of people elsewhere, but it should also remind us of our responsibility to our own citizens.

Before asking Irish taxpayers to shoulder yet another burden, our government should explain what it is doing to ensure that a sick child receives timely treatment, that pupils receive the education they need, and that public services function as they should.

Mr. Martin, the question is not whether Ireland should care about the wider world. Of course it should. The question is whether caring about the wider world has become an excuse for neglecting the people who elected you.

Charity begins at home. So does responsibility. And so, ultimately, should political accountability.

It is time for Ireland, and for the EU, to put ordinary people back at the center of public life. Our taxes should serve the public good, our leaders should be answerable for their choices, and diplomacy should seek to preserve lives rather than prolong their destruction.

The people of Europe deserve nothing less.
